The humpback whale (Megaptera novaeangliae) is a species of baleen whale found in oceans and seas around the world. Adults range in length from about 14 to 17 metres and weigh up to 40 tonnes. The species is distinguished by its very long pectoral fins — the longest of any whale, at up to a third of body length — its knobbly head, and its acrobatic surface behaviour, including breaching. Humpbacks are famous for the complex songs sung by males during the breeding season.

A humpback breaching — the species is known for energetic surface displays.

Behaviour and feeding

Humpbacks feed mainly on krill and small schooling fish. They are known for cooperative "bubble-net" feeding, in which a group of whales blows a curtain of bubbles to corral prey before lunging through the concentrated school. Populations migrate thousands of kilometres between cold, productive feeding grounds at high latitudes and warm breeding and calving grounds in tropical waters, fasting for much of the breeding season.

Song

Male humpbacks sing long, structured songs composed of themes repeated in cycles lasting from minutes to over an hour. All males in a population sing broadly the same song, which changes progressively over time; neighbouring populations can exchange song "revolutions" that spread across ocean basins. The function of song — most likely related to mating — remains debated.

Conservation

Commercial whaling reduced humpback numbers drastically; some populations fell by more than 90 per cent. A moratorium on humpback hunting was adopted by the International Whaling Commission in the 1960s, and most populations have since recovered substantially, leading the IUCN to assess the species globally as Least Concern, though some populations, such as that in the Arabian Sea, remain endangered. Current threats include entanglement in fishing gear, ship strikes, ocean noise, and climate-driven prey shifts.
