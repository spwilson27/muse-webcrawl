C-41 process

The C-41 process is a chromogenic photographic film development process introduced by Kodak in 1972 as the standard method for processing colour negative film. Virtually all colour negative films, regardless of manufacturer, are designed to be developed in C-41 chemistry, which made possible the worldwide network of one-hour photo minilabs.

Steps

At the standard temperature of 38 °C (100 °F), the process consists of: a colour developer (about 3 minutes 15 seconds), in which the reducing agent CD-4 develops exposed silver halide and oxidised developer reacts with dye couplers in the film to form colour dyes; a bleach, which converts metallic silver back to silver halide; a fixer, which removes all silver halide; washing; a stabiliser bath; and drying. The orange mask visible in negatives comes from coloured couplers that correct dye imperfections.

Variants and use

Black-and-white chromogenic films such as Ilford XP2 are also processed in C-41. Photographers sometimes deliberately develop E-6 slide film in C-41 chemistry, a technique called cross-processing, producing negatives with shifted colours and high contrast.
