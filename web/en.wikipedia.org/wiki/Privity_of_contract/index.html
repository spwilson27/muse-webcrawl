Privity of contract

The doctrine of privity of contract is a principle of common law contract law according to which a contract cannot confer rights on, or impose obligations on, a person who is not a party to it. Only the parties to a contract may sue or be sued on it, even where the contract was made for the third party's benefit.

[Image: Parties and third parties in contract]

Development

The modern English doctrine was consolidated in Tweddle v Atkinson (1861), where a man could not enforce a promise made between his father and his prospective father-in-law to pay him money on his marriage, and was firmly restated by the House of Lords in Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co Ltd v Selfridge & Co Ltd [1915], linking privity to the requirement of consideration: only a person who has provided consideration may enforce the bargain.

Exceptions and reform

The strict rule produced hardship and many workarounds: agency, assignment, collateral contracts, trusts of the contractual benefit, and actions in negligence or under specific statutes (for example, third-party rights under insurance and carriage-of-goods legislation). Lord Denning attacked the doctrine in several judgments, though the House of Lords reaffirmed it in Midland Silicones Ltd v Scruttons Ltd [1962]. Statutory reform followed the Law Commission's 1996 report: the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 now allows a third party to enforce a term where the contract expressly provides for it or the term purports to confer a benefit on an expressly identified third party, unless the parties intended otherwise. Comparable reforms exist in many Commonwealth jurisdictions, and US law has long recognised the intended third-party beneficiary.

Privity of estate

A related but distinct doctrine, privity of estate, governs the relationship between parties standing in the relationship of landlord and tenant under the same lease, allowing covenants that "touch and concern" the land to be enforced between them even without a direct contract.
