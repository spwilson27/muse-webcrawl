Cholera

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal infection caused by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae, contracted by drinking water or eating food contaminated with faeces. Its toxin hijacks intestinal cells into pouring out water and salts, producing profuse 'rice-water' stools; without treatment, dehydration can kill within hours. With prompt oral rehydration therapy — water with salts and sugar — mortality falls below 1%.

Cholera has caused seven recorded pandemics since 1817, spreading from its home in the Ganges delta. The 1854 London outbreak, in which John Snow traced cases to the Broad Street pump, became a founding story of epidemiology and public health. Today cholera signals broken water and sanitation systems — in refugee camps, war zones and the poorest cities — and remains endemic in parts of Africa and Asia, including a long-running epidemic in Yemen.

Control

Safe water, sanitation and hygiene are the definitive controls; oral cholera vaccines provide short-term protection in outbreaks and are stockpiled internationally. Surveillance and rapid rehydration centres are the core of outbreak response. The global roadmap aims to reduce cholera deaths by 90% by 2030.

Science

V. cholerae was isolated by Koch (1884, after Pacini's earlier observation). The cholera toxin's mechanism — locking a G protein 'on' so intestinal cells secrete chloride and water — is a classic of molecular medicine and underlies the design of oral rehydration solution, arguably the simplest life-saving therapy ever devised.
