Vaccine
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

A vaccine is a biological preparation that provides active acquired immunity to a particular infectious disease. It typically contains an agent resembling the disease-causing microorganism — weakened or killed microbes, their toxins or surface proteins, or (in newer platforms) genetic instructions such as mRNA — which stimulates the immune system to recognise and destroy the real pathogen later.

The term derives from variolae vaccinae (cowpox), reflecting Edward Jenner's 1796 demonstration that cowpox protected against smallpox. Vaccination has eradicated smallpox, nearly eradicated polio, and dramatically reduced measles, diphtheria, tetanus and many other diseases; vaccine hesitancy and access remain major public-health challenges. Types include live-attenuated, inactivated, subunit/conjugate, toxoid, viral-vector and mRNA vaccines.

How vaccines work

Vaccines expose the immune system to antigens, prompting antibody production and immunological memory in B and T cells, so a later real infection is met rapidly. Population-level 'herd immunity' protects those who cannot be vaccinated when coverage is high enough.
