Escapement

An escapement is the mechanism in a mechanical clock or watch that performs two functions: it advances the gear train by a fixed amount at regular intervals (letting the stored energy "escape" in controlled steps, producing the ticking sound), and it gives impulses to the timekeeping element — a pendulum or balance wheel — to replace the energy it loses to friction, keeping it swinging. The escapement is thus the link between the oscillator, which measures time, and the gear train, which counts and displays it.

History

The earliest European clocks (c. 1300) used the verge escapement, with a crown-shaped wheel engaging a vertical shaft with pallets; it was inaccurate but serviceable for centuries. The breakthrough came after Christiaan Huygens applied the pendulum (1657): the anchor escapement (c. 1670, associated with Robert Hooke and William Clement) allowed long, accurate pendulum clocks. The deadbeat escapement of George Graham (1715) removed recoil, and precision regulators used refinements such as the grasshopper escapement of John Harrison.

Watch escapements

Portable watches required escapements for a balance wheel. After cylinder and duplex designs, the Swiss lever escapement (developed from Thomas Mudge's 1754 design) became dominant: a jewelled lever, rocked by the balance, alternately locks and releases the escape wheel while delivering impulses through its pallet stones. Modern variants include Omega's co-axial escapement (George Daniels, commercialised 1999), which reduces sliding friction and lubrication needs.

Accuracy

An ideal escapement would impulse the oscillator exactly at the centre of its swing and otherwise leave it undisturbed ("free" escapement). In practice escapements are the main source of rate error in mechanical timekeepers; their design dominated horological research from the 17th to the 20th century, until quartz oscillators displaced mechanical escapements for precision timekeeping.
