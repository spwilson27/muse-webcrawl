Blue sky law

A blue sky law is a state law in the United States that regulates the offering and sale of securities in order to protect the public from fraud. Though the specific provisions of these laws vary among states, they all require the registration of securities offerings and sales, as well as of stock brokers and brokerage firms. Each state's blue sky law is administered by its appropriate regulatory agency, and most also provide private causes of action for private investors who have been injured by securities fraud.

History

Kansas enacted the first modern blue sky law in 1911, and many other states followed over the next two decades, before federal securities regulation arrived with the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. The term is commonly attributed to a remark by Kansas banking commissioner J. N. Dolley and was popularized in the 1917 U.S. Supreme Court cases upholding the laws, in which Justice Joseph McKenna referred to speculative schemes that have "no more basis than so many feet of 'blue sky'."

Relationship to federal law

Blue sky laws operate alongside federal securities laws. The National Securities Markets Improvement Act of 1996 preempted state registration requirements for many nationally traded "covered securities," though states retain antifraud authority and notice-filing requirements. Offerings not covered by federal exemptions generally must be registered or exempt under each state's law where they are sold.

Registration and exemptions

States typically allow registration by coordination (for offerings registered federally), by qualification (a full merit review), or by notification. Common exemptions include private placements, offerings to institutional investors, and isolated non-issuer transactions.
