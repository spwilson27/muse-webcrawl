Lamassu

Lamassu
CultureAssyrian (ancient Mesopotamia)

FunctionProtective deity; palace/city gate guardian

A lamassu (Akkadian: lamassu; Sumerian: lamma) is a protective deity in ancient Mesopotamian religion, most famously depicted in Assyrian palace sculpture as a colossal hybrid creature with the head of a human, the body of a bull or lion, and the wings of an eagle. The combination symbolised intelligence, strength, and freedom or dominion over the air.

Guardian colossi

In the Neo-Assyrian Empire (c. 9th–7th centuries BC), pairs of monumental lamassu — carved from single blocks of alabaster and weighing tens of tonnes — flanked the gateways of palaces and cities, including Ashurnasirpal II's palace at Nimrud, Sargon II's palace at Dur-Sharrukin (Khorsabad), and Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh. They were apotropaic guardians, intended to repel evil and impress visitors. A celebrated carving convention gave the Khorsabad bulls five legs, so that the figure appears complete both when viewed from the front (standing) and from the side (striding).

Legacy

Nineteenth-century excavations carried colossal lamassu to the Louvre, the British Museum, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the Oriental Institute in Chicago, where they became iconic emblems of Assyrian art. The destruction of lamassu and other antiquities at Nimrud and the Mosul Museum by the Islamic State in 2015 drew worldwide condemnation, and a lamassu sculpture by Michael Rakowitz, made from date-syrup cans, stood on the Fourth Plinth in London's Trafalgar Square from 2018 to 2020 as a memorial to Iraq's lost heritage.
