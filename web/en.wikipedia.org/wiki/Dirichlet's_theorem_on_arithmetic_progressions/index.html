Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions

In number theory, Dirichlet's theorem, also called the Dirichlet prime number theorem, states that for any two positive coprime integers a and d, there are infinitely many primes of the form a + nd, where n is a non-negative integer. In other words, the arithmetic progression a, a + d, a + 2d, ... contains infinitely many prime numbers whenever a and d have no common factor greater than 1.

History

The result was conjectured by Legendre and proved by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet in 1837; his proof, using mathematical analysis applied to number theory, founded analytic number theory. Dirichlet introduced characters and L-functions — now called Dirichlet characters and Dirichlet L-functions — and showed that L(1, χ) ≠ 0 for non-principal characters χ, the key step in the argument.

Distribution

Stronger forms, such as the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions, state that the primes are asymptotically equally distributed among the φ(d) reduced residue classes modulo d. The Green–Tao theorem on arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions of primes builds conceptually on this circle of ideas.
