Loki is a god in Norse mythology, son of the giant Fárbauti and Laufey, and blood-brother of Odin. A shapeshifter and trickster, he alternately helps and hinders the gods: he is responsible for many of their treasures — Odin's spear Gungnir, Thor's hammer Mjölnir, the ship Skíðblaðnir — obtained through his schemes, and also for escalating mischief that ends in catastrophe.

Myths

Loki's exploits, preserved chiefly in the Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (13th century), include transforming into a mare and giving birth to Odin's eight-legged horse Sleipnir; cutting off Sif's golden hair and replacing it with dwarf-forged gold; and tricking the blind god Höðr into killing the beloved Baldr with mistletoe. In the flyting poem Lokasenna he insults the assembled gods in turn. With the giantess Angrboða he fathered the wolf Fenrir, the world-serpent Jörmungandr and Hel, ruler of the dead — the monsters bound until Ragnarök.

Ragnarök and afterlife

As punishment for Baldr's death, Loki was bound in a cave with a serpent dripping venom onto his face, his wife Sigyn catching the drops in a bowl; his writhing was said to cause earthquakes. At Ragnarök he breaks free, steers the ship of the dead against the gods, and kills and is killed by the watchman Heimdall. Loki's nature — god, giant, villain, or necessary agent of change — is debated by scholars; no clear evidence of a historical cult of Loki survives. In modern culture he is best known through Wagner, comics, and the Marvel films, which have made him one of the most recognisable Norse figures.
