Biosimilar

Biosimilars are highly similar versions of approved biological medicines.

A biosimilar (also known as a follow-on biologic) is a biological medicine that is highly similar to, and has no clinically meaningful differences from, an already approved reference product. Unlike generic versions of small-molecule drugs, biosimilars cannot be identical copies, because biological medicines are large, complex molecules produced in living cells and are inherently variable.

Approval pathways

Because exact replication is impossible, regulators require an extensive comparability exercise: analytical studies, animal studies where needed, and clinical trials demonstrating equivalent safety, purity and potency. The European Union created the first biosimilar pathway in 2005 and approved the first biosimilar, a somatropin product, in 2006. In the United States, the Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act of 2009 created an abbreviated pathway under the Public Health Service Act; the first US biosimilar, filgrastim-sndz, was approved in 2015. Some US biosimilars receive an additional "interchangeable" designation, allowing pharmacy-level substitution subject to state law.

Significance

Biologic medicines — including monoclonal antibodies for cancer and autoimmune disease, and insulins — account for a large and growing share of drug spending. Biosimilars typically enter the market at prices 15 to 35 percent below the reference product and stimulate price competition, with substantial projected savings for health systems; adoption has varied widely by country and product class.
