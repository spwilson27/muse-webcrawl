Program music

Program music or programme music is a type of instrumental art music that attempts to musically render an extramusical narrative. The narrative itself might be offered to the audience through the piece's title, or in the form of program notes, inviting imaginative correlations with the music. A paradigmatic example is Hector Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique (1830), in which the composer provided an accompanying text describing the artist-protagonist's opium-induced dreams.

History

Descriptive music existed long before the term: Vivaldi's The Four Seasons (1725) was published with sonnets describing each season, and Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony (1808) depicts scenes in the countryside — though Beethoven insisted it was "more expression of feeling than painting". The term "program music" is often credited to Franz Liszt, who also developed the single-movement symphonic poem (tone poem) in works such as Les préludes.

The program vs absolute music debate

In the 19th century the concept became central to the so-called "War of the Romantics". Advocates of program music (Liszt, and later Richard Strauss, whose tone poems include Don Juan, Till Eulenspiegel and Also sprach Zarathustra) faced critics such as Eduard Hanslick, who argued that music's beauty is specifically musical and defended "absolute music" — music appreciated for its formal structure alone. The program symphony, exemplified by Berlioz and by Tchaikovsky's later symphonies, flourished alongside the debate.
