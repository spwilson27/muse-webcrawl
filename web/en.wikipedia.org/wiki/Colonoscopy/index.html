Colonoscopy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Colonoscopy allows direct visual inspection of the large intestine.

Colonoscopy is the endoscopic examination of the large bowel and the distal part of the small bowel with a camera on a flexible tube passed through the anus. It can provide a visual diagnosis of colorectal cancer, polyps, ulceration, and inflammatory bowel disease, and it gives the opportunity for biopsy or removal of suspicious lesions.

Procedure

Before the examination the colon must be emptied, typically by drinking a strong osmotic laxative preparation the day before. The patient is usually sedated. The endoscopist advances the colonoscope to the cecum and, in many cases, into the terminal ileum, carefully inspecting the mucosa during withdrawal. Polyps can be snared and removed (polypectomy), bleeding vessels cauterised, and tissue samples taken. The procedure generally takes 20 to 60 minutes.

Uses

Colonoscopy is the reference standard for colorectal cancer screening and for investigating rectal bleeding, chronic diarrhoea, unexplained anaemia, and abdominal symptoms. Removing adenomatous polyps during colonoscopy reduces the incidence of colorectal cancer. Guidelines in the United States recommend average-risk screening beginning at age 45, with colonoscopy every ten years one of several accepted options. "Virtual colonoscopy" by CT imaging is an alternative that cannot remove lesions.

Risks

Complications are uncommon but include perforation of the colon (roughly 1 in 1,000–2,000 procedures, more often after polypectomy), post-polypectomy bleeding, and adverse reactions to sedation. Bowel preparation can cause dehydration and electrolyte disturbance in vulnerable patients.
