Political anthropology is a subdiscipline of anthropology that studies power, authority, leadership, and governance across cultures, with special attention to societies without centralised states and to how politics operates in everyday life. It emerged as a distinct field in the 1940s with works such as African Political Systems (1940), edited by Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard, which compared stateless segmentary societies like the Nuer with centralised kingdoms like the Zulu.

Deliberation in a stateless society: a classic subject of political anthropology.

Development

Early political anthropology classified political systems into typologies — band, tribe, chiefdom, state (Elman Service) — and examined how kinship, ritual, and law maintained order without governments. From the 1960s, scholars such as Georges Balandier and the Manchester School shifted focus to process, conflict, colonialism, and historical change. Pierre Clastres's Society Against the State (1974) argued that some Amazonian societies actively ward off centralised power.

Contemporary themes

Modern political anthropology studies the modern state ethnographically — bureaucracy, borders, elections, NGOs, and development — as well as resistance and domination, notably in James C. Scott's work on everyday resistance and stateless "Zomia". It overlaps with legal anthropology, the anthropology of policy, and political science.
