Intertemporal choice

Intertemporal choice is the study of how people make decisions about what and how much to do at various points in time, when choices at one time influence the possibilities available at other points in time. Such choices involve trade-offs between costs and benefits occurring at different times, and are studied in economics, psychology, and neuroscience.

Discounting

People generally value a reward less the later it arrives — a phenomenon called delay discounting or time preference. Standard economic models, following Paul Samuelson's 1937 discounted-utility model, assume exponential discounting, in which preferences are time-consistent. Empirically, however, people discount hyperbolically: they are impatient over short delays but patient over long ones, producing preference reversals that exponential models cannot explain.

Behavioural findings

Intertemporal choices are affected by framing, magnitude (larger rewards are discounted less steeply), sign (losses are discounted less than gains), and emotion. Hyperbolic discounting helps explain procrastination, addiction, undersaving, and other self-control problems, and motivates commitment devices that constrain one's future choices.

Applications

The framework is applied to savings and retirement policy, health behaviour, environmental economics (where the social discount rate shapes climate policy), and the design of incentives in organisations.
