The École des Beaux-Arts (formally the École nationale supérieure des Beaux-Arts, ENSBA) is a French grande école whose primary mission is to provide high-level fine-arts education and training. Located in the Saint-Germain-des-Prés quarter of Paris, across the Seine from the Louvre, it descends from the royal academies of painting, sculpture and architecture founded in the 17th century and for two centuries set the standard for academic art and architecture education worldwide.

The glass-roofed courtyard of the Palais des Études, heart of the Paris school.

History

The school traces its origins to the Académie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture founded in 1648 and the Académie Royale d'Architecture of 1671. Suppressed during the Revolution, the academies' teaching was reorganised in 1817 into a single École des Beaux-Arts. Its buildings on the Left Bank, assembled under architects François Debret and Félix Duban, include the Palais des Études, whose glazed courtyard displays casts of classical and Renaissance sculpture used for student study.

Beaux-Arts method and influence

Training centred on drawing from casts and the live model, and on competitions, culminating in the Prix de Rome, which sent winners to study at the French Academy in Rome. Architecture students worked in ateliers under patron architects and learned to compose monumental, classically ordered plans — the "Beaux-Arts style" that shaped civic architecture in France, the United States and beyond; many American architects trained in Paris and carried the method home. The architecture section was separated from the school after the upheavals of May 1968, and today's ENSBA concentrates on the visual arts, offering studios in painting, sculpture, photography, video and multimedia.

Notable alumni

Alumni and teachers include painters such as Edgar Degas, Claude Monet (briefly), Georges Seurat and Henri Matisse, sculptors including Auguste Rodin (as teacher of drawing) — though Rodin himself was famously rejected as a student — and architects such as Charles Garnier, designer of the Paris Opéra. Schools named École des Beaux-Arts were also founded in many French provincial cities, several of which survive as regional art schools.
