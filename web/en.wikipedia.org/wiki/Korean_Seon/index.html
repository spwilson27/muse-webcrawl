Korean Seon

Seon (Korean: 선, 禪) is the Korean tradition of Chan Buddhism — the meditative school of Mahayana Buddhism known in Japan as Zen and in China as Chan. Introduced to Korea from China beginning in the Unified Silla period (7th–9th centuries), Seon became one of the defining traditions of Korean Buddhism.

History

Early Seon in Korea was associated with the "Nine Mountains" schools (Gusan Seonmun), lineages founded by masters returning from Tang China. During the Goryeo dynasty, the eminent monk Jinul (1158–1210) reformed Korean Seon, founding the community at Songgwangsa and teaching an approach that reconciled meditation with doctrinal study (gyo) and promoted hwadu (critical phrase) meditation — the distinctively Korean development of gongan (koan) practice. His near-contemporary systematisation still shapes Korean practice today.

Under the Joseon dynasty Buddhism was officially suppressed in favour of Neo-Confucianism, and Seon survived in mountain monasteries. In the 20th century masters such as Seongcheol revived strict retreat practice, and the Jogye Order — today the largest Korean Buddhist order — maintains Seon as its core discipline.

Practice

The heart of Korean Seon is sitting meditation (chamson) centred on the hwadu, typically the question "What is this?" Monastic life follows cycles of intensive retreat (gyeolje) in meditation halls during the summer and winter seasons, interspersed with periods of freer travel and study. Since the late 20th century, temple-stay programmes and international teachers — most famously Seung Sahn, founder of the Kwan Um School of Zen — have carried Korean Seon practice abroad.
