Reliabilism

Reliabilism, a category of theories in the philosophical discipline of epistemology, has been advanced both as an account of knowledge and of justified belief. Its central idea is that a belief is justified, or constitutes knowledge, when it is produced by a process that reliably yields true beliefs — whether or not the believer can articulate or even access the reasons supporting it.

Process reliabilism

The most influential version, developed by Alvin Goldman beginning in the 1970s, holds that a belief is justified if and only if it is produced by a reliable cognitive process — one that tends to produce a high proportion of true beliefs. Perception, memory and good reasoning count as reliable processes; wishful thinking, guessing and believing on the basis of a coin flip do not.

Criticisms

Reliabilism faces several well-known objections, including the "new evil demon" problem (a victim of a Cartesian demon whose experiences are systematically false seems justified even though their processes are unreliable in that world), the generality problem (how, exactly, a belief-forming process is to be individuated and typed is unclear), and clairvoyance counterexamples in which a reliable but bizarre process produces beliefs the subject has every reason to distrust.
