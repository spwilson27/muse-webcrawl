Money laundering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Money laundering is the process of illegally concealing the origin of money obtained from illicit activities — such as drug trafficking, corruption, embezzlement or gambling — by converting it into a legitimate source. The canonical model runs in three stages: placement (introducing illicit funds into the financial system), layering (complex transactions, shell companies, trade mis-invoicing and jurisdiction-hopping to obscure the trail) and integration (spending the "cleaned" wealth on assets, businesses or lifestyle).

The global regime

Modern anti-money-laundering (AML) law was built for the drug war and vastly expanded after 2001 to counter-terrorist financing: the Financial Action Task Force (FATF, founded 1989) sets 40 Recommendations, mutual-evaluates countries, and grey- and black-lists laggards; banks carry know-your-customer and suspicious-activity reporting duties backed by franchise-threatening penalties (multi-billion-dollar fines for sanctions and AML failures), creating a compliance industry and a de-risking problem (exclusion of whole countries, charities and remittance corridors). Estimated laundered flows (the UN's often-cited 2–5 per cent of global GDP) dwarf interdictions — commonly estimated at well under one per cent of flows seized — a gap that fuels both cynicism and the reform agenda: public beneficial-ownership registers, real-estate transparency, and attention to professional enablers.
