Changquan (simplified Chinese: 长拳; traditional Chinese: 長拳; pinyin: Chángquán; literally "Long Fist") refers to a family of external (wa-jia) martial arts styles from northern China. The forms of the Long Fist style emphasize fully extended kicks and striking techniques, and by appearance would be considered a long-range fighting system.

History

The core of Changquan was developed in the 10th century by Zhao Kuangyin, the founding emperor of the Song dynasty (960). Originally called Taizu Changquan ("Taizu Long Fist"), the style was later modified by later dynasties. During the Ming dynasty, general Qi Jiguang wrote extensively about the style, and the Shaolin Temple became a major centre for its practice and development.

Changquan became one of the core styles of modern wushu, codified by the Chinese Wushu Association in the 1950s and 1960s. It serves as the basis for the compulsory taolu (forms) performed in international wushu competition.

Techniques

The style is known for its long, extended movements, combining fast and powerful hand techniques with agile footwork, high jumps, and flexible body movements. Basic techniques include the five stances (bow stance, horse stance, drop stance, empty stance, and rest stance), kicks such as the front stretch kick, side kick, tornado kick, and lotus kick, and hand forms including fist, palm, and hook.

Emphasis is placed on accuracy, balance, and the coordination of the whole body. The saying associated with the style is that the practitioner should be "fast as the wind, still as a nail, heavy as a mountain, and light as a leaf."

Modern practice

Today Changquan is practiced worldwide, both as a competitive sport and as a traditional martial art. In wushu taolu competitions, athletes perform compulsory and optional routines that include aerial techniques such as the butterfly kick and jumping front kick. Changquan-derived forms are also used in the training of actors for martial arts cinema.
