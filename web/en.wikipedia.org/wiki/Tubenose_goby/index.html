The tubenose goby (Proterorhinus semilunaris), also called the western tubenose goby, is a small species of goby native to the fresh and brackish waters of the Black Sea and Caspian Sea basins in Eastern Europe. It takes its name from its elongated, tube-like nostrils, which project forward over the upper lip. It is one of the Ponto-Caspian gobies that have spread as invasive species through European waterways and into the North American Great Lakes.

Description

Tubenose gobies are small, bottom-dwelling fish, typically 6–9 cm (2.4–3.5 in) long and rarely exceeding 13 cm. The body is mottled brown-olive with dark blotches, the pelvic fins are fused into a suction disc like other gobies, and the anterior nostril is extended into a short tube hanging over the mouth. Unlike the closely related round goby, the tubenose goby lacks a prominent black spot on the first dorsal fin, having instead a pattern of crescent-shaped markings (reflected in its specific name semilunaris, "half-moon").

Distribution and invasion

Native to rivers such as the Danube, Dnieper, and Don and to the freshened margins of the Black and Caspian seas, the tubenose goby expanded westward up the Danube with canals and shipping, reaching the Rhine and the Netherlands in the 2000s. In North America it appeared in the St. Clair River and western Lake Erie in the early 1990s, almost certainly via ballast water, around the same time as the round goby; its spread in the Great Lakes has been slower and more localised than that of the round goby, favouring shallow, vegetated, slow-moving waters.

Ecology

The species feeds on aquatic insect larvae, small crustaceans (especially amphipods), and molluscs. Males guard eggs laid in cavities under stones or other hard substrate, as in other gobies. Its ecological impacts outside its range are considered modest compared with the round goby, but it competes with native benthic fishes such as darters and sculpins.
