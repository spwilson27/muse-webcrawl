Postage stamp

A postage stamp is a small piece of paper issued by a postal authority, usually adhesive on one side, that is purchased and displayed on an item of mail as evidence of payment of postage. Stamps are typically printed with a denomination, a design, and the name of the issuing country.

History

The first adhesive postage stamp, the Penny Black, was issued by the United Kingdom on 1 May 1840 as part of Sir Rowland Hill's postal reforms, which introduced uniform prepaid postage. The stamp bore the profile of the young Queen Victoria. Other countries quickly followed; the United States issued its first stamps in 1847, and by the 1860s stamps were in use worldwide.

Design and production

Stamps have been produced by engraving, lithography, photogravure, and offset printing. Designs commonly feature heads of state, national symbols, wildlife, landscapes, and commemorations of events and people. Most stamps are perforated to separate easily from sheets, though early issues were imperforate and cut with scissors. Modern stamps increasingly use pressure-sensitive adhesive instead of water-activated gum.

Collecting

Stamp collecting, or philately, became a popular hobby in the nineteenth century. Rare stamps, printing errors, and early issues can command high prices; famous rarities include the British Guiana 1c magenta and the Inverted Jenny. Collectors study stamps' paper, watermark, perforation, and postmarks as well as their designs.
