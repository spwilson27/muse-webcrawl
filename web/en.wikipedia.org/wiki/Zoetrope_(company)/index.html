American Zoetrope (also known as Zoetrope Studios) is an American film production company founded in 1969 by filmmakers Francis Ford Coppola and George Lucas. Based in San Francisco's Sentinel Building, the studio was created as an independent alternative to the Hollywood studio system, a place where young filmmakers could experiment outside the control of the major studios. It is named after the zoetrope, an early animation device; a zoetrope given to Coppola by filmmaker Mogens Skot-Hansen inspired the name.

History

The studio's first productions included George Lucas's THX 1138 (1971) and Coppola's The Conversation (1974). American Zoetrope produced or co-produced Coppola's acclaimed films of the 1970s, including The Godfather series and Apocalypse Now (1979), and financed early works by directors such as John Milius and Carroll Ballard. The studio also pioneered electronic cinema techniques, including early computer-assisted editing and previsualization.

Financial troubles and later work

The costly failure of Coppola's musical One from the Heart (1982), shot at the company's Los Angeles Zoetrope Studios facility, forced the company into bankruptcy proceedings and the sale of its Hollywood studio lot. American Zoetrope nonetheless continued producing Coppola family films, including works by Sofia Coppola such as Lost in Translation (2003), as well as The Virgin Suicides, Marie Antoinette, and Coppola's later features including Tetro and Twixt.

Legacy

Films produced by American Zoetrope have won numerous Academy Awards, and the company's stable of sound and post-production talent contributed to innovations such as Walter Murch's sound design. The studio remains a family-owned enterprise of the Coppola family and a landmark of the New Hollywood era.
