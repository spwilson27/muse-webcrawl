Tenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Tenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, the last of the Bill of Rights, was ratified on December 15, 1791. It expresses the principle of federalism: powers not granted to the federal government, nor prohibited to the states, are reserved to the states or the people.

"The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people."

Background

The amendment answered Anti-Federalist demands for an explicit reservation of powers; it echoes Article II of the Articles of Confederation, though it omits the word "expressly" before "delegated" — an omission James Madison defended and which later allowed implied federal powers.

Interpretation

In United States v. Darby (1941) the Supreme Court called the amendment a "truism". It gained force in later federalism cases: National League of Cities v. Usery (1976), New York v. United States (1992) and Printz v. United States (1997), which developed the anti-commandeering doctrine barring Congress from compelling state legislatures and executives to implement federal programs, and NFIB v. Sebelius (2012) on coercive conditions attached to federal spending.
