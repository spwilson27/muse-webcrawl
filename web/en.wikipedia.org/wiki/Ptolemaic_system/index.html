The Ptolemaic system is the geocentric model of the universe developed by the Alexandrian astronomer Claudius Ptolemy in his treatise the Almagest (c. AD 150). It placed a stationary Earth at the centre of the cosmos, with the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn revolving around it in that order, and the sphere of the fixed stars beyond. Assembled from Greek geometric astronomy reaching back to Apollonius and Hipparchus, Ptolemy's system predicted planetary positions with remarkable accuracy and remained the authoritative cosmology in the Islamic world and Christian Europe for more than 1,400 years.

Geometric devices

To reconcile circular motion with planets' varying speeds and retrograde loops, Ptolemy combined three devices. The deferent is a large circle centred near (but displaced from) the Earth; the epicycle is a small circle whose centre travels on the deferent while the planet travels on the epicycle; and the equant is a point, offset from the deferent's centre, from which the epicycle centre's motion appears uniform. The equant violated the classical dogma of uniform circular motion about the centre — a tension that troubled later astronomers, including Copernicus, and spurred reform.

Reception

Transmitted through Arabic commentaries and improved at observatories such as Maragha (where Ibn al-Shatir's models removed the equant), the system underpinned astrology, calendar-making and navigation. Nicholas Copernicus's De revolutionibus (1543) proposed a heliocentric alternative of comparable complexity; over the following century the observations and arguments of Tycho Brahe, Kepler (whose elliptical orbits discarded epicycles) and Galileo displaced the Ptolemaic system among astronomers, and Newton's gravitation supplied the physical explanation that geocentrism lacked.
