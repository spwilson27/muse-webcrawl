Richard Rado FRS (28 April 1906 – 23 December 1989) was a German-born British mathematician who made fundamental contributions to combinatorics and graph theory. A frequent collaborator of Paul Erdős, several major results bear their joint names.

Life

Rado was born in Berlin and studied mathematics at the University of Berlin and Göttingen, taking his doctorate in 1933 under Issai Schur. As a Jew, he left Nazi Germany that year with the help of a scholarship arranged through G. H. Hardy, and settled in England. After positions at Cambridge (Fitzwilliam College), Sheffield and King's College London, he became professor at the University of Reading in 1954, where he remained until his retirement in 1971. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1978.

Work

With Erdős and Chao Ko, Rado proved the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem (published 1961, though proved in 1938), a cornerstone of extremal set theory stating that intersecting families of k-element subsets of an n-element set, for n at least 2k, are largest when they consist of all sets containing one fixed element. With Erdős he proved the Erdős–Rado theorem in Ramsey theory, giving bounds for partition relations on infinite sets, and developed the theory of partition calculus.

The Rado graph (or random graph) — the unique countable graph containing every finite graph as an induced subgraph, and arising almost surely from random edge choices — was studied by Rado in 1964 and is named after him. He also worked on transversal theory (matroids), where his results extended Hall's marriage theorem, and on greedy algorithms.
