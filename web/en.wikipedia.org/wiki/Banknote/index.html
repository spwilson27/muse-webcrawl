Banknote

A banknote — also called a bill, paper money, or simply a note — is a type of negotiable instrument, originally a promissory note issued by a bank, payable to the bearer on demand. Together with coins, banknotes form the physical cash of modern economies, and today they are almost universally issued by central banks as legal tender.

History

Paper money originated in China: merchants of the Tang dynasty used private credit notes, and under the Song dynasty the state issued jiaozi notes in Sichuan in the eleventh century, centuries before paper currency appeared in Europe. European banknotes began as handwritten deposit receipts and promissory notes; the Stockholms Banco in Sweden issued the first European printed banknotes in 1661, and the Bank of England (founded 1694) gradually regularised its notes, printing fixed denominations from 1725. Through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, state monopolies on note issue replaced the earlier free-banking systems in most countries.

Materials and security

Traditional banknotes are printed on cotton-fibre paper, chosen for durability and feel. Since Australia issued the first circulating polymer note in 1988, many countries — including Canada, the United Kingdom, and much of Latin America and Asia — have adopted plastic (biaxially oriented polypropylene) notes, which last longer and resist counterfeiting. Modern security features include watermarks, security threads, intaglio printing, holograms and optically variable inks, microprinting, see-through registers, and the EURion constellation that deters digital copying.

Collecting

The study and collecting of paper money is called notaphily. Rare notes — misprints, low serial numbers, and issues of defunct states and failed banks — can command prices far above face value.
