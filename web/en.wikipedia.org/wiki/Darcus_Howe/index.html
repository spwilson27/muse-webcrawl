Darcus Howe (26 February 1943 – 1 April 2017) was a Trinidad-born British writer, journalist, broadcaster and civil liberties campaigner. A central figure in the Black British political movement of the 1970s and 1980s, he edited the journal Race Today, was a defendant in the Mangrove Nine trial, and helped organise the Black People's Day of Action march in 1981, a landmark protest against police racism and the handling of the New Cross house fire.

Early life

Howe was born in Moruga, Trinidad, the son of an Anglican priest who later became a headteacher. He emigrated to Britain in 1961, intending to study law, but left his studies to work in journalism and politics, becoming involved with the British Black Power movement and the circle around the Trinidadian historian C. L. R. James, a family connection by marriage.

The Mangrove Nine and Race Today

In 1970 Howe was one of nine Black activists tried at the Old Bailey over protests against repeated police raids on the Mangrove restaurant in Notting Hill. All were acquitted of the principal charge of incitement to riot, and the trial famously led the judge to acknowledge "evidence of racial hatred" on both sides. Howe subsequently became editor of Race Today, turning it into the house journal of Black radical politics in Britain.

1981 and broadcasting career

After the New Cross fire of January 1981, in which thirteen young Black people died, Howe helped organise the Black People's Day of Action, which brought some 20,000 people onto the streets of London. In later decades he became a television broadcaster, presenting the Channel 4 series Black on Black and The Devil's Advocate, and wrote a long-running column for the New Statesman. He returned to Trinidad in his later years but died in London in 2017.
