Liouville's theorem (complex analysis)

In complex analysis, Liouville's theorem, named after Joseph Liouville (although the theorem was first proven by Cauchy in 1844), states that every bounded entire function must be constant. That is, every holomorphic function f for which there exists a positive number M such that |f(z)| ≤ M for all z in the complex plane is a constant function.

Proof

The theorem follows from Cauchy's integral formula for the derivative: for a circle of radius R about a point a, |f′(a)| ≤ M/R. Since R may be taken arbitrarily large while the bound M stays fixed, f′(a) = 0 for every a, so f is constant.

Consequences

Liouville's theorem provides a short proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra: if a non-constant polynomial had no complex root, its reciprocal would be a bounded entire function, hence constant — a contradiction. More generally, an entire function with polynomial growth is a polynomial (a consequence of the Cauchy estimates). The theorem fails for real differentiable functions: the real function sin(x) is bounded and non-constant, illustrating how much rigidity holomorphy imposes.

Generalizations

The result generalizes to bounded holomorphic functions on many complex manifolds and underlies the related Picard theorems: a non-constant entire function can omit at most one complex value.
