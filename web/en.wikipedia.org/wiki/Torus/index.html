Torus

In geometry, a torus (plural tori, colloquially doughnut) is a surface of revolution generated by revolving a circle in three-dimensional space one full revolution about an axis that is coplanar with the circle. The torus is the surface of the solid known as a toroid, shaped like a ring doughnut, an inner tube, or a bagel.

The torus is a fundamental object in topology: it is the product space of two circles, S1 × S1, has genus 1, and has Euler characteristic 0. It admits a flat metric, and its fundamental group is Z × Z.

A ring torus: a circle of radius r revolved about an axis at distance R from its centre.

Definition

Let R (the major radius) be the distance from the centre of the tube to the centre of the torus, and r (the minor radius) the radius of the tube. Three types arise depending on the relative sizes of R and r:

  • Ring torus (R > r): the familiar doughnut shape with a hole.
  • Horn torus (R = r): the hole closes to a single point at the centre.
  • Spindle torus (R < r): the surface self-intersects, resembling an apple.

A point on the torus can be parametrised by two angles θ and φ:
x = (R + r cos θ) cos φ,   y = (R + r cos θ) sin φ,   z = r sin θ.

Properties

By Pappus's centroid theorem, the surface area of a ring torus is A = 4π²Rr and the volume of the solid toroid is V = 2π²Rr². The torus is a quartic (degree-four) algebraic surface.

Topologically, the torus can be obtained from a square by identifying opposite edges without reversal. This "flat torus" model is used, for example, in video games with wrap-around screens. Unlike the sphere, the torus admits a Riemannian metric of zero Gaussian curvature, and it can be smoothly embedded in four-dimensional space as the Clifford torus. Every closed orientable surface is a connected sum of tori; the torus itself is the genus-1 case.

Colouring maps on a torus may require as many as seven colours (the Heawood number for genus 1), a fact proved by the Ringel–Youngs theorem.

Generalizations

The n-dimensional torus Tn = (S1)n is the product of n circles; it arises in dynamical systems (invariant tori of integrable systems, and in KAM theory), in Fourier analysis, and as the configuration space of periodic systems. Higher-genus surfaces are informally called multi-tori (e.g. "double torus" for genus 2).

Applications

Toroidal geometry appears in toroidal inductors and transformers, in the tokamak and stellarator designs for magnetic-confinement fusion (where the plasma is held in a torus), in tires and O-rings, and in architecture and sculpture. In architecture, torus also names the large convex moulding at the base of a classical column.
