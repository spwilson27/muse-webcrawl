Negative (photography)

In photography, a negative is an image, usually on a strip or sheet of transparent plastic film, in which the lightest areas of the photographed subject appear darkest and the darkest areas appear lightest. This reversed order occurs because the extremely light-sensitive chemicals a camera film must use to capture an image fast enough for ordinary picture-taking are darkened, rather than bleached, by exposure to light and the subsequent photographic processing.

In the case of colour negatives, the colours are also reversed into their respective complementary colours. Typical colour negatives have an overall dull orange tint due to an automatic colour-masking feature that ultimately results in improved colour reproduction.

History

The first photo negative was created by the French scientist Nicéphore Niépce in 1826, though it was William Henry Fox Talbot who, in the late 1830s and early 1840s, pioneered the negative-positive process with his calotype process, in which a paper negative was used to print positive images on salt print paper. Once a negative is created, it can be used to create multiple positive prints through contact printing or with an enlarger.

Use

Negatives are normally used to make positive prints on photographic paper by projecting the negative onto the paper with a photographic enlarger or making a contact print. The paper is also darkened in proportion to its exposure to light, so a second reversal results which restores light and dark to their normal order.
