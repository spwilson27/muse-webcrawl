Highland Clearances

The Highland Clearances (Scottish Gaelic: Fuadaichean nan Gàidheal, "the expulsion of the Gaels") were the evictions of a significant number of tenants, predominantly from the Scottish Highlands, largely during the 18th and 19th centuries. The removals cleared land of people primarily to allow for the introduction of sheep pastoralism, which was much more profitable than mixed farming.

Background

After the Jacobite rising of 1745, the clan system was dismantled: heritable jurisdictions were abolished, tartan was proscribed for a time, and chiefs increasingly behaved as commercial landlords rather than patriarchs. Rising prices for wool and mutton, and later for kelp and cattle, made it profitable to replace the many small tenancies of the runrig townships with large sheep farms let to a few tenants, often from the Lowlands.

The clearances

Clearances varied from negotiated resettlement on small coastal crofts to brutal forced evictions. The removals on the Sutherland estate (1807–1821), managed by factor Patrick Sellar, became notorious; Sellar was tried and acquitted of culpable homicide after the death of an elderly woman in a burning cottage. The Highland Potato Famine of 1846–1856 accelerated emigration, much of it assisted — or compelled — by landlords. Tens of thousands emigrated to Canada, the United States, Australia and New Zealand.

Resistance and reform

Resistance culminated in the Crofters' War of the 1880s — rent strikes and land raids at places such as Glendale and Braes in Skye. The Napier Commission (1883) led to the Crofters' Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886, which granted security of tenure and fair rents to crofters, effectively ending the clearances.

Legacy

The Clearances remain a powerful and contested memory in Scotland and its diaspora, blamed for depopulating the Highlands and eroding Gaelic language and culture; townships of roofless ruins across the glens are their visible legacy.
