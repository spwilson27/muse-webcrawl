Sumi-e

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sumi-e (Japanese: 墨絵, "ink picture"), also called suiboku-ga (水墨画, "ink wash painting"), is a style of East Asian brush painting that uses black ink — as in East Asian calligraphy — in varying concentrations on paper or silk. It originated in China during the Tang and Song dynasties and was introduced to Japan in the fourteenth century by Zen monks, who practised it as a spiritual discipline as much as an art form.

Materials and technique

The painter grinds an ink stick (sumi) against an ink stone with a little water to produce ink of the required density; different dilutions yield a full range of tones from deep black to pale grey. Work is executed with animal-hair brushes on absorbent paper (washi) or silk. Because ink soaks in immediately, strokes cannot be corrected or painted over: every mark is final. The art therefore prizes spontaneity, economy of line and the expressive use of empty space (yohaku), relying on suggestion rather than detailed description.

Subjects and philosophy

Classic beginner exercises are the "Four Gentlemen" — bamboo, orchid, plum blossom and chrysanthemum — each teaching a distinct brushstroke and symbolising a season and a virtue. Landscapes, birds, fish and Zen subjects such as the ensō circle are also characteristic. In Japan, masters such as Sesshū Tōyō elevated suiboku-ga to the highest form of painting during the Muromachi period. The philosophy of sumi-e holds that the painting captures the spirit or inner essence of the subject, and that the preparation — grinding ink, quieting the mind — is part of the work itself.
