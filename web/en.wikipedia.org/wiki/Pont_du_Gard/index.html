The Pont du Gard is an ancient Roman aqueduct bridge built in the first century AD to carry water over 50 km (31 mi) to the Roman colony of Nemausus (Nîmes) in southern France. It crosses the river Gardon near the town of Vers-Pont-du-Gard. The Pont du Gard is the tallest preserved Roman aqueduct, as well as one of the best preserved, and was added to UNESCO's list of World Heritage sites in 1985.

Construction

The bridge has three tiers of arches: the lowest level carries a road and consists of six arches; the middle level has eleven arches; and the top level, which carries the water channel (specus), originally had 47 arches, of which 35 remain. It stands 48.8 metres high and was built from some 50,000 tonnes of limestone blocks, fitted together without mortar. The aqueduct's gradient is extremely shallow, dropping only about 25 centimetres over the length of the bridge, a testament to the precision of Roman engineering.

History

Construction was long attributed to Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, son-in-law of Augustus, around 19 BC, but later research suggests a date closer to the middle of the 1st century AD. The aqueduct supplied an estimated 40,000 cubic metres of water a day to the fountains, baths and houses of Nîmes. After the fall of the Roman Empire the bridge remained in use as a road bridge; in the 18th century it was widened for carriage traffic. Today it is one of France's most visited ancient monuments.
