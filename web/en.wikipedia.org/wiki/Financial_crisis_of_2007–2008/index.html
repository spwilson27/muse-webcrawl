The 2007–2008 financial crisis, often called the global financial crisis (GFC), was a severe worldwide economic crisis that began in the United States housing and mortgage markets and spread through the global banking system. It was the most serious financial crisis since the Great Depression and triggered the Great Recession, large falls in output and employment across advanced economies, and extensive government rescues of financial institutions.

Causes

In the early 2000s, low interest rates, abundant global capital and financial innovation fuelled a bubble in US house prices. Lending standards collapsed — including "subprime" and adjustable-rate mortgages made to borrowers with weak credit — while originators sold the loans into securitisation chains that packaged them as mortgage-backed securities and collateralised debt obligations, often carrying top credit ratings. Financial institutions held these assets with thin capital and heavy short-term borrowing, and derivatives such as credit default swaps multiplied exposure without reserves to back them. When house prices fell from 2006 and defaults rose, it became impossible to value the securities, and trust between banks evaporated.

Course of the crisis

The crisis is conventionally dated from 9 August 2007, when BNP Paribas froze three investment funds and interbank lending seized up. In September 2007 the British bank Northern Rock suffered the first run on a UK bank in over a century and was eventually nationalised. In March 2008 the investment bank Bear Stearns was rescued through a forced sale to JPMorgan Chase. The acute phase began in September 2008: the US government placed the mortgage giants Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac in conservatorship; on 15 September Lehman Brothers, laden with toxic real-estate assets, filed for bankruptcy — the largest in US history — and insurer AIG, crippled by credit default swaps, received an $85 billion Federal Reserve credit facility the next day. Money-market funds "broke the buck", stock markets crashed worldwide, and the US Congress, after an initial rejection, passed the $700 billion Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) on 3 October 2008. Governments in the UK and Europe recapitalised their banks, including the Royal Bank of Scotland and Lloyds/HBOS.

Aftermath and response

Central banks cut interest rates towards zero and began large-scale asset purchases ("quantitative easing"). The G20 coordinated fiscal stimulus at its London summit in April 2009. The downturn that followed — the Great Recession — drove US unemployment to 10 percent in October 2009 and caused millions of home foreclosures. Reform followed: the Dodd–Frank Act (2010) in the United States, higher capital requirements under Basel III, stress testing of banks, and new resolution regimes. In Europe, bank losses migrated onto sovereign balance sheets and fed into the European debt crisis of 2010–2012.
