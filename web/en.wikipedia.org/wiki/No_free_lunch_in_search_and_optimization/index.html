No free lunch in search and optimization

In computational complexity and optimisation, "no free lunch" is a result proved by David Wolpert and William Macready in 1995–1997: averaged uniformly over all possible objective functions (or all search problems), every search and optimisation algorithm performs identically. In particular, any two algorithms — including blind hill-climbing, genetic algorithms, and pure random search — yield exactly the same distribution of performance when their results are averaged over every possible problem.

Statement and intuition

The theorems are formalised for a finite search space and finite set of cost values, with algorithms that never revisit points. Because the space of all possible functions is dominated by functions with no exploitable structure, an algorithm that is cleverly adapted to one class of problems gains exactly offsetting losses on the complementary class. Superior performance on realistic problems is therefore only possible by using knowledge of the problem's structure — a "free lunch" never comes from the algorithm alone.

Implications

The results are commonly read as undercutting claims that any general-purpose optimiser (such as a genetic algorithm or simulated annealing) is universally superior, and as a theoretical justification for matching algorithms to problem domains — and for the field of metaheuristics to report the class of problems to which claims apply. They do not imply that all algorithms perform equally on real-world problems, since real problems occupy a minuscule, highly structured subset of all possible functions; subsequent work (for example by Droste, Jansen, and Wegener, and the "sharpened" results of Wolpert and Macready) has explored how far the conclusion extends to realistic problem classes.

In machine learning

A closely related family of "no free lunch" theorems (Wolpert, 1996) shows that, averaged over all possible target functions and data distributions, all learning algorithms have the same expected off-training-set error. This is often cited as a theoretical basis for the necessity of inductive bias and for empirical model selection such as cross-validation. The phrase itself derives from the saying "there ain't no such thing as a free lunch" (TANSTAAFL), popularised by Robert A. Heinlein and Milton Friedman.
