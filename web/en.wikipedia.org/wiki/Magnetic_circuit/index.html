Magnetic circuit

A magnetic circuit is made up of one or more closed loop paths containing a magnetic flux. The flux is usually generated by permanent magnets or electromagnets and confined to the path by magnetic cores consisting of ferromagnetic materials like iron, although there may be air gaps or other materials in the path. Magnetic circuits are employed to efficiently channel magnetic fields in many devices such as electric motors, generators, transformers, relays, lifting electromagnets, SQUIDs, galvanometers, and magnetic recording heads.

Analogy to electric circuits

The laws governing magnetic circuits resemble those of electric circuits. Magnetomotive force (MMF), measured in ampere-turns, plays the role of voltage; magnetic flux Φ the role of current; and reluctance the role of resistance. "Hopkinson's law", the magnetic analogue of Ohm's law, states that the MMF equals the flux multiplied by the reluctance: F = ΦR. In practical materials such as transformer cores, the analogy is complicated by saturation and hysteresis, but it remains a standard first-order design tool in electrical engineering.

Reluctance and materials

Reluctance depends on the geometry of the path and the permeability of the core material. High-permeability materials like electrical steel or ferrite carry flux with low reluctance, while air gaps — often introduced deliberately in inductors — have high reluctance and store most of the field energy. Magnetic circuits may be in series or parallel, and are analyzed with analogues of Kirchhoff's laws: the sum of fluxes at a junction is zero, and the sum of MMF drops around a loop equals the total ampere-turns applied.
