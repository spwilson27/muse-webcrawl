Private placement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A private placement is the sale of stocks, bonds, or other securities to a pre-selected group of investors and institutions, rather than to the public through an open-market offering. Private placements are exempt from the registration requirements that apply to public offerings, on the theory that the limited number of sophisticated investors involved can fend for themselves and obtain access to information.

United States

In the United States, private placements typically rely on the exemption in Section 4(a)(2) of the Securities Act of 1933 and Regulation D, which allows sales to an unlimited number of "accredited investors" and, in some cases, a limited number of non-accredited but sophisticated investors. Issuers generally file a Form D notice with the Securities and Exchange Commission after the first sale. Securities sold in a private placement are "restricted securities" and cannot be freely resold to the public; resale typically occurs under Rule 144 or in private transactions, or in sales to qualified institutional buyers under Rule 144A.

Advantages and disadvantages

Private placements can be completed more quickly and cheaply than public offerings, avoid some disclosure obligations, and allow terms to be negotiated with a small group of long-term investors such as pension funds and insurers. The trade-offs are reduced liquidity for investors, potentially less capital raised, and sometimes a discount in price relative to comparable public securities. Private placements are a major funding channel for mid-sized companies and for the private debt market.
