Clearcutting

Clearcutting, clearfelling, or clearcut logging is a forestry and logging practice in which most or all trees in an area are uniformly cut down. Along with shelterwood and seed tree harvests, it is used by foresters to create certain types of forest ecosystems and to promote select species that require an abundance of sunlight or grow in large, even-age stands. Logging companies and forest-worker unions in some countries support the practice for scientific, safety and economic reasons, while detractors consider it a form of deforestation that destroys natural habitats and contributes to climate change.

Effects

Clearcutting can have significant effects on the local environment, both for the habitat and for biodiversity. The removal of all trees in a single area destroys the physical environment for many species. Logging also removes habitat for species that use the trees, including a wide range of plants and animals, and the exposed soil is more vulnerable to erosion. Increased sediment and nutrient runoff can degrade nearby streams and aquatic habitat.

Clearcutting is also a major contributor to climate change when the harvested area is not allowed to regenerate, though in managed forestry the cleared stand is typically replanted or allowed to regrow.

Regeneration

After a clearcut, stands may be regenerated naturally from seed of surrounding trees, by planting seedlings, or by coppicing. Even-aged management based on clearcutting favours shade-intolerant species such as Douglas-fir, aspen, and lodgepole pine, whose seedlings require full sunlight.

Public perception

Clearcutting is one of the most controversial forestry practices, in part because a fresh clearcut is visually striking. Regulations in many jurisdictions now limit the size of clearcuts, require buffer strips along waterways, and mandate prompt reforestation.
