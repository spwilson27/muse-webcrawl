The Book of Psalms (from Greek ψαλμοί, psalmoi; Hebrew תְּהִלִּים, Tehillim, "Praises"), also known simply as the Psalms, is a book of the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament consisting of 150 individual psalms — religious songs, prayers and poems. It stands at the head of the Ketuvim ("Writings"), the third section of the Tanakh, and is the longest book of the Bible by chapter count. The psalms were composed over many centuries, from the early monarchy to the post-exilic period, and served as the hymnbook of the Second Temple in Jerusalem.

Structure

The Masoretic text divides the Psalter into five books, a structure often compared to the five books of the Torah, each closing with a doxology:

Book I1–41Psalm 41:13
Book II42–72Psalm 72:18–19
Book III73–89Psalm 89:52
Book IV90–106Psalm 106:48
Book V107–150Psalm 150 (whole psalm)

Numbering differs between the Hebrew/Greek traditions: the Septuagint and Vulgate combine and split certain psalms, so their numbering runs one behind the Hebrew for most of the book. The Eastern Orthodox canon includes a 151st psalm.

Authorship and composition

Superscriptions attribute 73 psalms to King David, others to Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses (Psalm 90) and others; modern scholarship treats the collection as an anthology assembled over centuries and edited into its present form in the Persian or early Hellenistic period. Psalm 137 ("By the rivers of Babylon") presupposes the exile of 586 BCE, while some hymns may preserve much older material.

Themes and genres

Form criticism (Hermann Gunkel and his successors) identifies recurring genres: hymns of praise, communal and individual laments (the largest group), songs of thanksgiving, royal psalms, wisdom psalms and the Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120–134) sung by pilgrims. Themes include the kingship of God, creation, covenant, the fate of the righteous and the wicked, and trust amid suffering.

Use in worship

The Psalms are central to both Jewish and Christian prayer. In Judaism the complete book is recited over a week or month in some traditions, and individual psalms punctuate daily liturgy. In Western Christianity the monastic Liturgy of the Hours distributed all 150 psalms across the week; the Anglican Book of Common Prayer provides for a monthly cycle, and a psalter — a physical volume of the Psalms — was for centuries among the most common devotional books.
