The Tale of Genji

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A scene from a Genji picture scroll (12th century): courtiers in layered robes in a palace interior, faces rendered in the hikime-kagihana style]

The Tale of Genji (Japanese: 源氏物語, Genji monogatari) is a classic of Japanese literature written by the court lady Murasaki Shikibu in the early eleventh century, in the flowering of Heian-kyō (Kyoto). Often called the world's first novel — and its first psychological novel — it runs to 54 chapters and over a thousand pages in translation, following the life, loves and descendants of Hikaru Genji, the shining prince, son of an emperor reduced to commoner status.

Genji's career — brilliant, amorous, exiled and restored — and after his death (which occurs between chapters, un-narrated) the sorrows of his supposed son Kaoru and grandson Niou, give the book its arc from splendour to impermanence, the Buddhist mono no aware, the sadness of things, that remains its keynote.

Authorship and world

Murasaki wrote in the kana script of court women (men wrote official Chinese), drawing on diary and tale literature to produce prose of extraordinary psychological finesse, interleaved with nearly 800 poems exchanged by the characters. The world it depicts — scent-guessing games, calligraphy judged like character, politics conducted through marriages — is the Heian court at its aesthetic zenith, drawn by an insider who served Empress Shōshi.

Texts and translations

No autograph survives; the text descends through medieval manuscripts and picture scrolls. Arthur Waley's pioneering English version (1925–33) made Genji a world classic; subsequent translations by Edward Seidensticker (1976), Royall Tyler (2001) and Dennis Washburn (2015) argue over how much of its formality and strangeness English should keep. In Japan it is the supreme classic, endlessly adapted to film, manga and opera.
