Malaria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Malaria is a mosquito-borne infectious disease caused by single-celled Plasmodium parasites, transmitted to humans through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. It causes fever, chills, headache and flu-like illness in cycles as the parasites multiply in red blood cells; P. falciparum malaria can progress rapidly to severe anaemia, cerebral malaria, coma and death, especially in young children.

Malaria still causes on the order of 600,000 deaths per year, overwhelmingly in sub-Saharan Africa and mostly in children under five, with roughly a quarter-billion cases annually. It has shaped human evolution — the sickle-cell trait and other red-cell variants persist because they protect carriers against it — and human history, influencing wars, settlement and the economics of the tropics.

Control and treatment

Prevention rests on vector control: insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor spraying, drainage, and now malaria vaccines (RTS,S, recommended by WHO in 2021, and R21) — the first vaccines against a human parasite. Artemisinin-based combination therapies are the standard treatment, though partial resistance in Southeast Asia and East Africa is monitored with concern. Quinine, from cinchona bark, was the historical remedy.

Eradication efforts

A mid-20th-century WHO eradication campaign eliminated malaria from many temperate regions but failed in much of Africa. Since 2000, funding through the Global Fund and national programmes cut deaths dramatically, though progress has plateaued; malaria remains endemic in around 80–90 countries.
