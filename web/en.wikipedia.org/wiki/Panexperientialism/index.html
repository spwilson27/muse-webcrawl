Panexperientialism is the philosophical view that experience is fundamental and universal — that all entities, at every level of nature, possess some form of experience. The term is most closely associated with the process philosophy of Alfred North Whitehead and its development by Charles Hartshorne and David Ray Griffin, who coined "panexperientialism" as a more precise replacement for the older term panpsychism.

Terminology

Griffin argued that "panpsychism" is misleading: it suggests that everything has a psyche or mind, including conscious thought, whereas the process view attributes to elementary entities only experience in a minimal sense — feeling, responsiveness, taking account of the past — not consciousness, which requires complex nervous systems. Panexperientialism thus holds that experience goes "all the way down", while consciousness does not.

Doctrine

In Whitehead's metaphysics, the basic units of reality are not inert material particles but "actual occasions" — momentary events of experience that prehend (feel) their predecessors and unify those feelings into a new standpoint, which then becomes data for successors. Matter is accordingly re-interpreted as societies of such occasions: an electron is an experient subject in an extremely trivial mode, and a human mind is a highly ordered society of occasions in which experience rises to consciousness. Panexperientialism is physicalist in the sense of taking experience to be natural, while rejecting the materialist claim that nature is "vacuous actuality" devoid of interiority.

Motivations and criticism

The view is offered as a solution to the hard problem of consciousness and the mind–body problem: if experience is fundamental, its emergence in brains requires no miraculous leap from wholly non-experiential matter. It also underlies process theology, in which God is the chief exemplification — not an exception — of metaphysical principles. Critics raise the "combination problem" (how micro-experiences yield unified subjects), question the empirical content of attributing experience to particles, and note the difficulty of reconciling the view with the conservation of energy and causal closure of physics.
