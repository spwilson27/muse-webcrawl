Deadbeat escapement

The deadbeat escapement is an escapement used in pendulum clocks, invented around 1715 by the English clockmaker George Graham, building on an earlier design by Richard Towneley from about 1675. It superseded the anchor escapement in precision clocks and remained the standard escapement for regulators and astronomical clocks into the twentieth century.

How it works

In the earlier anchor (recoil) escapement, after a tooth of the escape wheel lands on a pallet, the continuing swing of the pendulum pushes the wheel slightly backward — the "recoil" — which disturbs the pendulum and causes wear and rate error. In Graham's deadbeat design, the pallets have curved faces concentric with the pallet arbor: after the drop, the wheel tooth rests on this "dead" face and the wheel stays perfectly still during the rest of the pendulum's swing, with no recoil. This separation of the locking and impulse functions greatly improved timekeeping accuracy.

Use

Deadbeat escapements were used in the finest longcase "regulator" clocks, turret clocks, and observatory timekeepers. Their precision, combined with temperature-compensated pendulums, allowed the best pendulum clocks to keep time to within a second a week or better. A variant principle survives in some modern mechanical watch and clock designs.
