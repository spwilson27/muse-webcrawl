Porphyria

Porphyria is a group of disorders in which substances called porphyrins build up in the body, adversely affecting the skin or nervous system. The types that mainly affect the nervous system are known as acute porphyrias, and the ones that mainly affect the skin are known as cutaneous porphyrias. The disorders result from deficiencies of enzymes in the pathway by which the body makes heme, the iron-containing component of haemoglobin; the resulting accumulation of porphyrin precursors such as aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and porphobilinogen (PBG), or of porphyrins themselves, produces the symptoms.

Classification and types

Eight main types are generally recognised, each corresponding to a step in heme synthesis. The acute hepatic porphyrias include acute intermittent porphyria (AIP, the most common acute type), variegate porphyria, hereditary coproporphyria, and the very rare ALA dehydratase deficiency porphyria. The cutaneous porphyrias include porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT, the most common porphyria overall, usually associated with liver disease, alcohol, hepatitis C, or iron overload), erythropoietic protoporphyria, congenital erythropoietic porphyria, and hepatoerythropoietic porphyria. Most forms are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with low penetrance, though some are recessive or acquired.

Signs and symptoms

Acute attacks, typically triggered by certain drugs, alcohol, fasting, hormonal changes, or infection, cause severe abdominal pain, vomiting, constipation, rapid heart rate and high blood pressure, muscle weakness that may progress to paralysis, confusion, seizures, and hyponatraemia. During attacks the urine may darken or turn reddish-purple on standing — the colour that gives the porphyrins (from the Greek porphyra, purple) their name. Cutaneous forms cause fragile, blistering skin on sun-exposed areas (in PCT and related types) or immediate painful burning and swelling without blisters (in erythropoietic protoporphyria).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on measuring porphyrins and their precursors in urine, blood, and stool, supplemented by enzyme assays and genetic testing; urinary PBG is characteristically elevated during acute attacks. Management includes avoiding triggers and known precipitating drugs, treating attacks with intravenous hemin and high-dose glucose, managing pain and autonomic symptoms, and, for PCT, phlebotomy or low-dose hydroxychloroquine. The RNA-interference drug givosiran, approved in 2019, reduces attack frequency in acute hepatic porphyria by suppressing hepatic ALA synthase. With early recognition, most patients recover from attacks, though delayed treatment can cause permanent nerve damage.

History and culture

Porphyria has attracted wide cultural attention. Retrospective diagnoses have been proposed — controversially — for the "madness" of King George III, and historians have noted speculative links to the folklore of vampires and werewolves, though scholars generally regard these connections as unfounded. A notable founder effect of variegate porphyria exists among Afrikaners in South Africa, where the condition traces largely to a single 17th-century Dutch immigrant couple.
