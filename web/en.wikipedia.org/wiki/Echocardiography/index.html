Echocardiography, also known as cardiac ultrasound, is the use of ultrasound to examine the heart. It is one of the most widely used diagnostic tests in cardiology because it is non-invasive, portable, free of ionising radiation, and provides real-time images of cardiac structure and function. An echocardiogram displays the size and shape of the heart, the movement of its walls and valves, pumping capacity (ejection fraction), and blood flow measured with Doppler techniques.

Types

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) places the transducer on the chest wall and is the standard first-line test. A transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) passes a specialised probe down the oesophagus, giving clearer views of posterior structures such as the left atrium and mitral valve. Stress echocardiography images the heart before and after exercise or pharmacological stress to detect coronary artery disease. Other variants include fetal echocardiography, three-dimensional echo, contrast echo, and focused point-of-care studies.

History

The technique grew from the work of Swedish physician Inge Edler and physicist Carl Hellmuth Hertz in the 1950s, often called the fathers of echocardiography, who adapted industrial ultrasonic flaw detectors to record heart-valve motion.

Uses

Echocardiography diagnoses valve stenosis and regurgitation, heart failure, cardiomyopathies, pericardial effusion, endocarditis, congenital heart disease, and sources of embolic stroke.
