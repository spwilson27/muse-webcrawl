Nash embedding theorem

The Nash embedding theorems, named after mathematician John Forbes Nash Jr., state that every Riemannian manifold can be isometrically embedded into some Euclidean space. "Isometric" means preserving the length of every path. Intuitively, the theorem says that any abstract geometry defined by a metric tensor can be realised concretely as a surface (possibly highly crumpled and of high dimension) sitting inside a flat Euclidean space, without any stretching or shrinking.

Statements

Nash proved two theorems. The first (1954), for manifolds of class C1, gives an isometric embedding into Euclidean space of comparatively low dimension but allows the embedding to be merely once differentiable — producing surprisingly "wrinkled" embeddings, later developed by Nicolaas Kuiper. The second (1956), for Ck manifolds with k ≥ 3, requires a much higher-dimensional ambient space but preserves smoothness; its proof introduced Nash's hard implicit function theorem, an ancestor of the Nash–Moser iteration technique. For a compact n-dimensional manifold, Nash's smooth theorem gives an embedding in dimension n(3n+11)/2.

Significance

The theorems resolved a classical problem going back to Riemann and showed that the intrinsic theory of Riemannian geometry is no more general than the extrinsic geometry of submanifolds of Euclidean space. The counterintuitive C1 result seeded the modern theory of convex integration, applied by Gromov and others to many geometric problems.
