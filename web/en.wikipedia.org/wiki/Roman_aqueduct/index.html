Roman aqueduct

The Romans constructed numerous aqueducts (aquaeductus) to bring water from distant sources into their cities and towns, supplying public baths, latrines, fountains and private households. Roman aqueducts were built in all parts of the Roman Empire, from Germany to Africa, and especially in the city of Rome itself, where they formed an enormous system. At their height, Rome's eleven aqueducts supplied over one million cubic metres of water per day.

Construction

An aqueduct channel ran almost entirely by gravity along a very slight gradient, typically between 0.15 and 0.3 metres per kilometre. Most of the route was underground or at ground level in a covered masonry channel (specus); only where valleys had to be crossed did builders raise arcades or use inverted siphons of lead or stone pipe. Settling tanks (piscinae) removed sediment, and at the city the water entered a distribution tank (castellum divisorium) feeding lead pipes to districts, baths and fountains.

Major aqueducts of Rome

The first aqueduct, the Aqua Appia (312 BC), was followed by the Anio Vetus, Aqua Marcia, Aqua Tepula, Aqua Julia, Aqua Virgo, Aqua Alsietina, Aqua Claudia, Anio Novus, Aqua Traiana and Aqua Alexandrina. Sextus Julius Frontinus, appointed water commissioner in AD 97, described the system in detail in his treatise De aquaeductu.

Famous surviving examples

Surviving structures include the Pont du Gard in France, the Aqueduct of Segovia in Spain, the Valens Aqueduct in Istanbul, and long stretches of the Aqua Claudia and Anio Novus outside Rome.

Legacy

Although Germanic invasions and neglect destroyed most of the network, the Roman aqueduct set the standard for water engineering for more than a millennium, and several were restored to service in the Renaissance and early modern periods.
