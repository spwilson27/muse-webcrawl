Conn's syndrome

Conn's syndrome, or primary hyperaldosteronism, is a condition in which the adrenal glands produce excess aldosterone independently of the renin–angiotensin system, causing the kidneys to retain sodium and water and to excrete potassium. It is one of the commonest causes of secondary hypertension. The condition was described in 1955 by American endocrinologist Jerome W. Conn.

Causes

The principal causes are an aldosterone-producing adenoma of one adrenal gland (the classic Conn's syndrome) and bilateral adrenal hyperplasia; rarer causes include familial hyperaldosteronism and adrenal carcinoma. Somatic mutations in ion-channel and ATPase genes such as KCNJ5 are found in many adenomas.

Signs and diagnosis

Most patients have hypertension that may be resistant to treatment; low blood potassium, once considered typical, is present in only a minority. Screening is by the plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio, followed by confirmatory suppression tests and imaging; adrenal vein sampling distinguishes one-sided from bilateral disease.

Treatment

Unilateral adenomas are treated by laparoscopic adrenalectomy, which may cure or greatly improve the hypertension. Bilateral disease is managed with mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonists such as spironolactone or eplerenone.
