Soap opera

A soap opera, sometimes shortened to soap, is a genre of serialised drama, typically on television or radio, that follows the lives of a large ensemble cast and features multiple interwoven storylines focusing on emotional relationships. The genre originated on American radio in the 1930s, sponsored by soap and detergent manufacturers such as Procter & Gamble to advertise to housewives — hence the name.

Characteristics

Soap operas are characterised by open-ended, continuing narratives with no planned conclusion, frequent cliffhangers, and an emphasis on dialogue and personal relationships over action. Daytime soaps are traditionally filmed on videotape (later video) with interior sets, giving them a distinctive visual style. Plots are paced slowly with repetition so that viewers who miss episodes can follow.

History and examples

Early radio soaps included Painted Dreams (1930) and Irna Phillips's Guiding Light (1937), which later transferred to television and ran until 2009. Long-running television soaps include the American General Hospital, Days of Our Lives, The Young and the Restless and The Bold and the Beautiful; the British Coronation Street (since 1960) and EastEnders; and the Australian Neighbours and Home and Away. Prime-time soaps such as Dallas and Dynasty were worldwide phenomena in the 1980s, and Latin American telenovelas — which, unlike Anglo-American soaps, have a fixed ending — are exported globally.

Decline and legacy

Audiences for daytime soaps have declined sharply since the 1990s with changing work patterns and media fragmentation, and many long-running titles have been cancelled; only a handful of American daytime soaps remain. The genre's storytelling techniques — serial arcs and ensemble casts — have pervaded modern television drama.
