Traditional medicine

Traditional medicine (also known as indigenous medicine or folk medicine) comprises medical aspects of traditional knowledge that developed over generations within the folk beliefs of various societies, including indigenous peoples, before the era of modern medicine. The World Health Organization defines traditional medicine as "the sum total of the knowledge, skills, and practices based on the theories, beliefs, and experiences indigenous to different cultures, whether explicable or not, used in the maintenance of health as well as in the prevention, diagnosis, improvement or treatment of physical and mental illness".

Major systems

  • Traditional Chinese medicine — acupuncture, herbal medicine, tui na massage, and concepts of qi and yin–yang balance.
  • Ayurveda — the traditional medicine of India, emphasising balance among the three doshas and using herbal, dietary, and yogic practices.
  • Unani medicine — Greco-Arab humoral medicine preserved in South and Central Asia.
  • African traditional medicine — diverse systems combining herbalism with spiritual and community healing.
  • Indigenous medicines of the Americas and Oceania — including shamanic and plant-based practices.

Use and regulation

In many low- and middle-income countries, traditional practitioners are the main or only providers of health care for large parts of the population; the WHO estimates that a majority of the population in some Asian and African countries relies on traditional medicine for primary care. Many countries regulate traditional practitioners and products to varying degrees. In 2019 the WHO included a chapter on traditional medicine conditions in ICD-11, a step intended to improve statistical tracking and which proved controversial.

Scientific assessment

Some traditional remedies have yielded major modern drugs: artemisinin, developed from the Chinese herb Artemisia annua used for fever, earned Tu Youyou a share of the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, and aspirin, quinine, and morphine have plant origins in folk practice. Other traditional claims lack reliable evidence, and risks include toxicity, contamination, adulteration with pharmaceuticals, interactions with conventional medicines, and delay of effective treatment. Ethnobotany and pharmacognosy systematically investigate traditional remedies for active compounds.
