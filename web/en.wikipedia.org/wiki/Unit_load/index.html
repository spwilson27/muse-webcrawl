Unit load

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A unit load is a combination of items assembled into a single unit so that it can be handled, transported, and stored as one entity — typically goods stacked on a pallet and secured with stretch wrap, strapping, or netting, but also including slip-sheet loads, bales, crates, and intermodal containers. Unit load design is a core discipline of materials handling and logistics engineering: by moving goods in one lift instead of item by item, it dramatically reduces handling labour, damage, and theft, and allows standardised forklift, racking, and vehicle interfaces throughout the supply chain.

Design considerations

An effective unit load matches the load's footprint and height to the pallet standard in use (such as the 48 × 40 inch GMA pallet in North America or the 1200 × 800 mm EUR-pallet), to warehouse racking, and to truck or container dimensions, minimising wasted space. Engineers consider carton strength and stacking patterns (column versus interlocked), the coefficient of friction between layers, containment force from wrapping, and stability under transport vibration. The concept scales upward: containers are themselves unit loads for ships and trains, and concepts of the "unit load principle" — that it is cheaper to move goods in the largest practical combined unit — underlie break-bulk's replacement by palletisation and containerisation in the 20th century, a shift pioneered commercially by Malcolm McLean's container shipping from 1956.
