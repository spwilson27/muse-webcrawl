Trailer (promotion)

A trailer is a short promotional film or video advertising a forthcoming feature film, television programme, video game or similar work. Trailers consist of selected shots from the work, edited to attract an audience without revealing the whole plot; a typical theatrical trailer runs two to two and a half minutes. The name derives from the early cinema practice of showing them after ("trailing") the main feature, though they were soon moved before the film, where audiences would actually see them.

History

The first known trailer was produced in 1913 for the musical The Pleasure Seekers by Broadway promoter Nils Granlund. Early trailers were crude assemblies of scenes with title cards; by the 1960s, editors such as Pablo Ferro developed faster, montage-driven styles. Specialist trailer houses now produce most studio trailers, often before the film itself is finished, which is why trailers sometimes contain scenes absent from the released film.

Types

A teaser trailer is released early, often a year before the work, with little actual footage. "Red band" trailers carry age-restricted content in the US system, while "green band" trailers are approved for all audiences. Online, trailers are a major driver of opening-weekend box office, and their launch is treated as a media event in its own right.

Criticism

Trailers are frequently criticised for revealing major plot twists, misrepresenting the tone or genre of a film, and giving away its best jokes or action sequences. Some filmmakers, and many viewers, prefer minimal marketing; conversely, several films have been praised for deliberately misleading trailers that preserved surprises.
