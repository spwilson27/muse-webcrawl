Intubation

Intubation is the placement of a flexible plastic tube into the trachea (windpipe) to maintain an open airway or to serve as a conduit through which to administer certain drugs. The most common form is tracheal (endotracheal) intubation, performed to provide mechanical ventilation, protect the airway from aspiration, or facilitate surgery under general anaesthesia.

Technique

In the emergency and operating-room settings, intubation is usually performed after preoxygenation and induction of anaesthesia and muscle relaxation — a sequence called rapid sequence induction when aspiration risk is high. The clinician visualises the vocal cords with a direct or video laryngoscope and passes the tube between them; correct placement is confirmed by capnography (detection of exhaled carbon dioxide), chest rise and auscultation. An inflatable cuff seals the trachea once the tube is in place.

Complications and alternatives

Complications include failed intubation, oesophageal placement, hypoxia, damage to teeth and soft tissues, and tracheal injury from prolonged use; patients ventilated for long periods may instead receive a tracheostomy. Supraglottic airway devices such as the laryngeal mask airway provide alternatives in many elective and some emergency situations, and non-invasive ventilation can avoid intubation in selected patients with respiratory failure.

History

Although tracheostomy is ancient, routine endotracheal intubation developed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries with the work of figures such as William Macewen, Franz Kuhn and Ivan Magill, and became standard in anaesthesia and critical care after the mid-twentieth century.
