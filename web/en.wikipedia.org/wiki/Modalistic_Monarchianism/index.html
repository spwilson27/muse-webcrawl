Modalistic Monarchianism

Modalistic Monarchianism, also known as modalism or Sabellianism, is a Christian theological doctrine concerning the nature of God which emerged in the late second and third centuries AD. It holds that the Father, Son and Holy Spirit are not distinct persons of the Trinity but rather different modes, manifestations or roles of the one God.

Teaching

Monarchianism in general sought to preserve the absolute unity ("monarchy") of God against any suggestion of three gods. Modalistic Monarchians taught that the one God revealed himself successively or simultaneously in different modes: as the Father in creation and law, as the Son in redemption, and as the Holy Spirit in sanctification. Because the Son is simply the Father in another mode, critics charged the doctrine with implying that the Father suffered and died on the cross — a consequence labelled Patripassianism.

History

The doctrine is associated with Noetus of Smyrna, Praxeas (criticised by Tertullian in his treatise Against Praxeas), and above all Sabellius, a third-century theologian active in Rome, after whom it is often named Sabellianism. It was opposed by figures such as Hippolytus and Tertullian and was rejected by the mainstream church, which affirmed the eternal distinction of the three persons within the one Godhead — a position eventually expressed in the Nicene Creed.

Modern adherents

Views resembling modalism are held today by Oneness Pentecostal denominations, which baptise in the name of Jesus only and teach that Father, Son and Holy Spirit are manifestations of the one God rather than separate persons.
