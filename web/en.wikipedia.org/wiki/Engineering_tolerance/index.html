Engineering tolerance

A dimension specified with upper and lower tolerance limits

Engineering tolerance is the permissible limit or limits of variation in a physical dimension, a measured value, or a property of a manufactured object. Because no manufacturing process can produce parts to an exact nominal size, drawings specify a nominal dimension together with an acceptable range — for example 25.00 ± 0.05 mm — within which a part will function correctly.

Types

  • Dimensional tolerance: limits on linear and angular sizes, expressed as bilateral (plus and minus), unilateral (variation in one direction only), or as explicit limit dimensions.
  • Geometric tolerance (GD&T): limits on form, orientation, location and run-out — flatness, parallelism, position, concentricity — standardised by ASME Y14.5 and ISO 1101.
  • Fits: the relationship between mating parts (shaft and hole), classified as clearance, transition or interference fits, with standard systems such as ISO limits and fits (e.g. H7/g6).

Tolerance stack-up

In an assembly, the tolerances of individual parts combine ("stack up"). Designers analyse the worst-case stack and, where appropriate, use statistical (root-sum-square) methods to predict the variation of the assembled dimension, so that assemblies function reliably without tolerances tighter — and more expensive — than necessary.

Design considerations

Tolerance is an economic as well as technical decision: tighter tolerances require more precise (and costly) processes, more inspection, and higher scrap rates. A common rule is that cost rises steeply as tolerance is tightened beyond the natural capability of a process. Conversely, overly loose tolerances cause poor fits, vibration, leakage or premature wear. General tolerance standards (such as ISO 2768) provide default values for dimensions on which no individual tolerance is specified.
