Court of cassation

A court of cassation is a high-instance court that exists in many civil-law legal systems of continental Europe and elsewhere. Its role is to review decisions of lower courts for errors of law: if it finds such an error, it "quashes" (from the French casser, "to break") the decision and ordinarily remits the case to a lower court for rehearing. Unlike a common-law supreme court on appeal, a court of cassation generally does not re-examine the facts of the case.

The French model

The prototype is the French Cour de cassation, created in 1790 during the Revolution as the Tribunal de cassation. It sits atop the French judicial (ordinary) court order, separate from the administrative order headed by the Conseil d'État. It is divided into chambers covering civil, commercial, social, and criminal matters, and can sit in plenary assembly for cases of particular importance.

Other countries

Similar institutions include Italy's Corte Suprema di Cassazione, Belgium's Hof van Cassatie / Cour de cassation, Turkey's Court of Cassation (Yargıtay), and the courts of cassation of many francophone and Arab legal systems, such as those of Egypt, Lebanon and Morocco.
