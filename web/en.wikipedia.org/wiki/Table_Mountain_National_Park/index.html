Table Mountain National Park, previously known as the Cape Peninsula National Park, is a national park in the Western Cape province of South Africa, proclaimed on 29 May 1998 for the purpose of protecting the natural environment of the Table Mountain chain and the surrounding Cape Peninsula. The park is managed by South African National Parks (SANParks) and forms part of the Cape Floral Region Protected Areas, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Geography

Unusually for a national park, most of Table Mountain National Park's land is not contiguous and not enclosed: it consists of several mountain and coastal sections scattered along the Cape Peninsula from Signal Hill and Table Mountain in the north, through the Constantiaberg and Silvermine areas, to Cape Point in the south. Much of the park is open access, interwoven with the suburbs of Cape Town. Major attractions include the flat-topped Table Mountain itself (reached on foot or by the Table Mountain Aerial Cableway), the historic Cape of Good Hope and Cape Point, the African penguin colony at Boulders Beach near Simon's Town, and Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden on the mountain's eastern slopes (administered separately by SANBI).

Flora and fauna

The park protects one of the world's richest floral regions. Its fynbos vegetation, characteristic of the Cape Floristic Region, contains roughly 2,200 plant species in the park alone — more than the entire flora of the United Kingdom — a great many of them endemic. The sandstone mountains also support renosterveld and Afromontane forest in sheltered ravines. Wildlife includes chacma baboons, caracals, rock hyraxes (dassies), various antelope including the reintroduced klipspringer, and rich birdlife; the surrounding waters at Cape Point and Boulders support African penguins and other seabirds.

Tourism and issues

Table Mountain National Park receives millions of visitors annually and is among South Africa's most visited protected areas. Hiking, mountain biking, climbing, paragliding, and surfing are popular activities. The park faces persistent management challenges typical of an urban-interface reserve: alien invasive plants, wildfire (a natural part of fynbos ecology but dangerous near suburbs), baboon–human conflict, and visitor safety.
