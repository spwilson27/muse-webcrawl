Shannon–Hartley theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In information theory, the Shannon–Hartley theorem tells the maximum rate at which information can be transmitted over a communications channel of a specified bandwidth in the presence of noise. It is an application of the noisy-channel coding theorem to the archetypal case of a continuous-time analogue channel subject to Gaussian noise. The theorem establishes the channel capacity C, a theoretical upper bound on the information rate of data that can be communicated at an arbitrarily low error rate.

Statement

The theorem states that for a channel of bandwidth B hertz with average signal power S and Gaussian noise power N, the capacity in bits per second is:

C = B log2(1 + S/N)

The result builds on earlier work by Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley on the information capacity of telegraph channels, and was placed on a rigorous footing by Claude Shannon in his landmark 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication". The theorem is a converse as well as an achievability result: no coding scheme can communicate reliably above C, while schemes exist that approach C with arbitrarily small error probability.

Implications

Because capacity grows linearly with bandwidth but only logarithmically with signal power, the theorem underlies the engineering trade-offs of all modern communication systems, from modems and DSL to Wi-Fi, 4G and 5G radio. Combined with source coding, it also implies a fundamental limit relating bandwidth, power and energy per bit: reliable communication requires the energy per bit to noise spectral density ratio to exceed ln 2, about −1.59 dB, the so-called Shannon limit.
