Cádiz is a city and port in southwestern Spain, the capital of the province of Cádiz in Andalusia. Founded by the Phoenicians around 1100 BC as Gadir, it is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Western Europe. The old town occupies a narrow peninsula almost entirely surrounded by the Atlantic Ocean and the Bay of Cádiz.

History

After its Phoenician founding, Cádiz passed to Carthage and then to Rome, where as Gades it prospered on trade and garum production. In the age of exploration it became a principal port for trade with the Americas; from 1717 it held the monopoly of Spanish-American trade, transferred from Seville. During the Peninsular War, Cádiz resisted French siege and hosted the Cortes that proclaimed the liberal Spanish Constitution of 1812.

Sights and culture

Landmarks include the baroque-neoclassical Cádiz Cathedral with its golden dome, the Roman theatre, and the Torre Tavira watchtower. The city is famous for its Carnival, one of Spain's most celebrated, noted for satirical chirigota singing groups.
