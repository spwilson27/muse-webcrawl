Epidural administration

Epidural administration (from Greek epi, "on" + dura mater) is a medical route of administration in which a drug or contrast agent is injected into the epidural space of the spinal canal, the area outside the dura mater surrounding the spinal cord. Epidural anaesthesia and analgesia — most familiar as pain relief in childbirth — are produced by injecting local anaesthetics, often combined with opioids, into this space, where they act on spinal nerve roots as they exit the cord.

Technique

The procedure is usually performed with the patient sitting or lying on their side with the spine flexed. Using sterile technique, the anaesthetist advances a Tuohy needle between the vertebrae — most often in the lumbar region — and identifies the epidural space by loss of resistance to saline or air. A fine catheter may be threaded through the needle and left in place, allowing continuous infusion or repeated top-up doses over hours or days; single-shot injection through the needle alone is also possible. Epidurals can also be placed at thoracic or cervical levels for surgery and pain management, while the related caudal route enters through the sacral hiatus and is common in children.

Effects and uses

Blocking nerve roots in a band of spinal segments produces loss of sensation — and at higher doses, motor block — below the level of injection. Epidurals are used for labour analgesia (their most common application), as the sole or adjunct anaesthetic for abdominal, pelvic, and lower-limb surgery, and for post-operative and chronic pain, including epidural steroid injections for sciatica. Compared with spinal anaesthesia, epidural onset is slower, the block can be titrated and prolonged by catheter, and the dose of drug required is larger.

Risks

Complications include failure or patchy block, accidental dural puncture with resulting post-dural-puncture headache, hypotension from sympathetic blockade, urinary retention, and temporary leg weakness. Rare but serious complications include epidural haematoma or abscess, direct nerve injury, and high or total spinal block if the dose enters the subarachnoid space. Because of the haematoma risk, timing of epidural placement relative to anticoagulant drugs is carefully controlled.

History

Epidural injection was pioneered in the early 20th century; the Spanish surgeon Fidel Pagés described lumbar epidural anaesthesia in 1921, and the loss-of-resistance technique and curved-tip needle were refined by Achille Dogliotti and Mario Curbelo, who placed the first continuous catheter in the 1940s. Obstetric epidurals became widespread in the 1970s and 1980s with the introduction of safer local anaesthetics and infusion pumps.
