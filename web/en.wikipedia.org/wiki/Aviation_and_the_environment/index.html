Aviation and the Environment

The environmental impact of aviation arises because aircraft engines emit heat, noise, particulates, and gases — principally carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrogen oxides (NOx), water vapour, and soot — into the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere, where their effects on climate are amplified. Aviation contributes roughly 2–2.5% of global anthropogenic CO2 emissions, but its total warming effect is estimated to be considerably larger once non-CO2 effects such as contrail cirrus are included.

Climate effects

Besides CO2, aircraft emit NOx, which drives the formation of ozone (a greenhouse gas) and the destruction of methane. Water vapour condensing in exhaust can form persistent contrails that sometimes develop into cirrus clouds, trapping heat. Studies estimate that aviation's effective radiative forcing is roughly two to three times that attributable to its CO2 alone. Because air travel has grown rapidly — passenger numbers roughly doubled between the early 2000s and 2019 — total sector emissions have risen despite steady gains in fuel efficiency of about 1–2% per year.

Other impacts

Aircraft noise affects communities around airports and under flight paths, and local air quality is degraded by emissions during takeoff and landing. Airports themselves consume land and generate surface traffic. De-icing fluids and fuel handling can affect local water quality.

Mitigation

Mitigation measures include more efficient aircraft and engines, operational improvements such as more direct routing, sustainable aviation fuels (SAF) made from biomass or synthetic feedstocks, and market-based measures such as the EU Emissions Trading System and ICAO's CORSIA offsetting scheme. Radical options under study include hydrogen-powered and battery-electric aircraft, feasible at first only for short ranges. Social responses include the Swedish-born flygskam ("flight shame") movement encouraging reduced flying, and substitution of rail for short-haul flights, as with France's ban on certain domestic routes where a fast rail alternative exists.
