French Sign Language

French Sign Language (French: langue des signes française, LSF) is the sign language of the deaf community in France. It is estimated to be used by around 100,000 people. LSF is historically important as the ancestor of many other sign languages, including American Sign Language (ASL), which developed from it after Laurent Clerc, a French deaf teacher, helped found the first school for the deaf in the United States in 1817.

History

Old French Sign Language developed among the deaf population of Paris and was first systematically taught by the Abbé Charles-Michel de l'Épée, who founded a school for deaf pupils in Paris in the 1760s and combined the signs he learned from deaf Parisians with invented "methodical signs" for French grammar. His successors, including the Abbé Sicard, spread the method across Europe and beyond. Although deaf education later fell under the influence of oralism, especially after the Milan Congress of 1880, LSF survived in the deaf community.

Status and recognition

LSF was officially recognized in French law in 2005 as a language in its own right, and it is offered as an optional subject in French schools. It uses a one-handed manual alphabet derived from the system published by Épée's school.

Related languages

The French Sign Language family includes ASL, Russian Sign Language, Italian Sign Language, and many others descended from LSF through deaf schools founded on the French model. LSF is distinct from Belgian French Sign Language and Swiss French Sign Language, though they are related.
