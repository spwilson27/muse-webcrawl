The Sioux (pronounced "soo") are a group of Native American tribes and First Nations peoples in North America. The term can refer to any ethnic group within the Great Sioux Nation or to any of the nation's many language dialects. In the modern era, the Sioux are united politically in the Oceti Sakowin (Očhéthi Šakówiŋ, "Seven Council Fires"), a confederation of seven related peoples speaking three major dialects: Dakota, Nakota and Lakota.

Divisions

The Seven Council Fires comprise:

  • Santee (Eastern Dakota): Mdewakanton, Wahpeton, Wahpekute and Sisseton.
  • Yankton and Yanktonai (Western Dakota, often called Nakota).
  • Teton (Lakota): Oglala, Brulé (Sičháŋǧu), Hunkpapa, Miniconjou, Two Kettle (Oóhenuŋpa), Sans Arc (Itázipčo) and Sihasapa (Blackfeet Lakota).

Today many Sioux live on reservations in South Dakota — including Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Standing Rock, Cheyenne River and Lower Brule — as well as in North Dakota, Minnesota, Nebraska, Montana and Canada.

History

Origins and expansion

Siouan-speaking peoples likely lived for centuries in the upper Mississippi and Great Lakes woodlands before pressure from the Ojibwe, and access to horses and the fur trade, drew the western bands onto the northern Great Plains in the 17th and 18th centuries. By the early 1800s the Lakota dominated a vast territory centred on the Black Hills (Pahá Sápa), which are sacred to them.

Wars with the United States

Relations with the United States were formalised in treaties, including the Fort Laramie treaties of 1851 and 1868, the latter establishing the Great Sioux Reservation. Conflict followed gold discoveries in the Black Hills. In the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, Lakota and Northern Cheyenne warriors under leaders such as Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse defeated Lt. Col. George A. Custer's 7th Cavalry at the Battle of the Little Bighorn (25–26 June 1876). Despite that victory, military pressure and the destruction of the buffalo herds forced surrender; the Black Hills were seized in 1877 — a taking the U.S. Supreme Court in 1980 (United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians) ruled unconstitutional, awarding compensation the Sioux have refused in favour of the land itself.

Wounded Knee and the modern era

On 29 December 1890, U.S. soldiers killed some 250–300 Lakota, many of them women and children, at Wounded Knee Creek — the last major confrontation of the Indian Wars. In 1973, activists of the American Indian Movement occupied Wounded Knee for 71 days to protest treaty violations and corruption. In 2016, the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe led mass protests against the Dakota Access Pipeline near its reservation, drawing international attention.

Culture

Plains Sioux culture historically centred on the American bison, which provided food, clothing, shelter (tipis) and tools. Spiritual life includes the Seven Sacred Rites of the Lakota, the Sun Dance, sweat lodge (inipi) ceremonies, vision quests (hanblecheya) and reverence for Wakan Tanka, the Great Mystery. Renowned leaders and figures include Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, Red Cloud, Black Elk and Spotted Tail.
