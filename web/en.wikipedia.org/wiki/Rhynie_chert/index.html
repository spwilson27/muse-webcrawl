Rhynie chert

Rhynie chert
LocationNear Rhynie, Aberdeenshire, Scotland
AgeEarly Devonian, Pragian, c. 407 million years
TypeKonservat-Lagerstätte (exceptional fossil deposit)
PreservationSilicification by hot-spring waters
Discovered1912 (William Mackie)

The Rhynie chert is a Lower Devonian sedimentary deposit near the village of Rhynie in Aberdeenshire, Scotland, famous for containing exceptionally well-preserved fossils of some of the earliest land plants and terrestrial animals. Formed about 407 million years ago when silica-rich hot-spring waters flooded a lowland ecosystem and petrified it, the chert preserves organisms down to cellular—and sometimes subcellular—detail, making it one of the world's most important fossil Lagerstätten.

Discovery and geology

The chert was discovered in 1912 by Dr William Mackie while mapping the area. Between 1916 and 1921 Robert Kidston and William H. Lang described its plants in a classic series of papers. The unit consists of beds of chert (silicified sinter) interbedded with shales and sandstones of the Dryden Flags Formation, deposited on a river floodplain crossed by hot springs comparable to those of modern Yellowstone. Radiometric dating of associated andesitic rocks places the deposit in the Pragian stage of the Early Devonian.

Flora

The flora includes some of the earliest known vascular plants, many with complete anatomy: Rhynia gwynne-vaughanii, Horneophyton, Aglaophyton (long called Rhynia major), Asteroxylon, Nothia and Trichopherophyton, together with charophyte algae, cyanobacteria, fungi and lichens. The chert preserves mycorrhizal fungi inside plant tissues—evidence for the ancient symbiosis that helped plants colonise land—and shows some of the earliest stomata, conducting cells and sporangia.

Fauna

The animals are among the oldest known land faunas: trigonotarbid and harvestman arachnids (including Palaeocharinus and the harvestman Eophalangium sheari), mites, the springtail Rhyniella praecursor (among the earliest hexapods), myriapods, and the small crustacean Lepidocaris in aquatic settings. The site has also yielded nematode worms and the earliest known fungal fruiting structures.

Significance

Because whole communities—plants growing in life position, their fungi, and the animals that fed on and sheltered among them—were fossilised together, the Rhynie chert offers an unmatched window onto the first complex terrestrial ecosystems, the "greening of the land" in the Devonian.
