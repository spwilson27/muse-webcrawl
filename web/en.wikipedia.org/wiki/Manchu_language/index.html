Manchu (Manchu: ᠮᠠᠨᠵᡠ ᡤᡳᠰᡠᠨ, manju gisun) is a critically endangered Tungusic language native to the historical region of Manchuria in Northeast Asia. It was the native language of the Manchu people and one of the official languages of the Qing dynasty (1644–1912), which ruled China for nearly three centuries. Despite the large ethnic Manchu population today, numbering over ten million, the language has almost entirely been supplanted by Mandarin Chinese, and only a handful of elderly native speakers remain, mostly in Heilongjiang province.

History

Manchu descends from Jurchen, the language of the Jin dynasty (1115–1234). In 1599 the chieftain Nurhaci ordered the creation of a Manchu script adapted from the Mongolian alphabet; in 1632 the script was reformed by Dahai with diacritics to remove ambiguities. Under the Qing, Manchu was used in court documents, military communications of the Eight Banners, and bilingual inscriptions; the Kangxi Dictionary era also produced great Manchu dictionaries. However, from the eighteenth century the Manchu emperors themselves lamented the bannermen's loss of the language, and through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries Manchu speech retreated to remote communities such as Sanjiazi village in Heilongjiang and the Xibe (Sibe) people of Xinjiang, whose closely related spoken variety is sometimes treated as a separate language or a living descendant of Manchu.

Features

Manchu is an agglutinative language with vowel harmony, subject–object–verb word order, and no grammatical gender. Its traditional script is written vertically from top to bottom, in lines running left to right. The lexicon contains many loanwords from Mongolian, Chinese, and Sanskrit (via religious texts), and Manchu itself contributed words to Chinese and, through it, to other languages, such as "shaman" (from Tungusic saman).

Status and revitalisation

UNESCO classifies Manchu as critically endangered. In recent decades there has been a modest revival movement: courses are taught at some Chinese universities, primary schools in parts of the northeast offer Manchu classes, and enthusiasts study the language to access the vast Qing-era Manchu-language archives, which remain an important primary source for historians. The related Xibe language of Xinjiang, written in a modified Manchu script, retains tens of thousands of speakers and serves as a resource for learners.
