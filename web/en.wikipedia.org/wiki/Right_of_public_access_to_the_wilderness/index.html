Right of public access to the wilderness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The right of public access to the wilderness, often called the right to roam or freedom to roam, is the general public's right to access certain public or privately owned land, lakes and rivers for recreation and exercise. The right is closely associated with the Nordic countries, where it is known as allemansrätten in Sweden, allemannsretten in Norway, jokamiehenoikeus in Finland and almannaréttur in Iceland. In Scotland, the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 established a statutory right of responsible access.

Scope

The right typically includes walking, camping for short periods, cycling, horse riding, skiing, and foraging for wild berries, mushrooms and flowers, subject to obligations not to cause damage, not to disturb livestock or residents, and to stay away from houses and cultivated plots. Hunting, fishing and motorized access are generally excluded and remain the landowner's prerogative.

By country

  • Sweden: Allemansrätten is a long-standing custom, given constitutional protection, permitting broad access to uncultivated land and the shore.
  • Norway: the Outdoor Recreation Act of 1957 codified allemannsretten, with rules distinguishing outfields (accessible) from infields (restricted).
  • Finland: "everyman's right" allows extensive access, including picking berries and mushrooms, but not making fire without permission in dry conditions.
  • Scotland: statutory access rights apply to most land and inland water for recreation and education, governed by the Scottish Outdoor Access Code.
  • England and Wales: the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 created a right of access on foot to mapped mountain, moor, heath, down and registered common land.

Comparison

Most common-law countries instead rely on public rights of way along defined paths, and on public land. The Nordic model is distinctive in extending access across most uncultivated private land as a general right rather than by permission or designation.
