The Gran Sasso raid (German codename Unternehmen Eiche, "Operation Oak") was the rescue of the deposed Italian dictator Benito Mussolini by German special forces on 12 September 1943. Mussolini, overthrown by the Grand Council of Fascism and arrested on the orders of King Victor Emmanuel III on 25 July 1943, was imprisoned by Marshal Badoglio's government at the Hotel Campo Imperatore, a ski resort on the Campo Imperatore plateau high in the Gran Sasso massif of the Apennines, reachable only by cable car. The bloodless glider-borne coup de main became one of the most famous special operations of the Second World War.

Background

After the Allied invasion of Sicily and the bombing of Rome, Mussolini was voted out by his own Grand Council and arrested. Badoglio's government moved him between hiding places — Ponza, La Maddalena, and finally, on 28 August, the Campo Imperatore hotel — while secretly negotiating an armistice with the Allies, announced on 8 September. Germany, anticipating Italy's defection, executed Operation Achse to disarm Italian forces, and Hitler ordered Mussolini found and freed. SS officer Otto Skorzeny's intelligence work — including monitoring Italian radio traffic and aerial reconnaissance with photographs taken by Skorzeny himself leaning from an aircraft — located the Gran Sasso site.

The raid

The operation was organised by Luftwaffe paratroop general Kurt Student; the ground force of Fallschirmjäger of the 7th Parachute Regiment was led by Major Harald Mors, whose men simultaneously seized the cable-car valley station at Assergi. At about 14:00 on 12 September, ten DFS 230 gliders carrying about ninety paratroopers and Skorzeny crash-landed on the sloping meadow beside the hotel. Italian carabinieri guards, under orders not to let Mussolini be harmed but also confronted by the Italian police general Fernando Soleti, brought along by the Germans and ordered to command surrender, offered no resistance. Not a shot was fired. Mussolini was freed within minutes and greeted Skorzeny with the words "I knew my friend Adolf wouldn't desert me."

At around 15:00 Mussolini was flown off the mountain — from a makeshift strip at considerable risk — in a Fieseler Fi 156 Storch piloted by Heinrich Gerlach, with Skorzeny insisting on squeezing aboard. He was taken via Rome and Vienna to Hitler's headquarters at Rastenburg, and days later installed as head of the German puppet Italian Social Republic (the "Salò Republic") in northern Italy.

Propaganda and memory

Nazi propaganda magnified Skorzeny's personal role — he was promoted, decorated with the Knight's Cross and celebrated as "the most dangerous man in Europe" — at the expense of Mors and the paratroopers, a distortion resented by the Fallschirmjäger veterans and corrected by later historians. The raid remains a staple of special-forces history as a model of audacious planning and surprise, and the hotel room where Mussolini stayed is preserved as a small museum.
