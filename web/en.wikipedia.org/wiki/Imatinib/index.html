Imatinib

Trade namesGleevec, Glivec
ClassTyrosine kinase inhibitor
TargetsBCR-ABL, KIT, PDGFRA/B
RoutesBy mouth
Approved2001 (U.S. FDA)

Imatinib, sold under the brand names Gleevec and Glivec, is a medication used to treat certain cancers, most notably chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST). It was the first of the tyrosine kinase inhibitors — targeted drugs that block specific enzymes driving cancer growth — and its success transformed CML from a fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition for most patients.

Imatinib, the first BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor.

Mechanism of action

CML is caused by the BCR-ABL fusion gene, the product of the Philadelphia chromosome, which encodes a permanently active tyrosine kinase. Imatinib fits into the ATP-binding pocket of the ABL kinase and switches it off, halting the uncontrolled proliferation of leukemic cells. It also inhibits the KIT receptor mutated in most GISTs and the platelet-derived growth factor receptors, explaining its utility in several other tumors and blood disorders.

Development

Imatinib was developed at Ciba-Geigy (later Novartis) in the late 1980s and 1990s through the work of chemist Jürg Zimmermann and biologist Elisabeth Buchdunger, and championed clinically by Brian Druker of Oregon Health & Science University, together with Charles Sawyers and others. Phase I results in 1998–1999 were dramatic, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved the drug in May 2001 after an exceptionally rapid review.

Uses and side effects

Besides CML and GIST, imatinib is used for Philadelphia chromosome–positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia, certain myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative diseases, hypereosinophilic syndrome, and dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans. Common side effects include nausea, muscle cramps, edema (notably around the eyes), rash, fatigue, and diarrhea; serious toxicity is uncommon but includes myelosuppression and liver effects. Resistance can develop through mutations such as T315I in BCR-ABL, prompting the development of second- and third-generation inhibitors including dasatinib, nilotinib, and ponatinib.

Cost and access

Imatinib's price — rising above US$100,000 per year in the United States before generic entry in 2016 — became a prominent case in debates over drug pricing. In India, the Supreme Court's 2013 decision in Novartis v. Union of India upheld the rejection of a patent on the beta-crystalline form of imatinib, preserving supplies of inexpensive generics for patients in the developing world.
