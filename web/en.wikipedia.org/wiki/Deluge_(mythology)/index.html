Deluge (mythology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. This article is about flood myths in general. For the biblical account, see Genesis flood narrative.

A deluge, or flood myth, is a myth in which a great flood, usually sent by a deity or deities, destroys civilization, often in an act of divine retribution. Parallels are often drawn between the flood waters of these myths and the primeval waters of certain creation myths, as the flood represents a return to chaos and a subsequent re-creation of the world.

Flood myths are found in many cultures around the world. The best known examples include the story of Noah in the Book of Genesis, the Mesopotamian flood stories of Utnapishtim, Atrahasis and Ziusudra, the Greek story of Deucalion and Pyrrha, the Hindu story of Manu and the fish Matsya, and flood traditions in Chinese, Mesoamerican, and many Indigenous oral traditions.

Mesopotamian flood myths

The earliest recorded flood stories come from Mesopotamia. In the Sumerian flood story, the hero Ziusudra is warned of a coming flood and survives in a boat. The Akkadian Epic of Atrahasis tells how the gods, disturbed by human noise, send plagues and finally a flood; the god Enki warns Atrahasis, who builds a vessel and preserves life. In the Epic of Gilgamesh (tablet XI), Utnapishtim recounts how he survived the flood in a great cube-shaped ship, released birds to find land, and was granted immortality by the gods.

The biblical flood

The Genesis flood narrative describes God sending a flood to destroy a corrupt humanity, sparing only Noah, his family, and pairs of animals aboard the Ark. After the waters recede — traditionally onto the mountains of Ararat — God makes a covenant with Noah, symbolised by the rainbow, never again to destroy the earth by flood.

Greek flood myths

Greek tradition preserved several floods. In the most famous, Zeus sends a flood to destroy the wicked Bronze Age race of men; Deucalion, warned by his father Prometheus, builds a chest and survives with his wife Pyrrha. After landing on Mount Parnassus, they repopulate the earth by casting stones over their shoulders, which become men and women.

Other traditions

In Hindu tradition, the first man, Manu, is warned by the fish Matsya — an avatar of Vishnu — of an impending flood and builds a boat that the fish guides to safety. Chinese mythology tells of the Great Flood controlled by Yu the Great through years of dredging and channel-building. Flood stories also appear in Irish, Norse (the flood of Bergelmir), Aztec, Maya, Inca, and numerous North American Indigenous traditions.

Interpretations

Scholars have proposed that flood myths may preserve memories of real local flooding events, such as river floods in Mesopotamia or rising sea levels after the last Ice Age, although no single geological event explains the worldwide distribution of the motif. Comparative mythologists regard the deluge as an archetype of destruction and renewal.
