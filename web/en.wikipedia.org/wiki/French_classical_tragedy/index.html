French classical tragedy

French classical tragedy is the form of tragedy developed in 17th-century France under the influence of neoclassical rules derived (sometimes loosely) from Aristotle's Poetics and codified by critics such as Boileau. Its greatest exponents were Pierre Corneille, Jean Racine, and — in comedy — Molière.

Conventions

Classical tragedy observed the three unities of time, place and action; plays were written in rhymed alexandrine couplets, divided into five acts, kept violence offstage, and observed bienséance (decorum) and vraisemblance (plausibility). Corneille's Le Cid (1637) provoked a famous quarrel over the rules; Racine's Phèdre (1677) is often regarded as the form's supreme achievement, portraying overwhelming passion within an austere formal frame.
