Saitō Musashibō Benkei (西塔武蔵坊弁慶, 1155–1189), popularly called simply Benkei, was a Japanese warrior monk (sōhei) who became the loyal retainer and companion of the great general Minamoto no Yoshitsune. Few figures of Japanese history are more beloved in folklore, kabuki and Noh: the gigantic, fierce-eyed monk with his seven weapons is the very archetype of loyal strength.

Legends

Benkei was said to be the son of a temple head priest and, in some tales, of a demon; expelled from monastery after monastery for his violence, he took to collecting swords from defeated samurai at Gojō Bridge in Kyoto, vowing to gather a thousand. On his 999th victory quest he met the slight youth Yoshitsune, who — trained, some said, by the tengu of Mount Kurama — defeated him with ease. Benkei thereupon swore eternal loyalty and followed Yoshitsune through the Genpei War against the Taira.

The standing death

After the war, Yoshitsune was betrayed and outlawed by his brother Yoritomo. Disguised as porters and monks — the famous scene dramatised in the kabuki play Kanjinchō, where Benkei bluffs and even beats his own master to pass a checkpoint — the fugitives reached Hiraizumi. There, in 1189, Benkei made his last stand at Takadachi castle, holding a bridge alone against hundreds of Fujiwara soldiers so Yoshitsune could die by his own hand undisturbed. Pierced by arrows, Benkei died standing upright, and the terrified attackers dared not approach until his body finally fell — the legendary "standing death" (Benkei no tachi ōjō).

In culture

Benkei's size, weapons, weeping loyalty and death are staples of Japanese art, film, manga and games. The historical core is thin — contemporary chronicles mention him only briefly — but the legend has entirely eclipsed it.
