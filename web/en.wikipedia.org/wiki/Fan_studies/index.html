Fan studies is an academic discipline that analyses fans and fandom. It emerged within media and cultural studies in the late 1980s and early 1990s, in reaction to earlier portrayals of fans as obsessive, passive or pathological consumers. Fan studies examines how fans actively produce meaning, texts, identities and communities around media objects such as television series, films, music, sport, games and celebrities.

History and "waves"

Scholars commonly describe the field in waves. The first wave (early 1990s) — associated with Henry Jenkins's Textual Poachers (1992), John Fiske's writings on the "cultural economy" of fandom, and Camille Bacon-Smith's ethnography Enterprising Women (1992) — cast fandom as a site of resistance and creative "poaching" of corporate media texts. A second wave (late 1990s–2000s), influenced by Pierre Bourdieu, examined how fan communities reproduce wider social and cultural hierarchies of taste and capital. A third wave turned to everyday fan practices, the pleasures of fandom, and the industrial embrace of fans in the convergence era.

Key concepts

  • Participatory culture — Jenkins's term for cultures in which fans are producers as well as consumers.
  • Textual poaching — borrowed from Michel de Certeau, describing fans' appropriation of media texts.
  • Fan productivity — Fiske's categories of semiotic, enunciative and textual productivity (fan fiction, fan art, vidding, cosplay).
  • Anti-fandom and toxic fandom — later research on dislike, harassment and fan conflict.
  • Acafandom — scholars who are themselves fans, raising questions of positionality and autoethnography.

Institutions

The field has dedicated journals, notably Transformative Works and Cultures (launched 2008 by the Organization for Transformative Works) and The Journal of Fandom Studies (2013), as well as conferences and university courses in fan studies.
