An eating disorder is a mental disorder defined by abnormal eating behaviours that adversely affect a person's physical or mental health. The principal diagnoses recognised in the DSM-5 and ICD-11 are anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder, together with avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID), pica, rumination disorder and other specified feeding or eating disorders (OSFED). Eating disorders affect people of all genders, ages and backgrounds, though they are diagnosed more often in adolescent girls and young women.

Main types

  • Anorexia nervosa — restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight, an intense fear of gaining weight, and a disturbance in body image. It has the highest mortality rate of any eating disorder, from medical complications and suicide.
  • Bulimia nervosa — recurrent episodes of binge eating followed by compensatory behaviours such as self-induced vomiting, laxative misuse or excessive exercise, with self-evaluation unduly influenced by shape and weight.
  • Binge eating disorder — recurrent binge eating without regular compensatory behaviours, associated with marked distress; the most common eating disorder in adults.
  • ARFID — avoidant or restrictive eating (from sensory aversion, fear of choking or vomiting, or lack of interest) severe enough to cause nutritional deficiency or psychosocial impairment, without body-image disturbance.

Causes

No single cause has been identified. Twin and family studies indicate a substantial genetic contribution; risk is also associated with perfectionism, obsessive-compulsive traits, anxiety and depression, childhood adversity, and sociocultural pressures idealising thinness or muscularity. Dieting is a common precipitating factor but is not sufficient by itself. Neurobiological research implicates altered reward and interoceptive circuitry and serotonin and dopamine systems.

Complications

Starvation and purging can damage nearly every organ system: bradycardia and hypotension, electrolyte disturbances (a particular risk in purging), bone loss and osteoporosis, infertility, gastrointestinal damage, dental erosion, and growth failure in adolescents. Eating disorders frequently co-occur with mood, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive and substance-use disorders.

Treatment

Treatment typically combines medical monitoring and nutritional rehabilitation with psychotherapy. Enhanced cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-E) is a leading treatment for adults; family-based treatment (the "Maudsley approach") is recommended for adolescents with anorexia nervosa, enlisting parents in refeeding. Fluoxetine is approved for bulimia nervosa and lisdexamfetamine for binge eating disorder in some jurisdictions. Severe cases may require day-patient or inpatient care. Early intervention substantially improves outcomes.
