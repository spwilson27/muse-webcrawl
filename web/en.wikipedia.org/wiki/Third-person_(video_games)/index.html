Third-person (video games)

In video games, a third-person view is a graphical perspective in which the camera is positioned behind and slightly above the player character, so that the character is visible on screen, in contrast to a first-person view rendered from the character's own eyes. The term is applied to many genres — third-person shooters, action-adventure games, platformers and role-playing games among them. Showing the character allows players to see animations, judge jumps and melee spacing, and relate to a distinct protagonist; it also permits a wider view of the surroundings than first-person presentation, at the cost of less direct aiming and occasional camera collisions with scenery.

Variants

Early 3D games often used fixed cameras, cutting between predetermined angles as the character moved between rooms — a style associated with survival horror such as the original Resident Evil games, which combined it with pre-rendered backgrounds. Follow cameras track the character freely, as in Super Mario 64 (1996), whose camera system — personified by the character Lakitu — was foundational for 3D platformers. The over-the-shoulder view, popularised by Resident Evil 4 (2005), offsets the character to one side and tightens towards the shoulder when aiming, and became the standard presentation of the third-person shooter; Gears of War (2006) paired it influentially with cover-based combat.

History and usage

Two-dimensional games with a visible avatar, from Pac-Man to side-scrolling platformers, are loosely third-person, but the term gained its modern meaning with the move to 3D in the 1990s. Tomb Raider (1996) made the trailing camera behind Lara Croft iconic. Designers choose the perspective for genre reasons: character action games (Devil May Cry, Bayonetta) need the body visible for combo animation and spatial awareness, open-world games use it to display vehicles and environments, and some games let players switch between first- and third-person at will (The Elder Scrolls series). Camera control — handling occlusion, room interiors and player comfort — remains a central technical and design problem of the perspective.
