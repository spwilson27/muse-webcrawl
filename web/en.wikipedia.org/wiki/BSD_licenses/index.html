BSD licenses

The BSD licenses are a family of permissive free software licenses originally used for the Berkeley Software Distribution (BSD) Unix operating system developed at the University of California, Berkeley. They impose minimal restrictions on the use and redistribution of covered software: the main requirements are that redistributions retain the copyright notice, the license text and a disclaimer of warranty. Unlike copyleft licenses such as the GNU General Public License, BSD licenses permit the software to be incorporated into proprietary products.

Versions

The original four-clause license included an "advertising clause" requiring all advertising material mentioning the software to credit the copyright holder. This clause proved impractical and made the license incompatible with the GPL; it was formally rescinded by Berkeley in 1999. Removing it yields the three-clause (or "revised") BSD license, which adds a clause prohibiting use of the copyright holder's name to endorse derived products. Dropping that clause in turn gives the two-clause ("simplified") license, used by FreeBSD. A zero-clause variant (0BSD) places the software effectively in the public domain.

Usage

BSD-licensed code underlies the FreeBSD, NetBSD and OpenBSD operating systems and is widely reused in commercial products — for example, parts of the networking stacks and userlands of Apple's macOS and iOS, and the PlayStation operating systems, derive from BSD code.
