Computer science

[Image: a university lecture hall whiteboard showing a Turing machine diagram and big-O notation]

Computer science is the study of computation, information and automation — what can be computed, how efficiently, and how to design the machines and programs that compute. It spans theory (algorithms, computability, complexity, information theory), systems (operating systems, networks, databases), software, and applications such as Artificial intelligence, graphics, security and human–computer interaction.

Though named for computers, the field studies computation abstractly — the stored-program computer is its most powerful instrument, not its whole subject — and it draws on mathematics, Electrical engineering and logic.

Foundations and history

The field's foundations were laid before electronic computers: Alan Turing's 1936 paper introduced the Turing machine and the limits of computability, and Claude Shannon's 1937 thesis founded digital circuit design on Boolean algebra. The first electronic computers of the 1940s (ENIAC, Colossus, the Manchester machines) turned theory into engineering.

University departments of computer science appeared from the 1960s (Purdue's, in 1962, is often cited as the first in the US), and the field grew explosively with the personal computer, the Internet and, in the twenty-first century, data-driven machine learning.

Subfields

Major subfields include algorithms and data structures; programming languages and compilers; computer architecture; operating systems; computer networks; databases and information retrieval; software engineering; computer graphics and vision; robotics; cryptography and security; and theory of computation.

Computer science is both a science and an engineering discipline, and its professional societies (ACM, IEEE Computer Society) and prizes (the Turing Award, described as computing's Nobel Prize) mark its standing.
