Commuter airline

Commuter airlines operate small aircraft on short routes between regional airports and hubs.

A commuter airline is a type of airline that operates small aircraft on short routes, typically between smaller communities and larger hub airports. In the United States, the term historically referred to carriers operating under the commuter category of Federal Aviation Regulations, with aircraft of limited seating capacity, and it overlaps with the modern category of regional airlines.

Development

Commuter airlines expanded rapidly after the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 in the United States, as major carriers withdrew from thin short-haul routes. Small turboprops such as the Beechcraft 1900, de Havilland Canada Twin Otter, Embraer EMB 110 Bandeirante and ATR 42 became standard equipment. Many commuter carriers entered code-sharing and capacity-purchase agreements with major airlines, feeding passengers into their networks under brands such as United Express, American Eagle and Delta Connection.

Operations

Commuter operations emphasise frequency on short sectors, rapid turnarounds, and service to airports that cannot support larger aircraft. In remote regions — Alaska, northern Canada, island communities — commuter airlines provide essential, sometimes subsidised, lifeline services, in the US supported by the Essential Air Service programme.

Regulation

After accidents in the 1990s and the Colgan Air Flight 3407 crash in 2009, regulators moved to align commuter and regional airline safety standards, crew training and rest requirements more closely with those of major airlines.
