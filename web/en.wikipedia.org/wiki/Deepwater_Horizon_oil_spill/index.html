Deepwater Horizon oil spill

The Deepwater Horizon oil spill was an environmental disaster that began on 20 April 2010, off the coast of the United States in the Gulf of Mexico, on the BP-operated Macondo Prospect. It is considered the largest marine oil spill in the history of the petroleum industry and one of the worst environmental disasters in United States history.

The explosion and flow of oil

On the evening of 20 April 2010, a surge of natural gas from the Macondo well blasted through the concrete core recently installed to seal the well, travelled up the riser to the Deepwater Horizon drilling rig, and ignited, killing 11 workers and injuring 17. The rig, owned by Transocean and drilling for BP, sank two days later. The well discharged oil at the seabed, about 1,500 metres below the surface, for 87 days until it was capped on 15 July 2010. A "static kill" and cementing followed, and the well was declared effectively dead on 19 September 2010 after a relief well intercepted it.

Estimates of the volume discharged varied during the crisis; the US government's final estimate was about 4.9 million barrels (roughly 780,000 cubic metres) released from the reservoir, of which about 4.2 million barrels entered the Gulf after accounting for oil captured. Extensive response efforts included booms, skimming, controlled burns, the application of around 1.84 million US gallons of chemical dispersants (including Corexit) at the surface and at the wellhead, and the protection and cleaning of shorelines in Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Florida, and Texas.

Causes

Investigations, including the presidential National Commission and BP's internal inquiry, identified a chain of failures: a flawed cement job by contractor Halliburton, misreading of a negative pressure test by BP and Transocean personnel, failure of the rig's blowout preventer, and management and cost-cutting decisions that increased risk. The disaster prompted major regulatory reform, including the dissolution of the Minerals Management Service and new drilling safety rules.

Environmental and economic impact

Oil came ashore along hundreds of miles of coastline, damaging marshes, beaches, fisheries, and tourism. Wildlife including dolphins, sea turtles, birds, and deep-sea corals suffered elevated mortality and health effects documented for years afterwards. Fisheries were closed across large areas of the Gulf, and the spill caused severe losses to fishing, tourism, and coastal businesses.

Legal settlement and costs

BP established a $20 billion compensation fund in 2010. In 2015, BP reached a settlement with the US federal government and five Gulf states valued at $18.7 billion, the largest environmental settlement in US history; together with criminal penalties (including a $4 billion plea in 2013), cleanup costs, and private claims, BP's total costs exceeded $65 billion. The disaster is the subject of books, documentaries, and the 2016 feature film Deepwater Horizon.
