Palladium (classical antiquity)

Palladium
TypeWooden cult image (xoanon)
DepictsPallas Athena
Associated cityTroy; later Rome and others

In Greek and Roman mythology, a palladium or palladion was an ancient cult image of great antiquity on which the safety of a city was said to depend. The word derives from Pallas, an epithet of the goddess Athena. Most famously, the Palladium of Troy was a wooden statue of Athena said to have fallen from heaven; Troy was prophesied to be safe from capture so long as it remained within the city.

The Trojan Palladium

According to the Epic Cycle and later authors, the Palladium was stolen during the Trojan War by Odysseus and Diomedes, who crept into the citadel of Troy and carried it off, an episode told in the Little Iliad and dramatized in later literature. After the fall of Troy, various cities — Athens, Argos, and Rome among them — claimed to possess the genuine image.

The Roman Palladium

Rome's Palladium was kept in the Temple of Vesta in the Roman Forum, guarded by the Vestal Virgins as one of the secret pledges of empire (pignora imperii). It was rarely seen; the pontifex maximus Metellus famously rescued it from the burning temple in 241 BC and was blinded, according to tradition, for looking upon it.

Later use of the term

By extension, "palladium" came to mean anything believed to afford effectual protection or safety, and in English political writing a safeguard of rights — as in the description of habeas corpus or a free press as the palladium of liberty.
