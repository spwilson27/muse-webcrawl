Arc de Triomphe

LocationPlace Charles de Gaulle, Paris, France
DesignerJean Chalgrin
Construction1806–1836
Height50 m (164 ft)
HonoursFrench victories and generals of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars

The Arc de Triomphe de l'Étoile (French: [aʁk də tʁijɔ̃f də letwal], "Triumphal Arch of the Star") is one of the most famous monuments in Paris, France, standing at the western end of the Champs-Élysées at the centre of Place Charles de Gaulle, formerly named Place de l'Étoile, from whose junction twelve avenues radiate. It honours those who fought and died for France in the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, with the names of French victories and generals inscribed on its surfaces.

History

Napoleon I commissioned the arch in 1806 after his victory at Austerlitz, to a neoclassical design by Jean Chalgrin inspired by the Arch of Titus in Rome. Work halted during the Bourbon Restoration and was completed under Louis-Philippe in 1836. Napoleon's remains passed beneath the arch in 1840 on their way to Les Invalides.

Design and sculpture

The arch is 50 metres high, 45 metres wide, and 22 metres deep. Its four pillars carry celebrated sculptural groups: Le Départ (the "Marseillaise") by François Rude, Le Triomphe by Jean-Pierre Cortot, and La Résistance and La Paix by Antoine Étex. The attic bears the names of 96 battles; the interior walls list 660 generals, with those who died in battle underlined.

Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

Beneath the vault lies the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier from the First World War, interred in 1921. An eternal flame, first lit in 1923, is rekindled every evening — a ceremony maintained even through the German occupation. French national commemorations, including the Bastille Day military parade, take place at the arch, and it marks the finish of the Tour de France on the Champs-Élysées.
