Government debt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A national debt clock display ticking upward in a city centre]
A national debt clock display ticking upward in a city centre

Government debt (also public debt or sovereign debt) is the total financial liabilities of a government, accumulated through past budget deficits and owed to bondholders, banks, foreign states and the public. It is usually expressed as a percentage of GDP. Advanced-economy debt averages around 100% of GDP after the rises driven by the 2008 financial crisis and COVID-19 spending; Japan's ratio, the highest among major economies, exceeds 250%.

Mechanics

Governments borrow by issuing securities — bills, notes and bonds — in their own or foreign currency. Debt sustainability depends on the interest rate relative to growth (the r − g relationship), the currency of denomination, maturity structure and who holds the debt. Central banks may hold large shares (Quantitative easing), blurring monetary and fiscal policy; debt in a currency the state controls rarely ends in outright default, but can end in inflation or financial repression.

Crises

Foreign-currency and short-term debt has driven repeated crises: Latin America in the 1980s, Asia in 1997, the eurozone crisis of 2010–12 (Greece restructured in the largest sovereign default in history), and emerging-market defaults after 2020 (Sri Lanka, Zambia, Ghana). The IMF's lending and restructuring frameworks attempt to order the aftermath.

Debates

How much debt is too much is economics' perennial political question: fears of crowding out and burdening future generations contend with arguments that safe-asset demand, low rates and public investment justify high ratios, and with historical experience — Britain carried debt above 200% of GDP after 1945 into decades of growth. The contested 90% 'threshold' claim of Reinhart and Rogoff (undermined by a famous spreadsheet error) is a cautionary tale in evidence-based policy.
