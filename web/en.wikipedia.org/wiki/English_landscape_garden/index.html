The English landscape garden, also called the English landscape park, is a style of "natural" garden design that emerged in England in the early 18th century and spread across Europe, replacing the formal geometric French garden. It presented an idealised, picturesque view of nature — sweeping lawns, groves of trees, serpentine lakes and classical or Gothic follies — carefully composed like a landscape painting.

Origins

Writers such as Alexander Pope and Joseph Addison praised irregular, natural scenery, and designers William Kent and Charles Bridgeman began loosening the geometry of gardens at Stowe and Rousham, using the sunken fence or ha-ha to merge garden and parkland.

Capability Brown

Lancelot Brown (1716–1783), nicknamed "Capability" for his talk of a place's "capabilities", reshaped over 170 estates. His hallmarks were smooth rolling grass down to the water's edge, serpentine lakes made by damming streams, belts and clumps of trees, and the removal of formal avenues and parterres.

Later developments

Humphry Repton reintroduced terraces and flower gardens near the house, while the picturesque theorists Uvedale Price and Richard Payne Knight favoured rougher, wilder scenery. The style profoundly influenced public parks and suburban gardens worldwide.
