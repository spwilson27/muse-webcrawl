What Is a Nation?

"What Is a Nation?" (French: Qu'est-ce qu'une nation ?) is a famous lecture delivered by the French historian and philosopher Ernest Renan at the Sorbonne in Paris on 11 March 1882. It is regarded as one of the classic texts in the study of nationalism.

Summary

Renan began by rejecting the definitions of nationhood fashionable in his day: race, language, religion, geography, and shared material interests. None of these, he argued, was either necessary or sufficient. A race is a mixed and shifting thing; language invites but does not compel political union; religion had become a private matter; and rivers and mountain ranges do not make a fatherland.

Instead, Renan proposed a spiritual and voluntarist definition. A nation is a soul, a spiritual principle formed of two things: a rich legacy of shared memories, sacrifices, and glories in the past, and a present-day consent — the clearly expressed desire to continue living together and to perpetuate that inheritance. In his most quoted phrase, the existence of a nation is "a daily plebiscite" (un plébiscite de tous les jours): nationhood rests on the continuing will of its members, not on blood or soil.

Renan also stressed the role of forgetting: "Forgetting — I would even say historical error — is an essential factor in the creation of a nation." Citizens must forget the massacres and divisions of the past in order to remember a common story.

Context and influence

The lecture was delivered a decade after France's defeat in the Franco-Prussian War and Germany's annexation of Alsace-Lorraine. German scholars had justified the annexation on ethnic and linguistic grounds; Renan's answer was that the wishes of the Alsatians themselves should decide. The essay became a foundational text for the "civic" or voluntarist conception of the nation, contrasted with ethnic conceptions, and is still cited in debates over European integration, secession, and citizenship.
