Snap election

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A snap election is an election called earlier than the scheduled date, typically when a head of government or legislature chooses to seek a new mandate at a politically advantageous moment, or when a government falls. In parliamentary systems the power to dissolve the legislature and call a snap election historically belonged to the head of state on the prime minister's advice; many systems now limit it by fixed-term legislation, constructive no-confidence rules, or requirements that dissolution follow a failed government formation.

Snap elections are gambles: Theresa May's in 2017 (called to increase her majority) cost the British Conservatives their majority; others (Japan's frequent dissolutions of the Diet, a prerogative of the prime minister) usually consolidate the incumbent. They are also a normal instrument after coalition collapse or to resolve deadlock. In presidential systems with fixed terms there is no full equivalent, though recall elections and early presidential elections after resignation perform partly similar functions.
