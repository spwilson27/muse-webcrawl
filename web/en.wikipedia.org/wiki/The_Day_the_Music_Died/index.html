"The Day the Music Died" refers to the plane crash on February 3, 1959, that killed American rock and roll musicians Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens, and J. P. "The Big Bopper" Richardson, together with pilot Roger Peterson. The phrase was coined by singer-songwriter Don McLean in his 1971 song "American Pie", which memorialized the accident and the subsequent decade of American music and culture.

Background

The three musicians were headlining the Winter Dance Party tour of the upper Midwest, traveling on poorly heated buses in severe winter weather. After a show at the Surf Ballroom in Clear Lake, Iowa, Holly chartered a small plane to fly to the next stop, Moorhead, Minnesota, to gain rest and time to do laundry. Richardson, ill with flu, took the seat of Holly's bassist Waylon Jennings, while Valens won the seat of guitarist Tommy Allsup on a coin toss.

The crash

The single-engine Beechcraft Bonanza took off from Mason City Municipal Airport shortly before 1 a.m. in deteriorating weather. The pilot, 21-year-old Roger Peterson, was not certified to fly by instruments alone. The aircraft crashed into a cornfield minutes after takeoff, killing all four aboard. Investigations concluded that the crash resulted from the pilot's loss of control in adverse weather, aggravated by unfamiliarity with the aircraft's attitude indicator.

Legacy

The accident is widely regarded as a defining tragedy of early rock and roll. A monument marks the crash site, and the Surf Ballroom holds an annual Winter Dance Party memorial concert. Don McLean's "American Pie" (1971) made the date famous as "the day the music died", and films including The Buddy Holly Story (1978) and La Bamba (1987) dramatized the musicians' lives.
