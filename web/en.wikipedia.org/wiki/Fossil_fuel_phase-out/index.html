Fossil fuel phase-out

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fossil fuel phase-out is the gradual reduction of the use and production of fossil fuels — coal, oil and natural gas — to zero, as part of the transition to low-carbon energy needed to limit climate change. Climate scenarios assessed by the IPCC that limit warming to 1.5 °C require deep cuts in fossil fuel use by mid-century, and the International Energy Agency's net-zero pathway concluded in 2021 that no new oil and gas fields or coal mines need be developed if the target is to be met.

Measures

Phase-out policies include ending new exploration licences, removing fossil fuel subsidies, banning new internal-combustion vehicle sales, setting coal phase-out dates, carbon pricing, and substituting renewable energy, nuclear power, energy storage and efficiency measures. Coal, the most carbon-intensive fuel, has been retired rapidly in many OECD countries: the United Kingdom, once reliant on coal for most electricity, closed its last coal power station in 2024. A number of countries and institutions have also adopted fossil fuel divestment policies.

Debates

Disagreement centres on the pace of transition, the role of natural gas as a "bridge fuel", carbon capture and storage, energy security, and the treatment of producing countries and workers — the "just transition" question. At the COP28 climate conference in 2023, governments agreed for the first time to "transition away from fossil fuels" in energy systems, though the text stopped short of the "phase-out" wording sought by many parties. A proposed Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty has been endorsed by a growing group of states and cities.
