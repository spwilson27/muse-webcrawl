Samuel Marsden

Born25 June 1765, Farsley, Yorkshire, England
Died12 May 1838, Windsor, New South Wales
OccupationAnglican clergyman, magistrate, agriculturalist
Known forPrincipal chaplain of NSW; missionary to New Zealand

Samuel Marsden (25 June 1765 – 12 May 1838) was an English-born Anglican clergyman who became the senior Church of England chaplain in the colony of New South Wales, Australia. He is remembered as a successful farmer and magistrate — earning the nickname the "Flogging Parson" for the severity of punishments he ordered — and as the driving force behind the first Christian missions to New Zealand.

Career in New South Wales

Marsden arrived in Sydney in 1794 as assistant chaplain and became principal chaplain in 1800. He acquired extensive land at Parramatta and pioneered merino wool growing, helping establish the Australian wool industry through his flocks and his 1807 trip to England carrying samples of colonial wool. His dual roles as clergyman and wealthy landowner, combined with his harshness as a magistrate, made him one of the colony's most controversial figures.

Mission to New Zealand

Working through the Church Missionary Society, Marsden sponsored a mission to New Zealand, purchasing land at Rangihoua in the Bay of Islands. On Christmas Day 1814 he preached the first known Christian sermon in New Zealand, beginning "Behold, I bring you glad tidings of great joy." He made seven voyages to New Zealand, maintaining close, if sometimes fraught, relationships with Māori chiefs such as Ruatara and Hongi Hika, and promoting the mission as a civilising as much as evangelising project.

Legacy

Marsden's reputation remains divided: celebrated as an agricultural pioneer and the "apostle of New Zealand", he is also criticised for his treatment of convicts and his role in early colonial dispossession. Marsden Park in Sydney, schools, and the Marsden Fund for research in New Zealand are named for him.
