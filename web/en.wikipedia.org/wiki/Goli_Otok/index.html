Goli Otok (pronounced [ɡôliː ǒtok]; Italian: Isola Calva; literally "Barren Island") is a barren, uninhabited island in the northern Adriatic Sea, off the coast of present-day Croatia, between the mainland and the island of Rab. It is best known as the site of a notorious political prison and labour camp operated by the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia between 1949 and 1956, and as a prison until 1988.

The camp

After the Tito–Stalin split of 1948, Yugoslav authorities established the camp on the deserted island in 1949 to intern suspected supporters of the Soviet Union and the Cominform — so-called ibeovci. Prisoners, who included Communist Party members, army officers, intellectuals and ordinary citizens denounced by neighbours, were subjected to forced labour in stone quarries, systematic beatings and a regime of prisoner-on-prisoner violence intended to force them to "re-educate" themselves and prove loyalty to Tito.

Figures for the number of internees vary; estimates commonly range from about 16,000 to over 30,000 people passing through the camp and its associated facilities, including the women's camp on nearby Sveti Grgur. Several hundred prisoners died from beatings, disease, exhaustion and suicide, though exact numbers remain disputed.

Closure and legacy

The political camp was dissolved in 1956 as Yugoslav–Soviet relations normalised after Stalin's death. The island continued to operate as a prison for ordinary criminals until 1988, when it was abandoned. Since then the ruins of the camp have decayed, visited by tourists and by survivors' associations pressing for memorialisation. Goli Otok occupies a prominent place in Yugoslav and post-Yugoslav literature and film as a symbol of political repression, sometimes compared to the Soviet Gulag.
