Colonel

Colonel (abbreviated as Col., Col or COL) is a senior military officer rank used in many countries. It is also used in some police forces and paramilitary organizations. In the 17th, 18th and 19th centuries, a colonel was typically in charge of a regiment in an army. Modern usage varies greatly, and in some cases the term is used as an honorific title for individuals in a military, police force, or paramilitary capacity.

The rank of colonel is typically above the rank of lieutenant colonel and below the rank of brigadier general. In many modern armies, a colonel commands a brigade-sized formation or serves in senior staff appointments.

Etymology

The word colonel derives from the same root as the word column (Italian: colonna) and means "of a column", and, by implication, "commander of a column". The word is first attested in the mid-16th century in Italian as colonnello, meaning the commander of a column of soldiers. In English, the spelling "colonel" is pronounced /ˈkɜːrnəl/ ("kernel"), a pronunciation that developed through early modern French and Spanish variants such as coronel.

History

By the late 19th century, colonel was a professional military rank though still held typically by an officer in command of a regiment or equivalent group of battalions. Along with other ranks, it has become progressively more a matter of ranked duties in the professional military, and less an hereditary or social function. In many modern armies, colonels command brigades of several thousand soldiers, or hold senior positions at the joint or service staff level.

Colonel-in-chief and honorary colonels

In some Commonwealth armies, the position of colonel-in-chief is an honorary appointment, often held by a member of the royal family. Honorary colonels may also be appointed to individual units. In the United States, some states grant honorary colonelcies — famously the title of Kentucky Colonel, an honorary commission awarded by the Governor of Kentucky.
