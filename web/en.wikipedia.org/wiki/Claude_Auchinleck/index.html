Field Marshal Sir Claude John Eyre Auchinleck (21 June 1884 – 23 March 1981), nicknamed "The Auk", was a British soldier who served as a senior officer in the British Army during the Second World War. He commanded Allied forces in the North African campaign as Commander-in-Chief, Middle East, from 1941 to 1942, halting Erwin Rommel's advance at the First Battle of El Alamein before being replaced by General Bernard Montgomery. He later served as Commander-in-Chief, India, until the partition of India in 1947.

Early career

Auchinleck was educated at Wellington College and the Royal Military College, Sandhurst, and commissioned into the Indian Army in 1904. He served in Mesopotamia during the First World War, where he was mentioned in dispatches and awarded the Distinguished Service Order. Between the wars he rose through staff and command appointments in India, becoming Chief of the General Staff in India and commanding the Meerut district.

Second World War

In 1940 Auchinleck briefly commanded Allied forces in Norway, then Southern Command in England, before becoming Commander-in-Chief, India. In July 1941 he succeeded General Archibald Wavell as Commander-in-Chief, Middle East. His Operation Crusader (November–December 1941) relieved Tobruk but did not destroy the Axis forces. In mid-1942, after the fall of Tobruk and retreat into Egypt, Auchinleck took direct command of the Eighth Army and stopped Rommel at the First Battle of El Alamein in July 1942. Nevertheless, Churchill, dissatisfied with the pace of offensive action, replaced him in August 1942 with General Harold Alexander in the Middle East and Montgomery in the Eighth Army.

India and partition

Auchinleck returned as Commander-in-Chief, India in 1943 and oversaw the enormous expansion and support of the Fourteenth Army fighting in Burma. In 1946 he was promoted field marshal. As partition approached, he became Supreme Commander of the armed forces of India and Pakistan, a role he held until November 1947; he opposed the division of the Indian Army and worked to supervise it fairly. He retired in 1948.

Later life

Auchinleck settled in Marrakech, Morocco, where he lived quietly until his death in 1981 at age 96. Historians have increasingly credited his defensive victory at First Alamein and his appointment of capable subordinates with laying the groundwork for the later Allied success in North Africa, though his caution and his clashes with Churchill ended his field command.
