Fomite

A fomite (pronounced /ˈfoʊmaɪt/) or fomes (pronounced /ˈfoʊmiːz/; plural fomites) is any inanimate object that, when contaminated with or exposed to infectious agents (such as pathogenic bacteria, viruses or fungi), can transfer disease to a new host. Research has shown that a wide range of surfaces commonly encountered in everyday life can serve as fomites.

Transfer of pathogens by fomites

Transmission occurs when an object becomes contaminated — for example, when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or touches a surface — and a susceptible person later touches the same surface and then touches their mouth, nose, or eyes. Common fomites include door handles, handrails, hospital bed rails, light switches, mobile phones, clothing, utensils, and medical equipment.

The likelihood of fomite transmission depends on the pathogen's ability to survive on surfaces, the amount of pathogen transferred, and environmental factors such as temperature and humidity. Porous surfaces (such as fabrics) generally retain pathogens less readily than non-porous surfaces (such as stainless steel or plastic), on which some viruses can survive for hours to days.

History of research

The idea that objects could carry contagion is centuries old, and the term derives from the Latin fōmes, meaning kindling or tinder — material that feeds a fire, by analogy feeding the spread of disease. Fomite transmission played a significant role in historical outbreaks of diseases such as smallpox, and the concept underlies modern practices of disinfection, sterilisation of medical instruments, and hand hygiene in healthcare settings.
