TypePunishment device

The pillory is a device made of a wooden or metal framework erected on a post, with holes for securing the head and hands, used during the Middle Ages and in early modern times for punishment by public humiliation and often further physical abuse. The pillory is related to the stocks, which held the offender by the ankles in a seated position; in the pillory, by contrast, the victim stood with head and wrists locked in a horizontal hinged board.

History

Punishment by exposure in such devices dates back to antiquity, but the pillory became widespread in medieval Europe for offences including fraud, perjury, defamation, cheating in trade (such as selling short-weight bread), and disorderly conduct. The offender was typically displayed for a set period — often one hour at midday on market day — in a public place, where the crowd was free to jeer and throw refuse, rotten food, and stones, sometimes causing serious injury or death. In some cases offenders also had an ear nailed to the board or were branded or mutilated.

In England, the pillory was prescribed by statute for specific offences from the 13th century. Notable victims included the writer Daniel Defoe, pilloried in 1703 for seditious libel — legend holds that the crowd instead threw flowers and drank his health. In the United States, the pillory was used in colonial and early national times; Delaware was the last state to abolish it, retaining it formally until 1905, though the last use there was in 1852.

Abolition

Use of the pillory declined in the 18th and 19th centuries as opinion turned against public corporal punishment. France abolished it in 1832 and England in 1837. The word survives metaphorically: to be "pilloried" is to be subjected to public ridicule and abuse.
