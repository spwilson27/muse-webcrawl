An Associate Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States is a member of the Supreme Court other than the Chief Justice. The number of associate justices is set by Congress; since the Judiciary Act of 1869 there have been eight, giving a Court of nine.

Appointment and tenure

Under the Appointments Clause of Article II of the US Constitution, associate justices are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate. Like all federal judges under Article III, they hold office "during good Behaviour" — in practice, for life, until death, retirement, resignation, or removal by impeachment. Only one justice, Samuel Chase, has ever been impeached (in 1804; he was acquitted).

Duties

Associate justices hear cases, vote in conference, and write majority, concurring and dissenting opinions, which are assigned by the Chief Justice when the Chief is in the majority, or by the most senior associate justice in the majority otherwise. Each justice is also assigned as "circuit justice" for one or more federal judicial circuits, handling emergency applications arising there. Seniority among associate justices is determined by date of commission and structures seating, speaking order in conference, and office selection.

History

The original Court in 1789 had five associate justices. The number fluctuated during the 19th century — reaching ten during the Civil War era — before settling at eight in 1869. Retired justices may sit by designation on lower federal courts.
