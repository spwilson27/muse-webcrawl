The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) is the revenue service of the United States federal government, responsible for collecting federal taxes and administering the Internal Revenue Code. It is a bureau of the Department of the Treasury, headed by a Commissioner appointed by the President with Senate confirmation for a five-year term.

[Image: The Internal Revenue Building in Washington, D.C., a limestone federal building]

History

The office originated during the Civil War, when President Abraham Lincoln and Congress created the Commissioner of Internal Revenue in 1862 and enacted an income tax to fund the war effort. That tax was later repealed, revived, and struck down, before ratification of the Sixteenth Amendment in 1913 firmly established Congress's power to tax incomes. The agency was renamed the Internal Revenue Service in the 1950s during a reorganization that replaced patronage with a career civil service. A further restructuring followed the IRS Restructuring and Reform Act of 1998.

Organization and duties

The IRS processes well over two hundred million tax returns annually and collects the bulk of federal revenue. Its divisions serve wage earners and small businesses, large businesses and international taxpayers, tax-exempt and government entities, and retirement plans. Specialized functions include criminal investigation of tax fraud and money laundering, oversight of tax-exempt organizations, and the Office of Chief Counsel.

Tax collection

Most individual income tax is collected through employer withholding and estimated payments, with taxpayers filing annual returns — most commonly Form 1040 — to reconcile what was paid against what is owed. The IRS matches returns against information reports such as W-2 and 1099 forms, audits a small fraction of returns, and can assess penalties and interest or pursue liens and levies for unpaid taxes.
