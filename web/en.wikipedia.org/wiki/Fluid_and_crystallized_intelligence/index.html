Fluid and crystallized intelligence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychology, fluid and crystallized intelligence (abbreviated Gf and Gc) are factors of general intelligence (g) originally identified by Raymond Cattell. The concepts were developed further by Cattell's student John L. Horn and are central to the Cattell–Horn–Carroll theory of cognitive abilities.

Fluid intelligence

Fluid intelligence is the capacity to reason and solve novel problems independently of previously acquired knowledge. It involves perceiving relationships, drawing inferences, and comprehending implications in unfamiliar material, and is measured by tasks such as matrix reasoning (e.g. Raven's Progressive Matrices), series completion and figure classification. Fluid intelligence tends to peak in early adulthood and to decline with age, and it is relatively sensitive to brain injury and neurological condition.

Crystallized intelligence

Crystallized intelligence is the ability to use skills, knowledge and experience — the depth and breadth of a person's accumulated verbal and cultural knowledge. It is measured through vocabulary, general information and reading comprehension tests. Unlike fluid ability, crystallized intelligence typically continues to grow through middle age and remains comparatively stable into later life, as it depends on stored knowledge rather than novel processing.

Cattell proposed an "investment" theory: fluid intelligence is invested over the lifespan in learning, producing crystallized knowledge. The distinction has been influential in intelligence testing, developmental psychology and the study of cognitive ageing.
