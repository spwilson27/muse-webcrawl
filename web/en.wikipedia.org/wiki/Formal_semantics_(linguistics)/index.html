Formal semantics (linguistics)

In linguistics, formal semantics is the study of grammatical meaning in natural languages using formal tools from logic and theoretical computer science. It is an interdisciplinary field connecting linguistics, philosophy and computer science. Formal semanticists seek to explain the semantic behaviour of natural language expressions in terms of abstract models — typically assigning expressions denotations in a model (such as sets, truth values or functions) and computing the meanings of sentences compositionally from the meanings of their parts, following the principle of compositionality associated with Gottlob Frege.

The field grew out of the work of the logician Richard Montague in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Montague's famous claim that there is "no important theoretical difference between natural languages and the artificial languages of logicians" underlies Montague grammar, which used typed lambda calculus and intensional logic to give a rigorous semantics for a fragment of English including quantification and verb phrase modification.

Central topics

  • Quantification: the semantics of expressions like every, some and most, treated as relations between sets in generalised quantifier theory.
  • Tense, aspect and modality: how languages encode time, event structure and possibility/necessity.
  • Anaphora and binding: pronoun interpretation and its relation to syntax.
  • Scope and ambiguity: sentences with several possible readings arising from quantifier interactions.
  • Presupposition and implicature: meaning beyond truth conditions, interacting with formal models.
  • Dynamic semantics: meaning as context-change potential (discourse representation theory, file change semantics).

Relation to other fields

Formal semantics in linguistics is distinct from, but related to, formal semantics in logic and computer science, where the semantics of formal and programming languages is defined. Linguists borrow the machinery of model theory from logic while aiming to account for empirical phenomena in human languages — cross-linguistic variation, acquisition and processing.
