The Yup'ik (plural Yupiit) are an Alaska Native people of western and southwestern Alaska, ranging from the Norton Sound down along the Bering Sea coast to Bristol Bay, and inland along the lower Yukon and Kuskokwim rivers. Together with the closely related Cup'ik, Cup'ig and Sugpiaq (Alutiiq) peoples, they are among the Indigenous peoples commonly grouped as Yupik. The name is formed from yuk ("person") plus the postbase -pik ("real" or "genuine"), meaning "real people".

Language

The Central Alaskan Yup'ik language is one of the most widely spoken Indigenous languages in Alaska, with roughly ten thousand or more speakers. It is an Eskaleut language related to the Inuit languages and to Siberian Yupik, though not mutually intelligible with them. A practical writing system in the Latin alphabet was developed in the mid-20th century, notably through the work of linguists and native speakers at the University of Alaska, and the language is taught in immersion and bilingual programmes in many Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta schools.

Culture and subsistence

Yup'ik life has traditionally centred on a subsistence cycle of fishing — especially for salmon — hunting marine mammals and waterfowl, gathering berries and greens, and winter trapping. Extended family and community cooperation remain central, with food sharing networks distributing the harvest. Historic social institutions included the qasgiq (men's community house) and ceremonial cycles such as the Bladder Festival, in which the souls of hunted animals were honoured. Dance, drumming, storytelling and the carving of elaborate wooden masks continue as major cultural expressions.

History

Yup'ik ancestors have lived in the region for at least two thousand years. Intensive contact began in the early 19th century with the Russian-American fur trade, followed by Moravian, Catholic and Russian Orthodox missions, epidemics that sharply reduced the population, and the commercial salmon industry. Under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, Yup'ik communities are shareholders in regional and village corporations such as Calista. Today Yup'ik people navigate the challenges of erosion and climate change affecting many delta villages while maintaining one of the strongest Indigenous language communities in the United States.
