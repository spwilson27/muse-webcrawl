Māori culture

Māori culture
People: Māori of Aotearoa (New Zealand)
Language: te reo Māori
Origins: Polynesian settlement, c. 13th century
Key concepts: whakapapa, mana, tapu, whānau, iwi

Māori culture is the culture of the Māori, the Indigenous Polynesian people of New Zealand (Aotearoa), who arrived by voyaging canoe from East Polynesia around the thirteenth century. It developed in relative isolation for centuries, forming a tribal society organised around whānau (extended family), hapū (sub-tribe), and iwi (tribe).

Arts and traditions

Māori art is renowned for whakairo (wood carving), raranga (weaving, including flax garments and tukutuku panels), and tā moko (tattooing), which encodes genealogy and status. Oral traditions preserve whakapapa (genealogy), history, and mythology. Kapa haka — ensemble performance of song, dance, and the haka posture dance — remains a vibrant competitive and ceremonial art.

Society and values

Central concepts include mana (prestige and authority), tapu (sacred restriction), utu (reciprocity), and kaitiakitanga (guardianship of the environment). The marae, a complex centred on a carved meeting house, is the focus of communal and ceremonial life, including tangihanga (funerals) and formal welcomes (pōwhiri).

Colonisation and revival

From the nineteenth century, colonisation, land loss, and urbanisation eroded Māori language and institutions. Since the 1970s a cultural renaissance has revived te reo Māori through kōhanga reo language nests and broadcasting, restored marae, and advanced Māori claims under the Treaty of Waitangi.
