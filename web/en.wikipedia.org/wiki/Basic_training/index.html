Basic training

Recruits drilling during basic training.

Basic training, also called recruit training or colloquially boot camp, is the initial period of military instruction given to new personnel, which converts civilians into soldiers. It typically lasts from several weeks to several months and combines physical conditioning, drill, weapons handling, fieldcraft, first aid, and intense acculturation to military discipline, hierarchy and values. Graduates proceed to advanced or trade training in their assigned specialities.

Purpose and methods

The stated aims of basic training are to teach fundamental soldiering skills, instil obedience and unit cohesion, and build physical and mental resilience. Training regimes are deliberately stressful: recruits live communally, follow rigid schedules, are addressed by drill instructors (drill sergeants in the US Army), and are subject to corrective discipline. Sociologists describe the process as a form of resocialisation, in which civilian identity is broken down and a collective military identity is built up.

By country

In the United States, each service runs its own programme: Army Basic Combat Training lasts about 10 weeks; Marine Corps Recruit Training, the longest US programme, runs about 13 weeks at Parris Island or San Diego; Navy, Air Force and Coast Guard courses run roughly 8–10 weeks. In the United Kingdom, army recruits complete the Common Military Syllabus over about 14 weeks (longer for infantry) at centres such as Catterick and Winchester. Many other states with conscription run basic training phases of comparable length before conscripts join units.

Criticism and reform

Basic training has periodically been criticised over hazing, abuse scandals, recruit deaths and mental-health outcomes, prompting reforms in instructor oversight, safeguarding of young recruits, and restrictions on degrading punishments in many armed forces.
