Principality of Lippe

Placeholder image: the princely residence at Detmold

The Principality of Lippe (German: Fürstentum Lippe) was a state in Germany, ruled by the House of Lippe. It was located between the Weser River and the southeast part of the Teutoburg Forest. The county of Lippe, raised to a principality in 1789, survived as a constituent state of the Confederation of the Rhine, the German Confederation, and the German Empire until the German Revolution of 1918, when it became the Free State of Lippe.

History

The lordship of Lippe is first documented in 1123 under Bernhard I; the family's seat moved to Detmold in the 13th century. Lippe became Protestant during the Reformation and later adopted Calvinism under Simon VI, though a Lutheran minority persisted. Count Simon VII's descendants partitioned parts of the territory into the cadet lines of Lippe-Brake, Lippe-Alverdissen (later Schaumburg-Lippe) and Lippe-Biesterfeld.

19th and 20th centuries

Leopold I was raised to Prince of Lippe in 1789. Pauline of Anhalt-Bernburg, regent from 1802 to 1820, was a notable reformer who abolished serfdom. In 1895 the extinction of the senior line's eligible heirs triggered the Lippe succession dispute between Schaumburg-Lippe and Lippe-Biesterfeld, resolved in 1905 in favour of Leopold IV of Lippe-Biesterfeld, the last reigning prince. He abdicated on 12 November 1918.

Geography

The principality covered about 1,215 km² with a population of roughly 150,000 by 1910. Its economy was largely agricultural, with linen weaving and, later, small industry; many Lippe men worked seasonally as migrant brickmakers (Ziegler).
