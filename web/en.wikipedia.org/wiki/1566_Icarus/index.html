1566 Icarus

1566 Icarus, provisional designation 1949 MA, is an asteroid and near-Earth object of the Apollo group, notable for its extremely eccentric orbit, which carries it closer to the Sun than Mercury at perihelion. Approximately 1.4 kilometres in diameter, it was discovered by Walter Baade in 1949 and named after Icarus of Greek mythology, who flew too close to the Sun.

Orbit

Icarus has one of the most eccentric orbits known among numbered asteroids, with an eccentricity of about 0.827. Its perihelion of 0.187 AU lies well inside the orbit of Mercury, while its aphelion of about 1.97 AU takes it out beyond the orbit of Mars. It completes an orbit every 409 days. Because its orbit is inclined and Earth-crossing, Icarus periodically passes close to Earth; in June 1968 it passed within about 0.042 AU (roughly 6.4 million kilometres), an approach that attracted public attention and prompted early studies of asteroid impact deflection. It also makes close approaches to Mercury and Venus.

Physical characteristics

Radar and photometric observations suggest a roughly spheroidal body about 1.4 kilometres across, rotating once every 2.27 hours — among the fastest known rotations for an asteroid of its size. Its close passages to the Sun subject its surface to extreme heating, with perihelion surface temperatures estimated around 800 K.

Project Icarus

In 1967, students at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology undertook "Project Icarus", a systems-engineering study of how the asteroid might be deflected or destroyed if it were found to be on a collision course with Earth. The study became a landmark in planetary defence thinking and inspired subsequent impact-mitigation research.
