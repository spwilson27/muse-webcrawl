Drawing a bow stores elastic energy in its limbs; releasing the string converts that energy into the arrow's kinetic energy.

The bow and arrow is a ranged weapon system consisting of an elastic launching device (bow) and long-shafted projectiles (arrows). The bow is a spring: its two flexible limbs are joined by a string, and drawing the string bends the limbs, storing potential energy that is released into the arrow when the string is loosed. Archery, the art of using the system, was a crucial technology throughout human history — for hunting and warfare from prehistory until the widespread adoption of firearms — and survives today as a major sport and recreational pursuit.

History

The origins of archery are ancient. Tiny stone points from Sibudu Cave in South Africa, about 64,000 years old, have been interpreted as arrowheads, and bone points of similar age are known from other sites, though the dating and function of the earliest examples remain debated. Unambiguous bows survive from the Mesolithic: the elm Holmegaard bows of Denmark date to roughly 9,000 years ago. Ötzi, the Copper Age mummy found in the Alps (c. 3300 BCE), carried an unfinished yew longbow and arrows.

In the ancient world, composite bows — laminated from wood, horn, and sinew — gave mounted steppe peoples such as the Scythians, Parthians, Huns, and later the Mongols devastating mobile firepower; the Parthian shot became proverbial. Egyptian, Assyrian, and Persian armies fielded massed archers, and the longbow dominated English warfare in the Hundred Years' War, notably at Crécy (1346) and Agincourt (1415). Gunpowder weapons progressively displaced the bow from the 16th century onward, though archery persisted in hunting and, in Japan, as the disciplined art of kyūdō.

Construction

A traditional bow may be a self bow (a single piece of wood, such as the English longbow of yew), a composite bow of laminated materials, or a modern recurve or compound design. Recurve bows have tips that curve away from the archer, increasing energy storage; compound bows, invented in the 1960s, use cams and pulleys to reduce the holding weight at full draw ("let-off") and dominate modern hunting and much target archery. Bowstrings were historically made of sinew, gut, rawhide, or plant fibres; modern strings use synthetic fibres such as Dacron or Dyneema.

Types of bow

  • Longbow — a tall self bow, roughly the height of the user; the English and Welsh war bow could exceed 100 lb (45 kg) in draw weight.
  • Recurve bow — limbs curve forward at the tips; the standard for Olympic archery in modern form.
  • Composite bow — horn, wood, and sinew laminate, compact and powerful, suited to horseback use.
  • Compound bow — cam-assisted modern design with high let-off and arrow speeds.
  • Crossbow — a bow mounted horizontally on a stock with a mechanical trigger, storing far heavier draw weights (treated separately from hand bows).

Arrows

An arrow consists of a shaft (wood, bamboo, aluminium, or carbon fibre), a point or head (stone, bronze, iron, or steel in the past; field points, broadheads, or target points today), fletching (feathers or plastic vanes that stabilise flight, sometimes set helically to impart spin), and a nock that fits the string. War arrows carried specialised heads — bodkin points against armour, broadheads against unarmoured targets and horses. Arrows are matched to the bow by stiffness ("spine") so that they flex correctly around the bow as they are loosed, a phenomenon known as the archer's paradox.
