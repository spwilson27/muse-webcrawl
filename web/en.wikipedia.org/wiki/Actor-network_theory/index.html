Actor-network theory

Actor–network theory (ANT) is a theoretical and methodological approach to social theory where everything in the social and natural worlds exists in constantly shifting networks of relationships. It was developed in science and technology studies principally by Bruno Latour, Michel Callon and John Law in the 1980s.

[Image: Diagram of an actor-network]

Key concepts

ANT posits that nothing exists outside these relationships and assumes that all actors — human and non-human alike (objects, machines, animals, texts) — exert agency within a network; this is the principle of generalised symmetry. An "actor" (or actant) is anything that modifies a state of affairs. Social order, scientific facts and technologies are treated as effects produced by the successful stabilisation of networks, through processes Callon called translation: problematisation, interessement, enrolment and mobilisation.

Applications and criticism

ANT has been applied in studies of laboratories, technology design, organisations, geography and information systems. Influential works include Latour's Laboratory Life (with Steve Woolgar) and We Have Never Been Modern. Critics charge ANT with neglecting pre-existing power structures and social structures, and with an amoral, descriptive stance; defenders reply that it deliberately avoids explaining effects by invoking society as a hidden cause.
