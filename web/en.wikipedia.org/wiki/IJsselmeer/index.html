The IJsselmeer (Dutch: [ɛisəlˈmeːr]; West Frisian: Iselmar) is a shallow artificial lake in the central Netherlands bordering the provinces of Flevoland, North Holland, and Friesland. With an area of about 1,100 km² it is the largest lake in the Netherlands by surface area. It was created in 1932 when the Zuiderzee, an inland sea of the North Sea, was closed off by the completion of the Afsluitdijk.

History

Plans to enclose the Zuiderzee were developed by the engineer Cornelis Lely in 1891, motivated by chronic flooding, the desire for new agricultural land, and the need for freshwater reserves. The Zuiderzee Act of 1918 authorised the Zuiderzee Works. The Afsluitdijk, finished in 1932, converted the tidal Zuiderzee into a freshwater lake, named after the river IJssel, which flows into it from the southeast.

Polders and the Markermeer

Large polders were reclaimed from the lake in the following decades: the Noordoostpolder (1942), Eastern Flevoland (1957), and Southern Flevoland (1968) — the three later forming the province of Flevoland. The Houtribdijk, completed in 1976, divides the IJsselmeer from the Markermeer to the southwest; a planned Markerwaard polder was never built, leaving the Markermeer as open water.

Ecology and use

The lake is an important freshwater reservoir for drinking water and agriculture, a major area for recreation and sailing, and a significant habitat for water birds; parts are designated Natura 2000 and Ramsar sites. Commercial fishing, once directed at eel and pike-perch, has declined. Freshwater flow to the Wadden Sea is regulated through sluices in the Afsluitdijk at Den Oever and Kornwerderzand.
