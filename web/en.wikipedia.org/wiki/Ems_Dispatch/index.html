Ems Dispatch

The Ems Dispatch (French: Dépêche d'Ems; German: Emser Depesche) was an internal telegram sent on 13 July 1870 by Heinrich Abeken, a senior official of the Prussian foreign ministry, from the resort town of Bad Ems to Chancellor Otto von Bismarck in Berlin, reporting a conversation between King Wilhelm I of Prussia and the French ambassador, Count Vincent Benedetti. Bismarck's edited and condensed release of the telegram to the press made the encounter appear insulting to both nations and was a proximate trigger of the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71).

Wilhelm I and Ambassador Benedetti on the promenade at Bad Ems, 13 July 1870.

Background

France, alarmed by Prussia's rise after 1866, opposed the candidacy of Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen for the vacant Spanish throne. Under French pressure, Leopold's candidacy was withdrawn on 12 July 1870 — a diplomatic triumph for Napoleon III's government. Not satisfied, France demanded that Wilhelm I, as head of the Hohenzollern house, formally guarantee that the candidacy would never be renewed.

The encounter and telegram

On the morning of 13 July, Ambassador Benedetti approached the king on the promenade at Bad Ems and conveyed the demand. Wilhelm politely refused a perpetual guarantee, saying he could not bind himself for all time, but assured Benedetti that he approved of the withdrawal and considered the matter closed. Later that day he declined a further formal audience, sending an aide-de-camp to say he had nothing more to communicate. Abeken's telegram reported these events to Berlin.

Bismarck's edit

Bismarck, dining with generals Moltke and Roon, condensed the telegram — cutting the conciliatory context so that the French demand read as an ultimatum and the king's reply as a curt dismissal via an adjutant. He remarked that the edited version would have "the effect of a red rag upon the Gallic bull." Released to the press and foreign embassies on 14 July (Bastille Day), the dispatch inflamed French public opinion; mistranslation into French (rendering the aide-de-camp, an Adjutant, as a lowly "lieutenant") deepened the perceived insult. German opinion likewise bristled at France's presumptuous demand.

Consequences

France declared war on 19 July 1870. The Franco-Prussian War ended in catastrophic French defeat: Napoleon III captured at Sedan, the Second Empire replaced by the Third Republic, and — on 18 January 1871 — the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles, fulfilling Bismarck's goal of unifying Germany under Prussia. The episode remains a classic case study of how editing and publishing a diplomatic message can manufacture a casus belli.
