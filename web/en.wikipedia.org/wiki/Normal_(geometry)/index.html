Normal (geometry)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A polygon and two of its normal vectors.

In geometry, a normal is an object (such as a line, ray, or vector) that is perpendicular to a given object. For example, the normal line to a plane curve at a given point is the (infinite) line perpendicular to the tangent line to the curve at the point. A normal vector is a vector perpendicular to a curve or surface at a point; a vector of length one is called a unit normal vector.

Normal to surfaces in 3D space

For a plane given by the equation ax + by + cz + d = 0, the vector (a, b, c) is a normal. For a surface defined implicitly by F(x, y, z) = 0, the gradient ∇F is perpendicular to the tangent plane and hence gives the normal direction at each (regular) point. For a parametric surface r(u, v), the normal can be found from the cross product of the partial derivatives ∂r/∂u × ∂r/∂v.

Normals are defined only up to sign: a surface may be orientable, with a consistent choice of outward or inward normals (as for a closed polyhedron, where vertex normals point outward), or non-orientable, like the Möbius strip, for which no continuous global choice exists.

Normal to a curve

For a plane curve, the normal line at a point is perpendicular to the tangent at that point. In differential geometry of curves, the (principal) normal is the unit vector in the direction of the derivative of the unit tangent vector, appearing in the Frenet–Serret formulas; it points toward the centre of the osculating circle, with magnitude related to the curvature.

Uses

Surface normals are fundamental in computer graphics, where they determine how light reflects from a surface under shading models such as Lambertian reflectance and the rendering equation; techniques like normal mapping simulate fine surface detail by perturbing normals. Normals are also used in physics and engineering for computing pressure forces, contact forces, and flux through surfaces, and in ray tracing to compute reflection and refraction directions.
