Naraka

Naraka (Sanskrit: नरक) is the realm of hell in Indian religions, a place of torment where the souls of sinners are punished after death. The concept appears in Hinduism, Jainism, Buddhism and Sikhism, though the details differ between traditions. Unlike eternal damnation in some other religions, a stay in Naraka is temporary: after the soul has endured the appropriate suffering for its karma, it is reborn in accordance with its remaining merit.

In Hinduism

In Hindu cosmology Naraka lies in the south of the universe, below the earth, and is ruled by Yama, the god of death and justice, whose scribe Chitragupta keeps the record of every being's deeds. The Garuda Purana and other texts describe a series of hells — commonly 28 — each assigned to particular sins. Tamisra, a place of darkness, punishes theft; Raurava, filled with serpents, awaits those who harm other creatures; Kumbhipaka boils sinners in oil; and Kalasutra torments those who disrespect their parents or elders. The punishments are finite, measured against the gravity of the sin, after which the soul proceeds to rebirth.

In Jainism

Jain cosmology divides the universe into the heavens, the middle world and the lower world (adho loka), which contains seven hells of increasing suffering: Ratnaprabha, Sharkaraprabha, Valukaprabha, Pankaprabha, Dhumaprabha, Tamahprabha and Mahatamahprabha. Beings are born into these realms as hell beings (naraki) because of violence and other grave karma. Even here, suffering is not eternal: when the karma is exhausted the being is reborn elsewhere. Jains regard hell as a powerful deterrent against violence and deceit.

In Buddhism

Buddhist cosmology describes eight great hot hells, including Samjiva, Kalasutra, Samghata, Raurava, Maharaurava, Tapana, Pratapana and Avici, the deepest and most terrible, reserved for those who commit the five heinous acts such as killing one's parents or shedding the blood of a Buddha. Around the hot hells lie eight cold hells, where beings suffer freezing torments. Time in these realms is vastly dilated: a single day in some hells equals millions of human years. Nevertheless, Buddhist hells are impermanent, and beings eventually leave them when their karma ripens.

In Sikhism

Sikh scripture uses the imagery of Narak, but interprets it largely in terms of the suffering caused by ego, attachment and separation from God in this life and the cycle of rebirth, rather than a single fixed place of punishment.
