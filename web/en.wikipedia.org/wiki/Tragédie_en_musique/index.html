A tragédie en musique (French: [tʁaʒedi ɑ̃ myzik], "tragedy in music"), also called tragédie lyrique, is a genre of French Baroque opera created by Jean-Baptiste Lully and his librettist Philippe Quinault for the court of Louis XIV. It was the dominant form of serious French opera from the 1670s until the mid-18th century.

Form

A tragédie en musique typically consists of an allegorical prologue praising the king, followed by five acts drawing on Greek mythology or chivalric romance (from Ariosto or Tasso). Each act contains a divertissement of dances, choruses and airs celebrating the dramatic situation. Lully developed a flexible French recitative modelled on the declamation of classical theatre, accompanied by a five-part string orchestra, and integrated ballet far more deeply than Italian opera of the day. The overture in the French style (slow dotted section, fugal fast section) framed the work.

History

Lully and Quinault produced a near-annual series from Cadmus et Hermione (1673) through Atys, Phaëton, Armide (1686) and Acis et Galatée. The genre was continued by successors including Marc-Antoine Charpentier (Médée), André Campra and Marin Marais, and reached a late flowering in the operas of Jean-Philippe Rameau — Hippolyte et Aricie (1733), Castor et Pollux and Dardanus — which enriched its harmony and orchestration. The form declined amid the Querelle des Bouffons of the 1750s, which championed Italian comic opera.
