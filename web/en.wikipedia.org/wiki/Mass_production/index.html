Mass production

Mass production, also known as flow production, is the manufacture of large quantities of standardised products, often using assembly lines or automation technology. It is one of the three main production methods, alongside job production and batch production. Mass production is capital intensive and energy intensive, typically using a high volume and a low variety of goods.

History

Early examples of mass production include the Venetian Arsenal, which produced ships using prefabricated parts, and the Portsmouth Block Mills in England (1803), where Marc Isambard Brunel and Henry Maudslay built machinery to make ships' pulley blocks. The system of interchangeable parts, developed in American armouries in the early 19th century, made it possible to assemble products from standardised components.

The modern era of mass production began with the automobile industry. Ransom E. Olds used stationary assembly-line techniques, and Henry Ford perfected the moving assembly line at the Highland Park plant in 1913, cutting Model T chassis assembly time from over 12 hours to about 93 minutes. Ford combined mass production with high wages — the five-dollar day — a system later called Fordism.

Characteristics

Mass production relies on the division of labour, specialised machine tools, jigs and fixtures, and economies of scale: as production volume rises, unit cost falls. Products are typically manufactured to stock rather than to order. Later developments, including lean manufacturing pioneered by Toyota and computer-controlled flexible manufacturing, sought to reduce the rigidity of traditional mass production.
