In algebraic number theory, the adele ring (also called the ring of adeles) of a global field is a topological ring constructed by combining all the completions of the field — the field itself completed at every place, including both the finite (p-adic) completions and the infinite (real and complex) ones — into a single object, subject to a restricted-product condition requiring that almost every component lie in the corresponding ring of integers.

[Image: Schematic of the adele ring as a restricted product of local completions]

Definition

For a number field K, the adele ring AK is the restricted direct product of the completions Kv over all places v of K, with respect to the local rings of integers Ov for the finite places. An adele is thus a family (xv) with xv ∈ Kv for all v and xv ∈ Ov for all but finitely many finite v. With the restricted product topology, AK is a locally compact topological ring.

Ideles

The multiplicative group of the adele ring is the idele group AK×, the restricted product of the multiplicative groups of the completions. The field K embeds diagonally in its adeles, and K× embeds in the ideles; the quotient of the ideles by the embedded K× is the idele class group, the central object of class field theory, which encodes abelian extensions of K.

History and applications

The theory was developed by Claude Chevalley in the 1930s (under the name "idèles") and popularised by André Weil, whose coinage "adèle" puns on the French word for addition and the female name. John Tate's 1950 doctoral thesis showed that harmonic analysis on the adele ring yields the analytic continuation and functional equation of Hecke L-functions in a unified way. Adeles and ideles are foundational for the modern statements of global class field theory, the Langlands program, and the arithmetic of automorphic forms.
