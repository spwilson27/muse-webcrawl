Abydos (Arabic: أبيدوس; ancient Egyptian: Abdju) is one of the oldest and most important archaeological sites of ancient Egypt, located in Upper Egypt about 11 kilometres west of the Nile. It was the burial place of Egypt's earliest pharaohs and became the principal cult centre of Osiris, god of the afterlife, drawing pilgrims for more than two thousand years.

History

The royal necropolis at Umm El Qa'ab contains the tombs of the First and Second Dynasty kings (c. 3100–2700 BC) and of predynastic rulers before them, including the tomb (U-j) of the scorpion king whose grave goods include some of the earliest known Egyptian writing. In the Middle and New Kingdoms, Abydos flourished as the centre of the Osiris cult: Egyptians believed Osiris was buried there, and vast processions re-enacted his death and resurrection. The great Temple of Seti I (c. 1300 BC) preserves some of the finest relief carving in Egypt and the famous Abydos King List, a chronological list of pharaohs; behind it lies the mysterious sunken Osireion.

Excavations

The site was excavated by Auguste Mariette, Émile Amélineau, Flinders Petrie — whose work there revolutionised Egyptian archaeology — and, in recent decades, the German Archaeological Institute and the Pennsylvania–Yale expedition. Discoveries include the Abydos boats (c. 3000 BC), among the world's oldest planked vessels, buried beside a royal funerary enclosure.
