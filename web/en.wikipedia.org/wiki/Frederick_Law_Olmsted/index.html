Frederick Law Olmsted

Frederick Law Olmsted (April 26, 1822 – August 28, 1903) was an American landscape architect, journalist, social critic, and public administrator. He is regarded as the founder of American landscape architecture, best known for designing Central Park in New York City with Calvert Vaux and for creating park systems in cities across the United States.

Career

Before turning to design, Olmsted travelled the American South as a journalist, publishing influential accounts of slavery, and co-founded The Nation. In 1858 he and Vaux won the competition to design Central Park. During the Civil War he directed the U.S. Sanitary Commission. Afterwards his firm designed Prospect Park in Brooklyn, the grounds of the U.S. Capitol, the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, Buffalo's park system, Boston's Emerald Necklace, and the campus of Stanford University.

Philosophy and legacy

Olmsted believed parks should be democratic spaces where all citizens could enjoy the restorative effects of scenery. He also championed the preservation of natural wonders, helping protect Niagara Falls and Yosemite. His sons and successors continued his firm into the twentieth century, and many Olmsted landscapes are now protected historic sites.
