Archtop guitar

A classic archtop guitar with carved top and f-holes.

An archtop guitar is a hollow or semi-hollow steel-stringed acoustic or electric guitar whose top (soundboard) is carved or pressed into an arch shape, in the manner of a violin-family instrument. Archtops typically feature f-holes rather than a round sound hole, a floating bridge, and a tailpiece. They are strongly associated with jazz, where their loud, percussive, midrange-focused tone helped rhythm guitarists cut through big bands before amplification.

History

The design was pioneered around 1900 by Orville Gibson, who applied violin construction principles — a carved, arched top and back — to mandolins and guitars. Gibson's Lloyd Loar-designed L-5 of 1923 established the modern archtop form with f-holes and a 16-inch body. Rival makers such as Epiphone, Gretsch, Stromberg and the luthier John D'Angelico produced increasingly large instruments — up to 18 or 19 inches across — for orchestral rhythm playing.

Electric archtops

From the 1930s, pickups were added; Gibson's ES-150 (1936) was among the first commercially successful electric guitars. Many archtops have the pickup mounted to the pickguard or neck so the carved top can vibrate freely. Laminated-top models such as the Gibson ES-175 became workhorses of postwar jazz. Semi-hollow designs like the ES-335 combine an arched laminated top with a solid centre block, reducing feedback while retaining some archtop character.
