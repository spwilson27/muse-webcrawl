Eyewitness memory

Eyewitness identification is a major application — and concern — of eyewitness memory research

Eyewitness memory is a person's episodic recollection of an event they personally observed, most often a crime or accident. It is of central importance to the legal system, where eyewitness testimony can be highly persuasive to juries, yet decades of psychological research — associated especially with Elizabeth Loftus — show that such memories are reconstructive and vulnerable to error and suggestion.

Sources of error

Memory failures can arise at encoding, storage and retrieval. At encoding, stress, brief exposure, poor lighting, divided attention and the presence of a weapon (the "weapon focus" effect) reduce what is registered; witnesses also remember faces of their own racial group more accurately than others (the own-race bias). During storage, post-event information can distort the original memory — the misinformation effect: in classic experiments, the wording of a question ("smashed" versus "hit") changed witnesses' speed estimates and even created memories of broken glass that was never present. At retrieval, suggestive interviewing and biased line-up procedures can produce confident false identifications.

Legal significance

Mistaken eyewitness identification has been the leading contributing factor in convictions later overturned by DNA evidence, featuring in roughly two-thirds of the exonerations documented by the Innocence Project. In response, psychologists have proposed reforms including double-blind line-up administration, unbiased instructions, sequential presentation of suspects and recording witnesses' confidence at first identification. Research distinguishes estimator variables, which are beyond the justice system's control, from system variables, which procedures can improve.
