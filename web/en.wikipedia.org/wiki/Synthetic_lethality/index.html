Synthetic lethality

[Image placeholder: synthetic lethality]

Synthetic lethality occurs when a combination of mutations in two or more genes leads to cell death, whereas a mutation in only one of these genes does not affect viability. The concept arose from classical genetics in model organisms such as Drosophila and yeast, where systematic crosses and screens revealed pairs of genes whose simultaneous loss is lethal.

Mechanism

Synthetic lethal partners often act in parallel or compensating pathways — for example, in DNA repair — so that a cell can survive the loss of either pathway but not both. Defects in one gene can therefore create a specific dependency on another, exploitable as a therapeutic target.

Cancer therapy

The best-known clinical application is the use of PARP inhibitors in cancers with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations. BRCA-deficient tumour cells, unable to perform homologous recombination repair, are exquisitely sensitive to blockade of PARP-mediated single-strand break repair, while normal cells with intact BRCA genes tolerate the drug. Drugs such as olaparib have been approved for ovarian, breast, prostate, and pancreatic cancers carrying these mutations. Genome-wide CRISPR and RNAi screens are used to discover further synthetic lethal interactions.
