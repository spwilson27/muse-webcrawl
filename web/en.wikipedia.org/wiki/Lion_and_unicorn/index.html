Lion and unicorn

The lion and the unicorn are symbolic animals of the United Kingdom. They are, properly speaking, heraldic supporters of the full Royal coat of arms of the United Kingdom. The lion stands for England and the unicorn for Scotland. The combination therefore dates back to the 1603 accession of James I of England, who was already James VI of Scotland.

History

By extension, the animal pair came to represent the union of the two kingdoms. In the version of the arms used in Scotland, the unicorn takes the position of honour on the dexter side and is crowned, while within Scotland the unicorn is also chained — as in the general version — since in mythology the unicorn was a dangerous beast that could only be tamed by a virgin, and the chain signifies that Scottish kings had tamed it.

Nursery rhyme

The rivalry between the two beasts is celebrated in the traditional nursery rhyme:

The lion and the unicorn were fighting for the crown;
The lion beat the unicorn all around the town.
Some gave them white bread, and some gave them brown;
Some gave them plum cake and drummed them out of town.

Lewis Carroll used the pair in Through the Looking-Glass (1871), where the Lion and the Unicorn fight over the crown and are served plum cake by Alice, the White King and Haigha.

Symbolic uses

The crowned lion alone serves as the crest of England, while the unicorn — crowned and chained, holding the banner of St Andrew — is a national symbol of Scotland in its own right. The two supporters appear on British passports, coins and government buildings throughout the Commonwealth realms.
