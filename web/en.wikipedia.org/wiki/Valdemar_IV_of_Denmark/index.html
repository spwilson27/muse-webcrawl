Valdemar IV (c. 1320 – 24 October 1375), known as Valdemar Atterdag ("Another Day", i.e. "day again" or "resurgence"), was King of Denmark from 1340 to 1375. He is remembered for reuniting and restoring the Danish kingdom after a period of fragmentation and mortgaging to German counts, and for his wars with the Hanseatic League.

Restoration of the kingdom

Valdemar was the youngest son of Christopher II. When he ascended the throne in 1340, most of Denmark was pawned to the Holstein counts Gerard III and John III. Over two decades, through taxation (including the sale of Danish Estonia to the Teutonic Order in 1346), warfare and negotiation, Valdemar redeemed the mortgaged provinces one by one: Zealand and parts of Jutland first, then Funen and the rest of Jutland, and finally Scania and the other eastern provinces, recovered from Sweden by 1360.

War with the Hansa

In 1361 Valdemar conquered the island of Gotland, defeating its peasant militia outside Visby — a victory commemorated by the mass graves later excavated at Korsbetningen. His expansion into Baltic trade provoked war with the Hanseatic League and its allies. After initial successes, Denmark was defeated in the second war (1367–1370); the Treaty of Stralsund (1370) granted the Hansa extensive privileges and a veto over the Danish succession for a period.

Legacy

Valdemar died at Gurre Castle in 1375. He left no surviving sons; after a brief reign by his grandson Olaf, his daughter Margaret I became regent and went on to found the Kalmar Union. Valdemar's reign is generally seen as the revival of the medieval Danish monarchy after the disastrous early 14th century, achieved at the price of heavy taxation that earned him a mixed reputation in folklore.
