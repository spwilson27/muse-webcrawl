Fadak

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fadak (Arabic: فدك) was a village with fertile land and date-palm gardens in the Hejaz region of Arabia, roughly 140 kilometres north of Medina (in present-day Saudi Arabia). In early Islam, Fadak became the subject of a famous and consequential dispute over whether it was the personal property of the Prophet Muhammad — and thus inheritable by his daughter Fatima — or charitable property belonging to the Muslim community.

History

Fadak was inhabited by Jewish tribes, notably associated with the Banu Nadir's orbit, and after the Muslim conquest of Khaybar in 628 CE its inhabitants negotiated a settlement surrendering half of their land and produce. In Islamic tradition the revenues of Fadak were classified as fay — property obtained without battle — and were administered by Muhammad personally, supporting his household, travellers, and the poor.

The dispute

After Muhammad's death in 632, Fatima claimed Fadak as her inheritance (or as a gift made to her during his lifetime). The first caliph, Abu Bakr, refused, citing the saying attributed to Muhammad that prophets do not bequeath property and that what they leave is charity (sadaqa). Fatima's protest — including the sermon known as the Khutba Fadakiyya in Shia sources — and her estrangement from Abu Bakr until her death months later made Fadak an enduring symbol in Sunni–Shia disagreement over the succession and the rights of the Prophet's family.

Later history

Subsequent rulers repeatedly changed Fadak's status: the caliph Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz restored it to Fatima's descendants, later Abbasid rulers confiscated it again, and al-Ma'mun formally returned it in 822 CE before his successors reversed the decision. Its revenue, once substantial for a village of its size, declined with time.
