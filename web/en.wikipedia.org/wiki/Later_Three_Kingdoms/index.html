The Later Three Kingdoms (Korean: 후삼국, Husamguk) was a period of Korean history, lasting roughly from 892 to 936 CE, during which the unified kingdom of Silla fragmented and rival states — Later Baekje and Later Goguryeo (later renamed Taebong, then Majin) — contested control of the Korean Peninsula. The period ended when Wang Geon, founder of Goryeo, reunified the peninsula.

Background

By the late ninth century, Silla was weakened by court factionalism, heavy taxation, and peasant revolts. Regional strongmen (hojok) built fortified bases and effectively independent armies. In 892 the rebel leader Gyeon Hwon proclaimed Later Baekje in the southwest, claiming descent from the old Baekje kingdom. In 901 the Buddhist monk-turned-general Gung Ye proclaimed Later Goguryeo in the north, later moving his capital to Cheorwon and renaming the state Taebong.

Course of the wars

Gung Ye's increasingly erratic and tyrannical rule led his generals to depose him in 918 and install Wang Geon, who renamed the state Goryeo. Wang Geon pursued a policy of conciliatory alliances with local strongmen while fighting Later Baekje for the strategically vital Naju region and the central peninsula. In 935 the last Silla king, Gyeongsun, surrendered his kingdom to Goryeo peacefully and was given high office. The following year, after internal strife split Later Baekje — Gyeon Hwon himself defected to Goryeo — Wang Geon's forces defeated the Later Baekje army at the Battle of Gomso (Ilicheon) in 936, completing reunification.

Significance

The Later Three Kingdoms period is the last era of division on the peninsula before the long-lived Goryeo dynasty. Its history is recorded principally in the Samguk sagi and Samguk yusa, and it remains a popular setting for Korean historical drama.
