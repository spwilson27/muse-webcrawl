Myrrhbearers

The Myrrhbearers (Greek: Μυροφόροι, Myrophoroi; Latin: Unctrices) are the women mentioned in the canonical gospels who brought spices and aromatic myrrh to the tomb of Jesus to anoint his body, and who became the first witnesses of the empty tomb and of the Resurrection. In Eastern Orthodox tradition they are commemorated on the Sunday of the Myrrh-bearing Women, the third Sunday of Pascha (the second Sunday after Easter).

Accounts in the Gospels

According to the Gospel of Mark, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome came to the tomb at dawn on the first day of the week, found the stone rolled away, and were told by a young man dressed in white that Jesus had risen. Matthew adds an angel who descends amid an earthquake and addresses the women; Luke names Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, "and the other women", and describes two men in dazzling clothes; John focuses on Mary Magdalene, who encounters the risen Christ in the garden and mistakes him for the gardener.

Identity

The lists of the women differ among the gospels. Traditionally the Myrrhbearers are counted as seven or eight: Mary Magdalene; Mary, the mother of James and Joses (often identified with the Theotokos in some traditions, though more commonly with another Mary); Salome; Joanna, wife of Chuza; Susanna; Martha and Mary of Bethany; and others. Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus, who prepared Christ's body for burial, are also associated with the feast in Orthodox practice.

Liturgical commemoration

The Sunday of the Myrrh-bearing Women celebrates the women's courage: while the apostles hid in fear, the women came to the tomb and received the first announcement of the Resurrection, making Mary Magdalene "apostle to the apostles" in Western tradition. The feast is observed in the Eastern Orthodox, Byzantine Catholic, and Oriental Orthodox churches, and analogous devotion to the "three Marys" exists in Western Christianity.

In art

The subject of the women at the sepulchre — often depicted as two or three figures carrying alabaster jars of ointment and greeted by an angel seated on the tomb — is one of the earliest and most common images of the Resurrection in Christian art, appearing in Roman catacombs, Carolingian ivories, and medieval panel painting.
