Quill Corp. v. North Dakota

Quill Corp. v. North Dakota

Court: U.S. Supreme Court
Decided: 26 May 1992
Citation: 504 U.S. 298

Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, 504 U.S. 298 (1992), was a United States Supreme Court decision concerning the power of states to impose use-tax collection duties on out-of-state mail-order sellers. The Court held that a business must have a physical presence in a state before the state may require it to collect sales or use tax, reaffirming the rule of National Bellas Hess v. Illinois (1967) on Commerce Clause grounds.

Background

North Dakota sought to require Quill Corporation, an office-supply company incorporated in Delaware with no offices or employees in the state, to collect use tax on sales to North Dakota customers solicited by catalogue and mail. The state argued that economic presence sufficed.

Decision and aftermath

Justice Stevens's opinion distinguished due process (satisfied by Quill's purposeful direction of business at the state) from the dormant Commerce Clause, under which the physical-presence rule was retained for reasons of stare decisis and because Congress could legislate a different standard. The rule governed remote sales for a quarter-century until it was overruled in South Dakota v. Wayfair (2018), which permitted states to tax remote sellers based on economic nexus.
