Burns supper

A Burns supper is a celebration of the life and poetry of the Scottish poet Robert Burns (25 January 1759 – 21 July 1796), the author of many Scots-language poems. The suppers are normally held on or near the poet's birthday, 25 January, known as Burns Night (Scots: Burns Nicht), although they may in principle be held at any time of the year. Burns suppers are held by Scots and Burns clubs around the world, ranging from formal dinners to informal gatherings, and combine a traditional meal — centred on haggis — with recitations, speeches, song, and whisky.

History

The first supper was held in memoriam by Burns's friends in Alloway, Ayrshire, on 21 July 1801, the fifth anniversary of his death; the first Burns club was founded in Greenock in 1801, and the date soon shifted to his birthday. The format, including the Address to a Haggis, was established during the nineteenth century as Burns's cult spread through Scotland and the Scottish diaspora.

Order of the evening

A traditional formal supper follows a set running order. Guests are piped in and welcomed by the chair; the Selkirk Grace is said ("Some hae meat and canna eat…"). The haggis is piped in, carried by the cook, and the host recites Burns's Address to a Haggis, cutting it open with a dirk at the line "An' cut you up wi' ready slicht". After a whisky toast, the meal is served — traditionally haggis with neeps and tatties (turnips/swede and potatoes) — followed by other courses.

The speeches after dinner include the Immortal Memory, a serious tribute to Burns's life and work; the Toast to the Lassies, a humorous address to the women present; and the Lassies' reply, the Toast to the Laddies. Readings and songs — such as "Tam o' Shanter", "A Red, Red Rose", and "A Man's a Man for A' That" — fill the evening, which ends with the company joining hands to sing "Auld Lang Syne".
