Coriolanus (play)

Author: William Shakespeare

Date written: c. 1605–1608

First published: First Folio, 1623

Genre: Tragedy (Roman play)

Source: Plutarch's Life of Coriolanus (via North's translation)

Coriolanus (pronounced /ˌkɒriəˈleɪnəs/) is a tragedy by William Shakespeare, believed to have been written between 1605 and 1608. It is based on the life of the legendary Roman leader Gaius Marcius Coriolanus and draws chiefly on Thomas North's translation of Plutarch's Parallel Lives. It is generally considered one of Shakespeare's last tragedies, and the last of his "Roman plays" alongside Julius Caesar and Antony and Cleopatra.

Plot

Rome is in turmoil: plebeians, hungry after a grain shortage, riot against the patricians, and the tribunes of the people are created to represent them. The proud patrician soldier Caius Marcius wins glory in the war against the Volscians, single-handedly entering the city of Corioli and earning the agnomen Coriolanus. Pressed by his formidable mother Volumnia, he stands for consul, but his open contempt for the common people — whipped up by the tribunes Sicinius and Brutus — leads to his banishment. In exile he joins his former enemy, the Volscian general Tullus Aufidius, and marches on Rome. Only the embassy of his mother, wife Virgilia, and son persuades him to spare the city; returning to the Volscians, he is killed by Aufidius's conspirators.

Themes and criticism

The play is Shakespeare's most sustained study of class conflict and the politics of the body politic — memorably introduced by Menenius's fable of the belly and the members. Coriolanus himself, whose integrity and pride make compromise impossible, has been read both as an aristocratic hero and as a near-fascist strongman; the play's politics have made it combustible in performance — a 1930s Paris production inflamed political tensions, Nazi-era Germany admired it, and Bertolt Brecht adapted it as an argument about leadership cults. T. S. Eliot controversially judged Coriolanus Shakespeare's "most assured artistic success", ranking it above Hamlet. Notable modern productions and adaptations include Ralph Fiennes's 2011 film, which relocated the action to a Balkan-style war zone, and the Donmar Warehouse production of 2013 with Tom Hiddleston.

Characters
