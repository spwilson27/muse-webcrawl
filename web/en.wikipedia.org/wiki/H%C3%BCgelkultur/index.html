Hügelkultur

Hügelkultur (German pronunciation: [ˈhyːɡl̩kʊlˌtuːɐ̯], literally "mound culture") is a horticultural technique in which a raised bed is built from layers of decaying wood debris and other compostable biomass. The mound mimics the nutrient cycling of a forest floor: as the buried wood decomposes, it releases nutrients, retains moisture like a sponge, and slightly warms the bed, extending the growing season.

TypeGardening technique
OriginCentral/Eastern European tradition; popularised by Sepp Holzer
Key materialsLogs, branches, leaves, compost, topsoil

Construction

A hügelkultur bed is typically built by piling logs and woody debris — preferably partially rotted — into a mound, filling gaps with branches, leaves, grass clippings and manure, and capping the whole with compost and topsoil. Beds are often built about one metre high or more; they settle over several years as the wood decays. The Austrian farmer Sepp Holzer is credited with popularising the modern form of the technique within permaculture.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages claimed for the method include reduced irrigation needs, improved soil aeration and fertility, carbon sequestration in buried wood, and productive use of waste timber. Drawbacks include the large initial labour and material requirement, a temporary nitrogen drawdown while fresh wood decomposes, attraction of burrowing animals, and the unsuitability of certain woods — such as black walnut or fresh cedar — whose compounds can inhibit plant growth.
