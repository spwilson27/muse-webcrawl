Exact solutions in general relativity

In general relativity, an exact solution is a solution of the Einstein field equations whose derivation does not invoke simplifying approximations regarding the physical nature of the matter or fields present, beyond whatever physical assumptions were made in formulating the model. Because the Einstein equations are a system of nonlinear partial differential equations for the spacetime metric, exact solutions are comparatively rare and usually possess a high degree of symmetry.

Background

The Einstein field equations relate the curvature of spacetime, expressed through the Einstein tensor, to the distribution of energy and momentum described by the stress–energy tensor. Finding a metric that solves them exactly, together with a physically reasonable matter model, is called finding an exact solution. Such solutions serve as theoretical laboratories: they model black holes, gravitational waves, and cosmological spacetimes, and they provide benchmarks against which numerical relativity codes are tested.

Notable solutions

  • Schwarzschild metric (1916) — the first exact solution, describing the spherically symmetric vacuum field around a non-rotating mass; it predicts black holes and the bending of light.
  • Reissner–Nordström metric — the charged, non-rotating generalization of Schwarzschild.
  • Kerr metric (1963) — the rotating vacuum solution, describing spacetime around a spinning black hole, including frame dragging and the ergosphere.
  • Kerr–Newman metric — rotating and charged.
  • Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric — homogeneous, isotropic cosmology underlying the standard model of the expanding universe.
  • Gödel metric — a rotating cosmological solution containing closed timelike curves.
  • pp-wave and plane wave spacetimes — exact models of gravitational radiation.
  • Interior Schwarzschild and Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff models — exact (or semi-analytic) stellar interiors.

Classification

Exact solutions are commonly classified by their symmetry group (spherical, axial, stationary, static, homogeneous), by the algebraic type of the Weyl tensor (the Petrov classification), and by the physical type of matter present — vacuum, electrovacuum, perfect fluid, dust, null dust, or scalar field solutions.

Difficulties

Even when a metric is written down in closed form, interpreting it physically can be hard: coordinates may obscure the geometry, solutions may contain singularities or unphysical regions, and a formally exact solution may not correspond to any realizable astrophysical object. Accordingly, much research concerns the physical interpretation, stability, and matching of exact solutions to realistic sources.
