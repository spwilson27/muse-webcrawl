American Jews or Jewish Americans are American citizens who are Jewish, whether by religion, ethnic, or cultural identity. The American Jewish community consists primarily of Ashkenazi Jews who emigrated from Central and Eastern Europe, and their U.S.-born descendants, accounting for about 90–95% of the American Jewish population as of 2021. Many Sephardic and Mizrahi Jews have also immigrated.

History

Jewish settlement in what became the United States began in 1654, when 23 Sephardic refugees arrived in New Amsterdam (present-day New York City) from Recife, Brazil. Small communities formed in colonial port cities. A much larger migration came with German-speaking Jews in the mid-19th century, followed by the mass immigration of roughly two million Eastern European Jews between 1881 and 1924, fleeing pogroms and poverty; many settled in New York's Lower East Side and worked in the garment industry. Immigration was sharply curtailed by the Immigration Act of 1924. After the Holocaust, survivors and refugees arrived, and later waves included Jews from the Soviet Union, Israel, Iran, and South Africa.

Demographics

The United States has the largest or second-largest Jewish population in the world, rivaled only by Israel. Pew Research Center studies have estimated the adult Jewish population at about 5.8 million, with a total population (including children and those of partial ancestry) often estimated around 7.5 million, roughly 2% of the national population. Jewish Americans are concentrated in metropolitan areas, especially the New York region, which contains the largest urban Jewish population outside Israel.

Religion and culture

American Judaism developed denominations largely unique to the country, including Reform, Conservative, and Reconstructionist movements alongside Orthodoxy. A large share of American Jews are secular or culturally Jewish. American Jews have made notable contributions to American culture, science, business, politics, and entertainment.
