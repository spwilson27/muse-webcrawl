Sandhoff disease is a lysosomal storage disorder caused by deficiency of the enzyme hexosaminidase.

Sandhoff disease is a rare inherited lysosomal storage disorder characterised by the progressive destruction of nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord. It is caused by mutations in the HEXB gene, which encodes the beta subunit of the enzymes beta-hexosaminidase A and beta-hexosaminidase B. Without functional hexosaminidase, a fatty substance called GM2 ganglioside accumulates abnormally in lysosomes, particularly in neurons, leading to progressive neurological deterioration.

The disease is named after the German biochemist Konrad Sandhoff, who described it in 1968. Sandhoff disease is closely related to Tay–Sachs disease, in which the alpha subunit of hexosaminidase A (encoded by HEXA) is defective instead; the two conditions are clinically almost indistinguishable in their infantile forms and are grouped together as GM2 gangliosidoses.

Signs and symptoms

The classic infantile form presents between three and six months of age. Early signs include an exaggerated startle response to noises, loss of motor skills, muscle weakness and decreased responsiveness. As the disease progresses, affected infants develop seizures, vision and hearing loss, intellectual disability, and paralysis. A characteristic finding is a cherry-red spot on the retina of the eye. Death usually occurs by two to five years of age.

Juvenile and adult-onset forms also exist. These progress more slowly and may present with muscle weakness, loss of coordination (ataxia), speech problems, and psychiatric symptoms. The severity and course depend on the specific mutations and how much residual enzyme activity remains.

Genetics

Sandhoff disease is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern: a child must inherit a mutated copy of HEXB (located on chromosome 5) from each parent to be affected. Carriers, with one normal and one mutated copy, are typically healthy. Unlike Tay–Sachs disease, which is particularly common in Ashkenazi Jewish populations, Sandhoff disease occurs in many populations, with somewhat higher frequencies reported in some groups, including people of Northern Saskatchewan Métis, Lebanese, and Argentine Creole ancestry.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by measuring hexosaminidase A and B enzyme activity in blood or skin cells — in Sandhoff disease both enzymes are deficient — and confirmed by genetic testing. There is currently no cure and no treatment that alters the underlying course of the disease; care is supportive, aimed at managing symptoms, nutrition, and comfort. Research approaches under investigation include enzyme replacement, substrate reduction therapy, gene therapy, and pharmacological chaperones.
