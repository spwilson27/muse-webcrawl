The 1989 Tiananmen Square protests, commonly known in Chinese as the June Fourth Incident, were student-led demonstrations in Beijing in the spring of 1989 calling for political and economic reform, freedom of the press, and action against corruption. After weeks of occupation of Tiananmen Square and the declaration of martial law, the Chinese government ordered the People's Liberation Army to clear the city by force on the night of June 3–4, 1989, killing large numbers of protesters and bystanders.

Background and protests

The death on April 15, 1989, of Hu Yaobang, a reformist former general secretary who had been purged in 1987, triggered student mourning that grew into mass demonstrations. At their peak the protests drew perhaps a million people in Beijing and spread to hundreds of cities. Students occupied Tiananmen Square, erected the ten-metre "Goddess of Democracy" statue facing the portrait of Mao Zedong, and launched a hunger strike timed to the visit of Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, whose welcoming ceremony had to be moved. General Secretary Zhao Ziyang, sympathetic to dialogue, was purged and placed under house arrest for the rest of his life.

The crackdown

Martial law was declared on May 20. After initial troop advances were blocked by citizens, on the night of June 3–4 units of the PLA fought their way into central Beijing, firing on crowds along Chang'an Avenue and other western approaches. The number killed remains unknown and politically sensitive: estimates range from several hundred to several thousand. On June 5, a lone protester—the "Tank Man"—briefly halted a column of tanks, producing one of the 20th century's most famous photographs.

Aftermath

The crackdown brought international sanctions and condemnation, the purge of reformists, and the elevation of Jiang Zemin as general secretary. Discussion of the events remains heavily censored in mainland China, while annual vigils were long held in Hong Kong until their suppression in 2020.
