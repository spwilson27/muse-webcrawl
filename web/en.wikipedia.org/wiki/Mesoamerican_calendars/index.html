Mesoamerican calendars

Mesoamerican calendars are the calendrical systems devised and used by the pre-Columbian cultures of Mesoamerica. Besides keeping time, Mesoamerican calendars were also used in religious observances and social rituals, such as divination. The calendars combined several interlocking cycles, most importantly a 260-day sacred almanac and a 365-day solar year, which together repeated every 52 years in the so-called Calendar Round.

A carved calendar stone of the Aztec tradition

The 260-day almanac

The 260-day cycle, called the tzolk'in by the Maya and the tonalpohualli by the Aztecs, combined 20 named days with the numbers 1 through 13, producing 260 distinct day designations. It was primarily a divinatory calendar: the fate and character of a person were thought to depend on their birth date, and priests consulted almanacs (such as the Maya screenfold codices) to determine auspicious days for rituals, planting, marriage, and warfare. Its origin is debated; proposed explanations include the human gestation period, the interval between zenith passages of the sun at latitudes near 15°N, and the synodic period of Venus subdivisions.

The 365-day year

The solar calendar, the Maya haab' and Aztec xiuhpohualli, consisted of 18 months of 20 days each plus a final period of 5 days (the Maya wayeb, considered unlucky), totalling 365 days. It had no leap-day correction, so it drifted against the seasons over the centuries.

The Calendar Round and Long Count

Because 260 and 365 share a least common multiple of 18,980 days, the combination of a tzolk'in date and a haab' date repeated every 52 years — the Calendar Round, marked by the Aztecs with the New Fire ceremony. To record longer spans, the Maya and earlier cultures such as the Olmec and Zapotec used the Long Count, a modified base-20 count of days elapsed since a mythical creation date in 3114 BCE, expressed in units from the k'in (day) up to the b'ak'tun of 144,000 days. The completion of the 13th b'ak'tun on 21 December 2012 attracted wide popular attention.
