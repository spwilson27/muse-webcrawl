Parish
From: Greek paroikia, "sojourning; neighbourhood"
Types: Ecclesiastical parish; civil parish
Used by: Catholic Church, Church of England, Eastern Orthodoxy, Lutheran churches; civil government in England, Louisiana (US), and various countries

A parish is a territorial entity in many Christian denominations, constituting a division within a diocese. A parish is under the pastoral care and clerical jurisdiction of a parish priest or pastor, who might be assisted by one or more curates, and who operates from a parish church. Historically, a parish often covered the same geographical area as a manor.

Ecclesiastical usage

In the Catholic Church, a parish (Latin: paroecia) is a stable community of the faithful within a particular church (diocese), whose pastoral care is entrusted to a parish priest under the authority of the diocesan bishop. In the Church of England, the parish is the basic unit of local ministry: every part of England belongs to an ecclesiastical parish, each with its own parish church. Eastern Orthodox, Lutheran, Methodist and other churches also use parishes, though their structures vary.

Civil usage

Because ecclesiastical parishes long provided the framework of local administration, the term passed into civil government. In England, the civil parish is the smallest unit of local government, overseen by a parish council. In the US state of Louisiana, parishes are the equivalent of counties elsewhere in the United States — a legacy of French and Spanish colonial administration. Parishes also serve as civil subdivisions in countries including Jamaica, Barbados, Andorra, Ecuador, and parts of Canada and Australia.

History

The parish system developed in early medieval Europe as large minster territories were subdivided into smaller units served by local churches, supported by tithes. By the later Middle Ages the parish had become the fundamental unit of religious and much secular life across Latin Christendom.
