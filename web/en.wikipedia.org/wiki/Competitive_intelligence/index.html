Competitive intelligence

Competitive intelligence (CI) is the systematic and ethical process of gathering, analysing, and disseminating information about competitors, markets, customers, and other external factors that affect an organisation's competitive position, in order to support strategic decision-making. Practitioners distinguish competitive intelligence — based on legal and ethical collection from public and legitimately obtained sources — from industrial espionage, which involves illegal methods.

The intelligence cycle

CI work typically follows a cycle adapted from government intelligence practice:

  1. Planning and direction — identifying the decisions to be supported and the key intelligence topics required by decision-makers.
  2. Collection — gathering data from sources such as published financial reports, patents, regulatory filings, trade press, conferences, job postings, customer and supplier interviews, and web and social-media monitoring.
  3. Analysis — converting raw information into assessments, using tools such as SWOT analysis, Porter's five forces, competitor profiling, win/loss analysis, scenario planning, and war gaming.
  4. Dissemination — delivering findings in usable formats (briefings, newsletters, alerts, dashboards) to the people who need them.
  5. Feedback — evaluating usefulness and refining requirements.

Types and uses

Competitive intelligence supports strategic planning (market entry, mergers and acquisitions, long-range positioning), tactical decisions (pricing, product launches, sales enablement through "battle cards"), and early warning of competitor moves, technological disruption, and regulatory change. Related disciplines include market intelligence, customer intelligence, and technology intelligence; all draw on open-source intelligence (OSINT) methods.

Ethics and profession

The professional body for the field, Strategic and Competitive Intelligence Professionals (SCIP, founded in 1986), maintains a code of ethics requiring members to comply with all applicable laws, disclose their identity and affiliation when collecting information, and respect requests for confidentiality. Most large corporations maintain formal CI functions or purchase CI services from specialist firms.
