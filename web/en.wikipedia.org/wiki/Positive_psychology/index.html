Positive psychology

Positive psychology is the scientific study of what makes life worth living — the conditions and processes that contribute to the flourishing or optimal functioning of people, groups and institutions. The field was formally launched in 1998 by psychologist Martin Seligman during his presidency of the American Psychological Association, though its roots lie in humanistic psychology and in earlier work on happiness, character strengths and optimal experience.

Overview

Seligman argued that psychology after the Second World War had concentrated almost entirely on mental illness and pathology, neglecting the study of what makes people thrive. Positive psychology studies three areas: positive subjective experiences (happiness, pleasure, flow), positive individual traits (character strengths and virtues, talents, wisdom), and positive institutions (families, schools and societies that foster flourishing).

Major theories

In his 2011 book Flourish, Seligman proposed the PERMA model of well-being: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning and Accomplishment. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's concept of flow — complete absorption in a challenging activity — is a central pillar of the field. Peterson and Seligman's Character Strengths and Virtues (2004) offered a classification of six virtues and 24 character strengths, intended as a counterpart to the DSM's catalogue of disorders. Barbara Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory holds that positive emotions broaden people's thought–action repertoires and build durable personal resources.

Applications and criticism

Positive psychology interventions — gratitude exercises, "three good things", savouring, identifying signature strengths — have been applied in education ("positive education"), the workplace, the military (the US Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness programme) and clinical practice. Critics have charged the field with overstating effect sizes, neglecting cultural context and poverty, and encouraging a "tyranny of the positive attitude"; replication problems in some well-known studies have prompted methodological reforms.
