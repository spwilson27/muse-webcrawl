The Sunshine Policy (Korean: 햇볕정책, Haetbyeot Jeongchaek) was the foreign policy of South Korea towards North Korea from 1998 until 2008. Enunciated by President Kim Dae-jung, it sought to encourage North Korea to open up through engagement, economic cooperation and reconciliation rather than pressure and isolation, in the hope of achieving peaceful coexistence and, ultimately, reunification. The name derives from Aesop's fable of the North Wind and the Sun, in which the sun's warmth persuades a traveller to remove his coat where the wind's force failed.

Principles and achievements

The policy rested on three principles: no tolerance of armed provocation by the North; no attempt to absorb the North by unification by force; and active pursuit of reconciliation and cooperation. Its landmark achievement was the first inter-Korean summit in Pyongyang in June 2000, where Kim Dae-jung met Kim Jong-il and signed the June 15th North–South Joint Declaration — for which Kim Dae-jung received the Nobel Peace Prize. Other fruits of the policy included the Kaesong Industrial Complex, tours to Mount Kumgang, reunions of separated families, and restored road and rail links across the Demilitarized Zone. A second summit followed in 2007 under President Roh Moo-hyun.

Criticism and end

Critics, including South Korean conservatives and the US administration of George W. Bush, argued that engagement transferred resources to Pyongyang without ending its nuclear weapons programme, and revelations that a large cash payment preceded the 2000 summit damaged the policy's legitimacy. After North Korea's nuclear tests and the election of conservative President Lee Myung-bak in 2008, the policy was formally abandoned in favour of a harder line, though elements of engagement revived under later liberal administrations.
