Code of conduct

A code of conduct sets out expected behaviour in an organisation or community

Purpose: Standards of behaviour and ethics

Used by: Companies, professions, conferences, open-source communities

A code of conduct is a set of rules and expectations outlining the norms, responsibilities and proper practices of an individual party or organisation. Codes of conduct are common in business, the professions, education, conferences and online and open-source communities. They typically state both the values of the organisation and specific prohibited behaviours, together with the procedures by which violations are reported and enforced.

Types

Corporate codes address conflicts of interest, gifts and bribery, confidentiality, insider trading, workplace harassment and compliance with law; public companies in the United States are required by stock-exchange rules to adopt codes of ethics for senior officers. Professional codes — such as medical or legal ethics codes — may be enforceable through licensing bodies. Community codes aim to make collaborative environments, notably open-source software projects and conferences, welcoming and free of harassment. A widely adopted template is the Contributor Covenant, first published in 2014 and used by thousands of projects.

Contents and enforcement

A well-formed code usually includes a statement of purpose, a list of expected and unacceptable behaviours, instructions for reporting incidents, and a description of enforcement consequences, which may range from warnings to expulsion or termination of employment. Critics of community codes of conduct argue that vague clauses can be enforced inconsistently or used to settle unrelated disputes; supporters argue that explicit standards protect participants, clarify expectations and make enforcement more predictable and accountable.

Historical notes

Institutional codes of behaviour have ancient antecedents, from the Hippocratic Oath for physicians to military articles of war and monastic rules. The modern corporate code of conduct spread after the business scandals of the 1970s and was reinforced by legislation such as the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (1977) and the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (2002).
