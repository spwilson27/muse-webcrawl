Air service agreement

Air service agreement
TypeBilateral (or multilateral) treaty
SubjectInternational commercial air services
FrameworkChicago Convention (1944)
Key conceptsFreedoms of the air, designated airlines, capacity, routes

An air service agreement (also called an air services agreement, or ATA/ASA) is a bilateral treaty between two countries governing commercial air services between and beyond their territories. Because the 1944 Chicago Convention established that states have sovereignty over their airspace and that scheduled international services require permission, air services developed through a web of thousands of bilateral agreements rather than a single multilateral regime.

Contents

A typical agreement specifies the routes that may be served, the airlines each side may designate, capacity and frequency limits (in restrictive agreements), tariff provisions, and the traffic rights granted — expressed in terms of the "freedoms of the air", from the first freedom (overflight) through fifth-freedom rights (carrying passengers between two foreign countries) and beyond. Agreements also cover safety, security, customs and fair-competition clauses.

Bermuda agreements and liberalisation

The 1946 Bermuda Agreement between the United States and the United Kingdom became the template for post-war bilaterals, balancing route exchanges with capacity principles. From the late 1970s the US promoted "open skies" agreements removing capacity and pricing controls; the European Union went further, creating a single aviation market in which the agreement is effectively multilateral. The 2007 EU–US Open Skies agreement replaced the member states' separate bilaterals with the US.
