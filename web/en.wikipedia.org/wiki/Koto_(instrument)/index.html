The koto (Japanese: 箏) is a Japanese long zither and the national instrument of Japan. It is about 180 cm long, carved from paulownia wood, and has thirteen strings stretched over movable bridges, which the player adjusts to change the tuning. The strings are plucked with picks (tsume) worn on the thumb and first two fingers of the right hand while the left hand presses strings to bend pitches.

History

The koto descends from the Chinese zheng, introduced to Japan by the Nara period and used in the court music gagaku. In the Edo period, the blind musician Yatsuhashi Kengyō (1614–1685) transformed the court instrument into a vehicle for solo and vocal music (sōkyoku), founding the tradition taught today. In the 20th century, Michio Miyagi composed extensively for the koto, invented the 17-string bass koto, and brought the instrument to Western audiences; contemporary players perform in genres from jazz to rock.

Music and technique

Standard tunings such as hira-jōshi are pentatonic. Classic pieces include "Rokudan no Shirabe" (attributed to Yatsuhashi) and "Haru no Umi" ("The Sea in Spring") by Miyagi, a duet for koto and shakuhachi often heard in Japan at New Year. The koto features in school music education in Japan and is combined with shamisen and shakuhachi in the sankyoku ensemble.
