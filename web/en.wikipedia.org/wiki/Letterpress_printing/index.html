Letterpress printing

Letterpress printing is a technique of relief printing in which a raised, inked surface — traditionally movable type or an engraved block — is pressed directly against paper to produce an impression. It was the standard method of printing text from its development in the mid-15th century until the second half of the 20th century, when it was displaced by offset lithography.

History

Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type press of around 1440 spread rapidly across Europe. For roughly five centuries, nearly all books, newspapers, and job printing were produced by letterpress. Improvements included iron hand presses, the steam-driven cylinder press of Friedrich Koenig (1814), and the web-fed rotary presses used for mass-circulation newspapers.

Process

A compositor sets type by hand or machine (Linotype, Monotype) into a chase, locking it into a flat bed or wrapping plates around a cylinder. Rollers apply ink to the raised surface; paper is then pressed against it, either on a platen press (such as the Chandler & Price job press) or in flatbed and rotary configurations. Characteristic of letterpress is a slight debossed impression in the sheet.

Revival

Although commercially obsolete for most work, letterpress experienced a revival from the 1990s onward as an artisanal craft for wedding invitations, business cards, artists' books, and fine press editions, valued precisely for its tactile impression. Photopolymer plates have largely replaced metal type in modern craft shops.
