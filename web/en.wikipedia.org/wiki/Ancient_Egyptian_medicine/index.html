Ancient Egyptian medicine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The medicine of the ancient Egyptians is among the oldest documented medical practices in the world. From the Old Kingdom to the Persian invasion in 525 BC, Egyptian physicians — famed in the ancient world, with Herodotus calling Egypt a land where "every physician is a specialist in one disease" — combined empirical observation, surgery, pharmacology, and religious ritual. Their texts record anatomy, diagnosis, and treatments, some of which anticipated later practice.

The medical papyri

Knowledge of Egyptian medicine comes chiefly from surviving medical papyri:

  • The Edwin Smith Papyrus (c. 1600 BC, copying older material) — a surgical treatise describing 48 traumatic injury cases in a strikingly systematic format of examination, diagnosis ("an ailment I will treat / contend with / not treat"), and treatment, including the earliest known written reference to the brain.
  • The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BC) — the longest medical scroll, with over 700 remedies for diseases of the internal organs, eyes, skin, and women, plus incantations.
  • The Kahun Papyrus (c. 1800 BC) — the oldest known medical text, concerning gynaecology.
  • The Berlin, Hearst, London, and Chester Beatty papyri, covering topics from paediatrics to veterinary care.

Beliefs and practice

Egyptians explained many diseases through the concept of wekhedu, a putrefactive substance thought to circulate from the bowel through channels (metu) that also carried blood, air, and other fluids — hence the widespread use of purges, clysters, and emetics. Alongside such theories stood remarkably practical surgery: splinting fractures, setting dislocated jaws and shoulders, lancing abscesses, and stitching wounds. Remedies drew on honey (an effective antibacterial dressing), willow, myrrh, copper salts, animal products, and minerals; mouldy bread applied to wounds is sometimes cited as a foreshadowing of antibiotics, though its rationale was magical rather than microbial. Contraception recipes (such as a pessary of acacia gum, which ferments to lactic acid) also appear.

Physicians and institutions

Doctors (swnw) were trained in temple scriptoria called "Houses of Life". The earliest named physician is Hesy-Ra (c. 2650 BC), "chief of dentists and physicians"; Imhotep, vizier of Djoser, was deified as a god of medicine. Specialists served the court, the army, and workforces at sites such as the royal necropolis, where records show state-supported sick leave. Herodotus and Homer both praise Egyptian doctors, and Greek medicine from Hippocrates onward absorbed Egyptian influence.
