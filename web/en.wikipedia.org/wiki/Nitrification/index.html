Nitrification

Nitrification is the biological oxidation of ammonia (NH3) or ammonium (NH4+) to nitrite (NO2−) followed by the oxidation of nitrite to nitrate (NO3−). It is a central step of the nitrogen cycle in soils and aquatic environments, carried out by specialised chemolithoautotrophic bacteria and archaea that obtain energy from the oxidation reactions.

Stages

NH3 + O2 → NO2− + 3H+ + 2e− (ammonia oxidation)

NO2− + H2O → NO3− + 2H+ + 2e− (nitrite oxidation)

The first step is performed by ammonia-oxidising bacteria such as Nitrosomonas and by ammonia-oxidising archaea, using the enzyme ammonia monooxygenase. The second step is performed by nitrite-oxidising bacteria such as Nitrobacter and Nitrospira. In 2015, bacteria of the genus Nitrospira were shown to carry out both steps alone — a process called complete ammonia oxidation (comammox).

Environmental significance

Nitrification makes nitrogen available to plants in the form of nitrate, the principal form taken up by most crops, but nitrate is also readily leached from soils and can contaminate groundwater and drive eutrophication. The process produces hydrogen ions, acidifying soil, and is a source of the greenhouse gas nitrous oxide. In wastewater treatment, nitrification is deliberately promoted so that subsequent denitrification can remove nitrogen entirely; in drinking-water systems and aquaria, biological filters use nitrifying bacteria to detoxify ammonia excreted by fish.

History

The biological nature of nitrification was demonstrated by Schlösing and Müntz in 1877, and the responsible organisms were isolated by Sergei Winogradsky in 1890, who thereby discovered chemosynthesis.
