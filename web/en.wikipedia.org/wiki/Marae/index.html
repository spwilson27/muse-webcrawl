Marae

Marae
TypeCommunal and sacred meeting ground
CulturesMāori; wider Polynesian ( cognates: malae, malaʻe, meʻae )
Key structuresWharenui (meeting house), wharekai (dining hall), marae ātea (forecourt)

A marae (in Māori, Cook Islands Māori, Tahitian) or malaʻe (in Tongan and Samoan usage) is a communal and sacred place in Polynesian cultures used for religious and social gatherings. In the modern Māori context of New Zealand (Aotearoa), a marae is a complex of buildings and open ground belonging to a particular iwi (tribe), hapū (sub-tribe), or whānau (family) — the focal point of Māori community life.

Layout and function

The centrepiece is the wharenui (meeting house), its ridgepole and posts carved to represent ancestors, fronted by the marae ātea, the open courtyard where formal welcomes (pōwhiri) and speeches take place. A wharekai (dining hall) and modern amenities complete the complex. Marae host funerals (tangihanga), weddings, birthdays, reunions, tribal meetings, and cultural education; visitors are received according to tikanga (protocol) — a structured ceremony of calls, song, and oratory.

Origins and wider Polynesia

The word descends from Proto-Polynesian *malaqe, "cleared open space." In pre-Christian Polynesia, marae in French Polynesia and the Cook Islands were stone temples of tapu ritual; the grandest, such as Taputapuātea on Ra'iātea, were centres of navigation and worship and are now UNESCO World Heritage sites.

Modern role

From the 20th century, "urban marae" were built in New Zealand cities to serve Māori of many tribes and the wider public — schools, universities, prisons, and even museums now have marae-style spaces. Marae remain central to the Māori language and culture revival (te reo nests and immersion schools frequently sit on marae), and serve as emergency and welfare hubs, notably after the Christchurch earthquakes and during the COVID-19 pandemic.
