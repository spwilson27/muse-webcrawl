Battle of Cartagena de Indias

The Battle of Cartagena de Indias (March–May 1741) was a major amphibious assault by British forces under Admiral Edward Vernon against the Spanish colonial port of Cartagena de Indias (in present-day Colombia), during the War of Jenkins' Ear. One of the largest amphibious operations of the eighteenth century, it ended in a decisive Spanish victory and catastrophic British losses, chiefly from disease.

Vernon's fleet attacking Cartagena, 1741.

Forces

Vernon's armada comprised some 186 ships and roughly 27,000 men, including British regulars and American colonial troops — among them Lawrence Washington, the elder half-brother of George Washington, who later named his estate Mount Vernon after the admiral. The city was defended by about 3,000–4,000 Spanish regulars, militia, sailors and indigenous archers under the viceroy Sebastián de Eslava and the naval commander Blas de Lezo, a veteran who had lost a leg, an eye and the use of an arm in earlier battles.

The battle

The British forced the harbour entrance at Bocachica in March and landed troops to assault the city's landward defences. A night attack on the castle of San Felipe de Barajas on 20 April was repulsed with heavy losses after the attackers' scaling ladders proved too short for the walls. Meanwhile yellow fever, malaria and dysentery devastated the crowded British camps and ships. Vernon abandoned the siege in May and withdrew; British dead are estimated at between 9,000 and 11,500, the great majority from sickness.

Legacy

The victory preserved Spanish control of the Caribbean mainland and is celebrated in Colombia and Spain; Blas de Lezo, who died of wounds and disease shortly afterwards, is honoured with a statue in Cartagena. In Britain, commemorative medals had been struck prematurely in expectation of victory — a famous propaganda embarrassment.
