Goods and services tax

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A goods and services tax (GST) is a type of value-added tax levied on most goods and services sold for domestic consumption. The tax is paid by consumers but remitted to the government by the businesses selling the goods and services, with each business in the supply chain claiming credits for tax paid on its inputs so that the burden ultimately falls on the final consumer.

By country

Many countries operate a tax under this name. Canada introduced a federal GST in 1991 at 7% (later reduced to 5%), replacing a hidden manufacturers' sales tax. Australia introduced a 10% GST in 2000. New Zealand's GST, introduced in 1986 at 10%, was later raised to 15% and is notable for its very broad base with few exemptions. Singapore's GST began in 1994 at 3% and has risen in stages. India's GST, launched on 1 July 2017, was a major reform subsuming a web of central and state indirect taxes into a dual GST administered jointly by the Union and the states, with rates in several slabs.

Comparison with VAT

GST and VAT are conceptually the same invoice-credit consumption tax; the naming difference is largely historical and jurisdictional. In practice, GST systems vary in exemptions (often for basic food, health, and education), registration thresholds for small businesses, and treatment of exports, which are typically zero-rated.
