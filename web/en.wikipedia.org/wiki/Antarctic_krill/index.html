Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba) is a species of krill found in the Antarctic waters of the Southern Ocean. It is a small, swimming crustacean that lives in large schools, called swarms, sometimes reaching densities of 10,000–30,000 individuals per cubic metre. It feeds primarily on phytoplankton and is the keystone species of the Antarctic ecosystem, forming the staple diet of whales, seals, penguins, squid, and fish.

Description and life cycle

Adults grow to about 6 cm and 2 grams and can live for up to six years. Krill are bioluminescent, with photophores that produce light. Females lay several thousand eggs at a time near the surface; larvae develop through a series of stages as they descend and rise in the water column, grazing under sea ice during winter. The total biomass of Antarctic krill has been estimated at several hundred million tonnes, among the largest of any single animal species.

Fishery

Krill are harvested commercially for aquaculture feed, bait, and krill oil rich in omega-3 fatty acids. The fishery is managed by the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR), which sets precautionary catch limits. Climate change and shrinking sea ice threaten krill habitat and the predators that depend on them.
