EBITDA

EBITDA (pronounced variously, including /ɪˈbɪtdɑː/) is an acronym for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. It is a measure of a company's overall financial performance and is used as an alternative to net income in some circumstances.

Calculation

EBITDA can be calculated from an income statement as net income plus interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. Because it excludes financing costs, tax environments, and non-cash charges for wear and obsolescence of assets, it is intended to approximate the cash generated by core operations and to allow comparison between companies with different capital structures or asset bases.

Criticism

EBITDA is not a measure defined by generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP), and regulators restrict how it may be presented. Critics — including Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger — argue it can be misleading because it ignores capital expenditures needed to maintain assets, working capital changes, and the real economic cost of debt and taxes. Variants such as "adjusted EBITDA", which add back further items such as stock-based compensation and restructuring costs, attract additional scepticism. Ratios such as EV/EBITDA are nonetheless standard in valuation and leveraged lending.
