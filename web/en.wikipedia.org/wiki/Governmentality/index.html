Governmentality

Governmentality is a concept developed by the French philosopher Michel Foucault in his lectures at the Collège de France in the late 1970s, most fully in Security, Territory, Population (1977–78) and The Birth of Biopolitics (1978–79). A portmanteau of "government" and "mentality" (or, in Foucault's usage, a term for the "art of government"), governmentality refers to the way governments try to produce citizens best suited to fulfill their policies, and to the organized practices and rationalities through which subjects are governed. It has become a central concept in political science, sociology, anthropology and the interdisciplinary field known as "governmentality studies."

Foucault's formulation

In his 1978 lecture "Governmentality," Foucault defined the term in three ways:

  1. The ensemble of institutions, procedures, analyses, calculations and tactics that allow the exercise of a specific and complex form of power, which has as its target population, as its principal form of knowledge political economy, and as its essential technical means apparatuses of security.
  2. The tendency, over the course of Western history, for this type of power — "government" — to take precedence over sovereignty and discipline.
  3. The process by which the medieval "state of justice" became the modern "administrative state" in the 15th and 16th centuries.

Foucault traced a genealogy of governing from the Christian pastorate (pastoral power over souls) through raison d'État and the early modern "police" state, to liberalism, in which governing is limited by the idea that one can govern "too much," and finally to neoliberalism, in which the market becomes a site of truth and individuals are reimagined as entrepreneurs of themselves.

Key ideas

  • Conduct of conduct: government is the structuring of the possible field of action of others — power exercised over free subjects through guidance rather than mere prohibition.
  • Population: modern governmental power takes the population — its birth rates, health, longevity and wealth — as its object, giving rise to biopolitics.
  • Rationalities and technologies: each regime couples a way of thinking about rule (rationality or "mentality") with practical techniques (censuses, statistics, examinations, insurance, audits).
  • Subjectivation: government works by shaping how individuals understand and govern themselves — "technologies of the self."

Governmentality studies

From the early 1990s, scholars such as Nikolas Rose, Pat O'Malley, Mitchell Dean and Peter Miller developed an Anglo-Foucauldian "governmentality studies" literature, applying the concept to welfare reform, risk and insurance, crime control, public health, education, audit culture and neoliberal restructuring. Dean's Governmentality: Power and Rule in Modern Society (1999; 2nd ed. 2010) is a standard introduction. Critics have argued that the literature sometimes overstates coherence in governmental programs, underplays resistance and material interests, or uses Foucault's concept more loosely than his own genealogical method warrants.
