Deed of trust (real estate)

Deed of trust
PartiesTrustor (borrower), beneficiary (lender), trustee
PurposeSecuring a loan with real property

AlternativeMortgage

In real estate in the United States, a deed of trust or trust deed is a legal instrument used instead of a mortgage in many states. It involves three parties: the borrower (trustor), who conveys legal title to the property to a trustee, and the lender (beneficiary), whose loan the arrangement secures. The borrower retains possession and equitable title; if the loan is repaid, title is reconveyed, and if the borrower defaults, the trustee may sell the property.

How it works

The trustor signs a promissory note for the loan and a deed of trust that is recorded in the county land records. The trustee — often a title company or an attorney — holds bare legal title in trust. On full repayment, the beneficiary executes a deed of reconveyance, clearing the lien. The deed of trust typically contains a power of sale clause allowing non-judicial foreclosure.

Foreclosure

Because of the power of sale, foreclosure under a deed of trust can usually proceed without court action: after a notice of default and a statutory waiting period, the trustee advertises and conducts a trustee's sale. This is generally faster and cheaper for lenders than judicial foreclosure of a mortgage, a principal reason the instrument is favoured in states such as California.

Comparison with mortgages

  • A mortgage involves two parties (mortgagor and mortgagee); a deed of trust adds the trustee.
  • Mortgages historically required judicial foreclosure in many states; deeds of trust usually permit non-judicial sale.
  • Some states use only mortgages, some only deeds of trust, and some permit both.
