Parkinson's law

Parkinson's law is the adage that "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion". It was formulated by the British historian and author C. Northcote Parkinson in a humorous essay published in The Economist in 1955 and developed in his 1958 book Parkinson's Law: The Pursuit of Progress.

The essay

Parkinson opened the essay with the law as if it were a scientific discovery, supporting it with statistics on the British Admiralty: even as the number of ships in commission fell between 1914 and 1928, the number of Admiralty officials grew steadily. He proposed that officials multiply subordinates and make work for one another, irrespective of the amount of real work to be done.

Corollaries and influence

Parkinson proposed a mathematical formula for bureaucratic growth and derived corollaries, including the law of triviality — that organizations give disproportionate weight to trivial issues, later popularized as "bikeshedding". The law has been widely applied, and adapted, in computing (data expands to fill storage), project management, and economics, and remains a touchstone in discussions of bureaucracy.
