Arak (Arabic: عرق, "sweat" or "juice") is a distilled Levantine spirit of the anise family of drinks. It is traditionally made from grapes distilled in copper stills and flavoured with aniseed on a second or third distillation, then aged in clay jars. The name is also applied historically to distilled spirits more generally in the Middle East and Asia (see arrack, a different drink), but in the modern Levant it denotes this clear, anise-flavoured spirit.

Production

Ripe grapes are fermented into wine, which is distilled to a strong spirit; aniseed is added and the spirit is distilled again (often a third time) to refine the flavour. Quality arak is typically 50% alcohol or more before dilution, contains no added sugar, and is aged for a year or more. Lebanon's Bekaa Valley is a noted centre of production, and arak is claimed as a national drink in Lebanon, Syria, Jordan and Palestine.

Consumption

Arak is served with mezze and grilled meats, diluted with water (usually one part arak to one or two parts water) and poured over ice. Adding water turns it milky white — the "louche", locally called the "milk of lions" (halib al-sibah) — as the anise oils come out of solution, the same effect seen with ouzo and absinthe. In social drinking it accompanies long meals, and homemade or illicit arak appears widely alongside commercial brands.
