Breaking the fourth wall

Breaking the fourth wall
TypeNarrative technique / metafiction
MediaTheatre, film, television, literature, video games
Related conceptsAside, direct address, metafiction, Verfremdungseffekt

Breaking the fourth wall is a performing convention in which a fictional character acknowledges the audience's presence or the fictionality of the work itself. The "fourth wall" is the imagined wall at the front of a proscenium stage, through which the audience views the action; the term was popularised from the writings of Denis Diderot. While the convention of the invisible wall dates from the 19th-century realist theatre, direct address to the audience is much older — in the prologues and asides of Greek drama, Shakespeare, and pantomime.

Uses and effects

The device may be used for comedy, to create intimacy or complicity between character and audience, to deliver exposition, or to unsettle the audience and draw attention to the work's artifice — an effect systematised by Bertolt Brecht as the Verfremdungseffekt (alienation effect), intended to prevent passive identification and encourage critical judgement. In film and television, characters who speak to camera appear in works ranging from the Marx Brothers and Ferris Bueller's Day Off to the series House of Cards and Fleabag, and the superhero film Deadpool. In literature the equivalent techniques include unreliable and self-conscious narration in works like Tristram Shandy.

In other media

Video games frequently break the fourth wall through characters addressing the player, save-file manipulation in games such as Undertale and Metal Gear Solid, or controllers and hardware being referenced within the fiction. In comics, characters such as Deadpool are written as aware of being in a comic. Occasional, unscripted-feeling breaks also occur in ostensibly realist works, where they can produce a deliberately jarring rupture of the suspension of disbelief.
