Human Genome Project

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Human Genome Project
Duration: 1990–2003
Cost: About US$3 billion
Result: The (nearly) complete sequence of human DNA — about 3 billion base pairs

The Human Genome Project (HGP) was an international scientific research project to determine the complete sequence of human DNA and to identify all the genes of the human genome. Launched in 1990, principally by the US National Institutes of Health and the Department of Energy with partners including the UK's Wellcome Trust, it declared a working draft in 2000 — announced jointly with the private rival effort of Celera Genomics — and a finished sequence in 2003, two years ahead of schedule.

Findings and legacy

The project's surprises reshaped molecular biology: humans have only about 20,000 protein-coding genes — far fewer than expected, and little more than a worm; the genome is mostly non-coding, much of it repetitive or of viral origin; and any two people are about 99.9% identical in sequence. Genes (see genus for a different sense of biological naming) turned out to be only the beginning of the story — regulation, splicing and epigenetics carried much of the complexity.

The HGP's commitment to rapid public data release (the Bermuda Principles) set genomics' open culture, and sequencing costs have since fallen from billions to hundreds of dollars per genome, enabling personalised medicine, ancestry testing and the pandemic-era genomic surveillance that tracked SARS-CoV-2. The last gaps — the centromeres and other repeats — were closed by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium in 2022.
