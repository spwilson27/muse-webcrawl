Pell's equation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pell's equation is a Diophantine equation of the form x² − ny² = 1, where n is a given positive nonsquare integer and integer solutions are sought for x and y. It is named after the English mathematician John Pell, although the equation was studied much earlier, notably by Brahmagupta in India and later by Fermat, who posed it as a challenge in 1657.

Solutions

For every nonsquare positive n, Pell's equation has infinitely many integer solutions. The smallest positive solution is called the fundamental solution; all others can be generated from its powers in the ring of quadratic integers. Joseph-Louis Lagrange proved in the 18th century that a solution always exists, using the theory of continued fractions for √n.

History

Brahmagupta (7th century) gave methods for composing solutions, and Bhaskara II (12th century) described the cyclic chakravala method, which finds the minimal solution, famously for n = 61. In Europe the equation was popularised by Fermat's challenge and solved in special cases by Wallis and Brouncker; Euler mistakenly attached Pell's name to it.

Applications

Pell's equation underlies the arithmetic of real quadratic fields, the theory of units in algebraic number theory, and rational approximations to square roots. It is closely related to Diophantine equations more generally and to the continued fraction expansion of quadratic irrationals.
