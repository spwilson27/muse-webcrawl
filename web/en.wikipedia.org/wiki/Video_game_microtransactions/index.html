Video game microtransactions

Microtransactions are a business model used in video games where users can purchase virtual goods with micropayments. Microtransactions are often used in free-to-play games to provide a revenue source for the developers. While microtransactions are a staple of the mobile app market, they are also available on PC platforms such as Steam, as well as console platforms.

Free-to-play games that include microtransactions are sometimes referred to as "freemium". "Pay-to-win" is sometimes used as a derogatory term to refer to games where paying for in-game items can give the player an advantage over other players, particularly if the items cannot be acquired without payment.

History

One of the earliest widely discussed microtransactions was the horse armor downloadable content for The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion (2006), sold for US$2.50, which drew criticism as poor value. The model expanded rapidly with mobile and social games in the early 2010s, and later became common in full-price console and PC games through in-game stores, battle passes, and loot boxes.

Loot boxes and regulation

Randomized reward systems such as loot boxes have drawn scrutiny from regulators in several countries over similarities to gambling. Belgium and the Netherlands restricted certain loot box implementations, and other jurisdictions have debated disclosure of odds and age restrictions. Publishers have also faced lawsuits and consumer backlash, notably over Star Wars Battlefront II (2017), which prompted several companies to remove or rework paid loot boxes.

Impact

Microtransactions generate a large share of revenue for many publishers and have shaped game design toward live-service models with continuous content updates. Critics argue the model can encourage manipulative design patterns, while defenders note it funds ongoing development and allows free access to games.
