William Cunnington

William Cunnington FSA (1754 – 31 December 1810) was an English antiquarian and self-taught archaeologist of international renown. A wool merchant from Heytesbury in Wiltshire, he systematically excavated some 379 burial mounds (barrows) on Salisbury Plain and around Stonehenge between about 1798 and 1810, work that laid foundations for the scientific study of prehistoric Britain.

Cunnington's barrow diggings on Salisbury Plain uncovered rich Bronze Age burials.

Excavations

After ill health forced Cunnington to take up outdoor exercise, he developed an interest in the ancient monuments of Wiltshire. Sponsored first by H. P. Wyndham and then by Sir Richard Colt Hoare of Stourhead, he excavated hundreds of barrows with the help of workmen including Stephen and John Parker. Cunnington kept careful records, employed early forms of stratigraphic observation, and preserved the finds — unusually rigorous practice for his time.

His most famous discovery was the Bush Barrow near Stonehenge (1808), a richly furnished Bronze Age burial containing gold ornaments, including the celebrated gold lozenge, along with bronze daggers and a sceptre-head — one of the richest burials of the Wessex culture. Hoare published their results in The Ancient History of Wiltshire (1810–1812).

Legacy

Cunnington's collection was bought by the Wiltshire Archaeological and Natural History Society and forms the core of the Wiltshire Museum in Devizes. His meticulous methods led later archaeologists to honour him as a founding figure of British archaeology; he was dubbed, in the words of a modern assessment, "the father of English barrow digging." His grandson, also named William Cunnington, and great-grandson Edward Cunnington continued the family's antiquarian work.
