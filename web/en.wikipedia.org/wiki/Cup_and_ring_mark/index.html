Cup and ring mark

[Image: A rock surface carved with cup and ring marks, concentric circles around central depressions]

Cup and ring marks are a form of prehistoric rock art found in Atlantic Europe and elsewhere in the world. They consist of a concave depression, the "cup", pecked into a rock surface and often surrounded by one or more concentric grooves, the "rings", sometimes with a radial channel running out from the centre.

Distribution and dating

In Britain and Ireland thousands of examples are known, with notable concentrations in Kilmartin Glen in Argyll, Northumberland, Dumfries and Galloway, and Ilkley Moor in Yorkshire. They are also widespread in Galicia in north-west Spain, in Portugal, and in other parts of the world including India and Australia. Most are attributed to the Neolithic and Early Bronze Age, roughly 4000–1500 BC.

Interpretation

The meaning of the marks is unknown. Proposed explanations include territorial or route markers, maps of settlements or stars, ritual symbols connected with water or the landscape, and signs made during the extraction of rock for axes or querns. Their frequent placement on outcrops with wide views over the surrounding landscape, and their reuse in burial cairns and standing stones, suggests they held symbolic significance for the communities that made them.

Recording

Community recording projects, such as Scotland's Rock Art Project, have catalogued thousands of carved panels using photography and 3D photogrammetry, frequently discovering previously unrecorded examples.
