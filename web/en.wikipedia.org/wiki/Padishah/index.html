Padishah

Padishah (Persian: پادشاه; Ottoman Turkish: پادشاه, padişah) is a superlative sovereign title of Persian origin, composed of pād ("master") and shāh ("king"). It is roughly equivalent to the Latin concept of an emperor or "great king", and was adopted by several monarchs claiming the highest rank, roughly equivalent to that of the ancient Persian Shahanshah ("King of Kings").

History

The title was used by rulers of various Islamic empires. Ottoman sultans adopted the title padişah from the fifteenth century, particularly after the conquest of Constantinople in 1453, asserting their status as the pre-eminent Muslim rulers — a status also reflected in the style Sultan of Sultans.

In the Mughal Empire of India, the emperor bore the title Padishah (also rendered Badshah), as in the case of Shah Jahan, "Padishah of the world". The title was used by Mughal rulers until the deposition of Bahadur Shah II in 1857.

The rulers of the Durrani Empire in Afghanistan and several other dynasties in the Persianate world also used the title. In Persian usage itself, pâdeshâh could denote any king, but in diplomatic usage it implied imperial rank.

Padshahnama

The Padshahnama ("Chronicle of the Emperor") is the official illustrated chronicle of the reign of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan, produced in lavish manuscript editions during the 1630s–1650s, famous for its paintings of court ceremonies, battles and royal processions.

In fiction

The title appears in modern fiction, notably in Frank Herbert's Dune series, where the ruler of the known universe is the "Padishah Emperor".
