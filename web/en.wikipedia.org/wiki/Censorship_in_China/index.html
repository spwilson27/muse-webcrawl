Censorship in China

Censorship in the People's Republic of China is mandated by the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and implemented extensively by the government. It is one of the most stringent censorship regimes in the world. The government and the CCP censor information for mainly political reasons — to maintain control over the population and prevent criticism of the CCP and the Chinese government — but also to control the flow of information in general and to shape public opinion. Censorship applies to all media capable of reaching a wide audience, including television, print media, radio, film, theatre, text messaging, instant messaging, video games, literature, and the Internet.

Organisation and scope

Censorship is administered through a network of state bodies, historically including the General Administration of Press and Publication and the State Administration of Radio, Film, and Television (later merged into the National Radio and Television Administration), the Cyberspace Administration of China, and the CCP's Publicity Department, which sets the overall editorial line. Media outlets are required to be licensed, and journalists must pass examinations on state ideology. Chinese censors employ both prior restraint — approval required before publication — and post-publication sanctions.

Internet censorship

The People's Republic of China censors Internet content through the system widely known as the Great Firewall, part of the Golden Shield Project, which blocks access to many foreign websites and services (including Google, Facebook, YouTube, Twitter/X, and Wikipedia at various times) and filters domestic content for sensitive keywords. Domestic platforms such as Weibo and WeChat are required to police user content, deleting posts and closing accounts that touch on sensitive topics. Common sensitive subjects include the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests and massacre, the independence movements of Tibet, Xinjiang, and Taiwan, the Falun Gong movement, and criticism of senior leaders.

Justifications and criticism

The Chinese government argues that censorship is necessary to maintain social stability, national unity, and moral order, and that all countries regulate harmful content. Critics, including press-freedom organisations that consistently rank China near the bottom of world press-freedom indices, argue that the system suppresses legitimate speech, journalism, and historical memory, and note that China has repeatedly been described as one of the world's leading jailers of journalists.

Hong Kong and beyond

Historically, Hong Kong enjoyed much greater press freedom under the "one country, two systems" framework, but the imposition of the Hong Kong national security law in 2020 was followed by the closure of pro-democracy outlets such as Apple Daily and Stand News and is widely seen as having sharply curtailed freedoms there as well. Self-censorship by individuals, media, and businesses — inside and outside China — is also a major feature of the system.
