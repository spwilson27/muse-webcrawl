Digital signal

A digital signal is a signal that represents data as a sequence of discrete values; at any given time it can only take on one of a finite number of values. This contrasts with an analog signal, which represents continuous quantities with continuous values.

Representation

In most digital circuits, the signal can have two possible valid values; this is called a binary signal or logic signal. They are represented by two voltage bands: one near a reference value (typically termed ground or zero volts), and the other a value near the supply voltage. These correspond to the two values "zero" and "one" (or "false" and "true") of the Boolean domain. Multi-level signals with more than two states are used in some communications channels to carry several bits per symbol.

Advantages

Digital signals are resistant to noise because a receiver only needs to decide which of a small set of discrete states was sent, and signals can be regenerated exactly at repeaters. They can be processed by digital circuits and computers, compressed, and protected with error-correcting codes.

Digitisation

Analog signals are converted to digital form by sampling and quantisation in an analog-to-digital converter; the Nyquist rate sets the minimum sampling rate needed to preserve the signal's bandwidth.
