Journey to the West (Chinese: 西遊記; pinyin: Xīyóu Jì) is a Chinese novel published in the 16th century during the Ming dynasty and attributed to Wu Cheng'en. It is one of the Four Great Classical Novels of Chinese literature. The novel retells, in fantastical form, the pilgrimage of the Tang-dynasty monk Xuanzang (Tripitaka), who travelled to India in the 7th century to obtain Buddhist scriptures. In the novel he is escorted by four disciples — the irrepressible Monkey King Sun Wukong, the gluttonous Zhu Bajie, the steadfast Sha Wujing, and the dragon prince who serves as his horse — through eighty-one ordeals of demons and monsters, in a story that blends adventure, comedy, satire of bureaucracy, and Buddhist, Daoist and Confucian allegory.

Synopsis

The first seven chapters tell the origin of Sun Wukong, a monkey born from a stone who masters the 72 transformations, rebels against Heaven, and is imprisoned by the Buddha beneath the Five Elements Mountain for 500 years. The following chapters introduce the monk Tang Sanzang — called Tripitaka after the scriptures he seeks — sent west by the Tang emperor at the behest of the bodhisattva Guanyin. Freed by Tripitaka, Sun Wukong becomes his protector, controlled when unruly by a tightening golden headband and a spell. Zhu Bajie, a disgraced heavenly marshal reborn part-pig, and Sha Wujing, a former curtain-raising general, join the quest. Over eighty-one trials — demons who covet the monk's flesh for its promise of immortality, the White Bone Demon, the Flaming Mountains, kingdoms of women and of monks, and the schemes of spurned gods and runaway celestial servants — test the pilgrims until they reach the Thunderclap Monastery in India, receive the scriptures, and return east to enlightenment and apotheosis.

Origins and influence

The historical Xuanzang's own record of his journey, the Great Tang Records on the Western Regions, and centuries of storytelling, drama and earlier narrative versions fed the Ming novel. Journey to the West has been continuously popular in East Asia for four centuries: Sun Wukong is among the most widely recognised characters in world literature, adapted in Peking opera, countless films and television series, the Japanese manga and anime Dragon Ball, and video games such as Black Myth: Wukong (2024). Abridged English translations by Arthur Waley (Monkey, 1942) and the complete translation by Anthony C. Yu made it a classic in the West as well.
