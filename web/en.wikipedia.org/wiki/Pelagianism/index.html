Pelagianism

Augustine of Hippo wrote extensively against the Pelagian teachings.

Pelagianism is a Christian theological position named after the British monk Pelagius (c. 354 – c. 418/420). It holds that human beings are not tainted by original sin in a way that destroys free will, and that people can take the initial steps toward salvation by their own moral effort, without the necessity of divine grace for each act.

Teachings

Pelagius, an ascetic moral teacher active in Rome in the late fourth and early fifth centuries, emphasized human responsibility and the goodness of created nature. Pelagian teaching denied that Adam's sin is transmitted to his descendants as inherited guilt; infants, on this view, are born in the condition Adam occupied before the Fall. Grace was understood primarily as the gift of free will itself, the law and teaching of Christ, and the forgiveness of sins, rather than as an interior power required for every good act.

Controversy and condemnation

Pelagius's ideas were opposed vigorously by Augustine of Hippo, whose doctrine of original sin and prevenient grace was shaped by the controversy. Pelagianism was condemned by the Council of Carthage in 418 and by the Council of Ephesus in 431, and Pelagius was exiled and disappears from the record. A moderated position later labelled "Semi-Pelagianism", associated with John Cassian and the monks of southern Gaul, was in turn addressed by the Second Council of Orange in 529.

Legacy

Pelagian themes recurred in later theology, and "Pelagian" has often been used as a polemical label for views stressing human initiative in salvation. Modern scholars debate how accurately Augustine's and the councils' portrayals reflect Pelagius's own writings, several of which survive, including his commentary on the Pauline epistles and the letter to Demetrias.
