Hiccup

A hiccup (scientific name singultus, from Latin for "sob" or "gasp") is an involuntary contraction (myoclonic jerk) of the diaphragm that may repeat several times per minute. The reflex arc involves the phrenic and vagus nerves and a medullary centre; each contraction is followed by the abrupt closure of the vocal cords, which produces the characteristic "hic" sound.

Causes

Brief bouts are commonly triggered by eating too quickly, carbonated drinks, alcohol, sudden excitement, swallowing air, or sudden changes in temperature. Hiccups lasting more than 48 hours are termed persistent, and those lasting more than a month intractable; prolonged hiccups may result from nerve irritation, central nervous system disorders, metabolic problems, or certain medications, and warrant medical evaluation.

Treatment

Most bouts resolve spontaneously. Common home remedies — breath-holding, the Valsalva manoeuvre, sipping cold water, breathing into a paper bag — aim to interrupt the reflex arc, often by raising carbon dioxide levels or stimulating the vagus nerve. For persistent cases, medications such as chlorpromazine, baclofen or metoclopramide may be used, and rarely procedures such as phrenic nerve block are considered.

Notable cases

The American Charles Osborne had hiccups continuously from 1922 to 1990, an estimated 430 million hiccups, an episode recognised by Guinness World Records. Hiccups are also observed in utero in fetuses and are common in infants.
