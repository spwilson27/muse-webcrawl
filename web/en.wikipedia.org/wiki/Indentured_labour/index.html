Indentured labour

Indentured labour is a system of bonded labour under which a worker agrees, by contract (an indenture), to work for an employer for a fixed period, often in exchange for passage to a new country, lodging, food and sometimes a small wage. Historically it took several major forms.

Indentured servitude in the Americas

In 17th- and 18th-century British North America, many European migrants paid for their passage by working, typically for four to seven years. Indentured servants formed a large share of early settlers in colonies such as Virginia and Maryland.

Indian and Chinese indenture

After the abolition of slavery in the British Empire (1834) and elsewhere, plantation owners recruited large numbers of Indian labourers — and also Chinese and others — for colonies in the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, Natal and Malaya. Between the 1830s and 1917, well over a million Indians migrated under indenture. Conditions were frequently harsh and the system was criticised as "a new system of slavery"; Indian indenture was ended during World War I. Descendant communities form the Indo-Caribbean, Indo-Fijian and other diasporas.
