Systemic sclerosis

Systemic sclerosis, or systemic scleroderma, is a rare chronic autoimmune disease characterized by excessive collagen deposition causing fibrosis of the skin and internal organs, small-vessel vasculopathy, and production of characteristic autoantibodies. It differs from localized scleroderma (morphea) in that internal organs may be involved.

Classification

Two major subsets are recognized by the extent of skin involvement:

  • Limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis — skin thickening restricted to the hands, forearms, feet, and face; often associated with the CREST features (calcinosis, Raynaud's phenomenon, oesophageal dysfunction, sclerodactyly, telangiectasia) and anticentromere antibodies.
  • Diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis — widespread skin thickening including the trunk and proximal limbs, with earlier and more frequent internal organ involvement, often associated with anti-topoisomerase I (Scl-70) or anti-RNA polymerase III antibodies.

Organ involvement

The lungs (interstitial lung disease and pulmonary arterial hypertension), gastrointestinal tract, heart, and kidneys may all be affected. Scleroderma renal crisis — malignant hypertension with acute kidney injury — was historically a leading cause of death but outcomes improved markedly with ACE inhibitors. Nearly all patients experience Raynaud's phenomenon, often years before other features.

Cause and treatment

The cause is unknown; genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers (such as silica exposure) are implicated. There is no cure; management is organ-based, using immunosuppressants such as mycophenolate for skin and lung disease, antifibrotics such as nintedanib for interstitial lung disease, vasodilators for Raynaud's and pulmonary hypertension, and proton-pump inhibitors for reflux.

Epidemiology

Systemic sclerosis is uncommon, with prevalence estimates in the range of roughly 50–300 cases per million people. It affects women about four times as often as men and typically begins between ages 30 and 50.
