Church–Turing thesis

The Church–Turing thesis states that a function on the natural numbers is computable by an effective procedure (an algorithm) if and only if it is computable by a Turing machine. It is a thesis, not a theorem, because "effective procedure" is an intuitive notion that the thesis identifies with the formal models of computability.

History

The thesis emerged in the 1930s from the work of Alonzo Church on the lambda calculus and Alan Turing's 1936 paper introducing Turing machines, written to settle Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem in the negative. Equivalent models — Gödel and Kleene's general recursive functions, Post machines, and later register machines — all define the same class of functions, lending the thesis strong support.

Significance

The thesis underlies theoretical computer science: it justifies treating any reasonable model of computation as equivalent in power to a Turing machine. Stronger claims, such as the physical Church–Turing thesis about machines in the physical world, remain debated, particularly in connection with quantum computing, which does not refute the original thesis but challenges complexity-theoretic extensions of it.
