The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA axis or HTPA axis) is a complex set of direct influences and feedback interactions among three components: the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland, and the adrenal glands. These interactions form a major neuroendocrine system that controls reactions to stress and regulates many body processes, including digestion, the immune system, mood and emotions, sexuality, and energy storage and expenditure.

Anatomy and mechanism

The axis operates as a hormonal cascade. Neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus synthesise and secrete corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) into the hypophyseal portal system. CRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) into the bloodstream. ACTH acts on the adrenal cortex, stimulating the synthesis and release of glucocorticoids, principally cortisol in humans.

Cortisol in turn exerts negative feedback at the pituitary and hypothalamus, suppressing further release of ACTH and CRH and thereby keeping the response self-limiting. This feedback is mediated by glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptors and operates over fast, intermediate, and slow timescales.

Circadian rhythm

HPA activity follows a pronounced daily rhythm driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Cortisol typically peaks shortly after waking — the cortisol awakening response — and declines across the day to a nadir around the middle of the night. Superimposed on this rhythm, physical and psychological stressors provoke additional pulses of activity.

Functions

Through cortisol, the HPA axis mobilises energy stores (promoting gluconeogenesis and lipolysis), modulates cardiovascular tone, restrains inflammation and immune activity, and influences cognition and mood. Together with the sympathoadrenal system, which acts more rapidly via adrenaline, it coordinates the body's response to threat — the classic fight-or-flight pattern.

Dysregulation and disease

Overactivity of the axis occurs in Cushing's syndrome and is associated with melancholic depression, anorexia nervosa, and chronic stress. Underactivity occurs in Addison's disease and after withdrawal of long-term glucocorticoid therapy. Altered HPA function has also been reported in post-traumatic stress disorder, fibromyalgia, and chronic fatigue states, and early-life adversity is thought to programme the axis's set points with long-lasting consequences for health.
