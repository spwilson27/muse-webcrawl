Textile manufacture during the Industrial Revolution

A steam-powered cotton mill in Lancashire (placeholder)

The textile industry was the first to be transformed by the Industrial Revolution in Britain. Between roughly 1760 and 1850, a chain of inventions mechanised the spinning and weaving of cotton, moved production from cottages to water- and steam-powered factories, and created the factory system, the industrial town, and a new working class. Cheap British cotton cloth was exported worldwide, supplied by raw cotton grown largely by enslaved labour in the American South.

Before mechanisation

Cloth production was organised under the "putting-out" or domestic system: merchants distributed raw fibre to rural households, whose members carded, spun (mostly women and girls), and wove (mostly men) in their homes. Output was limited, especially in spinning — several spinners were needed to keep one weaver supplied.

Key inventions

John Kay's flying shuttle (1733) doubled weaving speed and worsened the yarn bottleneck. James Hargreaves' spinning jenny (1764) let one worker spin many threads at once; Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769) used rollers and water power to spin stronger thread and anchored the first cotton mills; Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779) combined jenny and frame to produce fine, strong yarn in quantity. Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785), improved over decades, gradually mechanised weaving, displacing handloom weavers by the 1830s–40s. Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) mechanised cleaning of short-staple cotton in America, entrenching slavery's expansion. Steam engines (Watt's rotative engine) freed mills from rivers and concentrated them in coalfield towns such as Manchester — "Cottonopolis."

Factory system and social effects

Mechanisation imposed factory discipline: long hours, child labour, dangerous machinery, and crowded mill towns with poor sanitation. Workers resisted — the Luddites (1811–16) smashed frames in wool and cotton districts — and conditions prompted factory legislation (Factory Acts from 1833 limiting child labour and hours) and trade unionism. Productivity gains were dramatic: the price of cotton yarn and cloth fell steadily, clothing became cheap, and Britain came to dominate world textile trade, processing the majority of the world's raw cotton by the mid-19th century.
