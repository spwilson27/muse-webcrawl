Attenuation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, attenuation is the gradual loss of flux intensity through a medium. A signal — light, sound, an electrical wave, or a beam of radiation — becomes weaker as it travels, owing to absorption and scattering within the material it passes through. Attenuation is commonly expressed in decibels per unit length and often follows an exponential decay described by the Beer–Lambert law.

In optics and electromagnetism

For electromagnetic waves, attenuation results from absorption and scattering by the medium; the attenuation coefficient measures how readily a material makes a beam opaque. In optical fibers, attenuation (mainly from Rayleigh scattering and material absorption) falls to about 0.2 dB/km in the low-loss windows around 1310 and 1550 nm, which sets the spacing of repeaters and amplifiers in long-haul networks. In radio engineering, attenuation includes free-space path loss and losses from rain, foliage, and building materials.

In acoustics and seismology

Sound waves lose energy through viscosity, thermal conduction, and scattering; medical ultrasound imaging depends on tissue attenuation that increases with frequency, trading penetration depth against resolution. In seismology, attenuation of seismic waves (described by the quality factor Q) provides information about the Earth's interior and must be corrected for when locating earthquakes and imaging structures.

In telecommunications and electronics

Attenuation in cables and transmission lines arises from conductor resistance (skin effect) and dielectric losses, growing with frequency and length; equalizers and amplifiers compensate for it. The term also describes a deliberate reduction of signal level by an attenuator device, and in brewing, the apparent attenuation of wort as yeast converts sugars to alcohol.
