New Zealand English phonology

The short front vowels of New Zealand English are notably raised compared with other varieties.

New Zealand English phonology describes the sound system of New Zealand English (NZE). Like most varieties descended from the English of southeastern England, New Zealand English is non-rhotic: the sound /r/ is pronounced only before a vowel. NZE is distinguished from Australian English principally by its raised and centralised short front vowels and by the merger of the vowels in near and square for many speakers.

Vowels

The most salient feature of the New Zealand accent is the raising of the short front vowels. The vowel of trap is raised toward [ɛ], the vowel of dress is raised toward [e] or even [ɪ], and the vowel of kit is centralised to a schwa-like [ə]. This chain shift is why New Zealanders are stereotypically heard by outsiders as saying "fush and chups" for fish and chips. In addition, the diphthongs of near and square have merged for many younger speakers, so that beer and bear can be homophones.

Consonants

As in other non-rhotic varieties, linking and intrusive /r/ occur. Many speakers have a dark or vocalised /l/ in syllable codas, so that milk may sound close to "miwk". The distinction between /w/ and /ʍ/ (as in wine vs whine) is retained by some older speakers, especially in the south, but has largely been lost. In the Southland region, a trilled or tapped "Southland burr" /r/ may be heard, reflecting Scottish settlement.

Prosody

New Zealand English is noted for the high rising terminal, or "uptalk", in which declarative statements end with a rising intonation. This feature, also found in Australian English, is especially common among younger speakers and women.
