Comitative case

The comitative case (abbreviated COM) is a grammatical case that denotes accompaniment — the meaning "with" or "together with" in the sense of company, as in "he came with his friend". It is named from the Latin comitatus, "accompanied". Comitative marking must be distinguished from the instrumental case, which denotes the tool or means by which an action is performed ("with a knife"); some languages use one form for both, while others keep them strictly separate.

The comitative marks an accompanying participant: "X came with-Y".

In various languages

The comitative is a well-developed case in many Uralic languages. In Estonian it is formed with the suffix -ga (e.g. sõbraga, "with a friend") and is used with both singular and plural nouns; Finnish uses the rare suffix -ine- plus possessive endings, mainly in formal registers, preferring adpositions in everyday speech. Hungarian uses the suffix -val/-vel, which merges comitative and instrumental functions. Basque marks accompaniment with -(r)ekin. In Mongolian the comitative suffix -тай/-той/-тэй also carries possessive-like meaning ("having"). Quechua has a comitative suffix -wan, which likewise covers instrumental uses.

Semantics

Comitative relations typically involve two participants of similar status acting jointly — an animate accompanier accompanying the main participant. Languages vary in whether inanimate accompaniments ("he left with the money") pattern with the comitative or the instrumental. In typological research, the comitative is often analysed as marking a companion in a "conjunction-like" configuration, differing from true coordination (forming a plural group) in that the companion is syntactically subordinate.
