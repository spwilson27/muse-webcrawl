Gesso

Gesso (Italian for "chalk", from Latin gypsum) is a white paint mixture consisting of a binder mixed with chalk, gypsum or pigment, used to prepare a surface — wood panel, canvas or sculpture — for painting. It provides a smooth, slightly absorbent ground that improves the adhesion and brightness of paint layers applied over it.

Traditional gesso

Traditional gesso is made from animal glue (usually rabbit-skin glue) combined with chalk or gypsum and white pigment. In medieval and Renaissance panel painting, as described by Cennino Cennini in Il Libro dell'Arte (c. 1400), craftsmen applied many thin coats: a coarse gesso grosso followed by a fine gesso sottile, scraping and sanding between coats to achieve a surface like ivory. The brilliant white ground was essential to tempera painting and to water gilding, in which gold leaf is laid over gesso and coloured clay bole.

Modern gesso

Most modern "gesso" is an acrylic dispersion ground containing calcium carbonate and titanium dioxide in an acrylic polymer medium. It is flexible, quick-drying and can be used on canvas, wood and other supports for oil and acrylic painting, though purists note its properties differ from glue gesso, which remains preferred for traditional egg tempera and gilding.
