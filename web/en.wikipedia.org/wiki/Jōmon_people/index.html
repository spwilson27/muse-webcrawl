Jōmon people

The Jōmon people (縄文人, Jōmon jin) were the inhabitants of the Japanese archipelago during the Jōmon period (c. 14,000 – 300 BCE), a long span in which hunter-gatherer communities developed some of the earliest pottery in the world. The name Jōmon ("cord-marked") refers to the patterns impressed on their ceramics with twisted cords, a term introduced after pottery found at the Ōmori Shell Mounds was described by Edward S. Morse in 1877.

Lifestyle

Jōmon communities were semi-sedentary, living in pit dwellings gathered into villages such as Sannai-Maruyama in Aomori, one of the largest known settlements. Their subsistence combined hunting (deer, boar), fishing and shellfish gathering — leaving great shell middens (kaizuka) — with intensive gathering and management of plant foods, including chestnuts, walnuts, acorns and horse chestnuts, and possibly small-scale cultivation of plants such as lacquer trees and perilla. Rich marine and forest environments allowed unusually stable, complex societies without full agriculture.

Material culture

Jōmon pottery evolved from plain early wares into elaborate vessels, including the flame-rimmed pots (kaen doki) of the Middle Jōmon. The culture is known for its clay figurines, dogū, many with heart-shaped faces or snow-goggle eyes, whose purpose remains debated, and for stone circles such as Ōyu in Akita. In 2021, seventeen Jōmon sites in Hokkaidō and northern Tōhoku were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Origins and legacy

Genetic studies indicate that the Jōmon descend from Palaeolithic populations that reached Japan more than 30,000 years ago and remained relatively isolated. They contributed a major component of ancestry to modern Japanese populations, alongside later Yayoi-period migrants from continental Asia who introduced wet-rice agriculture. Jōmon ancestry is highest among the Ainu of Hokkaidō and, to a lesser degree, Ryukyuan populations.
