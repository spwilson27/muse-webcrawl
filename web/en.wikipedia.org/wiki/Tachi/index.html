A tachi (太刀) is a type of Japanese sword, traditionally made and worn primarily by mounted samurai of feudal Japan. The tachi predates the katana and is distinguished by being more curved, somewhat longer, and — most recognisably — by the way it was worn: slung from cords with the cutting edge facing down, suspended at the left hip, in contrast to the katana, which is thrust through the belt edge-up.

History and description

The tachi developed during the Heian period as the sidearm of mounted warriors, its deeper curvature (sori) suiting draw-cuts delivered from horseback. Blade lengths typically ran 70–80 cm. Mountings were often elaborate, with lacquered scabbards and decorative cord wrappings. Because it was worn suspended, the signature of the smith (mei) was placed on the tang so as to be read with the sword in wearing position — the opposite side from the katana, a key feature used by experts to distinguish the two.

Use and legacy

From the Muromachi period the shorter uchigatana and later the katana, worn edge-up for fast drawing on foot, displaced the tachi in everyday use, but long tachi remained in ceremonial and shrine contexts, and many were shortened (suriage) into katana. Fine tachi by smiths such as the Bizen school are preserved as National Treasures of Japan.
