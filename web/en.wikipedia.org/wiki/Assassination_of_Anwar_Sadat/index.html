The assassination of Anwar Sadat occurred on 6 October 1981, when the President of Egypt was shot dead during the annual military parade in Cairo celebrating the eighth anniversary of Egypt's crossing of the Suez Canal at the start of the Yom Kippur War. Sadat was killed by members of the Egyptian Islamic Jihad who had infiltrated the parade.

The attack

As a formation of jets flew overhead, an army truck that was part of the parade stopped in front of the reviewing stand. First Lieutenant Khalid Islambouli and three accomplices jumped out, threw grenades and opened fire with assault rifles on the stand. Sadat, apparently mistaking the men for part of the display, stood to salute; he was struck by multiple rounds and died in hospital a few hours later. Eleven others were killed, including the Cuban ambassador and a Coptic Orthodox bishop, and 28 were wounded, among them Vice President Hosni Mubarak, who succeeded Sadat.

Motives and aftermath

The assassins were motivated by Islamist opposition to Sadat's peace treaty with Israel (the Camp David Accords of 1978 and the Egypt–Israel peace treaty of 1979), by his suppression of dissent — thousands had been arrested in a September 1981 crackdown — and by a fatwa approving the killing issued by Omar Abdel-Rahman. Islambouli and four others were tried, convicted and executed in April 1982. The assassination shocked the world but did not derail the peace treaty; Mubarak ruled Egypt for nearly thirty years.
