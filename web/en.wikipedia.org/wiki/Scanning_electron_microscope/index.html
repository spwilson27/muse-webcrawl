Scanning electron microscope

A scanning electron microscope (SEM) is a type of electron microscope that produces images of a sample by scanning its surface with a focused beam of electrons. The electrons interact with atoms in the sample, producing signals that reveal surface topography and composition.

Scanning electron microscope
Type: Electron microscope
Principle: Focused electron beam raster scan
Signals: Secondary electrons, backscattered electrons, X-rays
Resolution: ~1 nm (instrument dependent)

Principle

An electron gun generates a beam focused by condenser and objective lenses and deflected in a raster pattern. Secondary electrons emitted from near the surface give high-resolution topographical images; backscattered electrons and characteristic X-rays give compositional information (EDS/EDX).

Sample preparation

Samples must generally be vacuum-compatible and electrically conductive, often achieved by sputter-coating non-conductors with gold or carbon. Environmental SEMs allow imaging of wet or uncoated samples.

History

Early work by Max Knoll (1935) and Manfred von Ardenne led to commercial SEMs in 1965 (Cambridge Stereoscan), developed from Charles Oatley's group at Cambridge.
