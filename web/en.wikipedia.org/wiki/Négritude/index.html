Négritude

Négritude
OriginParis, 1930s
Key figuresAimé Césaire, Léopold Sédar Senghor, Léon-Gontran Damas
Key textsCahier d'un retour au pays natal (1939); Présence Africaine journal

Négritude is a framework of critique and literary theory developed by francophone African and Caribbean intellectuals, writers, and politicians in Paris during the 1930s and 1940s. Its founders — the Martinican poet Aimé Césaire, the Guianese poet Léon-Gontran Damas, and the future Senegalese president Léopold Sédar Senghor — sought to affirm a shared Black African cultural identity and values in the face of French colonial racism and the policy of assimilation. The word itself, coined by Césaire in his 1939 poem Cahier d'un retour au pays natal (Notebook of a Return to the Native Land), reclaimed a term derived from the slur nègre.

Development

The movement grew out of journals such as La Revue du Monde Noir (1931) and L'Étudiant noir (1935), drawing inspiration from the Harlem Renaissance, whose writers the Paris circle read and translated, and from surrealism, Marxism, and ethnographic writing on Africa. After the Second World War the journal Présence Africaine, founded in 1947 by Alioune Diop, became its principal platform, and the First Congress of Black Writers and Artists in Paris in 1956 marked its international high point.

Ideas and criticism

Négritude celebrated rhythm, emotion, communal values, and a distinctive African relationship to nature as counterweights to what it portrayed as cold European rationalism and individualism — Senghor famously wrote that "emotion is Black, as reason is Greek". It inspired anti-colonial movements and later Black consciousness and Black Arts movements. Yet it was also criticised — by Frantz Fanon for glossing over colonial material realities, by Wole Soyinka (who quipped that "a tiger does not proclaim his tigritude"), and by later critics such as Stanislas Adotevi — for essentialising Black identity and accepting a binary invented by colonialism itself. Césaire and Senghor themselves differed: Césaire's version was historical and political, Senghor's more cultural and civilisational.
