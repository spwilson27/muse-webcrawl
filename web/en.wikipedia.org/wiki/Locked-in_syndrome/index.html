Locked-in syndrome (LIS), also known as pseudocoma, is a condition in which a patient is aware and awake but cannot move or communicate verbally because of complete paralysis of nearly all voluntary muscles in the body except, typically, those controlling the eyes. It results from damage to specific parts of the brainstem, most often the ventral pons, which severs the motor pathways between the brain and the body while leaving consciousness and cognition intact. The term was introduced by Fred Plum and Jerome Posner in 1966.

Causes

The most common cause is a stroke of the basilar artery that infarcts the pons. Other causes include traumatic brain injury, tumours, demyelinating disease such as central pontine myelinolysis, infection, and the late stages of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or Guillain–Barré syndrome. Unlike a persistent vegetative state, in which awareness is lost, the cerebral cortex in locked-in syndrome is essentially undamaged; the patient is fully conscious but "locked in" to an unresponsive body.

Forms and communication

Clinicians distinguish classical locked-in syndrome (paralysis with preserved vertical eye movement and blinking), incomplete forms (some additional voluntary movement), and total locked-in syndrome, in which even eye movements are lost and communication may only be possible through brain–computer interfaces using EEG or implanted electrodes. Most patients communicate by blinking or moving their eyes in response to questions, or by spelling out words as a partner recites the alphabet—a technique known as partner-assisted scanning.

Notable cases

The best-known account is The Diving Bell and the Butterfly (1997), dictated letter by letter through blinks of his left eyelid by Jean-Dominique Bauby, editor of Elle magazine, after a massive stroke; it was later adapted into an acclaimed film. Studies of chronic locked-in patients have found that many report a meaningful quality of life once stable care and a reliable communication method are established, a finding that has influenced medical ethics debates about the condition.

Prognosis and treatment

There is no cure; treatment is supportive, focusing on preventing complications of immobility, respiratory care, and rehabilitation. Mortality is highest in the first months, particularly for vascular causes, but patients who survive the acute phase may live for decades. Some recovery of movement occurs in a minority of cases, more often in non-vascular causes. Assistive technologies—including eye-tracking computers and experimental brain–computer interfaces—have substantially expanded patients' ability to communicate and control their environment.
