Parthian shot

The Parthian shot is a light cavalry military tactic made famous by the Parthians, an ancient Iranian people. While performing a real or feigned retreat at full gallop, horse archers would turn their bodies back to shoot at the pursuing enemy. The manoeuvre required superb equestrian skills, since the rider's hands were occupied by his composite bow; stirrups had not yet been invented, so the rider relied entirely on pressure from his legs to guide his horse.

History

The tactic was refined by the nomadic peoples of the Eurasian steppe, including the Scythians, Huns, Turks and Magyars, as well as the Parthians. It proved devastating at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BCE, where the Parthian general Surena used feigned retreats and arrow barrages to annihilate the Roman army of Crassus. Camel trains carrying reserve arrows allowed the archers to sustain their fire indefinitely.

Idiom

By folk etymology, "Parthian shot" later gave rise to the English phrase "parting shot" — a final cutting remark delivered as one departs. The two expressions are distinct in origin, but the martial image of a last blow struck while retreating made the association natural, and "parting shot" is now the common idiom.
