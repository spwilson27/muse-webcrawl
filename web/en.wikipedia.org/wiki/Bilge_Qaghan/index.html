Bilge Qaghan (Old Turkic: Bilgä Qaγan, "wise ruler"; 683–734) was the fourth qaghan of the Second Turkic Khaganate. Together with his brother Kul Tigin and the counsellor Tonyukuk, he restored and consolidated the Göktürk empire in the decades after its revival by his father, Ilterish Qaghan, and his uncle Qapaghan Qaghan.

The Bilge Qaghan memorial in Mongolia's Orkhon Valley.

Early life and rise

Bilge was the elder son of Ilterish, founder of the restored khaganate. As a young prince he campaigned extensively with his father and uncle, and served as a subordinate ruler (shad) over part of the empire. When Qapaghan was killed in battle in 716, a succession crisis followed; after a brief struggle Bilge took the throne in 716–717, making Kul Tigin crown prince and commander of the armies.

Reign

With Tonyukuk as his chief adviser, Bilge pursued a policy of consolidation: he campaigned against rebellious subject peoples, renewed pressure on the Karluks and other western groups, and managed a careful diplomacy with Tang China, alternating war and negotiation. He famously declined advice to build walled cities and adopt Buddhism or Taoism, arguing in the Orkhon inscriptions that such steps would weaken the nomadic strength of the Turks — though in later years he softened on some of these questions.

Death and legacy

Bilge died in 734, reportedly poisoned by a court official, Buyruk Chor, who was executed for the deed. His great memorial stele in the Orkhon Valley, erected beside that of Kul Tigin, bears one of the longest texts in the Old Turkic runiform script: the Orkhon inscriptions, in which Bilge addresses future generations, recounting the rise, fall, and revival of the Turkic state and warning against submission to China. The inscriptions were deciphered in 1893 by Vilhelm Thomsen and remain foundational sources for Turkic history.
