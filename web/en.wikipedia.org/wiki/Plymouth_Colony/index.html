Plymouth Colony was an English colonial venture in America from 1620 to 1691, at various points encompassing the modern areas of Plymouth, Massachusetts and Bristol County, Massachusetts, among others. The colony was founded by a group of Puritan Separatists initially known as the Brownists, later called the Pilgrims, together with other settlers recruited by the Merchant Adventurers.

It was the second successful English colony in America, after the Jamestown Colony in Virginia (1607). Plymouth Colony was never formally chartered by the Crown but governed itself under the Mayflower Compact (1620) and later patents. In 1691 it was merged into the Province of Massachusetts Bay.

Founding

The colonists sailed on the Mayflower in 1620, landed first at Provincetown Harbor and then at Plymouth — the site of the Wampanoag village of Patuxet, emptied by epidemics in 1616–1619 — in December. The first winter killed about half of the 102 passengers. In March 1621 the colony concluded a treaty with Massasoit, sachem of the Wampanoag, and with the help of Tisquantum (Squanto) the survivors planted successful crops, celebrating a harvest feast in the autumn of 1621 that is remembered as the first Thanksgiving.

Development

The colony's economy rested on farming, fishing, timber and the fur trade, trading as far as the Kennebec and Penobscot rivers in Maine. In 1627 the colonists' "Undertakers" assumed the colony's debts to the Merchant Adventurers in exchange for a trade monopoly, and the debts were finally paid off in the 1640s. The settlement spread into towns including Duxbury, Scituate, Sandwich, Taunton, Barnstable and Rehoboth. Unlike Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth never received a royal charter; its government rested on the compact, patents from the Council for New England, and the General Court, which in 1636 adopted a written code of laws. Plymouth was a member of the New England Confederation from 1643.

King Philip's War

The colony was devastated by King Philip's War (1675–1676), fought against Metacom (King Philip) and his Native allies. Plymouth towns such as Swansea, Rehoboth and Dartmouth were attacked, a large fraction of the colony's men were killed, and its economy was nearly ruined, though the Native peoples of southern New England suffered even more catastrophic losses.

End of the colony

In 1686 Plymouth was absorbed into the short-lived Dominion of New England under Governor Edmund Andros. When the Dominion collapsed in 1689 Plymouth resumed its government, but the new Massachusetts charter of 1691 incorporated Plymouth into the Province of Massachusetts Bay, ending 71 years of separate existence. The colony's last governor was Thomas Hinckley.

Legacy

Plymouth's influence on American tradition — the Mayflower Compact, Thanksgiving, Plymouth Rock — is disproportionate to its small size. Records include William Bradford's Of Plymouth Plantation and the colony's published laws and court records.
