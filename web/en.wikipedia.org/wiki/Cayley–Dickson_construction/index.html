In mathematics, the Cayley–Dickson construction, named after Arthur Cayley and Leonard Eugene Dickson, is a method for producing a sequence of algebras over the field of real numbers, each with twice the dimension of the previous one. Starting from the real numbers, it generates the complex numbers, then the quaternions, then the octonions, then the sedenions, and so on. The algebras produced are called Cayley–Dickson algebras.

Definition

Given an algebra A with a conjugation operation, the construction forms a new algebra whose elements are ordered pairs (a, b) of elements of A. Addition is componentwise. Multiplication is defined by (a, b)(c, d) = (ac − d*b, da + bc*), where * denotes conjugation in A. The conjugate of a pair is (a*, −b). Each new algebra carries a norm satisfying N(x) = xx*, and the norm of a pair is the sum of the norms of its components.

Properties lost at each stage

Each application of the construction weakens the algebraic structure. The complex numbers are a field but lose the ordering compatible with arithmetic that the reals possess. The quaternions lose commutativity of multiplication. The octonions lose associativity, retaining only alternativity. The sedenions lose even that: they are not a division algebra and possess zero divisors, so their norm is no longer multiplicative in the strong sense required by Hurwitz's theorem.

By Hurwitz's theorem, the only normed division algebras over the reals are the reals, complexes, quaternions, and octonions — the first four algebras of the sequence. The Cayley–Dickson algebras beyond dimension 8 are nevertheless studied for their symmetries and applications, including connections to the exceptional Lie groups and to models in theoretical physics.

History

The doubling idea appears implicitly in Cayley's 1845 presentation of the octonions (which John T. Graves had discovered in 1843), and Dickson systematised the general construction in the early twentieth century while studying the quaternions and their generalisations.
