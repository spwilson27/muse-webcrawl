The durian is the edible fruit of several tree species belonging to the genus Durio in the mallow family Malvaceae. Native to Borneo and Sumatra, it is widely cultivated throughout Southeast Asia. The fruit is distinctive for its large size, strong odour and thorn-covered rind, and is commonly called the "king of fruits" in the region.

Description

Durian trees can grow up to 50 metres tall. The fruit can grow as large as 30 centimetres long and 15 centimetres in diameter, typically weighing one to three kilograms. Its husk is covered in sharp thorns, and inside, the edible flesh ranges from pale yellow to deep orange depending on the species and cultivar. The flesh emits a powerful odour described variously as resembling almonds, caramelised onions, turpentine or even rotting matter; the smell persists even through the husk, and the fruit is banned from many hotels and public transport systems in Southeast Asia.

Culinary use and culture

Despite — or because of — its smell, durian flesh is prized for its rich, custard-like flavour. It is eaten fresh, used in ice cream, cakes, candies and traditional desserts such as dodol, and the seeds can be boiled or roasted. Popular cultivars include Thailand's Monthong and Malaysia's Musang King, the latter commanding high export prices, especially in China. The naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace famously praised the fruit's flavour as worth a journey to the East to experience.

Cultivation

The durian is an ultratropical crop requiring abundant rainfall and lowland conditions. Thailand is the largest producer, followed by Malaysia and Indonesia, with exports expanding rapidly to meet Chinese demand.
