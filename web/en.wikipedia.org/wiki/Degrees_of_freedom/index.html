Degrees of freedom

Degrees of freedom
MeaningNumber of independent ways a system can vary
Used inPhysics, mechanics, statistics, chemistry
ExampleA rigid body in space: 6 (3 translation + 3 rotation)

Degrees of freedom is a general concept across the sciences: the number of independent parameters or dimensions of variation in a system. The phrase takes specific, related meanings in mechanics, statistical mechanics, and statistics.

Mechanics and physics

In mechanics, the degrees of freedom of a mechanical system are the number of independent coordinates needed to specify its configuration. A single particle moving freely in space has three; a rigid body has six — three of translation and three of rotation. Constraints reduce the count: a train on a track has one degree of freedom. In robotics the term describes the independent motions of a manipulator or joint.

Statistical mechanics and chemistry

In statistical mechanics, degrees of freedom are independent modes in which a system can store energy — translational, rotational, and vibrational modes of molecules — and they determine, for example, the molar heat capacity of a gas via the equipartition theorem. In chemistry and thermodynamics, Gibbs's phase rule (F = C − P + 2) gives the degrees of freedom of a system at equilibrium from the numbers of components and phases.

Statistics

In statistics, degrees of freedom are the number of values in a calculation that are free to vary. For a sample of n observations used to estimate a mean, the sample variance uses n − 1 degrees of freedom because one degree is spent estimating the mean itself. The parameter governs the shapes of the chi-squared, Student's t, and F distributions used in hypothesis tests.
