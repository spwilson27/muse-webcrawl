Ceasefire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ceasefire (or truce) is a temporary stoppage of a war or armed conflict, in which each side agrees with the other to suspend aggressive actions, often for a fixed period and within defined lines. Ceasefires may be unilateral, bilaterally agreed, or imposed/demanded by the UN Security Council; they may precede peace negotiations or humanitarian pauses (for aid deliveries, evacuations, vaccination campaigns), and may harden into long-term frozen conflicts when no settlement follows.

Practice

Famous instances include the spontaneous Christmas truces of 1914, the Korean Armistice (1953 — technically a ceasefire persisting to this day, no peace treaty having followed), Ramadan and Eid truces in several conflicts, and the monitored ceasefires of the OSCE in Donbas (2014–22) whose thousands of recorded violations documented the instrument's fragility. Successful ceasefires require monitoring, communication channels and verification (observer missions, joint commissions); spoiler violence is the classic failure mode studied by peace-process scholarship. Humanitarian law continues to apply during ceasefires, and their legal character — binding agreement under international law where states are parties — can matter for responsibility for violations.
