Audit

An audit is an independent examination of the financial information of an entity, whether profit oriented or not, irrespective of its size or legal form. An audit is conducted to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, and are prepared in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework. Audits are considered an essential part of corporate governance and capital markets.

Types of audit

  • External audit: performed by an independent firm; in many jurisdictions a statutory requirement for public and large private companies.
  • Internal audit: performed by employees or an outsourced function within an organisation to evaluate risk management, control, and governance processes.
  • Government audit: examinations of public bodies by supreme audit institutions, such as the U.S. Government Accountability Office.
  • Tax audit: a revenue authority's examination of a taxpayer's returns.
  • Other engagements: operational, compliance, forensic, information-systems, and environmental audits.

Process

An external financial audit typically involves gaining an understanding of the entity and its controls, assessing the risk of material misstatement, testing transactions and balances through inspection, observation, confirmation and reperformance, and evaluating the financial statements as a whole. The auditor issues a report giving an unmodified (clean) opinion or, where problems exist, a qualified, adverse, or disclaimer of opinion.

Standards and regulation

Audits are performed under auditing standards — internationally, the International Standards on Auditing issued by the IAASB; in the United States, standards of the PCAOB for public companies and the AICPA for others. Auditor independence, professional scepticism, and quality control are central requirements, tightened after failures such as the Enron collapse in 2001.
