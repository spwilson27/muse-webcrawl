Life history theory

Life history theory is an analytical framework in evolutionary biology used to study the strategies organisms use to allocate resources among growth, maintenance and reproduction across their lives. A life history is the pattern of survival and reproduction events typical for a member of a species — including age at first reproduction, number and size of offspring, reproductive lifespan, and total lifespan.

Trade-offs

Because resources are finite, organisms face trade-offs: energy spent on early reproduction cannot be spent on growth or survival. Life history theory predicts how natural selection shapes these trade-offs in response to the environment, especially mortality patterns. Traits studied include:

  • Size at birth and at maturity
  • Age and size at first reproduction
  • Number, size and sex ratio of offspring
  • Frequency of reproduction (semelparity vs. iteroparity)
  • Parental care and lifespan

r/K selection

An influential early scheme distinguished r-selected species — small, fast-maturing, producing many offspring with little care, suited to unstable environments — from K-selected species — large, slow-maturing, producing few offspring with heavy investment, suited to stable, competitive environments. Although now regarded as an oversimplification, it remains a useful heuristic.

History

The field developed from the work of Ronald Fisher, who formalised reproductive value, and from demographic models of the mid-20th century. Eric Charnov, Stephen Stearns and others in the 1970s–1990s built optimisation models predicting, for example, optimal clutch size and age at maturity from mortality schedules.

Applications

Life history theory is applied in fisheries management, conservation biology, the study of invasive species, and human evolutionary psychology, where variations in development speed and reproductive timing are analysed as adaptive responses to environment.
