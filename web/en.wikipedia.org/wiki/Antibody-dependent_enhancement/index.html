Antibody-dependent enhancement

Antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) is a phenomenon in which binding of a virus to sub-optimal or non-neutralizing antibodies enhances the virus's entry into host cells, increasing infection and sometimes worsening disease severity. Instead of neutralizing the virus, the antibody–virus complex is taken up by immune cells (such as macrophages) through Fc gamma receptors, providing the virus with an additional route into cells it can replicate in.

Mechanisms

In extrinsic ADE, non-neutralizing antibodies promote viral uptake into Fc-receptor-bearing cells, expanding the pool of infected cells. In intrinsic ADE, the signaling triggered by Fc receptor engagement suppresses antiviral responses within the cell, such as interferon pathways, further boosting viral replication. ADE is most clearly documented in dengue fever, where a second infection with a different serotype can produce severe dengue hemorrhagic fever, aided by cross-reactive antibodies from the first infection.

Clinical significance

ADE is a central concern in vaccine and antibody-therapy design. It contributed to the increased risk of severe dengue among seronegative recipients of the Dengvaxia vaccine, which led to restrictions on its use. Similar enhancement was observed historically with experimental vaccines against respiratory syncytial virus and feline infectious peritonitis virus; vaccine developers for SARS-CoV-2 monitored for ADE, which was not observed as a significant clinical problem with authorized COVID-19 vaccines.
