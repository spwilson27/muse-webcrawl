Catharism

Catharism was a Christian dualist movement that thrived in parts of Europe, especially Languedoc in southern France and northern Italy, between the 12th and 14th centuries. Its adherents, called Cathars (from Greek katharoi, "the pure") or Albigensians (after the city of Albi), held that the material world was created by an evil power and that the human soul must be liberated from the flesh through rebirth and purification.

Beliefs and practices

Cathars rejected the Catholic priesthood, sacraments and church buildings. Their spiritual elite, the perfecti ("Perfects"), lived ascetically, abstained from meat and sex, and administered the movement's only sacrament, the consolamentum, a spiritual baptism normally received near death by ordinary believers (credentes). Cathar theology had links, debated by historians, with the Bogomils of the Balkans.

Suppression

Pope Innocent III proclaimed the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) against the Cathars and their protectors among the southern nobility, a war marked by atrocities such as the massacre at Béziers. The Treaty of Paris (1229) and the subsequent Papal Inquisition progressively destroyed the movement; the last known Cathar Perfect, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned in 1321. The hilltop fortresses of the region, such as Montségur — which fell in 1244 with the mass burning of over 200 Perfects — became enduring symbols of the Cathars.
