The Green Line is the demarcation line established in the 1949 Armistice Agreements between the armies of Israel and those of its neighbours (Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan and Syria) after the 1948 Arab–Israeli War. It separated Israel from the territories administered by its neighbours: the Jordanian-ruled West Bank (including East Jerusalem), the Egyptian-held Gaza Strip, the Syrian Golan Heights and Lebanon. The name comes from the green ink used to draw the line on the map during the armistice talks on Rhodes.

History

The armistice agreements explicitly stated that the lines were not political or territorial borders, but ceasefire lines pending a final settlement. Between 1949 and 1967 the Green Line functioned in practice as Israel's de facto boundary. In the Six-Day War of June 1967 Israel captured the territories beyond the line, which have since been administered under Israeli military occupation (with the Golan Heights and East Jerusalem later annexed in moves not recognised internationally).

Status and significance

The Green Line remains the most widely referenced basis for a negotiated two-state settlement: most international proposals, including the Arab Peace Initiative, envisage a Palestinian state in the West Bank and Gaza Strip based on the 1967 lines with agreed land swaps. The route of the Israeli West Bank barrier, built from 2002, deviates substantially from the Green Line, incorporating many settlement blocs on the western side. Israeli settlements established beyond the Green Line after 1967 are considered illegal under international law by most of the international community, a position Israel disputes.
