Assertiveness

Assertiveness is the quality of being self-assured and confident without being aggressive. In psychology and communication theory, it is a learnable skill describing a mode of communication in which a person stands up for their own rights, needs, and opinions while respecting those of others.

Overview

Assertive communication is commonly contrasted with two other styles: passive communication, in which a person suppresses their own needs, and aggressive communication, in which a person pursues their needs at others' expense. Assertiveness involves expressing feelings and requests directly and honestly — often taught through "I" statements — while listening to and acknowledging the other party.

History

Assertiveness training became popular in the mid-20th century, notably through the work of Andrew Salter, who described assertive behaviour in his 1949 book Conditioned Reflex Therapy, and Joseph Wolpe. In the 1970s, popular books such as Manuel Smith's When I Say No, I Feel Guilty (1975) and Robert Alberti and Michael Emmons's Your Perfect Right brought assertiveness training to a wide audience.

Applications

Assertiveness skills are taught in psychotherapy (particularly for social anxiety and depression), in workplace communication training, in healthcare, and in education. Research associates assertiveness with lower stress, better relationships, and reduced risk of being bullied or exploited, although cultural norms influence how assertive behaviour is perceived.
