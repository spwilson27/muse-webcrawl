Passport

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A passport is a travel document, usually issued by a country's government to its citizens, that certifies the holder's identity and nationality and entitles them to travel under the state's protection abroad. Modern passports are standardised booklets (ICAO Doc 9303) containing a data page with photograph and biographical details and, since the mid-2000s, an RFID chip (biometric or e-passport) storing the holder's facial image and sometimes fingerprints.

The passport system's modern form crystallised during and after the First World War, when wartime controls were retained and standardised through League of Nations conferences (1920); before that, passports were exceptional in much of Europe. A passport's practical value depends on visa-free access: rankings such as the Henley Passport Index measure this, with the strongest passports (in recent years, states such as Singapore, Japan and several EU countries) opening 190-odd destinations, and the weakest (such as Afghanistan's) fewer than 30. States may refuse or revoke passports (for debt, criminal process, or national-security grounds), and some persons — stateless people and refugees — travel on substitute documents under the 1951 and 1954 conventions.
