Modacrylic

Modacrylic is a synthetic copolymer. Modacrylics are soft, strong, resilient and dimensionally stable. They can be easily dyed, show good press and shape retention, and dry quickly. They are resistant to chemicals and solvents, are non-allergenic, and are resistant to moths, mildew and sunlight degradation.

Modacrylic fibers are produced by extruding a copolymer of acrylonitrile and vinyl chloride into a solvent-spun or wet-spun fiber. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission defines a modacrylic fiber as a manufactured fiber in which the fiber-forming substance is any long-chain synthetic polymer composed of less than 85%, but at least 35% by weight of acrylonitrile units.

Properties

Modacrylic fibers are notable for their inherent flame resistance, a property that distinguishes them from ordinary acrylic fibers. They also have good resistance to acids and alkalis, good abrasion resistance, and moderate resilience. Modacrylics have a density of about 1.30–1.37 g/cm³ and a relatively low melting point, which makes them unsuitable for high-temperature pressing but useful in applications where softness and drape are desired.

Uses

Modacrylic fibers are used in apparel, home furnishings and industrial applications. Common uses include flame-retardant protective clothing, faux fur garments, wigs and hairpieces, paint rollers, filters, blankets, and stuffed toys. Because the fibers can be readily crimped and textured, they are widely used to imitate animal fur.

Production

Modacrylic fibers are produced by copolymerizing acrylonitrile with other monomers such as vinyl chloride or vinylidene chloride, which impart flame resistance. The polymer is dissolved in a solvent such as acetone and extruded through spinnerets, after which the solvent is evaporated or washed out to form the fiber.
