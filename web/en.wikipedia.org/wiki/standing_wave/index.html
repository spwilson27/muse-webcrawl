Standing wave

In physics, a standing wave, also known as a stationary wave, is a wave that oscillates in time but whose peak amplitude profile does not move in space. The peak amplitude of the wave oscillations at any point in space is constant with respect to time, and the oscillations at different points throughout the wave are in phase. The locations at which the absolute value of the amplitude is minimum are called nodes, and the locations where the absolute value of the amplitude is maximum are called antinodes.

Formation

Standing waves were first described scientifically by Michael Faraday in 1831. Standing waves occur when two waves with the same frequency and amplitude travel in opposite directions and interfere with each other. They commonly arise when waves are reflected at the boundaries of a medium, such as the vibrating string of a musical instrument fixed at both ends.

Examples

Common examples include waves on guitar and violin strings, organ pipes, and electromagnetic standing waves inside microwave ovens and optical cavities. In a string fixed at both ends, the allowed standing wave patterns form a harmonic series that determines the musical pitch produced. See also resonance and normal mode.

Mathematical description

A standing wave can be written as the sum of two travelling waves of equal amplitude and frequency moving in opposite directions: y(x,t) = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt). The factor sin(kx) fixes the spatial shape while cos(ωt) gives the oscillation in time, so the wave pattern does not propagate.
