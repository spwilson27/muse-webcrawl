Gymnasium (ancient Greece)

In ancient Greece, the gymnasium (Greek: γυμνάσιον, gymnasion, from γυμνός, "naked") was a public institution where young men trained in athletics, exercised, bathed and socialised, and increasingly also a place of intellectual discussion and education.

Function

Greek athletes trained and competed nude, anointing their bodies with olive oil and scraping it off with a strigil after exercise. A gymnasium complex typically included a palaestra (wrestling ground and courtyard), running tracks, baths and colonnades, and was supervised by an official called the gymnasiarch. Athens had three famous gymnasia — the Academy, the Lyceum and the Cynosarges — associated respectively with Plato, Aristotle and the Cynics.

Education and culture

By the classical and Hellenistic periods the gymnasium had become the central educational institution of the Greek city, where ephebes (young men) received physical and military training alongside instruction in music, literature and philosophy. Under Roman rule the institution spread across the eastern Mediterranean, often combined with Roman-style bath buildings.

Legacy

The word survives for sports halls in English; in Germany and much of Europe, by contrast, "Gymnasium" came to denote an academic secondary school — a reflection of the institution's later intellectual role.
