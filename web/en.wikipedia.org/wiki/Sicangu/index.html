Sicangu

The Sicangu are one of the seven subdivisions of the Lakota people, part of the Oceti Sakowin (Seven Council Fires) of the Great Sioux Nation. Their name in Lakota, Sičháŋǧu Oyáte, translates roughly as the "Burnt Thighs Nation," rendered in French as Brulé, by which name they are also widely known in English-language sources.

Name and origin

The name Sičháŋǧu is traditionally explained by a story in which the ancestors of the group were caught by a prairie fire while moving camp, and many had their legs or thighs burned as they fled. The French rendering Brulé ("burnt") became the standard term in fur-trade records and treaty documents.

History

By the early nineteenth century the Sicangu ranged across the northern Great Plains, hunting bison and trading with French, British, and later American traders. They were party to major agreements with the United States, including the Fort Laramie Treaties of 1851 and 1868, which established the Great Sioux Reservation. Prominent Sicangu leaders of the era included the chief Spotted Tail (Siŋté Glešká), a noted diplomat and warrior who advocated a pragmatic course in dealing with the United States.

Following the division of the Great Sioux Reservation in 1889, the Sicangu were assigned the Rosebud Indian Reservation in south-central South Dakota. Today their government is the Rosebud Sioux Tribe, headquartered at Rosebud, South Dakota. The tribe operates Sinte Gleska University, a tribal college founded in 1970 and named for Chief Spotted Tail.

Culture

Like other Lakota divisions, the Sicangu traditionally organized society around the tiyošpaye, the extended family group, and placed central cultural weight on generosity, kinship obligations, the vision quest (haŋbléčheyapi), and the Sun Dance. Revitalization of the Lakota language is an ongoing priority of tribal schools and Sinte Gleska University.
