Prime minister

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A prime minister (PM) is the head of government in a parliamentary or semi-presidential system — the leader of the executive who governs through, and depends on the confidence of, the legislature. The office differs from a president (often both head of state and government) in that the prime minister is usually not directly elected to the post: they are the leader of the party or coalition able to command a parliamentary majority, appointed by the head of state (monarch or president), and removable by a vote of no confidence — the fusion, in Bagehot's phrase, of executive and legislature that defines the Westminster model.

Origins and spread

The office crystallised in Britain after 1688 as the king's "prime" minister; Robert Walpole (1721–42) is conventionally the first, though he would have denied the title, then an insult. From Britain it spread through the Empire and Commonwealth (Canada, Australia, India — whose prime minister heads the world's largest democracy — New Zealand, Jamaica) and across Europe: Germany's equivalent is the Chancellor, Spain's the President of the Government, Ireland's the Taoiseach, Israel's and Japan's prime ministers. Semi-presidential systems (France, Russia) split executive power between a president and a prime minister, sometimes in tense "cohabitation". Powers vary hugely — a British PM with a disciplined majority has been called an "elective dictator" (Lord Hailsham), while coalition PMs in the Netherlands or Israel bargain daily for survival — but the core is constant: chief executive, cabinet chair, party leader, and the person who answers, weekly and in person, to parliament, a ritual (Prime Minister's Questions) exported worldwide from the constitutional practice of Westminster.
