Elephant clock

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The elephant clock was a medieval Islamic water clock invented by the engineer Ismail al-Jazari (1136–1206) and described in his Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices (1206). It is one of the most celebrated of al-Jazari's automata, notable for combining working elements drawn from several cultures.

Description

The timekeeping mechanism is a sinking-bowl regulator concealed inside the body of an elephant figure. Every half hour, a sequence of movements and sounds is triggered: a bird on the dome chirps and turns, a driver figure strikes the elephant, dragons move, and a ball falls into a serpent's mouth, causing a figure to point and a cymbal to sound. The driver hits the elephant's head with a mallet as part of the mechanism's cycle.

Cultural elements

Al-Jazari deliberately combined motifs from many traditions: the elephant is Indian, the dragons Chinese, the phoenix Egyptian, the water mechanism Greek, and the turban of the driver figure Islamic — an early example of multicultural design in engineering.

Modern reconstructions

Full-size working reconstructions have been built in modern times, including one at the Ibn Battuta Mall in Dubai and a replica at the Château des Baux in France, demonstrating that the mechanism described in the manuscript functions as intended.
