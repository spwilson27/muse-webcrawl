Hospice

Hospice care is a type of health care that focuses on the palliation of a terminally ill patient's pain and symptoms and attending to their emotional and spiritual needs at the end of life. Hospice care prioritises comfort and quality of life by reducing pain and suffering. Although hospice and palliative care share a philosophy, hospice is generally reserved for patients whose prognosis is six months or less if the disease runs its usual course, and who have chosen to forgo curative treatment.

History

The word derives from the Latin hospitium, a guest-house; medieval hospices sheltered pilgrims and the sick. The modern movement began with Dame Cicely Saunders, who founded St Christopher's Hospice in London in 1967, introducing systematic pain management — including the concept of "total pain" — and regular oral morphine dosing. Florence Wald brought the model to the United States, founding the Connecticut Hospice in 1974, and in 1982 the Medicare Hospice Benefit made hospice a covered service in the US.

Services

Hospice care is delivered by an interdisciplinary team — physicians, nurses, social workers, chaplains, aides, and trained volunteers — usually in the patient's home, but also in dedicated inpatient hospices, hospitals, and nursing homes. Services include symptom control, medical equipment and medication related to the terminal diagnosis, respite care for family caregivers, and bereavement support for the family for a period after the patient's death.

Philosophy

Hospice affirms life and regards dying as a normal process, intending neither to hasten nor postpone death. Its growth reflects wider changes in attitudes toward death and patient autonomy, and hospice programmes now serve millions of patients annually worldwide; in the United States roughly half of Medicare decedents receive hospice care.
