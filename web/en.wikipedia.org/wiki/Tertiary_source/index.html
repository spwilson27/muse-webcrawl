Tertiary source

A tertiary source is an index or textual consolidation of already published primary and secondary sources that does not itself contain any original ideas or interpretation. Common examples are encyclopedias, dictionaries, textbooks, almanacs, bibliographies, directories and other reference works that distil and present material originally published elsewhere.

Encyclopedias are classic tertiary sources

Characteristics

Tertiary sources aim to summarise, organise and provide entry points into a subject rather than to advance an argument. They are typically written or edited with an awareness of the scholarly consensus and are frequently used by readers to gain a first orientation, to check basic facts, and to find the primary and secondary literature through their bibliographies.

Relation to primary and secondary sources

A primary source is an original record or artefact created at the time under study (a diary, dataset, statute or photograph); a secondary source analyses or interprets primary sources (a scholarly monograph or review article). Tertiary sources sit one step further back, compiling secondary material. The same work can serve different roles depending on the research question: a textbook is tertiary for a report on established knowledge, but a primary source for a historian studying how that knowledge was taught.

Because they lag behind current research and compress nuance, tertiary sources are generally acceptable for background reading but are rarely cited as the sole authority in academic work.
