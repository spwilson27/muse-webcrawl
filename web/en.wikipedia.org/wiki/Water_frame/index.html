Water frame

The water frame is a water-powered spinning frame developed by Richard Arkwright and patented in 1769. It produced strong cotton yarn in large quantities and, with Arkwright's mills at Cromford in Derbyshire, is regarded as a founding technology of the factory system of the Industrial Revolution.

How it works

The frame draws out cotton rovings through successive pairs of rollers turning at increasing speeds, then twists the strands and winds them onto bobbins. Unlike James Hargreaves's spinning jenny, which made soft yarn suitable only for weft, the water frame's roller drafting produced firm yarn strong enough for warp, allowing all-cotton cloth.

Impact

Because the machine needed water power and continuous operation, it moved spinning from cottages into purpose-built mills with wage labour working set hours — the pattern copied across Lancashire, New England and continental Europe. Arkwright's patents were challenged and eventually overturned in 1785, after which the design spread freely.
