History of printing

The history of printing starts as early as 3000 BCE, when the proto-Elamite and Sumerian civilizations used cylinder seals to certify documents written in clay tablets. Other early forms include block seals, hammered coinage, pottery imprints, and cloth printing. Woodblock printing originated in China during the Tang dynasty, and movable type was invented there by Bi Sheng around 1040 CE. Printing spread throughout East Asia and, by the late medieval period, to Europe, where Johannes Gutenberg's system of movable-type printing, developed around 1440, dramatically lowered the cost of producing books and helped usher in the Printing Revolution.

Woodblock printing

Woodblock printing is a technique for printing text, images, or patterns that was used widely throughout East Asia. It originated in China in antiquity as a method of printing on textiles and later on paper. The earliest surviving woodblock-printed fragment dates to the Han dynasty; the oldest dated printed book is the Diamond Sutra of 868 CE, produced during the Tang dynasty.

Movable type in East Asia

Around 1040, the Chinese artisan Bi Sheng created the first known movable-type system, using baked-clay type pieces arranged in iron frames. Ceramic type was followed by wooden type under Wang Zhen (1297) and bronze metal type during the Ming dynasty. In Korea, metal movable type was in use by the 13th century; the Jikji, printed in 1377, is the oldest extant book printed with metal movable type.

Gutenberg and the European printing press

Around 1440, the German goldsmith Johannes Gutenberg developed a printing system based on a hand mould for casting individual metal type, oil-based ink, and a mechanical screw press adapted from wine and paper presses. His workshop printed the Gutenberg Bible (c. 1455), the first major European book produced with movable type. Printing presses spread rapidly — by 1500, presses in more than 250 European cities had produced an estimated ten million books, transforming the availability of texts and contributing to the Renaissance, the Reformation, and the Scientific Revolution.

Industrialisation

The iron press of Lord Stanhope (c. 1800) and Friedrich Koenig's steam-powered press (1814), adopted by The Times of London, multiplied output. The rotary press (1843), hot-metal typesetting machines such as the Linotype (1884), and offset lithography in the 20th century further industrialised newspaper and book production.

Digital era

Phototypesetting and then desktop publishing in the 1980s — built around page-description languages such as PostScript — moved composition to computers. Digital printing and print-on-demand followed, while the internet shifted much publishing away from print altogether.
