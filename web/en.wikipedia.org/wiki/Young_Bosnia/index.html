Young Bosnia (Serbo-Croatian: Mlada Bosna) was a loosely organised revolutionary movement of schoolboys and students — mostly Bosnian Serbs, but including Bosnian Muslims and Croats — active in Austrian-ruled Bosnia and Herzegovina before the First World War. Its members sought the liberation of Bosnia from Habsburg rule and its unification with Serbia or within a Yugoslav state. The movement is best known for the Sarajevo assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand on 28 June 1914, the event that triggered the July Crisis and the outbreak of the First World War.

Background and ideology

Austria-Hungary's annexation of Bosnia in 1908 humiliated Serbian national opinion and radicalised a generation of Bosnian youth. Young Bosnians were influenced by Serbian nationalism and the Chetnik tradition, by the assassin Bogdan Žerajić — who had attempted to kill the Bosnian governor in 1910 and became the movement's martyr-hero — and by pan-Yugoslav ideas circulating through student societies in Sarajevo, Vienna and Belgrade. The writer Ivo Andrić, later a Nobel laureate, moved in Young Bosnia circles as a student.

The Sarajevo assassination

In June 1914, a cell of Young Bosnians — Gavrilo Princip, Nedeljko Čabrinović and Trifko Grabež, assisted by others including Muhamed Mehmedbašić and Vaso Čubrilović — obtained pistols, bombs and cyanide through networks linked to the Serbian organisation known as the Black Hand and Serbian military intelligence. On 28 June, Čabrinović's bomb missed the Archduke's car; later that morning Princip, positioned by chance on the motorcade's altered route near the Latin Bridge, shot Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie at point-blank range, killing both.

Trial and aftermath

Most conspirators were arrested. Because several were under twenty, they escaped the death penalty under Austrian law: Princip received twenty years' hard labour and died of tuberculosis in the Theresienstadt fortress prison in April 1918. Older accomplices, including Danilo Ilić, were hanged. In Yugoslav memory Princip was celebrated as a national hero; after the 1990s wars his legacy became contested, reflecting the divided memory of 1914 in Bosnia and Serbia.
