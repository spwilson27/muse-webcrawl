The Glass–Steagall Act of 1932 was a United States federal law, enacted on February 27, 1932, that liberalized the Federal Reserve's lending powers and expanded the assets eligible as collateral for Federal Reserve notes. It should not be confused with the better-known Banking Act of 1933, also commonly called the Glass–Steagall Act, which separated commercial and investment banking and created federal deposit insurance.

Background and provisions

Passed during the Great Depression, amid widespread bank failures, gold outflows, and deflation, the 1932 act amended the Federal Reserve Act to permit the Federal Reserve Banks, in unusual and exigent circumstances, to make advances to member banks on a broader range of collateral, including government securities, and to issue Federal Reserve notes backed by United States government obligations. It also allowed advances to groups of member banks and, in exceptional circumstances, to individuals and corporations secured by direct obligations of the United States.

Significance

The Act was one of the first attempts to fight the Depression through monetary expansion and an early step toward freeing the Federal Reserve from the constraints of the gold standard. Its emergency lending provisions were later broadened by the Banking Act of 1935 and are the ancestors of the Fed's modern Section 13(3) emergency lending authority.
