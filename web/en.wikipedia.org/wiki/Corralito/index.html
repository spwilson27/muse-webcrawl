Corralito

[Image placeholder: queues outside an Argentine bank, 2001]

The corralito (Spanish for "little corral" or "playpen") was the informal name given to the bank deposit freeze imposed by the government of Argentina on 3 December 2001 during the country's economic crisis. Announced by Economy Minister Domingo Cavallo, the measures sharply restricted cash withdrawals — initially to 250 pesos per week per account holder — froze most bank transfers abroad, and were intended to stop a run on the banking system and the haemorrhage of reserves underpinning the peso's one-to-one convertibility with the US dollar.

Rather than calming depositors, the corralito provoked widespread anger, particularly among the middle classes whose savings were trapped. Protests known as cacerolazos — the banging of pots and pans — spread through Buenos Aires in mid-December 2001 and culminated in riots and looting. On 19–20 December the government declared a state of siege; President Fernando de la Rúa resigned on 20 December after violent clashes in which dozens of people died.

Aftermath

The corralito was followed in January 2002 by the corralón ("big corral") under interim president Eduardo Duhalde: deposits were compulsorily rescheduled, dollar deposits were converted into pesos at an unfavourable rate ("pesificación"), and Argentina defaulted on roughly $100 billion of sovereign debt — then the largest sovereign default in history. Restrictions on deposits were progressively eased and the corralito was formally lifted on 2 December 2002, but litigation by savers (amparos) continued for years, and the measures left a lasting distrust of the Argentine banking system.
