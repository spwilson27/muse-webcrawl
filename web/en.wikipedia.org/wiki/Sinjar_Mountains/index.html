The Sinjar Mountains (Kurdish: Çiyayê Şingalê; Arabic: Jabal Sinjār) are a mountain range in northwestern Iraq, in the Sinjar District of Nineveh Governorate, running roughly east–west for about 100 kilometres near the Syrian border. The range rises abruptly from the surrounding Mesopotamian plain to a highest point of about 1,463 metres. The town of Sinjar lies at the southern foothills.

Geography and geology

The mountains form a distinct anticlinal ridge of folded sedimentary rock isolated in the steppe of the Jazira region. The arid slopes support sparse grazing and some fig, pomegranate and other orchard cultivation in the valleys. Dozens of villages, predominantly inhabited by Yazidis, dot the northern and southern flanks; the mountain is sacred in the Yazidi faith, which regards it as a place of refuge created by God, and in Yazidi tradition it is associated with the landing place of Noah's Ark in some local tellings.

History

Because of its defensible heights and water sources, Mount Sinjar has served for centuries as a refuge for the Yazidi community during periods of persecution. In August 2014 the range became internationally known when the Islamic State (ISIL) attacked Sinjar, committing massacres and enslavement of Yazidis that the United Nations recognised as genocide. Tens of thousands of Yazidis fled up the mountain, where they were besieged without water or food in extreme heat until a corridor to Syria was opened by Kurdish forces, assisted by coalition airstrikes and humanitarian airdrops. Kurdish and allied forces retook the town of Sinjar in November 2015, but much of the district was destroyed, and many residents remained displaced years later, with the mountain area still dotted with refugee settlements.

Flora and fauna

The range's oak and wild pistachio scrub, now much reduced, once supported gazelles and other steppe wildlife; the area is an important stopover for migratory birds crossing the northern Mesopotamian plain.
