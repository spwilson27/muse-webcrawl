Declaration of war by the United States

A declaration of war by the United States is a formal act of Congress by which the United States declares war on a foreign state or government. Article I, Section 8 of the U.S. Constitution gives Congress the power "To declare War". Congress has formally declared war eleven times, in five conflicts, most recently against Romania on 5 June 1942 during the Second World War. Since then, American forces have fought major wars under congressional authorisations for the use of military force (AUMFs) or UN mandates rather than declarations.

Procedure

A declaration typically follows a presidential address to a joint session of Congress — famously Franklin D. Roosevelt's "Day of Infamy" speech of 8 December 1941 — and is enacted as a joint resolution approved by majorities of both houses and signed by the President. Declarations have usually recited the existence of a state of war "which has thus been thrust upon the United States" and authorised and directed the President to employ the entire naval and military forces and government resources. Votes have occasionally been nearly unanimous; the declaration against Japan passed with a single dissent, that of pacifist Representative Jeannette Rankin, who had also voted against war in 1917.

Undeclared wars

Many American military engagements lacked declarations, including the Quasi-War with France (1798–1800), the Civil War (a domestic conflict in the federal view), the Korean War (fought under UN Security Council resolutions as a "police action"), and the Vietnam War (authorised by the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of 1964). The War Powers Resolution of 1973, passed over President Nixon's veto, sought to limit presidential warmaking by requiring notification to Congress within 48 hours and withdrawal within 60–90 days absent authorisation. The 2001 AUMF against those responsible for the September 11 attacks and the 2002 Iraq AUMF became the legal basis for two decades of operations.

Debate

Scholars and politicians debate whether the declaration requirement has become obsolete in an age of standing forces and instant conflict, or whether its disuse has improperly shifted the war power to the presidency. Attempts to repeal or revise the 2001 AUMF have repeatedly been proposed in Congress.
