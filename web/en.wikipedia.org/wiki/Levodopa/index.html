Levodopa

Trade namesSinemet (with carbidopa), Madopar (with benserazide), others
ClassDopamine precursor
RoutesBy mouth, intestinal gel, inhalation
FormulaC9H11NO4

Levodopa, also known as L-DOPA, is an amino acid that is a precursor of the neurotransmitters dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine. It is the most effective medication for the motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease and is also used in dopa-responsive dystonia and restless legs syndrome.

Medical use

Unlike dopamine itself, levodopa crosses the blood–brain barrier and is converted by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase into dopamine in the brain, replenishing the neurotransmitter lost as dopaminergic neurons die in Parkinson's disease. It is almost always given with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor — carbidopa or benserazide — which reduces premature conversion outside the brain, decreasing nausea and cardiovascular side effects and increasing the dose reaching the brain. It is also combined with COMT inhibitors such as entacapone in some preparations.

Side effects and long-term complications

Common side effects include nausea, low blood pressure on standing, and somnolence. With years of treatment, many patients develop motor fluctuations — "wearing off" and unpredictable "on–off" periods — and levodopa-induced dyskinesia (involuntary movements). Impulse control disorders and hallucinations can also occur, particularly at higher doses.

History

L-DOPA was first isolated from broad bean seedlings in 1910 and synthesised in 1911. Its therapeutic potential emerged from the work of Arvid Carlsson in the 1950s, who showed it reversed reserpine-induced immobility in animals, and from clinical trials by George Cotzias in 1967 demonstrating the benefit of high oral doses. Combined with carbidopa as Sinemet, it was approved in 1975 and remains the gold standard of Parkinson's therapy.
