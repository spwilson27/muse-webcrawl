Atomism is a natural philosophy proposing that the physical universe is composed of fundamental indivisible components known as atoms. According to ancient Greek atomism, atoms are indestructible, homogeneous particles of differing shapes and sizes that move in an infinite void; all apparent change and diversity in the world results from their collisions, combinations and separations.

Greek atomism

Atomism was founded by Leucippus and developed by his pupil Democritus (c. 460–370 BCE), partly as a response to the Eleatic arguments of Parmenides against the possibility of motion and plurality. Democritus held that "by convention sweet and by convention bitter... in reality atoms and void".

Epicurus and Lucretius

Epicurus adopted atomism as the physical basis of his ethics, adding the doctrine of the atomic "swerve" (clinamen) to allow for free will. The fullest surviving exposition is Lucretius's Latin poem De rerum natura (1st century BCE), whose rediscovery in 1417 influenced Renaissance thought.

Later history

Atomistic ideas also appeared in Indian philosophy (the Vaiśeṣika school) and Islamic theology. In early modern Europe, Pierre Gassendi revived Epicurean atomism, and corpuscularianism informed the work of Boyle and Newton. The philosophical doctrine eventually converged with the scientific atomic theory of Dalton in the 19th century, though modern atoms proved divisible.
