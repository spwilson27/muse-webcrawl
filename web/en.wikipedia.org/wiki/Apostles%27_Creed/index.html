Apostles' Creed

The Apostles' Creed (Latin: Symbolum Apostolorum) is an early Christian statement of faith widely used in Western Christianity for catechesis, baptism, and liturgy. In twelve articles it professes belief in God the Father; in Jesus Christ — his conception, birth, suffering, crucifixion, death, burial, descent into hell, resurrection, ascension, and future return to judge; and in the Holy Spirit, the holy catholic Church, the communion of saints, forgiveness of sins, resurrection of the body, and life everlasting.

TypeCreed / symbol of faith
OriginsDeveloped from the Old Roman Symbol, c. 2nd–5th centuries; final form by c. 8th century
UseRoman Catholic, Lutheran, Anglican, Methodist, and other Western churches

History

Tradition held that the twelve apostles each contributed one article, but scholarship traces the creed to the baptismal confessions of the Roman church (the Old Roman Symbol), attested in forms from the 2nd–4th centuries. The received text crystallised in Gaul and was standard across the Latin West by the Carolingian era. It is shorter and less doctrinally technical than the Nicene Creed and is not used in Eastern Orthodoxy.

Text and use today

The creed is recited in the Roman Catholic Mass (as an alternative to the Nicene Creed), in the Rosary, at baptisms, and in the daily offices and services of many Protestant churches. Its article "he descended into hell" and the phrase "holy catholic Church" are frequent points of theological commentary.
