Movable type

Movable type
Inventedc. 1040s, Bi Sheng (China); metal type in Korea by 13th century; European system c. 1440, Johannes Gutenberg
UsePrinting text with reusable individual characters

Movable type is a system of printing in which individual characters or symbols are cast as separate pieces of type that can be assembled to form a page of text, printed, and then redistributed and reused. It dramatically reduced the cost of producing books compared with hand copying and woodblock printing of whole pages.

History

The first known movable type was invented in China by the artisan Bi Sheng around the 1040s during the Song dynasty, using fired ceramic pieces, as recorded by the scholar Shen Kuo. Wooden type followed, and by the 13th century metal movable type was in use in Goryeo Korea; the oldest surviving book printed with metal type is the Jikji (1377).

Gutenberg

Around 1440 in Mainz, Germany, Johannes Gutenberg independently developed a complete European system: a hand mould for casting uniform metal type in a lead-tin-antimony alloy, oil-based ink, and a screw press adapted from wine and paper presses. His Bible of about 1455 demonstrated the system's power, and printing shops spread across Europe within decades, transforming scholarship, religion and the spread of information.

Later development

Movable-type letterpress remained the dominant printing method for over 400 years, until hot-metal typesetting machines such as the Linotype (1884) mechanised composition, and offset printing and digital typesetting displaced it in the 20th century.
