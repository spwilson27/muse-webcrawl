Chronobiology

Chronobiology
FieldBiology — study of biological rhythms
Rhythm classesCircadian (~24 h), ultradian (<24 h), infradian (>24 h)
Key mechanismTranscription–translation feedback loops (clock genes)
Nobel PrizePhysiology or Medicine 2017 (Hall, Rosbash, Young)

Chronobiology is the scientific study of periodic (cyclic) phenomena in living organisms and their adaptation to solar and lunar rhythms. Its central finding is that nearly all life keeps time with endogenous biological clocks — molecular oscillators that generate roughly daily (circadian), shorter (ultradian) and longer (infradian) rhythms, synchronised to the environment by cues called zeitgebers, chiefly light.

Molecular clocks

In animals, circadian rhythms are driven by transcription–translation feedback loops of clock genes (notably period, timeless and clock), first worked out in the fruit fly Drosophila — research recognised by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to Jeffrey C. Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael W. Young. In mammals, the master clock is the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, which coordinates clocks in nearly every tissue.

Applications

Chronobiology underlies sleep medicine, the treatment of shift-work and jet-lag disorders, and chronotherapy — timing drug delivery to the body's rhythms (e.g. chemotherapy or blood-pressure medication) to maximise efficacy and minimise side effects. Disrupted circadian rhythms are linked to metabolic disease, mood disorders and certain cancers. In plants and animals the field also explains photoperiodism — seasonal responses such as flowering and migration.
