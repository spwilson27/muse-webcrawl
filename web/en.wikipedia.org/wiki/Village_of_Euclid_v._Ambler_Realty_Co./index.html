Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.

Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., 272 U.S. 365 (1926), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court that established the constitutionality of zoning ordinances. The Court upheld the zoning scheme of the village of Euclid, Ohio, against a facial challenge under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Background

Ambler Realty owned 68 acres in Euclid, a suburb of Cleveland, and planned to sell the land for industrial development. In 1922 the village adopted a comprehensive zoning ordinance that divided the area into districts and restricted Ambler's land largely to residential use, sharply reducing its value. Ambler sued, arguing the ordinance was an uncompensated taking and a violation of due process.

Decision

In a 6–3 decision written by Justice George Sutherland, the Court upheld the ordinance, holding that zoning is a valid exercise of the police power so long as it is not clearly arbitrary and has a substantial relation to public health, safety, morals, or general welfare. Sutherland famously compared apartment houses in single-family districts to "a mere parasite, constructed in order to take advantage of the open spaces and attractive surroundings created by the residential character of the district".

Legacy

The decision gave constitutional legitimacy to comprehensive zoning — thereafter called "Euclidean zoning" — and underpins modern American land-use law. It remains a foundational case in property and constitutional law, though critics have blamed Euclidean zoning for urban sprawl and exclusionary housing patterns.
