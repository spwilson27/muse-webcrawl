Évariste Galois

Évariste Galois (25 October 1811 – 31 May 1832) was a French mathematician and political activist. While still in his teens, he was able to determine a necessary and sufficient condition for a polynomial to be solvable by radicals, thereby solving a problem standing for 350 years. His work laid the foundations for Galois theory and group theory, two major branches of abstract algebra. He died at age 20 from wounds suffered in a duel—an affair shrouded in mystery—having spent the night before writing down his mathematical testament.

Early life

Galois was born in Bourg-la-Reine, near Paris, to Nicolas-Gabriel Galois, a school director and mayor, and Adélaïde-Marie Demante, who educated him at home until he was twelve. At the Lycée Louis-le-Grand in Paris he at first excelled in Latin, but in 1826–27 he discovered mathematics through Legendre's geometry, which he reportedly read "like a novel". He quickly moved beyond the curriculum, reading Lagrange and Abel, and began original research on polynomial equations. Twice he failed the entrance examination to the École Polytechnique—legend attributes one failure to his throwing an eraser at an examiner who annoyed him—and instead entered the less prestigious École Normale.

Mathematical work

In 1829 Galois published his first paper, on continued fractions, and submitted memoirs on the theory of equations to the Academy of Sciences. One manuscript was entrusted to Augustin-Louis Cauchy and never published; another, submitted for the Academy's Grand Prize in 1830, was lost after the death of its referee, Fourier. A third memoir, sent to Poisson in 1831, was returned as "incomprehensible". Despite these frustrations, Galois developed the core idea by which his name is known: associating to a polynomial a group of permutations of its roots (the Galois group), and showing that the polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if this group is a solvable group. This settled the general quintic: there is no formula by radicals for equations of degree five and above, complementing the earlier work of Ruffini and Abel.

Politics and death

Galois was a fervent republican during the turbulent years following the July Revolution of 1830. Expelled from the École Normale for a letter criticising its director, he joined the artillery of the National Guard, was tried and acquitted for threatening the king's life in a toast, and later imprisoned at Sainte-Pélagie for wearing a banned uniform. Released in April 1832 during a cholera epidemic, he became entangled within weeks in a duel—possibly over a romantic affair, possibly engineered by political enemies. On the evening of 29 May 1832 he wrote his famous letter to his friend Auguste Chevalier, sketching his discoveries and asking that they be shown to Jacobi and Gauss. Shot in the abdomen on 30 May, he died the next day in the Cochin hospital; his reputed last words to his brother were, "Don't cry, Alfred! I need all my courage to die at twenty!"

Legacy

Chevalier preserved Galois's manuscripts, and in 1843 Joseph Liouville recognised their importance, publishing them in 1846. The ideas—groups, fields, and the correspondence between field extensions and subgroups—were developed by Dedekind, Kronecker, Jordan, and others into modern Galois theory. The finite fields called Galois fields are fundamental to coding theory and cryptography. Galois is often cited as one of the most striking examples of precocious genius in the history of mathematics, and his death has made him a romantic icon; his collected work amounts to scarcely sixty pages.
