United States Numbered Highway System

The United States Numbered Highway System (often called U.S. Routes or U.S. Highways) is an integrated network of roads and highways numbered within a nationwide grid in the contiguous United States. As the designation and numbering of these highways was coordinated among the states, they are sometimes called Federal Highways, but the roadways were built and have always been maintained by state or local governments since their initial designation in 1926.

Numbering system

Numbers are generally assigned in a grid pattern. East–west routes bear even numbers that increase toward the south, while north–south routes bear odd numbers that increase toward the east — the reverse of the Interstate Highway System's grid, so that the two systems rarely share a number in the same state. Major east–west routes end in 0 (such as U.S. Route 20 and U.S. Route 50), and major north–south routes end in 1 or 5 (such as U.S. Route 1 and U.S. Route 101). Three-digit routes are generally auxiliary routes of the two-digit "parent" route whose number they share.

History

The system was created by the American Association of State Highway Officials (AASHO, now AASHTO) and approved on November 11, 1926, replacing the chaotic patchwork of named auto trails maintained by private associations. The famous U.S. Route 66, established in 1926 and decommissioned in 1985, became one of the most celebrated highways in American culture. Since the creation of the Interstate Highway System in 1956, many U.S. routes have been paralleled, truncated, or decommissioned, but the system remains an essential part of the national road network.

Administration

Route numbering is coordinated by AASHTO's Special Committee on U.S. Route Numbering, but each highway is owned and maintained by the state or states it passes through. Unlike Interstates, U.S. highways are not built to any single design standard and may pass directly through towns and cities.
