The Congo–Ocean Railway (French: Chemin de fer Congo-Océan, CFCO) is a railway line in the Republic of the Congo linking the Atlantic port of Pointe-Noire with the capital, Brazzaville, on the Congo River — a distance of 512 km (318 mi). It was built between 1921 and 1934 under French colonial rule at a notoriously high cost in human lives.

Construction

The line was built to bypass the unnavigable Livingstone Falls on the lower Congo River and give French Equatorial Africa an ocean outlet. Construction began in 1921 using mass conscripted African labour recruited from across the federation and beyond. Workers died in large numbers from disease, malnutrition and brutal conditions: estimates commonly cited range from 15,000 to more than 20,000 deaths. The scandal prompted the writer André Gide's exposé Voyage au Congo (1927) and became a symbol of colonial exploitation. The line was inaugurated on 10 July 1934.

Route and operations

From Pointe-Noire the line climbs through the difficult Mayombe mountain range — the most demanding section — before crossing the Niari valley and the Batéké plateau to Brazzaville. It connects with the river transport network that serves the Congo basin, and with a branch to Mbinda near the Gabonese border (built for manganese traffic, linked to the COMILOG cableway). A realignment of the Mayombe section (the Bilinga deviation) opened in 1985.

Recent history

The railway was a lifeline during the Republic of Congo Civil War (1997–99), when fighting severed the line. It has suffered from chronic underinvestment, derailments and landslides, though it remains a vital freight artery for manganese, timber and containers between the port and the interior.
