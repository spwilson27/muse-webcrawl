Exposure therapy

Exposure therapy
TypeBehaviour therapy
UsesPhobias, anxiety disorders, PTSD, OCD

Exposure therapy is a technique in behaviour therapy used to treat anxiety disorders. It involves exposing the patient to the feared object or context without any danger, in order to overcome their anxiety and distress. Procedurally, it is similar to the fear extinction paradigm developed for studying the brain.

Overview

Numerous studies have demonstrated exposure therapy's effectiveness for disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), and specific phobias. The patient is confronted with anxiety-provoking stimuli in a safe, controlled environment, and with repetition the fear response diminishes through habituation and inhibitory learning.

Types

In vivo exposure directly confronts the feared object or situation in real life. Imaginal exposure asks the patient to vividly imagine the feared stimulus. Virtual reality exposure uses computer-generated environments, and interoceptive exposure deliberately induces feared bodily sensations, such as a racing heart, for conditions like panic disorder. Exposure is often arranged as a graded hierarchy from least to most feared, although flooding — beginning with the most feared stimulus — is also used.

Medical uses

Exposure and response prevention (ERP), a variant in which the patient also refrains from compulsive rituals, is a first-line treatment for OCD. Prolonged exposure therapy is widely used for PTSD. Exposure-based treatments are considered among the most empirically supported psychological interventions.

History

The roots of exposure therapy lie in early behaviourism and in Mary Cover Jones's 1924 work counterconditioning a child's fear of rabbits. Joseph Wolpe's systematic desensitisation, developed in the 1950s, combined relaxation with graded exposure; later practice generally found that exposure itself, without relaxation, was the active ingredient.
