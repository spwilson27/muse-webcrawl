Insulin resistance

Insulin resistance (IR) is a pathological condition in which cells in muscle, fat, and the liver respond poorly to the hormone insulin and cannot take up glucose from the blood as effectively as they should. As a result, the pancreas produces more insulin to achieve the same effect (compensatory hyperinsulinaemia). Over time, if pancreatic beta cells cannot keep up, blood glucose rises, leading to prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. Insulin resistance is a central component of the metabolic syndrome and is associated with obesity, cardiovascular disease, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (fatty liver).

Mechanism

Insulin normally binds its receptor on cell surfaces, triggering a signalling cascade through IRS proteins and PI3K/Akt that moves the glucose transporter GLUT4 to the membrane in muscle and fat tissue and suppresses glucose production in the liver. In insulin resistance, this signalling is blunted. Contributing mechanisms include ectopic fat deposition in liver and muscle (lipotoxicity), chronic low-grade inflammation from adipose tissue, elevated free fatty acids, endoplasmic reticulum and oxidative stress, and genetic factors. Some insulin pathways remain sensitive while others fail — "selective" insulin resistance — which helps explain how hyperinsulinaemia can drive fat storage and sodium retention even as glucose uptake falters.

Causes and risk factors

The strongest risk factors are excess body weight, particularly visceral and ectopic fat, and sedentary behaviour. Other contributors include genetic predisposition and ethnicity, ageing, pregnancy (a physiological insulin-resistant state), sleep disorders, glucocorticoid and some antipsychotic medications, and rare genetic syndromes of severe insulin resistance such as lipodystrophies and insulin receptor mutations. Visible signs can include acanthosis nigricans, velvety darkened skin in body folds.

Measurement

The reference method is the hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp, which directly measures glucose disposal under fixed insulin infusion, but it is confined to research. In practice, surrogate indices from fasting blood tests are used, notably HOMA-IR (fasting insulin × fasting glucose), along with assessments of the wider metabolic syndrome profile: waist circumference, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and blood pressure.

Management

Weight loss and regular aerobic and resistance exercise substantially improve insulin sensitivity; exercise increases GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake even independently of weight change. Dietary patterns emphasizing whole foods and reduced refined carbohydrate are commonly recommended. Medications that improve sensitivity include metformin and thiazolidinediones (such as pioglitazone), and GLP-1 receptor agonists improve glycaemia and weight. Treating insulin resistance early can delay or prevent progression to type 2 diabetes, as shown by the Diabetes Prevention Program.
