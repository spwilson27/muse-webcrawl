Qajaq

Qajaq (Greenlandic; also spelled qayaq; Inuktitut: ᖃᔭᖅ) is the word from which the English word "kayak" derives. It denotes the traditional skin-on-frame watercraft of the Inuit and other Indigenous peoples of the Arctic coasts of Greenland, Canada, Alaska, and Siberia, used for hunting and transport for thousands of years.

Construction

A traditional qajaq is built to the measure of its owner's body: a light frame of driftwood or bone is lashed together (without nails) and covered with sealskin, the seams waterproofed with seal oil and fat. The deck has a small cockpit in which the paddler sits, sealed in with a spray skirt (tuilik) so that the craft and paddler can roll upright together after a capsize — the origin of the kayak roll. Regional designs varied widely: long, narrow Greenland hunting qajaqs, broader Canadian forms, and the large open umiaks used for carrying families and goods.

Revival

Colonial-era disruption nearly extinguished Greenlandic qajaq culture, but from the 1980s a revival — led by organisations such as Qaannat Kattuffiat (the Greenland Kayak Association, founded 1985) — re-established traditional building and rolling skills. Greenland's national kayaking championships include dozens of rolling manoeuvres, and the qajaq remains a symbol of Greenlandic identity.
