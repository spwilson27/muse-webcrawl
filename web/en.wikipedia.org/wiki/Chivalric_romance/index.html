Chivalric romance

Knights in a medieval romance manuscript

A chivalric romance is a type of prose and verse narrative that was popular in the noble courts of high medieval and early modern Europe. In the telling, they are tales of marvellous adventures, usually of a knight-errant with heroic qualities, who undertakes a quest. Romances emphasize love and courtly manners, distinguishing them from the older chansons de geste, which focus on heroic deeds in battle.

Development

The genre emerged in 12th-century France, drawing on classical and Celtic sources. Chrétien de Troyes (fl. c. 1160–1191) is the most celebrated early practitioner, writing Arthurian verse romances such as Lancelot, the Knight of the Cart and Perceval, the Story of the Grail. The corpus is often grouped into "matters": the Matter of Britain (Arthurian legend), the Matter of France (Charlemagne and his paladins) and the Matter of Rome (classical antiquity).

Romances spread across Europe — in England (Middle English romances), Germany (Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival), Spain and Italy. In the late medieval period long prose cycles such as the Lancelot-Grail (Vulgate Cycle) compiled the Arthurian world. Print-era Spanish romances such as Amadís de Gaula (1508) were immensely popular — and are parodied in Cervantes's Don Quixote (1605), which both satirizes and mourns the genre.

Features and legacy

Characteristic motifs include quests, enchantments, disguises, tournaments, supernatural adversaries, and love tested by separation; the genre's ideals of chivalry, courtesy and honour shaped European ideals of conduct for centuries and were revived in the Romantic and Pre-Raphaelite movements and in modern fantasy literature.
