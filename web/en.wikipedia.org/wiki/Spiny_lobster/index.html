Spiny lobster

FamilyPalinuridae
Genera12 genera, about 60 species, including Panulirus, Palinurus and Jasus
HabitatWarm oceans worldwide, rocky reefs and crevices

Spiny lobsters, also known as rock lobsters, are a family (Palinuridae) of about 60 species of achelate crustaceans, in the Decapoda. Spiny lobsters are found in almost all warm seas, including the Caribbean and the Mediterranean Sea, but are particularly common in Australasia, where they are referred to commonly as crayfish, sea crayfish or kōura.

Unlike true lobsters, spiny lobsters lack large claws (chelae); instead they are named for the very long, thick, spiny antennae that project forward from the head, often longer than the body itself. The carapace is covered in forward-pointing spines, and the animals defend themselves in part by whipping the antennae and by producing a rasping sound—stridulation—by rubbing a file-like structure at the base of the antennae against a smooth "plectrum" on the head.

Life cycle and ecology

Spiny lobsters live in crevices and dens on rocky reefs, emerging at night to feed on snails, crabs, sea urchins, clams and carrion. Females carry large masses of bright orange eggs under the tail; the larvae pass through a long-lived, leaf-like planktonic stage called a phyllosoma before settling to the bottom. Many species undertake mass migrations, marching in lines across the seabed.

Fisheries

Spiny lobsters support valuable commercial fisheries worldwide, including the Caribbean spiny lobster (Panulirus argus), the western rock lobster of Australia (Panulirus cygnus) and the red rock lobster of New Zealand (Jasus edwardsii). They are caught mainly in baited traps (pots) and are marketed live or as frozen tails.
