Theodor Herzl (2 May 1860 – 3 July 1904) was an Austro-Hungarian Jewish journalist, lawyer, and playwright who founded modern political Zionism. His pamphlet Der Judenstaat ("The Jewish State", 1896) argued that the Jewish question was a political problem requiring a sovereign Jewish state recognized by international law, and he organized the First Zionist Congress at Basel in 1897, which founded the World Zionist Organization. Herzl is regarded in Israel as the visionary of the state and is pictured in its Declaration of Independence.

Life

Herzl was born in Pest (now Budapest) to a secular, German-speaking Jewish family and moved to Vienna, where he studied law and became a feuilleton editor of the Neue Freie Presse. As the paper's Paris correspondent (1891–1895), he covered the Dreyfus affair, whose antisemitic mobs chanting "Death to the Jews" helped convince him that assimilation could not solve antisemitism. In Der Judenstaat he proposed a chartered Jewish state, in Palestine or elsewhere; his utopian novel Altneuland (1902) imagined that state with the motto "If you will it, it is no dream."

Herzl spent his remaining years in relentless diplomacy — meeting the Ottoman Sultan Abdul Hamid II, the German Kaiser Wilhelm II, and British officials, who in 1903 offered a Jewish territory in East Africa (the "Uganda Scheme"), which split the Zionist movement. Exhausted and ill, he died of heart failure in 1904, aged 44. In 1949 his remains were reinterred on Mount Herzl in Jerusalem.
