Second strike

In nuclear strategy, a second strike is a retaliatory nuclear attack launched after a country has already absorbed a first strike by an adversary. A secure second-strike capability — the assured ability to respond with devastating force even after the worst plausible enemy attack — is the foundation of mutual assured destruction (MAD) and of deterrence: if no first strike can disarm the victim, no rational attacker can gain by striking first.

Requirements

A credible second strike requires weapons that can survive an attack and a command-and-control system that can order their use afterwards. Survivability has been pursued by hardening missile silos, dispersing mobile launchers, keeping bombers on alert, and above all by deploying nuclear-powered ballistic-missile submarines (SSBNs) on concealed patrol, which are considered the most survivable leg of the nuclear triad. Retaliation doctrines range from countervalue targeting of cities to counterforce targeting of the enemy's remaining military forces, and some states have developed "launch on warning" postures to fire before incoming warheads arrive, at the cost of accident risk.

Stability implications

Strategists distinguish stable from unstable arsenals. Weapons that are accurate, MIRVed, and vulnerable — able to destroy many enemy warheads but easy to destroy — create "use them or lose them" pressures in a crisis and are considered destabilising; survivable, less accurate systems designed purely for retaliation are considered stabilising. Arms-control agreements from SALT onward were shaped by this logic. The concept also structures the declared policies of newer nuclear states: India and China have adopted no-first-use policies explicitly premised on assured second-strike forces, while Pakistan, lacking strategic depth, relies on a first-use threat to deter conventional attack.

History

The term entered strategic vocabulary during the Cold War debates of the late 1950s and 1960s, in the work of analysts such as Bernard Brodie, Albert Wohlstetter (whose 1959 essay "The Delicate Balance of Terror" warned of the vulnerability of retaliatory forces), and Herman Kahn. The constant patrols of the U.S. Polaris submarine fleet from 1960 onward were the first embodiment of a genuinely invulnerable second-strike force, a role since inherited by the Ohio- and Columbia-class submarines and their equivalents in other navies.
