Dinghy sailing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dinghy sailing is the activity of racing or cruising small sailing boats known as dinghies. A sailing dinghy is typically between about 2 and 6 metres in length, has a centreboard or daggerboard rather than a fixed ballast keel, and relies primarily on the weight of the crew to provide stability. Dinghy sailing is one of the most popular forms of the sport of sailing and the usual entry point for beginners, including most Olympic sailing classes in recent history.

Characteristics

Because dinghies lack a heavy keel, they are light, responsive, and can be launched from beaches and slipways and transported on road trailers. Their responsiveness makes them excellent training boats, but they can capsize, and crews learn to right the boat and recover. To keep the boat upright in stronger winds, crews "hike out" — sitting out on the windward side — and on high-performance classes one or both sailors use a trapeze wire from the mast.

Many dinghies are built to strict one-design class rules so that competition depends on crew skill rather than equipment. Famous classes include the Optimist (the standard junior trainer), the Laser (ILCA), the 420 and 470, the Mirror, the Enterprise, the Flying Dutchman, and high-performance skiff types such as the 49er.

Racing

Dinghy races are sailed on short courses, usually windward-leeward or triangular, in fleets. Scoring in regattas typically uses a low-point system, and larger events may qualify sailors through a series of heats. Dinghy classes have featured in the Olympic Games since 1900, and dinghy racing is organised worldwide by national authorities affiliated with World Sailing.
