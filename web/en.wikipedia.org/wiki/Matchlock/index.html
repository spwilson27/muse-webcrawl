Matchlock

The matchlock was the first mechanism invented to facilitate the firing of a hand-held firearm. Before its development in the 15th century, handguns were fired by manually applying a smouldering match or hot wire to the touch hole. The matchlock mechanism holds the burning slow match in a movable arm — the serpentine — so that a pull of a lever or trigger dips the match into the priming pan, leaving both hands free to aim.

Mechanism

The slow match is a length of cord, typically made from hemp or flax treated with saltpetre so that it burns very slowly. The cord is clamped in the jaws of the S-shaped serpentine. A pan beside the barrel holds priming powder, covered by a sliding pan cover to keep it dry. When the shooter presses the trigger lever (in early designs a bar beneath the stock; later a true trigger), the serpentine pivots down and plunges the glowing end of the match into the pan, igniting the priming and, through the touch hole, the main charge.

History

Matchlock mechanisms appeared in Europe by the early to mid-15th century and became standard on arquebuses and later muskets. The system was simple and cheap but had serious drawbacks: the match burned continuously, consuming cord; rain could extinguish it; and its glow and smell betrayed soldiers at night. More sophisticated wheellock and flintlock mechanisms replaced the matchlock in Europe during the 16th and 17th centuries, though matchlocks remained in use in Japan — where the design arrived with Portuguese traders in 1543 as the tanegashima — well into the 19th century.
