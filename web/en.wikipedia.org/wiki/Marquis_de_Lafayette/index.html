Marie-Joseph Paul Yves Roch Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de La Fayette (6 September 1757 – 20 May 1834), known in the United States as Lafayette, was a French nobleman and military officer who volunteered to join the Continental Army, led by General George Washington, in the American Revolutionary War. Lafayette was ultimately permitted to command Continental Army troops in the decisive Siege of Yorktown in 1781, the Revolutionary War's final major battle, which secured American independence. After returning to France, Lafayette became a key figure in the French Revolution of 1789 and the July Revolution of 1830 and continues to be celebrated as the "Hero of Two Worlds".

American Revolution

Born into France's landed nobility, Lafayette commissioned as an officer at age 13 but grew determined to fight for American independence after hearing accounts of the struggle. Although the French king initially forbade officers from going, Lafayette purchased his own ship and sailed in 1777. Congress commissioned him a major general; at Brandywine he was wounded rallying retreating troops, and Washington came to trust him deeply. He fought at Gloucester, spent the winter at Valley Forge, helped expose the Conway Cabal, served in the Rhode Island campaign, and in 1781 skillfully shadowed Lord Cornwallis in Virginia before the Franco-American victory at Yorktown.

French Revolution and later life

In 1789 Lafayette helped draft the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen and commanded the Paris National Guard, seeking a constitutional monarchy. The radical turn of the Revolution drove him into exile and five years of imprisonment in Prussia and Austria. Released in 1797, he later opposed Napoleon's dictatorship, sat in the Chamber of Deputies under the Restoration, and in the July Revolution of 1830 again commanded the National Guard and presented Louis-Philippe as a popular king. His triumphal farewell tour of the United States in 1824–25, when he visited all 24 states, cemented his fame; he died in Paris in 1834 and was buried under soil from Bunker Hill. In 2002 he was made an honorary citizen of the United States, one of only eight people so honoured.
