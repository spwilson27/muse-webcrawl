Bingham plastic

A Bingham plastic is a viscoplastic material that behaves as a rigid body at low stresses but flows as a viscous fluid at high stress. It is named after Eugene C. Bingham (1878–1945), who proposed its mathematical form in 1916, building on observations of paints and slurries. Bingham plastics are a widely used idealization in rheology for materials with a yield stress.

Description

A Bingham plastic does not flow until the applied shear stress exceeds a threshold, the yield stress τ0. Below that stress the material deforms only elastically (or not at all). Above it, the shear stress increases linearly with the shear rate, in the manner of a Newtonian fluid with a "plastic viscosity" μp:

τ = τ0 + μp·γ̇   for τ > τ0;    γ̇ = 0  for τ < τ0

Common everyday examples include toothpaste, mayonnaise, ketchup, mustard, peanut butter, and certain foams and slurries: they hold their shape on a surface but flow when squeezed or spread. Drilling muds are engineered as Bingham plastics so that they suspend rock cuttings when circulation stops but flow when pumped.

Applications

The Bingham model is used in the design of pipelines and processing equipment for slurries, concrete, sewage sludge, and food products; in petroleum engineering to model drilling fluids; and in geophysics to model mudflows and lava. In pipe flow, a Bingham plastic exhibits a characteristic unsheared "plug" region near the center of the pipe where the local shear stress is below the yield stress.

Extensions

The Herschel–Bulkley model generalizes the Bingham plastic by allowing the post-yield behavior to be shear-thinning or shear-thickening, replacing the linear term with a power law. Real materials usually show more gradual yielding than the idealized Bingham model predicts, and their apparent yield stress can depend on measurement conditions — a topic of ongoing discussion in rheology.
