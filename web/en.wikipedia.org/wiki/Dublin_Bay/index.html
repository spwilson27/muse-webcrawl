Dublin Bay

Dublin Bay (Irish: Cuan Bhaile Átha Cliath) is a C-shaped inlet of the Irish Sea on the east coast of Ireland. The bay is about 10 kilometres wide across its north–south base and about 7 kilometres long from its base at the mouth of the River Liffey to its extremities at Dalkey Head in the south and Howth Head in the north. The city of Dublin stands at its western edge, and the bay is ringed by the suburbs and towns of the greater Dublin area.

Geography

Several rivers enter the bay, chiefly the Liffey, the Tolka, and the Santry River. The bay is shallow and was historically notorious for sandbanks and shipwrecks; the North Bull and South Bull banks flank the Liffey channel. The construction of the Great South Wall (completed in the 18th century, then one of the longest sea walls in the world) and the Bull Wall in the 19th century used the scouring action of the ebb tide to deepen the channel, and in doing so created Bull Island, an artificial island now a UNESCO biosphere reserve, with the beach of Dollymount Strand. North Bull Island and the bay's mudflats and saltmarshes are important habitats for overwintering wading birds and are protected under Irish and EU designations.

Features and surroundings

Landmarks around the bay include the Poolbeg chimneys of the former power station, the Pigeon House, the lighthouses at the end of the Great South Wall and at Howth, Dún Laoghaire harbour with its two long piers, and the Martello towers built during the Napoleonic era — one of which, at Sandycove, is the setting for the opening of James Joyce's Ulysses and now houses a Joyce museum. The bay is a popular venue for sailing, with yacht clubs at Dún Laoghaire, Howth, and Clontarf, and for swimming at spots such as the Forty Foot.

History

The bay gave sheltered — if shallow — anchorage to the Viking settlement of Dyflin and later to the port of Dublin. It has seen numerous shipwrecks, among them the RMS Leinster, torpedoed in 1918 with the loss of over 500 lives, the worst disaster in the Irish Sea. Ferries and freight traffic for Dublin Port cross the bay daily.
