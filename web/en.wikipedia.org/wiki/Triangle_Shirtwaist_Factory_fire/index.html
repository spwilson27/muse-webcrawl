Date25 March 1911
LocationAsch Building, Manhattan, New York City

The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in the Greenwich Village neighborhood of Manhattan, New York City, on 25 March 1911, was the deadliest industrial disaster in the history of the city, and one of the deadliest in United States history. The fire caused the deaths of 146 garment workers — 123 women and girls and 23 men — who died from the fire, smoke inhalation, falling, or jumping to their deaths. Most of the victims were recent Italian or Jewish immigrants aged 14 to 23.

The fire

The Triangle Waist Company factory occupied the 8th, 9th, and 10th floors of the Asch Building (now the Brown Building) at 23–29 Washington Place. The fire broke out shortly before closing time on a Saturday afternoon, probably from a discarded match or cigarette in a bin of fabric scraps. Workers on the 8th floor were alerted, but the warning never reached the 9th floor in time. Doors to the stairwells had been locked — a common practice to prevent theft and unauthorized breaks — and the single fire escape collapsed under the weight of fleeing workers. Elevator operators made as many trips as they could before the cars stopped working. Dozens of workers jumped from the windows to escape the flames.

Trial and aftermath

Factory owners Max Blanck and Isaac Harris were tried for manslaughter and acquitted, though they later lost a civil suit and paid compensation of $75 per deceased victim. The disaster led to sweeping reforms: New York's Factory Investigating Commission, influenced by future Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins, who witnessed the fire, produced dozens of new workplace safety laws addressing fire exits, sprinklers, alarms, and working conditions. The fire also galvanized the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union and remains a landmark in labor history, commemorated annually at the site.
