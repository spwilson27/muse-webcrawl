East European Craton

The East European Craton (EEC) is the core of the Baltica proto-plate and consists of three crustal segments — Fennoscandia (including the Baltic Shield and the Ukrainian Shield), Sarmatia and Volgo-Uralia — that were fused together by the early Proterozoic, between about 2.2 and 1.8 billion years ago. It underlies much of Eastern Europe and western Russia and forms the basement of the vast East European Platform, where it is covered by relatively undisturbed sedimentary rocks.

Extent

The craton is bounded to the northwest by the Caledonides of Scandinavia, to the east by the Ural Mountains, to the south by the Alpine-Carpathian belt, and to the west by the Trans-European Suture Zone, which runs across Poland and separates the ancient craton from the younger crust of Western Europe. Within the craton, the Precambrian basement is exposed in the Baltic Shield of Scandinavia and Karelia and in the Ukrainian Shield, while elsewhere it lies beneath sedimentary cover up to several kilometres thick.

Geology

The craton preserves some of the oldest crust in Europe, including Archaean rocks more than 3 billion years old in Karelia and the Kola Peninsula. Its cold, thick lithosphere — in places over 200 km thick — has remained largely stable since its assembly, accumulating sedimentary basins that host important reserves of oil, gas and minerals. The Voronezh Massif and the Kursk Magnetic Anomaly, source of enormous iron ore deposits, lie within the Sarmatian segment.
