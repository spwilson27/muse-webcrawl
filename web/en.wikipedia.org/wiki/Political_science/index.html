Political science

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Political science is the scientific study of politics — the systematic study of the state, government and power: how political behaviour, institutions and processes work, and how they ought to work. Its classical roots run from Plato and Aristotle (whose Politics surveyed constitutions) through Machiavelli, Hobbes, Locke, Montesquieu, Tocqueville and Marx, to its professionalisation as a university discipline in the late 19th and 20th centuries.

Subfields

The discipline is conventionally divided into political theory (normative and historical study of concepts like justice, liberty and democracy), comparative politics (comparison of political systems across countries), international relations, public administration and public policy, and (in the US) American politics — with political methodology (statistics, experiments, game theory, qualitative methods) cutting across. The mid-20th-century behavioural revolution displaced institutional-legal description with empirical study of behaviour; rational-choice, institutionalist (historical, sociological, discursive) and interpretive schools contend today, alongside growing fields — political economy, gender and politics, environmental politics and the study of democratic backsliding.

Methods and relevance

Political scientists run surveys and field experiments, build cross-national datasets (Polity, V-Dem's democracy indices, election archives), model strategic interaction formally, and do archival and ethnographic work. Their findings inform electoral-system design, constitution-making, development policy and public debate, and the discipline periodically debates its public relevance and internal pluralism.
