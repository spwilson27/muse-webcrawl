Strait of Otranto


The Strait of Otranto connects the Adriatic Sea with the Ionian Sea.

The Strait of Otranto (Albanian: Kanali i Otrantos; Italian: Canale d'Otranto) connects the Adriatic Sea with the Ionian Sea and separates Italy from Albania. At Punta Palascìa, near Otranto, it is less than 72 km (45 miles) across. Its name derives from the Italian city of Otranto.

History

Since ancient times the Strait of Otranto was of vital strategic importance. The Romans used it to transport their troops eastward; legions marched to Brundisium (now Brindisi), had a day's sail across to modern Albania, and could then move eastward following the Via Egnatia.

World War I

During World War I, the strait was of strategic significance. The Allied navies of Italy, France, and Great Britain, by blockading the strait with the Otranto Barrage, mostly light naval forces and lightly armed fishing vessels known as drifters, hindered the Austro-Hungarian Navy from freely entering the Mediterranean Sea, keeping it effectively bottled up in the Adriatic. The barrage was attacked in May 1917 in the Battle of the Strait of Otranto.

Cold War and after

In 1992, Albania and Italy signed a treaty that delimited the continental shelf boundary between the two countries in the strait. In 1997 the strait was the scene of a tragedy when the Albanian ship Kateri i Rades, crowded with refugees fleeing unrest, collided with the Italian naval vessel Sibilla, with the loss of many lives.
