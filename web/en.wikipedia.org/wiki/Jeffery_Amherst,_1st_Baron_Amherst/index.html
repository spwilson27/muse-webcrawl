Field Marshal Jeffery Amherst, 1st Baron Amherst, KB (29 January 1717 – 3 August 1797) was a British Army officer and Commander-in-Chief of the Forces in the British Army. Amherst is best known as the architect of Britain's successful campaign to conquer the territory of New France during the Seven Years' War. Under his command, British forces captured the cities of Louisbourg, Quebec City and Montreal, as well as several major fortresses. He was also the first British Governor General in the territories that eventually became Canada. Numerous places and streets in Canada and the United States are named for him.

Amherst's legacy is controversial among historians because of his expressed desire to spread smallpox among Native American tribes during Pontiac's War, an episode in which blankets possibly exposed to the disease were distributed at Fort Pitt. The town of Amherst, Massachusetts, and Amherst College are named after him, a fact that has prompted modern debate, and in 2016 Amherst College discontinued the use of its unofficial mascot, the "Lord Jeff".

Early life

The son of lawyer Jeffrey Amherst and Elizabeth Amherst (née Kerrill), Jeffery Amherst was born in Sevenoaks, Kent, on 29 January 1717. At an early age he became a page to the Duke of Dorset. Amherst became an ensign in the Grenadier Guards in 1735. He served during the War of the Austrian Succession, taking part in the Battle of Dettingen in June 1743 and the Battle of Fontenoy in May 1745. Promoted to lieutenant colonel on 25 December 1745, he also saw action at the Battle of Rocoux in October 1746.

Seven Years' War

In February 1756, Amherst was appointed commissary to the Hessian forces assembled to defend Hanover as part of the Army of Observation. As the French seemed to threaten an invasion of Britain itself, he was ordered to move thousands of German soldiers to Britain in April 1756, but after the threat passed he helped move them back to Hanover the following year.

Louisbourg

In 1758 Amherst was given command of an expedition to capture the French Fortress of Louisbourg on Cape Breton Island, Nova Scotia. With naval support from Admiral Edward Boscawen, his forces landed at Gabarus Bay on 8 June 1758 and, after a siege of several weeks, the French garrison surrendered on 26 July. The victory opened the St. Lawrence River to a British advance on Quebec.

Quebec and Montreal

Following the capture of Quebec City by James Wolfe in 1759, Amherst, now commander-in-chief in North America, coordinated a three-pronged advance on Montreal in 1760. On 8 September 1760 the French governor, the Marquis de Vaudreuil, signed the Articles of Capitulation of Montreal, surrendering the whole of Canada to Britain.

Pontiac's War and the smallpox letters

In 1763 a confederation of Native American tribes under leaders including Pontiac rose against British occupation of the Great Lakes region. During the siege of Fort Pitt, Amherst wrote to Colonel Henry Bouquet asking: "Could it not be contrived to send the Small Pox among those disaffected tribes of Indians? We must on this occasion use every stratagem in our power to reduce them." Blankets from the fort's smallpox hospital were given to envoys of the besieging tribes. The effectiveness of the attempt is debated by historians, but the correspondence has made Amherst a symbol of early biological warfare.

Later life

Amherst returned to Britain in 1763 and was raised to the peerage as Baron Amherst in 1776. He served twice as Commander-in-Chief of the Forces (1778–1782 and 1793–1795) and was promoted to field marshal in July 1796. He died at his home, Montreal Park, in Sevenoaks on 3 August 1797.
