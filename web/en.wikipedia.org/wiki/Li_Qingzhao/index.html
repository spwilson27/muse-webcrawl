Li Qingzhao

Li Qingzhao (Chinese: 李清照; pinyin: Lǐ Qīngzhào; 1084 – c. 1155), courtesy style Yi'an Jushi (易安居士, "the Householder of Yi'an"), was a Chinese poet and essayist of the Song dynasty. She is widely regarded as the greatest woman poet in Chinese history and one of the finest writers of ci (lyric poetry) in any era. Her surviving work — some fifty-odd ci poems, a smaller number of shi poems, and prose pieces including the celebrated postface to her husband's catalogue of inscriptions — charts a life transformed by the fall of northern China to the Jurchen Jin dynasty in 1127.

Life

Li Qingzhao was born into a family of scholar-officials in what is now Shandong. Her father, Li Gefei, was an essayist and associate of Su Shi; her mother was also from a scholarly family, and Li received an unusually good literary education for a woman of her time. In 1101 she married Zhao Mingcheng, a young scholar from a prominent official family. The couple shared a celebrated passion for collecting books, bronze vessels and stone inscriptions; her later account describes their modest but happy life of collecting, comparing finds, and drinking tea while testing each other's memory of passages — doubling the pleasure when the loser was predicted.

Their happiness ended with the Jurchen conquest of the north (the Jingkang Incident, 1127). The couple fled south, losing most of their collection in stages to war, theft and fire. Zhao died of illness in 1129 while travelling to take up an official post. Li spent her remaining decades as a refugee in the south, briefly and disastrously remarrying a man named Zhang Ruzhou in 1132 — she divorced him within months, winning a lawsuit at the cost of a brief imprisonment. She spent her later years teaching, writing and trying to preserve what remained of the collection; she presented some items to the court. She died around 1155 in obscurity.

Poetry

Li's early ci, written to inherited tunes, are delicate lyrics of love, longing and spring melancholy — famously the youthful "Like a Dream" (Ru meng ling), recalling a drunken boating excursion among lotuses. After 1127 her style deepened into grief at national collapse, widowhood and exile: her masterpiece "Slow, Slow Tune" (Sheng sheng man) opens with a celebrated cascade of seven doubled words — "Searching, searching, seeking, seeking; cold, cold, dreary, dreary; sad, sad, mournful, mournful, grieving, grieving" — and ends, "this sorrow — how could the single word 'sorrow' ever encompass it?" Her shi poems and prose, by contrast, are often politically pointed and patriotic. Her theoretical essay "On Ci" (Ci lun) criticised senior poets for metrical carelessness and argued that ci was a distinct art requiring musical precision — bold criticism from a woman in a male literary world.

Legacy

Much of Li's work was lost; her collected works were already incomplete in the Southern Song. A crater on Mercury and a crater on Venus are named in her honor. Memorial halls stand in Jinan (by the Baotu Spring) and in her ancestral home of Zhangqiu. English translations include those by Kenneth Rexroth and Ling Chung, and Ronald Egan's scholarly study The Burden of Female Talent (2013).
