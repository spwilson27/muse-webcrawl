Vulcanization (British English: vulcanisation) is a range of processes for hardening rubbers. The term originally referred exclusively to the treatment of natural rubber with sulfur, which remains the most common practice, but it has grown to include the hardening of other (synthetic) rubbers via various means. In vulcanization, sulfur forms cross-links (bridges) between the long chains of the rubber polymer, greatly improving its elasticity, strength, durability, and resistance to heat and chemicals.

History

Natural rubber, while elastic, is sticky in warm weather and brittle in cold, limiting its uses. The American inventor Charles Goodyear discovered in 1839 that heating rubber with sulfur produced a stable, resilient material; he received a US patent in 1844. In Britain, Thomas Hancock — who had examined samples of Goodyear's treated rubber — filed a patent in 1843, weeks before Goodyear's British filing. The process was named after Vulcan, the Roman god of fire. The discovery transformed rubber into an industrially vital material and laid the foundation of the modern tire industry.

Chemistry

In sulfur vulcanization, polysulfide cross-links form at reactive allylic sites along the polyisoprene chains. The reaction is typically accelerated with compounds such as mercaptobenzothiazole, activated by zinc oxide and stearic acid, and conducted at temperatures around 140–180 °C. The degree of cross-linking controls the product's properties: lightly cross-linked rubber is soft and elastic, while rubber treated with 30–40% sulfur becomes ebonite, a hard, rigid material once used for bowling balls and electrical insulation.

Other methods

  • Peroxide curing, used for rubbers lacking double bonds, such as silicone and ethylene-propylene rubber.
  • Metal oxide curing, used for chloroprene rubber (neoprene).
  • Radiation curing, using electron beams or gamma rays.
  • Resin curing, used to impart heat resistance to butyl rubber.

Devulcanization

Because vulcanized rubber is a thermoset, it cannot simply be melted and reshaped, complicating recycling. Devulcanization processes — thermal, mechanical, chemical, and ultrasonic — aim to break sulfur cross-links selectively so that scrap tires and factory waste can be reused.
