The phrase "51st state" refers to a hypothetical additional state of the United States of America, beyond the current fifty. It is used both for serious statehood movements in US territories and the District of Columbia, and colloquially — sometimes pejoratively — for countries or regions perceived as heavily influenced by the United States.

Statehood candidates

The two most prominent candidates are the District of Columbia, whose residents lack voting representation in Congress despite paying federal taxes and which has repeatedly passed statehood measures backed by local referendums, and Puerto Rico, a US territory whose voters have chosen statehood in several non-binding referendums (notably 2012, 2017 and 2020). Other proposals have included splitting existing states (such as Jefferson in northern California/southern Oregon, or dividing New York or Illinois), and admitting other territories such as Guam or the US Virgin Islands.

Process

Admission of new states is governed by the Admissions Clause of the US Constitution (Article IV, Section 3), which gives Congress the power to admit new states; no state may be formed from the territory of another without that state's consent. Alaska and Hawaii, admitted together in 1959, were the most recent additions.
