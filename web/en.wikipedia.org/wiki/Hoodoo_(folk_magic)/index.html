Hoodoo (folk magic)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conjure supplies used in hoodoo practice

Hoodoo is a set of spiritual practices, traditions, and beliefs that were created by enslaved African Americans in the Southern United States from various traditional African spiritualities and elements of indigenous North American botanical knowledge. Practitioners are often called rootworkers, conjure doctors, conjure men or women, root doctors, or two-headed doctors, and the practice itself is sometimes called rootwork, conjure, or root doctoring.

Hoodoo developed during the era of slavery and drew on West and Central African religions, adapted to the plants, animals, and conditions of North America. It later absorbed influences from European folklore, grimoires, and Native American herbal lore. Unlike Vodou or Santería, hoodoo is not a formal religion with a priesthood or pantheon; it is a body of folk magic centered on practical concerns such as healing, protection, love, money, and luck.

Practices

Common hoodoo workings include the preparation of mojo bags (also called mojo hands or gris-gris), small flannel bags containing roots, herbs, minerals, and curios believed to carry spiritual power; the use of condition oils, powders, and washes for dressing candles, anointing the body, or cleansing a home; candle burning with petitions placed beneath; and spiritual baths for uncrossing and purification. Practitioners also use the Bible, especially the Psalms, as a source of spells and prayers.

Divination methods include reading cards, casting bones or shells, and interpreting dreams and omens. Herbs and roots central to the materia medica include High John the Conqueror root, High John, Low John, five-finger grass, devil's shoestring, and lodestones fed with magnetic sand.

History

Hoodoo emerged in the plantation South as enslaved Africans preserved and transformed their spiritual traditions under conditions of oppression. After emancipation, rootwork spread through African American communities in the rural South and, during the Great Migration, to cities such as Chicago, Detroit, and Memphis. In the early twentieth century, mail-order companies and spiritual supply shops commercialized hoodoo products, and blues musicians recorded songs rich with hoodoo imagery, including references to mojo hands, black cat bones, and goofer dust.

Relationship to other traditions

Hoodoo is distinct from but often practiced alongside African American church traditions, and many practitioners identify as Christian. It shares some roots and vocabulary with Louisiana Voodoo but lacks that tradition's organized priesthood and veneration of the lwa. Scholars of African diaspora religion study hoodoo as a key example of cultural retention and creolization in the Americas.
