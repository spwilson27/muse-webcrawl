Rubber hand illusion

The classic rubber hand illusion setup

The rubber hand illusion is an illusion of body ownership in which a person comes to feel that a visible rubber hand is part of their own body. It is induced by simultaneously stroking the hidden real hand and the visible fake hand with brushes while the participant watches the fake hand.

The experiment

The illusion was reported by Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen in 1998. A participant's hand is hidden behind a screen while a lifelike rubber hand is placed in view. When both hands are stroked synchronously, most participants report within minutes that they feel the touch on the rubber hand and that it feels like their own hand. If the stroking is asynchronous, the illusion largely disappears. Measures such as "proprioceptive drift" — judging the real hand to be closer to the fake one — and skin conductance responses to threats against the rubber hand confirm the effect objectively.

Interpretation

The illusion shows that body ownership is constructed by the brain from the integration of visual, tactile and proprioceptive signals, and can be manipulated. Related paradigms include the full body illusion and the enfacement illusion. The paradigm has been used to study disorders of body perception, prosthesis acceptance and the neural basis of selfhood.
