The Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) extinction event, also known as the Cretaceous–Tertiary (K–T) extinction, was a sudden mass extinction of some three-quarters of the plant and animal species on Earth, approximately 66 million years ago. It marks the end of the Cretaceous Period and of the Mesozoic Era, and the beginning of the Cenozoic Era. With the exception of some ectothermic species such as sea turtles and crocodilians, no tetrapods weighing more than 25 kilograms survived. The event famously eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs.

Evidence

In 1980, a team led by Luis Alvarez and his son Walter Alvarez proposed that a ~10 km asteroid struck Earth at the boundary, based on a worldwide layer of sediment enriched in iridium — an element rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids. The hypothesis gained support from shocked quartz, impact spherules, and the eventual discovery of the buried Chicxulub crater, about 180 km wide, beneath Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, dated to the boundary.

Extinction mechanisms

The impact would have ejected vast quantities of dust and sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere, blocking sunlight, halting photosynthesis, and collapsing food chains, alongside wildfires, acid rain, tsunamis and a subsequent "impact winter" followed by warming. Marine plankton, ammonites, and large herbivorous dinosaurs dependent on abundant vegetation were devastated; detritus-based food webs, freshwater animals, burrowers and seed-eating birds fared better.

Role of volcanism

The massive Deccan Traps flood-basalt eruptions in India straddle the boundary, and their contribution — via climate warming and acidification before and around the impact — remains debated, though most researchers regard the impact as the primary cause.

Recovery

In the vacated ecosystems, mammals and birds radiated rapidly, and within a few million years biodiversity and body sizes rebounded, beginning the "Age of Mammals".
