Psychological stress

In psychology, stress is a feeling of emotional strain and pressure — the state produced when a person perceives that the demands placed on them exceed the resources they have to cope. Stress can arise from external events (workload, bereavement, conflict, financial difficulty) or from internal appraisal, and moderate stress may be motivating ("eustress"), while chronic stress is associated with anxiety, depression, cardiovascular disease and impaired immune function.

Models

Hans Selye introduced the physiological concept of stress with his general adaptation syndrome (alarm, resistance, exhaustion), describing the body's hormonal response, mediated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and the hormone cortisol. Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman's transactional model (1984) shifted the emphasis to cognition: stress arises from a person's appraisal that a situation is threatening and their secondary appraisal of their coping resources. Thomas Holmes and Richard Rahe's Social Readjustment Rating Scale (1967) attempted to quantify stress by "life-change units" for events such as divorce or job loss.

Coping and management

Coping strategies are broadly divided into problem-focused coping (acting on the stressor) and emotion-focused coping (regulating the emotional response), supplemented by social support. Techniques with empirical support include exercise, sleep hygiene, cognitive-behavioural therapy, mindfulness-based stress reduction, and organisational interventions such as reducing job strain. The assessment of demands relative to control — the demand-control model of Robert Karasek — is widely used in occupational health.
