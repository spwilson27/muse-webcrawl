Practical syllogism


Aristotle, originator of the practical syllogism

The practical syllogism is a form of reasoning about action discussed by Aristotle in his works on ethics and the movement of animals. Unlike the theoretical syllogism, whose conclusion is a proposition, the conclusion of a practical syllogism is an action.

Structure

A practical syllogism typically combines a major premise expressing a general desire or value ("dry food is good for every human"), a minor premise perceiving a particular as falling under it ("this is dry food"), and concludes immediately in acting. Aristotle uses the schema to explain how reason and desire jointly produce action, and how weakness of will (akrasia) can disrupt it.

Influence

The concept was taken up by Aquinas and, in the twentieth century, by philosophers of action such as G. E. M. Anscombe and Georg Henrik von Wright, who formalised practical inference.
