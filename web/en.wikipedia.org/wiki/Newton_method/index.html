Newton's method

TypeRoot-finding algorithm
Named afterIsaac Newton, Joseph Raphson
ConvergenceQuadratic (near a simple root)
Also known asNewton–Raphson method

In numerical analysis, Newton's method, also known as the Newton–Raphson method, is a root-finding algorithm that produces successively better approximations to the roots (zeroes) of a real-valued function. Starting from an initial guess, the method uses the tangent line to the graph of the function to jump to a closer approximation.

Description

Given a differentiable function f and an initial guess x0, the iterates are

xn+1 = xn − f(xn) / f′(xn)

Geometrically, each step takes the tangent to the curve at the current point and follows it to the x-axis. The idea goes back to Newton's work of 1669 (published posthumously) on polynomial equations and to Joseph Raphson's simplified version of 1690. A special case — approximating square roots by averaging a guess with the quotient — was already known to the Babylonians and to Heron of Alexandria.

Convergence and limitations

Near a simple root, and with a sufficiently good starting guess, the method converges quadratically: the number of correct digits roughly doubles at each step. It underlies practical computations such as division and square-root routines in computer arithmetic and the fitting procedures inside statistical software. However, the iteration can fail or misbehave when the derivative is near zero, when the initial guess is far from any root, or near multiple roots, where convergence degrades to linear; iterates may also cycle or diverge chaotically, behaviour visualized by Newton fractals. Extensions include methods for systems of equations (using the Jacobian matrix), quasi-Newton methods such as BFGS for optimization, and safeguarded variants that combine Newton's method with bisection.
