Techne


Techne: craft knowledge embodied in making

Techne (Ancient Greek: τέχνη, tékhnē, "craft, skill, art") is a term in Greek philosophy and rhetoric that refers to making or doing — productive knowledge, the kind of knowing involved in creating something. It is the root of the modern words technology and technique. Techne is most often contrasted with episteme, theoretical or scientific knowledge.

In Greek thought

For Plato, a techne was an accountable skill: the true craftsman could give a rational account (logos) of what he was doing and why, distinguishing genuine crafts such as medicine from mere knacks (empeiria) such as flattery or cookery, a contrast drawn sharply in the Gorgias. Aristotle, in Book VI of the Nicomachean Ethics, defined techne as a truth-attaining rational quality concerned with making — a productive disposition involving reason — and ranked it among the five intellectual virtues, alongside episteme, phronesis (practical wisdom), sophia, and nous.

The physician writers of the Hippocratic corpus used the term for the art of medicine, and the sophists applied it to rhetoric, the craft of persuasion, which could be taught for a fee — one reason Plato was suspicious of it.

Later usage

In rhetoric, techne came to denote a systematic handbook of the art, as in the rhetorical technai of the Hellenistic schools. Twentieth-century thinkers revived the concept: Martin Heidegger, in "The Question Concerning Technology", treated ancient techne as a form of poiesis, a "bringing-forth" of truth, contrasted with the challenging-forth of modern technology. Scholars of rhetoric and of science and technology studies continue to use the term to describe embodied, practical know-how as against abstract theory.
