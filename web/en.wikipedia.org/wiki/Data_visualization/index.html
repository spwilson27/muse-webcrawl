Data visualization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A wall of charts: bar charts, line graphs, maps and scatter plots]

Data and information visualization is the practice of representing data in graphical form — charts, graphs, maps, dashboards — to make patterns, trends, and outliers visible to human perception. It is a core step in data analysis and the public communication of statistics.

Modern statistical graphics began with William Playfair, who invented the line and bar chart (Commercial and Political Atlas, 1786) and the pie chart (1801). Famous exemplars include Florence Nightingale's polar-area diagrams of Crimean War mortality (1858) and Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 flow map of Napoleon's Russian campaign, often called the best statistical graphic ever made.

Practice

Edward Tufte's The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983) codified principles — maximise data-ink, avoid 'chartjunk', show comparisons — still taught today. Digital tools (from spreadsheets to D3.js and Tableau) made charts cheap and interactive, and also made misleading graphics easy: truncated axes, cherry-picked ranges, and 3D distortion are standard fallacies to check.

Visualization is also diagnostic: Anscombe's quartet (1973) — four datasets with identical summary statistics but wildly different plots — is the classic argument for always graphing data.
