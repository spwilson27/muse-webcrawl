Philosophical zombie

A philosophical zombie (or p-zombie) is a hypothetical being in the philosophy of mind that is physically and behaviourally identical to a normal human being but does not have conscious experience, qualia, or sentience. For example, a zombie could stub its toe and cry out exactly as a conscious person would, while feeling no pain at all.

The zombie argument

The concept is central to the zombie argument against physicalism, developed in detail by David Chalmers in The Conscious Mind (1996). The argument runs: (1) if zombies are conceivable, they are metaphysically possible; (2) if zombies are metaphysically possible, then conscious experience is not reducible to physical facts alone, since a zombie shares all our physical facts without consciousness; (3) therefore physicalism is false. Chalmers connects this to his distinction between the "easy problems" of consciousness and the "hard problem".

Responses

Physicalists dispute each step. Some, such as Daniel Dennett, argue that zombies are not genuinely conceivable — that anyone who imagines one merely imagines a being with subtler physical differences, and that a true functional duplicate would necessarily be conscious. Others accept conceivability but deny it implies metaphysical possibility. Still others, including phenomenal-concept theorists, hold that our intuitions about zombies arise from the special way we think about experience rather than from any non-physical fact.

Related concepts

Zombie arguments are closely related to Frank Jackson's knowledge argument (Mary's room) and to Thomas Nagel's question of what it is like to be a bat. Chalmers distinguishes p-zombies from behavioural zombies and functional zombies, and considers partial zombies, such as a being identical to a human except for one missing quale.
