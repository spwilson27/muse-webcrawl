Ethnic issues in China

Ethnic issues in China concern the treatment, status and relations of the country's officially recognised ethnic groups. The People's Republic of China officially recognises 56 ethnic groups (minzu), of which the Han Chinese form the overwhelming majority, accounting for roughly 91 percent of the population. The remaining 55 groups, collectively termed ethnic minorities (shaoshu minzu), number more than 125 million people and are concentrated disproportionately in China's border regions, including Xinjiang, Tibet, Inner Mongolia, Guangxi and Yunnan.

Ethnic policy in China has been shaped by the tension between the state's goals of national unity and territorial integrity and the constitutional promise of regional autonomy for minority areas. Critics, including foreign governments and human rights organisations, have raised concerns about religious and cultural restrictions in Tibet and Xinjiang, while the Chinese government maintains that its policies promote development, stability and the protection of minority cultures.

Classification

The classification of ethnic groups was carried out in the 1950s under the Ethnic Classification Project (minzu shibie), in which researchers, drawing on Stalinist criteria of common language, territory, economic life and culture, reduced more than 400 self-reported groups to 54 recognised groups; a 55th, the Lhoba, was added in 1965, and the Jino were recognised in 1979, bringing the total to 56. Some groups remain officially unrecognised and are classified under larger groups.

Autonomous regions

China has five provincial-level autonomous regions: the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, the Tibet Autonomous Region, the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region and the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region. Below these are autonomous prefectures and counties. In practice, the most senior government posts in these regions are frequently held by Han officials, and the regional people's congresses exercise limited independent authority.

Preferential policies

Ethnic minorities have historically benefited from preferential policies (youhui zhengce), including additional points on the national university entrance examination (gaokao) in many provinces, exemptions or relaxations under the former one-child policy, and targeted poverty-alleviation and development funding. Some of these preferences have been scaled back since the 2010s as part of a broader emphasis on national cohesion and a shared Chinese national identity.

Xinjiang and Tibet

The most internationally prominent ethnic issues concern the Uyghurs of Xinjiang and the Tibetans. Since 2017, reports of mass internment of Uyghurs and other Turkic Muslims in Xinjiang, together with allegations of forced labour, surveillance and restrictions on Islamic practice, have drawn condemnation from Western governments; the United Nations human rights office stated in 2022 that China's treatment of the Uyghurs may constitute crimes against humanity. China describes the facilities as vocational education and training centres and says its policies counter terrorism and separatism. In Tibet, critics point to restrictions on religious practice, the exile of the 14th Dalai Lama since 1959, and policies promoting Mandarin-medium education.

Language and education

Debates over bilingual education have been contentious. Reforms in Inner Mongolia in 2020 reducing Mongolian-medium instruction in favour of Mandarin sparked protests. Similar shifts toward Mandarin as the medium of instruction in Tibetan and Uyghur schools have been criticised as assimilationist, while officials argue they improve minority students' access to higher education and employment.
