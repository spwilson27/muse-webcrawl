The Battle of Crécy took place on 26 August 1346 near Crécy-en-Ponthieu in northern France. It was one of the most important engagements of the Hundred Years' War, in which an English army under King Edward III defeated a larger French force under King Philip VI.

Background

Edward III landed in Normandy in July 1346, marched toward Paris, then retreated north across the Somme. The French pursued, expecting to overwhelm the smaller English army. Edward chose a defensive position on a ridge near Crécy, dismounting his men-at-arms and placing archers equipped with the longbow on the flanks.

Battle

Late in the afternoon the French attacked piecemeal. Genoese crossbowmen advanced first but were outshot by English longbowmen and withdrew in disorder. French knights then charged repeatedly — perhaps fifteen times — uphill into storms of arrows and the prepared English line. King John of Bohemia was among the many French and allied nobles killed. Edward's son, Edward the Black Prince, commanded one division and distinguished himself in the fighting.

Aftermath

French losses were very heavy compared with English losses. Edward marched on to besiege Calais, which fell in 1347 and remained an English bridgehead in France for more than two centuries. Together with Poitiers (1356) and Agincourt (1415), Crécy demonstrated the effectiveness of dismounted men-at-arms combined with massed archery against mounted chivalry — though modern historians stress terrain, discipline and French command failures as much as the longbow itself.
