Alanine transaminase

Alanine transaminase (ALT)
EC number: 2.6.1.2
Also called: Alanine aminotransferase; serum glutamate-pyruvate transaminase (SGPT)
Reaction: L-alanine + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ pyruvate + L-glutamate
Clinical use: Liver function test

Alanine transaminase (ALT), also called alanine aminotransferase, is a transaminase enzyme (EC 2.6.1.2) that catalyses the reversible transfer of an amino group from L-alanine to α-ketoglutarate, forming pyruvate and L-glutamate. It requires the cofactor pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6). ALT is found in plasma and in various body tissues but is most concentrated in the liver.

Clinical significance

Measurement of serum ALT is a standard component of liver function tests. Because ALT is relatively specific to the liver, elevated levels usually indicate hepatocellular injury, as in viral hepatitis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, alcoholic liver disease, or drug-induced injury (for example from paracetamol overdose). ALT is typically interpreted together with aspartate transaminase (AST); their ratio can hint at alcoholic hepatitis, where AST usually exceeds ALT. Normal reference ranges are roughly 7–55 units per litre, varying by laboratory and sex.

Function

Physiologically, ALT is central to the glucose–alanine cycle, which transports amino groups from skeletal muscle to the liver for urea synthesis while returning glucose to muscle.
