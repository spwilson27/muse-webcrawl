Karma yoga

Karma yoga (Sanskrit: कर्म योग), also called Karma marga, is one of the four classical spiritual paths in Hinduism, one based on the "yoga of action", the others being Jnana yoga (path of knowledge), Raja yoga (path of meditation) and Bhakti yoga (path of loving devotion). To a karma yogi, right action is a form of prayer and a path of unselfish action pursued for the sake of others or in service of God, without attachment to the results.

Teachings

Karma yoga is most fully expounded in the Bhagavad Gita, where Krishna instructs the warrior Arjuna to perform his dharma (duty) without attachment to success or failure. The central principle is nishkama karma — selfless action performed without desire for the fruits of the action. According to this teaching, attachment to results binds the soul to the cycle of rebirth (samsara), while equanimity in action purifies the mind and leads towards liberation (moksha).

The Gita's famous verse (2.47) is commonly rendered: "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions." The karma yogi offers the results of work to the divine and acts as an instrument rather than as the egoistic doer.

Practice

In practice, karma yoga involves:

  • Performing one's duties honestly and diligently, as a form of worship.
  • Letting go of praise, blame, profit and loss as motivations.
  • Selfless service (seva) to society and to those in need.
  • Maintaining evenness of mind in success and failure.

Modern teachers such as Swami Vivekananda emphasised karma yoga as a path open to everyone regardless of occupation, presenting work itself — done without selfishness — as worship. Mahatma Gandhi likewise drew on the Gita's doctrine of selfless action in shaping his philosophy of service and non-violent action.

Relationship to other paths

Hindu traditions differ on the relationship among the yogas: some treat karma yoga as a preliminary discipline purifying the mind for knowledge or devotion, while others regard the paths as complementary and suited to different temperaments. In the Gita they are integrated: action, knowledge and devotion are presented as aspects of a single discipline directed toward the divine.
