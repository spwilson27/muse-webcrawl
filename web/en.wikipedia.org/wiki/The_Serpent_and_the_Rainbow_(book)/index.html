The Serpent and the Rainbow

The Serpent and the Rainbow is a 1985 non-fiction book by Canadian ethnobotanist and anthropologist Wade Davis. It recounts Davis's travels in Haiti investigating the zombie phenomenon — the claim that certain people could be poisoned, declared dead, buried, and later revived in a trance-like state to work as slaves.

Summary

Commissioned by pharmaceutical researchers interested in the anaesthetic properties of the zombie poison, Davis travelled to Haiti in 1982 to investigate the case of Clairvius Narcisse, a man who had been declared dead in 1962, buried, and who reappeared some 18 years later, claiming he had been dug up and forced to work on a sugar plantation. Davis hypothesised that the "zombification" process involved two powders: the first, containing tetrodotoxin (from puffer fish) and other toxins, induced a death-like coma; the second, containing dissociative plants such as Datura stramonium, kept the victim in a suggestible, trance-like state after revival. He also argued that the phenomenon was embedded in the social and religious structures of Haitian Vodou and the secret Bizango societies.

Reception and adaptation

The book became a bestseller and popularised ethnobotany for a general audience. Its scientific claims were controversial: other researchers disputed whether the samples Davis collected contained meaningful amounts of tetrodotoxin, and critics questioned the verifiability of the zombie accounts. The book was adapted into the 1988 horror film The Serpent and the Rainbow, directed by Wes Craven and starring Bill Pullman, which took considerable liberties with the source material, recasting it as a supernatural thriller.
