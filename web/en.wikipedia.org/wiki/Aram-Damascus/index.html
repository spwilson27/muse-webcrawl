Aram-Damascus (Imperial Aramaic: 𐡀𐡓𐡌) was an Aramean polity centred on the city of Damascus in present-day Syria during the Iron Age. It emerged in the late 12th century BC, following the Late Bronze Age collapse, and grew into the most powerful Aramean kingdom, dominating the southern Levant before its destruction by the Neo-Assyrian Empire in 732 BC.

History

The kingdom is known from the Hebrew Bible, Assyrian royal inscriptions, and Aramaic stelae such as the Tel Dan Stele. In the 10th and 9th centuries BC, kings such as Ben-Hadad I and Hazael made Aram-Damascus the leading power of the region. Hazael (c. 842–800 BC) extended its control over much of Syria, Israel, and Transjordan, repeatedly defeating the kingdoms of Israel and Judah.

In 853 BC Aram-Damascus joined a coalition of Levantine kings that fought the Assyrian king Shalmaneser III at the Battle of Qarqar. Despite Assyrian claims of victory, the coalition checked Assyrian expansion for a decade.

Decline and fall

In the 8th century BC the kingdom weakened under pressure from Assyria and internal loss of territory. King Rezin joined Pekah of Israel in the Syro-Ephraimite War against Judah (c. 734 BC), prompting Ahaz of Judah to appeal to Tiglath-Pileser III. Assyria besieged and captured Damascus in 732 BC, executed Rezin, deported part of the population, and turned the kingdom into Assyrian provinces — ending independent Aram-Damascus, though the Aramaic language went on to become the lingua franca of the Near East.

Religion and culture

The chief god was Hadad, the storm god, worshipped at the temple of Hadad-Ramman in Damascus, on or near the site of the later Roman temple of Jupiter and the present Umayyad Mosque. Kings also bore the throne name Ben-Hadad, "son of Hadad".
