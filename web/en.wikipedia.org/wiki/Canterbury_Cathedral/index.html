Canterbury Cathedral is one of the oldest and most famous Christian structures in England. Founded in 597 by St Augustine, sent by Pope Gregory I, it is the seat of the Archbishop of Canterbury, leader of the Church of England and the worldwide Anglican Communion.

History

The Romanesque cathedral built after the Norman Conquest was partly destroyed by fire in 1174; its rebuilding under the French master mason William of Sens produced some of the earliest English Gothic architecture, with pointed arches and ribbed vaults. The cathedral is inseparable from the murder in 1170 of Archbishop Thomas Becket by knights of King Henry II in the north-west transept — the Martyrdom. Becket's shrine in the Trinity Chapel became one of Christendom's greatest pilgrimage destinations, the goal of the pilgrims in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, until its destruction in 1538 under Henry VIII.

Architecture and features

Notable features include the Perpendicular nave, the central "Bell Harry" tower, outstanding medieval stained glass — among England's finest — and the tombs of Edward the Black Prince and King Henry IV. The site of Becket's shrine is now marked by a single candle. Together with St Augustine's Abbey and St Martin's Church, the cathedral forms a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
