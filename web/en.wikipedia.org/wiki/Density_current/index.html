Density current

A density current, also called a gravity current, is a flow within a fluid driven by differences in density, in which a denser fluid spreads beneath a less dense one under the influence of gravity. Density currents occur in the atmosphere, the oceans, and lakes, and range in scale from small laboratory flows to vast oceanic and atmospheric phenomena.

Occurrence

In the atmosphere, cold air flowing out from thunderstorms forms a gust front that behaves as a density current, as do sea breezes and katabatic winds draining cold air downhill. In the ocean, dense water formed by cooling or evaporation at high latitudes sinks and spreads along the seabed, driving thermohaline circulation. Turbidity currents — density currents laden with sediment — flow down submarine slopes and can carve submarine canyons and deposit turbidites on the deep-sea floor.

Structure

A density current characteristically has a raised head at its front, higher than the trailing body, in which strong mixing occurs. Lobes and clefts form along the leading edge. The speed of the front is commonly described by a Froude number relating the flow speed to the square root of the reduced gravity times the current depth.

Examples

  • Thunderstorm outflow boundaries and haboobs
  • Turbidity currents in reservoirs and the deep ocean
  • Salinity and temperature-driven underflows in lakes and estuaries
  • Powder-snow avalanches and pyroclastic flows (particle-laden density currents)
