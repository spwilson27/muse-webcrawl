Joseph Banks Rhine (September 29, 1895 – February 20, 1980), usually known as J. B. Rhine, was an American botanist who founded the field of parapsychology as a branch of psychology. He founded the Parapsychology Laboratory at Duke University, the Journal of Parapsychology, the Foundation for Research on the Nature of Man, and the Parapsychological Association.

Career

Rhine trained as a botanist at the University of Chicago, but after hearing a lecture by Arthur Conan Doyle on spiritualism, he turned to the experimental study of claimed psychic phenomena. In 1927 he joined Duke under psychologist William McDougall, who encouraged laboratory research into psychical phenomena.

Rhine coined the term extrasensory perception (ESP) and popularised the term "parapsychology". His best-known experiments used Zener cards — decks of 25 cards bearing five symbols (circle, cross, wavy lines, square, star) — to test subjects for clairvoyance and telepathy, most famously with subject Hubert Pearce.

Criticism and legacy

Rhine's findings were published in books including Extra-Sensory Perception (1934) and New Frontiers of the Mind (1937). His work attracted extensive criticism from mainstream scientists, who pointed to methodological flaws, inadequate controls against sensory leakage and cheating, and the failure of independent researchers to replicate his results. After retiring from Duke in 1965, Rhine continued his work at his own institute in Durham, North Carolina, today known as the Rhine Research Center. His laboratory studies remain the most famous attempt to bring psi phenomena under experimental psychology, and are regarded by skeptics as a classic case study in experimental error.
