Dry Falls is a 3.5-mile-long (5.6 km) scalloped precipice in central Washington state, in the heart of the Grand Coulee, that was once the site of one of the largest waterfalls known to have existed on Earth. During the last ice age, cataclysmic glacial floods carved the cliff, whose lip stands about 400 feet (122 m) high—roughly twice the height of Niagara Falls and several times its width. Today the falls are completely dry, with desert lakes (including Dry Falls Lake) lying in the plunge-pool basin below.

Geology

At the end of the last glacial period, roughly 13,000–15,000 years ago, the Cordilleran Ice Sheet repeatedly dammed the Clark Fork River in Montana, creating glacial Lake Missoula. Each time the ice dam failed, an immense volume of water—estimated peak flows many times the combined flow of all modern rivers—swept across the Columbia Plateau in the Missoula Floods. Where the floodwater poured into the Grand Coulee, it eroded headward through the Columbia River basalt, and Dry Falls formed as the receding cataract of that torrent; geologists estimate water about 300 feet deep passed over the lip at high speed. When the floods ended, the coulee was left dry.

The falls were a central piece of evidence in J Harlen Bretz's once-controversial "catastrophic flooding" theory of the Channeled Scablands in the 1920s; his ideas, resisted for decades by uniformitarian geologists, were vindicated and Dry Falls is now interpreted within that framework.

Sun Lakes–Dry Falls State Park

The site is protected within Sun Lakes–Dry Falls State Park, a 3,774-acre Washington state park near Coulee City offering camping, fishing lakes in the plunge basin, and an interpretive overlook from which the full scale of the amphitheater can be appreciated.
