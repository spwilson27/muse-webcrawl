Algebraic closure

[Image placeholder: diagram of a field and its algebraic closure]

In abstract algebra, an algebraic closure of a field K is an algebraic extension of K that is algebraically closed — that is, an extension field K̄ containing K such that every non-constant polynomial with coefficients in K̄ has a root in K̄.

Existence and uniqueness

Ernst Steinitz proved in 1910 that every field has an algebraic closure, and that the closure is unique up to isomorphism fixing K: any two algebraic closures of K are isomorphic as extensions of K (though not uniquely so). The proof uses Zorn's lemma, hence the axiom of choice. By the fundamental theorem of algebra, the complex numbers ℂ are an algebraic closure of the real numbers ℝ.

Examples

  • The field of algebraic numbers — the set of complex numbers that are roots of polynomials with rational coefficients — is the algebraic closure of the rational numbers ℚ. Unlike ℂ, it is countable.
  • Every finite field 𝔽q has an algebraic closure obtainable as a union of the finite fields 𝔽qn over all n; it is countably infinite.
  • The field of Puiseux series provides the algebraic closure of the field of formal Laurent series over ℂ.

Properties

The algebraic closure of K is the smallest algebraically closed field containing K, and every algebraic extension of K embeds into it. If K is infinite, its closure has the same cardinality as K; if K is finite, the closure is countably infinite. In model theory and algebraic geometry the closure is a fundamental construction: in Galois theory, the absolute Galois group of K is the automorphism group of its algebraic closure over K.
