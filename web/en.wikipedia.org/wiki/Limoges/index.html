Limoges

Limoges is a city and commune in south-central France, the capital of the Haute-Vienne department and of the former Limousin region, now part of Nouvelle-Aquitaine. The city lies on the banks of the River Vienne. Its population is around 130,000 within the commune and about 280,000 in the metropolitan area.

Limoges is world-renowned for its fine porcelain, produced since the late 18th century following the discovery of kaolin deposits at nearby Saint-Yrieix-la-Perche. The city is also famous for medieval and Renaissance enamels (Limoges enamel), oak barrels used in cognac production, and its historic troubadour culture.

History

Founded by the Romans as Augustoritum around 10 BC, the town was an important crossing point on the Vienne. It was evangelised by Saint Martial in the 3rd century, who became its first bishop. The town suffered in the Middle Ages but prospered through its abbeys and the enamel workshops of the 12th century. Richard the Lionheart was mortally wounded in 1199 at nearby Châlus and, by tradition, his entrails were buried in Limoges.

In the 19th century Limoges industrialised around porcelain, leather and printing. It became a stronghold of the French labour movement; the CGT trade union was founded there in 1895. The artist Pierre-Auguste Renoir was born in Limoges in 1841.

Geography

Limoges is located on the western foothills of the Massif Central, about 400 km south of Paris. The Vienne river flows through the city, joined by the Auzette and Valoine streams. The climate is oceanic with continental influences.

Main sights

The Saint-Étienne Cathedral, begun in 1273, is the city's principal Gothic monument. Other sights include the Bishop's Gardens, the old quarter of La Cité, the half-timbered Rue de la Boucherie with the Saint-Aurélien chapel, the Adrien Dubouché National Porcelain Museum, and the Limoges-Bénédictins railway station, an Art Deco landmark built in 1929.
