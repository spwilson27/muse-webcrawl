Salami

Salami is a type of cured sausage consisting of fermented and air-dried meat, typically pork. Historically, salami was popular among Southern, Eastern, and Central European peasants because it can be stored at room temperature for up to 45 days once cut, supplementing a potentially meagre fresh meat supply. Countries and regions across Europe make their own traditional varieties of salami.

Etymology

The word "salami" comes from the Italian salame, derived from sale ("salt") with the suffix -ame, denoting a collective noun; it entered English via French and is used as both singular and plural in Italian practice.

Production

A traditional salami is made from a mixture of minced meat — usually pork, though beef, venison, poultry, and horse are used in some regions — combined with salt, fat cut into small cubes, and seasonings such as garlic, wine, herbs, and spices. The mixture is stuffed into casings and fermented, typically with lactic acid bacteria, which lowers the pH and preserves the meat; the sausages are then slowly air-dried for weeks or months, losing moisture and developing flavour, and often acquire a coating of harmless white mould (Penicillium). The fat remaining distinct in the finished sausage gives salami its characteristic marbled appearance.

Varieties

Well-known varieties include Milanese and Felino salami from Italy, Hungarian winter salami (téliszalámi) with its smoked, mould-ripened profile, French saucisson sec, Spanish salchichón, and Genoa and hard salami as made in the United States. Pepperoni, the popular pizza topping, is a spicy American variety of salami.
