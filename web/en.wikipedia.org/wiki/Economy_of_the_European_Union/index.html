Economy of the European Union

[Image: the European Parliament and office towers in Brussels, seat of EU economic governance]

The economy of the European Union is one of the world's two or three largest, with GDP of roughly US$18–19 trillion (mid-2020s) across 27 member states and about 450 million people. Its core is the single market (completed in name by 1993): free movement of goods, services, capital and people, underwritten by common competition, state-aid and regulatory rules; twenty members share the euro and the European Central Bank.

Structure and challenges

The EU is an export powerhouse in machinery, vehicles, chemicals and pharmaceuticals, with Germany its largest economy; intra-EU trade dominates members' commerce. Common policies cover agriculture (the CAP, historically the largest budget item), regional cohesion funds, trade negotiations conducted collectively through the World Trade Organization and bilateral deals, and an increasingly interventionist industrial and digital policy. Weaknesses debated since the 2000s include slow productivity and technology growth relative to the US, fragmented capital markets, demographic ageing and the fiscal-monetary tensions exposed by the euro-area debt crisis (2010–12) and partly addressed by banking union and the post-COVID NextGenerationEU recovery fund — the Union's first large common borrowing.
