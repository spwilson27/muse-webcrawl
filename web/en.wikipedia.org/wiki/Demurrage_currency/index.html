Demurrage currency

Stamp scrip from Wörgl, Austria — the best-known demurrage-currency experiment

Demurrage currency is money that loses value over time — a form of carrying cost, or negative interest, designed to discourage hoarding and encourage spending and circulation. It is associated above all with the German-Argentine economist Silvio Gesell, who proposed it in his work The Natural Economic Order (1916) under the name Freigeld ("free money").

Theory

Gesell argued that ordinary money's storability gives holders an unfair advantage over sellers of perishable goods, generating interest and economic stagnation. A demurrage charge — for example a monthly stamp that must be purchased to keep a banknote valid — would make money decay like other goods, increase its velocity of circulation, and in theory eliminate interest and stimulate investment.

Experiments

The most famous experiment took place in Wörgl, Austria, in 1932–1933, where the mayor issued stamp scrip during the Great Depression; local reports credited it with reducing unemployment and funding public works before the Austrian central bank suppressed it. Stamp scrip was also issued in numerous American communities in the 1930s, and economist Irving Fisher promoted the idea. Modern echoes include local currencies and proposals for expiring digital cash.
