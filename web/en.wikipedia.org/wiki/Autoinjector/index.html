Autoinjector

An autoinjector (or auto-injector) is a medical device designed to deliver a dose of a particular drug. The injectors were initially designed to overcome the hesitation associated with self-administration of needle-based drugs. Most autoinjectors are one-shot, spring-loaded syringes. By design, autoinjectors are easy to use and are intended for self-administration by patients, or administration by untrained personnel.

The site of injection depends on the drug loaded, but typically is administered into the patient's thigh or buttocks. Autoinjectors may be used by pressing them against the site and pressing a button, or in some cases by swinging and jabbing. Autoinjectors are typically stocked by governmental and medical organisations in offices, stores, clinics, hospitals and the home.

Uses

The best-known autoinjector is the epinephrine autoinjector (such as EpiPen), used to treat acute allergic reactions (anaphylaxis). Others deliver:

  • Atropine and antidotes such as pralidoxime for nerve-agent and organophosphate poisoning — standard military issue (e.g. the Mark I NAAK kit).
  • Naloxone for opioid overdose (discontinued designs; many formulations now use nasal spray).
  • Sumatriptan for migraine attacks.
  • Biologic drugs for chronic self-injection, such as adalimumab for autoimmune diseases, and methotrexate.
  • Insulin — though pen injectors are more common, several autoinjector-like devices exist.

Design

An autoinjector conceals the needle before injection, drives it to a controlled depth by spring or gas pressure, delivers the drug, and usually shields the needle afterwards to prevent needlestick injury. Audible and tactile feedback (clicks) confirms that delivery is complete. Devices are drug-specific: the design and the relevant regulations typically tie a device to a particular drug and dose, and trainers without needles or medication are supplied for practice.
