The lateral line, also called the lateral line organ (LLO), is a system of sense organs found in fish and aquatic amphibians that detects movement, vibration, and pressure gradients in the surrounding water. It consists of a line of mechanoreceptive units called neuromasts, each containing hair cells similar to those of the vertebrate inner ear.

Structure

Neuromasts occur in two forms: superficial neuromasts scattered on the skin, and canal neuromasts housed within fluid-filled canals that run beneath the skin along the flanks and over the head. Pores connect the canals to the outside water. Each neuromast contains a cluster of hair cells whose cilia are embedded in a gelatinous cupula; displacement of the cupula by water flow bends the cilia and generates nerve signals, relayed to the brain via the lateral line nerves.

Function

The lateral line allows fish to detect nearby objects, orient to water currents (rheotaxis), avoid predators, and coordinate schooling behaviour. Predatory fish use it to track the hydrodynamic wake of prey, and some cave fish that lack functional eyes navigate almost entirely by their lateral line. The system is sensitive mainly to low-frequency stimuli, complementing hearing.

Evolution

The lateral line is an ancient feature of aquatic vertebrates, present in lampreys and all jawed fish lineages. It was lost in terrestrial vertebrates along with other aquatic sensory systems, though aquatic amphibians such as frogs in their larval stage and fully aquatic salamanders retain it.
