Spectrophotometry is a method to measure how much a chemical substance absorbs light by measuring the intensity of light as a beam passes through a sample solution. It is used in many disciplines, including chemistry, physics, biochemistry, materials science, and clinical laboratories, for the quantitative analysis of substances and the study of their optical properties.

Principles

The basic principle is that each compound absorbs or transmits light over a certain range of wavelengths. As light passes through the sample, some is absorbed; the detector compares the transmitted intensity I with the incident intensity I0 to give transmittance, and absorbance A = −log10(I/I0). According to the Beer–Lambert law, absorbance is proportional to the concentration of the absorbing species and the path length (A = εlc), allowing concentrations to be determined from a calibration curve.

Instrumentation

A spectrophotometer consists of a light source (tungsten lamp for visible light, deuterium lamp for ultraviolet), a monochromator (prism or diffraction grating) to select wavelengths, a sample holder (cuvette), and a detector (photodiode, photomultiplier, or CCD array). Instruments may be single-beam or double-beam; modern diode-array instruments measure an entire spectrum at once. Measurements are typically made in the ultraviolet and visible ranges (about 200–800 nm); near-infrared spectrophotometry extends to about 2,500 nm.

Applications

Common applications include determining concentrations of solutes, measuring nucleic acid and protein concentrations in molecular biology (e.g. absorbance at 260 and 280 nm), enzyme kinetics, water-quality testing, colour measurement, and pharmaceutical quality control.
