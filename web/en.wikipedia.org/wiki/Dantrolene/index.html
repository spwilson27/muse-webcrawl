Dantrolene

Dantrolene sodium is a postsynaptic muscle relaxant that lessens excitation–contraction coupling in skeletal muscle. It is the primary drug used in the treatment and prevention of malignant hyperthermia, a rare but life-threatening reaction to volatile anaesthetics and succinylcholine. Brand names include Dantrium, Ryanodex and Revonto.

Mechanism of action

Dantrolene acts directly on the ryanodine receptor RyR1 in the sarcoplasmic reticulum of skeletal muscle cells, inhibiting the release of calcium ions that triggers contraction. By reducing intracellular calcium availability it weakens muscle contraction without acting at the neuromuscular junction or on the central nervous system.

Medical uses

Intravenous dantrolene is the specific antidote for malignant hyperthermia and is stocked wherever general anaesthesia is given; its introduction reduced mortality from the condition from around 80% to under 5%. Orally, dantrolene is used for chronic spasticity arising from upper motor neuron disorders such as cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis and spinal cord injury, though concerns about liver toxicity have limited this use. It is also used off-label in neuroleptic malignant syndrome and certain drug-induced hyperthermias.

Adverse effects

The most serious adverse effect is hepatotoxicity, which can be fatal; liver function is monitored during chronic therapy and the drug carries boxed warnings in some jurisdictions. Other effects include muscle weakness, drowsiness, dizziness and gastrointestinal upset. Weakness is a particular concern in patients who depend on spasticity for posture or ambulation.

History

Dantrolene was synthesised by Snyder and colleagues in 1967 and characterised as a skeletal muscle relaxant; its efficacy in malignant hyperthermia was demonstrated in the 1970s, chiefly by Gaisford Harrison in swine experiments, and it became available clinically around 1979.
