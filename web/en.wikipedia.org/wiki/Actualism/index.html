Actualism

FieldMetaphysics, modal logic
Key figuresAlvin Plantinga, Robert Adams, Roderick Chisholm
Opposed toPossibilism; modal realism

In analytic philosophy, actualism is the view that everything there is — everything that exists, in the widest sense — is actual. It denies that there are merely possible objects: things that do not exist but could have existed, or possible worlds that are as real as the actual world. Actualism is opposed to possibilism, most famously David Lewis's modal realism, which holds that possible worlds and their inhabitants exist just as concretely as we do.

Theory

Actualists such as Alvin Plantinga and Robert Merrihew Adams accept talk of possible worlds but analyse it in terms of actual abstract objects: for Plantinga, a possible world is a maximal possible state of affairs, and all such states of affairs exist (actually) even though only one obtains. Similarly, an individual essence (haecceity) can exist without being exemplified; thus "Socrates might have had a son" is true in virtue of actually existing abstract objects rather than a merely possible child. Actualists typically also adopt serious actualism — the principle that an object can have properties only if it exists — a thesis named by Plantinga.

Debate

The dispute turns on the semantics of modal logic and quantifying into modal contexts. Possibilists charge that actualism cannot adequately model possibilities concerning objects that never actually exist; actualists reply that abstract surrogates suffice, and that their ontology is far more parsimonious and epistemically tractable than Lewis's plurality of concrete worlds.
