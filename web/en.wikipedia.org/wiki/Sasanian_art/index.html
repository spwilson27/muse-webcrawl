Sasanian art

Sasanian art is the art produced under the Sasanian Empire of Persia, which ruled from 224 to 651 CE. It combined Iranian traditions with influences from Rome, Byzantium, and Central Asia, and in turn strongly influenced early Islamic and medieval art.

Architecture

Sasanian architects developed the iwan — a vaulted hall open on one side — and monumental domes on squinches, exemplified by the palace at Firuzabad, the great iwan of Taq Kasra at Ctesiphon, and fire temples (chahar taq) with their characteristic four-arched domed structures housing the sacred Zoroastrian flame.

Other media

Rock reliefs at Naqsh-e Rostam, Naqsh-e Rajab, and Taq-e Bostan depict royal investitures and triumphs. Sasanian silver vessels — plates, bowls, and ewers decorated with royal hunts, court scenes, and Zoroastrian imagery — were exported widely and imitated as far away as China. The period also produced elaborate stucco decoration, rock-crystal and glass work, seals and coinage, and silk textiles whose motifs of confronted animals in roundels spread along the Silk Road.
