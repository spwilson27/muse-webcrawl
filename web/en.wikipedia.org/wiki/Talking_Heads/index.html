Talking Heads was an American rock band formed in New York City in 1975. Consisting of vocalist and guitarist David Byrne, drummer Chris Frantz, bassist Tina Weymouth and guitarist/keyboardist Jerry Harrison, the band was a pioneer of new wave music, integrating elements of punk, funk, art rock and world music. They are widely regarded as one of the most influential bands of their era.

History

Byrne, Frantz and Weymouth met at the Rhode Island School of Design and moved to New York in 1975, joining the scene around the club CBGB. Their debut album Talking Heads: 77 (1977) included the single "Psycho Killer". Working with producer Brian Eno, they released More Songs About Buildings and Food (1978), Fear of Music (1979) and Remain in Light (1980), the last drawing heavily on Afrobeat and featuring "Once in a Lifetime".

Speaking in Tongues (1983) produced the band's biggest US hit, "Burning Down the House", and the acclaimed concert film Stop Making Sense (1984), directed by Jonathan Demme. Later albums Little Creatures (1985), True Stories (1986) and Naked (1988) explored American pop and world rhythms. Byrne's increasing focus on solo projects strained the band, which formally split in 1991.

Legacy

Talking Heads were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2002, performing together for the occasion. Four of their albums appear on Rolling Stone's list of the 500 greatest albums of all time, and a 2023 restoration of Stop Making Sense introduced the band to a new generation. Members have reunited for promotional appearances but not for touring.
