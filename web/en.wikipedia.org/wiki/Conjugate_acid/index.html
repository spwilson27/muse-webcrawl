Conjugate acid

An acid and its conjugate base differ by a single proton.

A conjugate acid, within the Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory, is a chemical compound formed when a base accepts a proton (H+). Conversely, a conjugate base is what is left after an acid has donated a proton in a chemical reaction. Hence, a base reacts with an acid to form the acid's conjugate base and the base's conjugate acid.

The concept was introduced independently in 1923 by Johannes Nicolaus Brønsted and Thomas Martin Lowry. In the reaction

HA + B → A− + HB+

the species A− is the conjugate base of acid HA, and HB+ is the conjugate acid of base B. For example, when ammonia (NH3) reacts with water, it forms its conjugate acid, the ammonium ion NH4+, while water becomes its conjugate base, the hydroxide ion OH−.

Strength relationships

The stronger an acid, the weaker its conjugate base, and vice versa. This inverse relationship is quantified through the acid dissociation constant (Ka) of the acid and the base dissociation constant (Kb) of its conjugate base, whose product equals the ionisation constant of water. Conjugate pairs are central to the behaviour of buffer solutions.
