Enceinte

Plan of a fortress enceinte with bastioned trace

Enceinte (from Latin incinctus, "girdled" or "surrounded") is a French term used in fortification for the principal defensive enclosure of a fortified place — the continuous line of walls, ramparts and bastions that completely surrounds a town, castle or fortress. The word is also applied more generally in archaeology to the enclosing wall or bank of any fortified or enclosed site.

Usage in fortification

In the vocabulary of bastioned fortification, the enceinte is the main body of the place: the ring of curtain walls connecting the bastions, together with the bastions themselves, as distinct from outworks such as ravelins, tenailles, hornworks and crownworks that lie beyond the main ditch. A fortress might have a single enceinte or several concentric ones; the innermost was the last line of defence once the outer works had fallen.

As artillery improved in the 16th and 17th centuries, enceintes evolved from high medieval curtain walls into the low, thick, earth-backed ramparts of the trace italienne and the systems of engineers such as Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban, whose fortresses — for example the citadel of Lille and the walled town of Neuf-Brisach — are classic examples of the bastioned enceinte.

Archaeological usage

Archaeologists use "enceinte" for the perimeter of prehistoric and early historic enclosures, such as the ditch-and-bank circuits of Neolithic causewayed enclosures, the ramparts of Iron Age hillforts, and the stone or earthen enclosures surrounding ritual or settlement sites. In Egyptian archaeology the term is also used for temple precinct walls.
