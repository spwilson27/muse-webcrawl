The Constitution Act, 1982 is a part of the Constitution of Canada, enacted as Schedule B to the Canada Act 1982 passed by the Parliament of the United Kingdom. It completed the "patriation" of Canada's constitution, ending the need for amendments to be made by the British Parliament, and entrenched the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and a wholly domestic amending formula. Queen Elizabeth II proclaimed the Act in a ceremony on Parliament Hill in Ottawa on 17 April 1982.

Contents

Part I (sections 1–34) is the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, guaranteeing fundamental freedoms, democratic rights, mobility rights, legal rights, equality rights and official-language rights, subject to the "reasonable limits" clause of section 1 and to the legislative override in section 33 (the notwithstanding clause). Part II (section 35) recognises and affirms the existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the Aboriginal peoples of Canada. Part III (section 36) commits governments to equalisation and the reduction of regional disparities. Part V (sections 38–49) sets out the amending procedures: a general formula requiring resolutions of the Senate and House of Commons plus the legislative assemblies of at least two-thirds of the provinces representing at least half the population (the "7/50" rule), a unanimity rule for certain matters such as the offices of the Queen and the composition of the Supreme Court, and simpler procedures for amendments affecting only some provinces. Section 52 declares the Constitution of Canada to be "the supreme law of Canada".

Background and Quebec

Patriation followed decades of failed federal–provincial negotiation. In November 1981, after the Supreme Court's Patriation Reference held that substantial provincial consent was constitutionally conventional, the federal government and nine provinces reached agreement in the so-called "Kitchen Accord", which included the notwithstanding clause as a compromise. Quebec's government, under René Lévesque, rejected the settlement, and the National Assembly has never assented to the 1982 Act, although it legally applies in Quebec. Two later attempts to secure Quebec's formal adherence — the Meech Lake Accord (1987–1990) and the Charlottetown Accord (defeated in a 1992 referendum) — both failed.
