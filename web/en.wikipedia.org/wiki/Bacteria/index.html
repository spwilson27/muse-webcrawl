Bacteria

[Image: rod-shaped bacteria under a scanning electron microscope, coloured purple, clustered on a surface]

Bacteria are ubiquitous, mostly free-living single-celled microorganisms — prokaryotes whose cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus. Typically a few micrometres long, they occur in spherical (cocci), rod (bacilli) and spiral (spirilla) forms, and inhabit virtually every environment on Earth: soil, water, hot springs, deep rock, the open ocean and the bodies of plants and animals, including about as many bacterial cells in a human body as human cells.

Bacteria were among the first life forms to appear on Earth, about four billion years ago, and for most of the planet's history they were its only inhabitants. They drive the global cycles of carbon, nitrogen and sulphur, and made the oxygen in the atmosphere.

Benefit and harm

Most bacteria are harmless or essential: gut microbes aid digestion and immunity, soil bacteria fix nitrogen for plants, and bacteria ferment foods (yoghurt, cheese, kimchi), produce medicines and clean up pollution. A minority cause disease — tuberculosis, cholera, plague, pneumonia, food poisoning — treated since the 1940s with antibiotics, whose overuse now drives dangerous antimicrobial resistance.

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek first observed bacteria ('animalcules') with his microscopes in the 1670s; the germ theory of disease, established by Pasteur and Koch in the nineteenth century, revealed their medical importance, and genetics began with bacterial experiments in the twentieth.

Classification

Bacteria form one of the three domains of life (with Archaea and Eukarya, in Woese's system based on ribosomal RNA). Their diversity is immense and mostly uncultured; modern microbiology identifies them largely by DNA sequencing of environmental samples.
