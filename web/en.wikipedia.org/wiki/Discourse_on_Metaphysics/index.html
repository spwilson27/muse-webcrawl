Discourse on Metaphysics

The Discourse on Metaphysics (French: Discours de métaphysique) is a short treatise written by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in 1686, though it was not published in his lifetime. Consisting of 37 sections, it presents an early statement of many of Leibniz's characteristic doctrines.

Content

In the treatise, Leibniz argues that God, acting with perfect wisdom, chose the best of all possible worlds. He develops the idea that the complete concept of an individual substance contains, from God's point of view, everything that will ever happen to it — the so-called predicate-in-subject principle. He also discusses the nature of substance, the distinction between necessary and contingent truths, miracles, and the harmony between the kingdoms of nature and grace.

Reception

Leibniz sent a summary of the sections to the theologian Antoine Arnauld, prompting a famous correspondence in which Arnauld objected to the implications for human freedom. The manuscript was rediscovered and first published in the 19th century; it is now regarded as one of the key texts for understanding Leibniz's mature philosophy, alongside the Monadology and the Theodicy.
