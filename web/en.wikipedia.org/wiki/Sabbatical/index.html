Sabbatical

A sabbatical (from the Hebrew Shabbat, "Sabbath"; in Latin sabbaticus; Greek sabbatikos) is a rest or break from work. The concept derives from the biblical practice of the sabbatical year (shmita), in which agricultural land was left fallow every seventh year and debts were remitted.

Academic sabbaticals

In modern usage, the term is most closely associated with academia. Many universities offer tenured faculty members a period of paid leave, typically one semester or one academic year, after every six or seven years of service. During a sabbatical, academics typically conduct research, write books or papers, collaborate with colleagues at other institutions, or develop new courses. The practice was pioneered at Harvard University in 1880 and has since become a standard feature of academic employment in many countries.

The stated purpose of the academic sabbatical is to allow scholars time for intellectual renewal, research productivity, and professional development free from teaching and administrative duties. Universities generally require applicants to submit a plan of work and, on return, a report on what was accomplished.

Corporate and other sabbaticals

Outside academia, some companies offer sabbatical leave to long-serving employees, ranging from a few weeks to several months, paid or unpaid. Sabbaticals are also taken by clergy, medical professionals, and individuals who arrange extended breaks in their careers for travel, study, volunteering, or personal projects. A related concept is the "gap year", usually taken by students before or after university.
