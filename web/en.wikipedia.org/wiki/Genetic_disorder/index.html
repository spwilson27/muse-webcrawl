A genetic disorder is a health condition caused by one or more abnormalities in the genome. It may be inherited from a parent or arise from a new (de novo) mutation. Genetic disorders range from diseases caused by a single defective gene to syndromes involving entire chromosomes, and to common conditions influenced by many genes together with environment.

Single-gene disorders

Monogenic disorders follow recognizable inheritance patterns: autosomal dominant (e.g. Huntington's disease, Marfan syndrome), autosomal recessive (e.g. cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, Tay–Sachs disease), and X-linked (e.g. haemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy). Thousands of single-gene disorders are catalogued; individually most are rare, but together they affect a substantial fraction of births.

Chromosomal disorders

These result from changes in chromosome number or structure, such as trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), Turner syndrome (45,X) and Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), or deletions and duplications of chromosome segments.

Complex and mitochondrial disorders

Many common diseases — type 2 diabetes, most heart disease, schizophrenia — are multifactorial, reflecting many genetic variants plus lifestyle and environment. A smaller class is caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA and is transmitted maternally.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis uses pedigree analysis, karyotyping, chromosomal microarrays, gene panels and whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing; newborn and prenatal screening detect some conditions early. Most genetic disorders have no cure, but many can be managed; enzyme replacement, dietary therapy, and increasingly gene therapies offer targeted treatments for a growing number.
