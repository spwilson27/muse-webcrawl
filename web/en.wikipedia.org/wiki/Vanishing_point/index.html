Vanishing point

Vanishing point
FieldPerspective drawing, projective geometry, computer vision
Defined byConvergence of parallel lines in projection

A vanishing point is a point on the image plane of a perspective drawing where the two-dimensional perspective projections of mutually parallel lines in three-dimensional space appear to converge. It is the point where parallel lines seem to meet at the horizon when viewed from a particular vantage point.

Properties

When a set of parallel lines is projected onto a picture plane, their images converge at a single vanishing point unless the lines are parallel to the picture plane itself, in which case they remain parallel in the drawing. A drawing may have one vanishing point (one-point perspective), two (two-point perspective) or three (three-point perspective), depending on the orientation of the scene relative to the viewer. Vanishing points for lines lying in a common plane lie on that plane's vanishing line; the vanishing line of the ground plane is the horizon line.

History

The systematic use of vanishing points emerged in Renaissance Italy. The architect Filippo Brunelleschi is credited with demonstrating linear perspective around 1415, and Leon Battista Alberti set out the method in his treatise De pictura (1435). The vanishing point became a foundational device of Western art, architecture and technical drawing. Mathematically, vanishing points correspond to points at infinity in projective geometry, a framework formalised in the seventeenth century by Girard Desargues and others.

Applications

In computer vision, vanishing points detected in an image are used for camera calibration, three-dimensional reconstruction, and the rectification of documents and architectural photographs. They are also important in road and lane detection for driver-assistance systems.
