Gdynia (Polish: [ˈɡdɪɲa]; German: Gdingen) is a city in northern Poland on the Baltic Sea coast, in the Pomeranian Voivodeship. Together with Gdańsk and Sopot it forms the Tricity (Trójmiasto) metropolitan area. Gdynia is one of Poland's largest ports and is noted for its modernist city center, built largely in the interwar period.

History

The site was a small Kashubian fishing village for centuries. After World War I, the Treaty of Versailles placed the nearby city of Danzig (Gdańsk) under League of Nations administration as a free city, and Poland decided in the early 1920s to build a new deep-water port of its own at Gdynia. Construction, directed by engineer Tadeusz Wenda, transformed the village into a major harbor; Gdynia received city rights in 1926 and grew rapidly into one of the busiest ports on the Baltic.

At the start of World War II in September 1939, the Polish Navy's defense of the nearby Hel Peninsula and the coast around Gdynia was an early episode of the war. Germany occupied and renamed the city Gotenhafen, using it as a naval base; it was the departure point of the refugee ship Wilhelm Gustloff, sunk in January 1945 with enormous loss of life. Gdynia returned to Poland after 1945.

Economy and culture

The Port of Gdynia handles container, bulk, and ferry traffic and hosts shipbuilding and maritime industries. The city is home to the Polish Naval Academy, the oceanographic tall ship Dar Pomorza as a museum ship, and the annual Open'er Festival, one of Poland's largest music festivals.
