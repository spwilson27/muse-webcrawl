Sir Oswald Ernald Mosley, 6th Baronet (16 November 1896 – 3 December 1980) was a British politician and aristocrat who rose to prominence in the 1920s before becoming the leader of the British Union of Fascists (BUF) in the 1930s. Once regarded as a potential prime minister, he was interned during the Second World War and remained a marginal, controversial figure thereafter.

Early life and Parliament

Mosley was born into a wealthy Staffordshire landowning family. Educated at Winchester and Sandhurst, he served in the First World War with the 16th The Queen's Lancers and the Royal Flying Corps, and was injured in a flying accident. Elected to Parliament in 1918 as a Conservative at the age of 21 — then the youngest MP — he soon crossed the floor, sat as an independent, and joined the Labour Party in 1924. He narrowly contested Neville Chamberlain in Birmingham Ladywood and then represented Smethwick from 1926.

In Ramsay MacDonald's Labour government of 1929 Mosley became Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster and drafted the "Mosley Memorandum", an ambitious programme of public works and economic planning to combat unemployment influenced by Keynesian ideas. When it was rejected in 1930, he resigned and founded the New Party, which lost every seat at the 1931 election.

British Union of Fascists

After visiting Mussolini's Italy, Mosley founded the British Union of Fascists in 1932, adopting black-shirted uniforms, mass rallies, and an increasingly antisemitic programme. The movement peaked at perhaps 40,000–50,000 members but was damaged by the violence of its stewards at the Olympia rally of June 1934, after which press magnate Lord Rothermere withdrew support. In October 1936, attempts by Mosley's supporters to march through London's East End were blocked by anti-fascist demonstrators in the Battle of Cable Street. The Public Order Act 1936 banned political uniforms.

Internment and later life

Mosley married Diana Mitford in 1936 at Joseph Goebbels's Berlin home, with Hitler present. In May 1940, after the outbreak of war, he was interned without trial under Defence Regulation 18B and held in Brixton and Holloway prisons; he was released on health grounds in 1943 and kept under house arrest. After the war he founded the Union Movement, advocating a united Europe ("Europe a Nation"), and stood unsuccessfully for Parliament several times, including in North Kensington in 1959 amid the aftermath of the Notting Hill race riots. He spent his later years largely in France, wrote his autobiography My Life (1968), and died at Orsay in 1980.
