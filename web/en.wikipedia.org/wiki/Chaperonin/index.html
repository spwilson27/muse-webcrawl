Chaperonin

Chaperonin
FunctionProtein folding (molecular chaperone)
ExamplesGroEL/GroES (bacteria), TRiC/CCT (eukaryotes), thermosome (archaea)
Energy sourceATP hydrolysis

Chaperonins are protein complexes that assist the folding of other proteins. They are a subfamily of molecular chaperones characterized by a stacked double-ring structure, each ring forming a central cavity in which an unfolded or misfolded substrate protein can fold in isolation. Chaperonins use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to drive conformational changes that encapsulate and release their substrates.

Groups

Group I chaperonins, such as bacterial GroEL and its mitochondrial homologue Hsp60, work with a detachable lid-like cofactor (GroES/Hsp10). Group II chaperonins, found in archaea (the thermosome) and the eukaryotic cytosol (TRiC/CCT), have a built-in lid formed by protrusions of their subunits and eight or nine distinct subunits per ring.

Mechanism

An unfolded substrate binds to hydrophobic sites lining the open ring. ATP binding and hydrolysis, together with the cofactor lid, enclose the substrate in a hydrophilic chamber — an "Anfinsen cage" — where it can fold free from aggregation-prone neighbours. After a few seconds, ATP hydrolysis triggers release of the folded protein.

Significance

Chaperonins are essential in all domains of life; roughly 5–10% of cytosolic proteins interact with TRiC in eukaryotes, including actin and tubulin. Defects in chaperonin subunits are linked to neurodegenerative and developmental diseases.
