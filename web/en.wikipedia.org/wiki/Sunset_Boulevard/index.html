Sunset Boulevard

Sunset Boulevard (styled Sunset Blvd.) is a 1950 American film noir directed by Billy Wilder and starring William Holden as Joe Gillis, a down-on-his-luck screenwriter, and Gloria Swanson as Norma Desmond, a forgotten silent-film star who draws him into her decaying mansion and her delusions of a comeback. Named after the famous Los Angeles boulevard, it is a corrosive portrait of Hollywood and one of the most acclaimed films ever made.

Plot

The film opens with police pulling a man's body from a swimming pool; the dead man, Joe Gillis, narrates in flashback. Fleeing creditors, Gillis hides his car in the garage of a seemingly empty mansion on Sunset Boulevard, home to Norma Desmond, a silent-era star living on fan mail secretly written by her butler Max — once her director and first husband. Norma hires Joe to edit her comeback script, a bloated Salome, and keeps him as a kept man. When he tries to leave, and her hopes of returning to Paramount collapse, Norma shoots him; descending her staircase before news cameras in a final madness, she delivers the famous line: "All right, Mr. DeMille, I'm ready for my close-up."

Production

Wilder and Charles Brackett's script is dense with Hollywood self-reference: Swanson had herself been a great silent star; Erich von Stroheim, playing Max, had directed her; Cecil B. DeMille appears as himself; Norma's bridge-playing "waxworks" include Buster Keaton. The film won three Academy Awards from eleven nominations, including Best Screenplay and Franz Waxman's score.

Legacy

Selected among the first group of films for the National Film Registry in 1989, Sunset Boulevard ranks near the top of the AFI's greatest-films lists. Andrew Lloyd Webber's stage musical adaptation premiered in 1993. Its lines — "I am big! It's the pictures that got small" — have passed into the language.
