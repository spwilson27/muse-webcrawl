Motu proprio

Motu proprio (Latin for "on his own impulse") is a legal formula in canon law denoting a document issued by the pope on his own initiative, without a prior formal request or petition. The term also designates the document itself. A decree issued motu proprio may address matters of doctrine, discipline, or administration, and can dispense from or derogate existing law.

Usage

The first document known to bear the formula was issued by Pope Innocent VIII in 1484. Since the 15th century, motu proprios have been a routine instrument of papal governance. A motu proprio traditionally begins with a statement that the pope acts with certain knowledge and on his own authority ("Motu proprio, certa scientia, et de apostolicae potestatis plenitudine…").

Notable examples

Well-known motu proprios include Summorum Pontificum (2007) by Benedict XVI, liberalizing use of the Tridentine Mass, and its partial reversal Traditionis Custodes (2021) by Francis. John Paul II's Ecclesia Dei (1988) responded to the unauthorized episcopal consecrations by Archbishop Lefebvre. Francis has used the instrument frequently for curial and legal reforms, such as Vos Estis Lux Mundi (2019) on accountability for abuse cover-ups.
