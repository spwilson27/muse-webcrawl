Indo-Iranian languages

The Indo-Iranian languages (also known as Indo-Iranic languages) are the largest and southeasternmost extant branch of the Indo-European language family. They consist of over 300 languages spoken by more than 1.5 billion people across South and Central Asia and the Iranian plateau, principally in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Iran, Afghanistan and Tajikistan. The branch is conventionally divided into three groups: Indo-Aryan, Iranian, and Nuristani.

Proto-Indo-Iranian

The reconstructed ancestor, Proto-Indo-Iranian, is generally dated to the late 3rd millennium BCE and associated with the Sintashta culture of the Eurasian steppe and its successor, the Andronovo culture. Distinctive features of the branch include the merger of the Proto-Indo-European vowels *e, *a, *o into a single vowel *a, the development of retroflex consonants (in Indo-Aryan under Dravidian influence), and a shared religious vocabulary: terms for the sacrificial ritual, the sacred drink soma/haoma, and deities such as Mitra and Indra appear in both the Rigveda and the Avesta.

Old Indo-Iranian

The oldest attested forms are Vedic Sanskrit (the language of the Rigveda, composed roughly 1500–1200 BCE in the Punjab) and Avestan (the language of Zoroastrian scripture) together with Old Persian, known from Achaemenid cuneiform inscriptions such as the Behistun Inscription of Darius I. Indo-Aryan words and divine names also appear in the records of the Mitanni kingdom of northern Syria (c. 1500 BCE), including the horse-training manual of Kikkuli.

Branches

Indo-Aryan

The Indo-Aryan group includes Hindi, Bengali, Punjabi, Marathi, Gujarati, Urdu, Nepali, Odia, Assamese, Sinhala and Romani, among many others. Through Middle Indo-Aryan (Prakrits, Pali) the languages developed from the synthetic grammar of Sanskrit toward more analytic structures.

Iranian

The Iranian group includes Persian (Farsi, Dari, Tajik), Pashto, Kurdish, Balochi, Ossetian and the extinct Scythian, Sogdian and Bactrian languages, historically spread from Central Asia to the Caucasus and eastern Europe.

Nuristani

The small Nuristani group, spoken in the Hindu Kush of eastern Afghanistan, is often treated as a third, coordinate branch that split off early.
