Hachikō (ハチ公, 10 November 1923 – 8 March 1935) was a Japanese Akita dog remembered for his remarkable loyalty to his owner, Hidesaburō Ueno, for whom he waited at Shibuya Station in Tokyo for over nine years after Ueno's death. Hachikō has become a national symbol of loyalty in Japan.

Life

Hachikō was born on a farm near the city of Ōdate, Akita Prefecture, and was brought to Tokyo in 1924 by Hidesaburō Ueno, a professor of agriculture at Tokyo Imperial University. Each morning the dog accompanied Ueno to Shibuya Station and each evening returned to the station to greet him on his arrival home. The routine continued until 21 May 1925, when Ueno suffered a cerebral haemorrhage while giving a lecture and died, never returning to the station.

After Ueno's death Hachikō was given away but repeatedly escaped, returning to wait at the station at the time of the evening train. Station staff and local people fed and cared for him. He continued this daily vigil for nine years, nine months and fifteen days, until his own death on 8 March 1935; his body was found in a street in Shibuya. An autopsy decades later identified cancer and worm infection among the causes of death. His remains were stuffed and are exhibited at the National Museum of Nature and Science in Ueno, Tokyo.

Legacy

Hachikō's story became widely known after a 1932 newspaper article, and in 1934 a bronze statue by Teru Andō was erected at Shibuya Station in his presence; it was recycled for the war effort and replaced in 1948 by a new statue by Takeshi Andō, the original sculptor's son. The Shibuya statue is one of Tokyo's most famous meeting points, and an annual ceremony is held there on 8 March. A further statue in Ōdate, replicas in the United States, and films — notably Hachikō Monogatari (1987) and the American remake Hachi: A Dog's Tale (2009), starring Richard Gere — have spread his story worldwide. In 2015 a statue depicting Hachikō finally reunited with Professor Ueno was unveiled at the University of Tokyo.
