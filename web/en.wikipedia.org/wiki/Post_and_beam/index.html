Post and beam

Post and beam construction is a building method that relies on heavy timbers joined together to form a structural frame, rather than the dimensional lumber ("stick framing") used in most modern houses. Together with timber framing, to which it is closely related, it is one of the traditional methods of building with heavy timbers — vertical posts carrying horizontal beams, which in turn carry joists, rafters and the roof.

Description

In modern usage, "post and beam" often denotes construction in which the timbers are joined with metal fasteners, plates and bolts, whereas "timber framing" properly describes frames joined by carved wooden joinery — mortise and tenon joints secured with wooden pegs. Traditional frames use bents (cross-sections of posts and beams assembled flat and raised into place), braces for lateral stability, and non-structural infill walls between the timbers, historically of wattle and daub, brick nogging or plaster, and today often of structural insulated panels wrapped around the frame so the timbers remain visible inside.

History

Post-and-beam construction is ancient, found in Neolithic longhouses, classical Greek and Roman timber work, Chinese chuandou and Japanese minka framing, and in the medieval timber-framed buildings of Europe. Because large timbers span wider spaces than stud walls, the method permits open-plan interiors and large glazed openings, and it enjoyed a revival in late-20th and 21st-century custom home building in North America, where exposed Douglas fir, oak or reclaimed timbers are valued aesthetically as well as structurally. Glued laminated timber (glulam) and steel connectors have extended the technique to large commercial and civic buildings in contemporary mass-timber construction.
