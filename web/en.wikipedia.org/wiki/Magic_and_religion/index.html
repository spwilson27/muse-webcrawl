Magic and religion

Magic and religion are both forms of belief and practice concerned with supernatural powers, and scholars have long debated how — or whether — they can be distinguished. Magic is commonly defined as the use of rituals, symbols, actions, or language to manipulate supernatural forces for particular ends, while religion is generally understood as a communal system of worship directed at deities or sacred powers. In practice, the two overlap heavily, and many traditions treat them as points on a single continuum.

The evolutionist account

Nineteenth-century anthropologists proposed an evolutionary sequence. Edward Burnett Tylor treated magic as a primitive form of reasoning based on mistaken associations. James George Frazer, in The Golden Bough, argued that magic rested on two "laws" of sympathetic magic — the law of similarity (like produces like) and the law of contagion (things once in contact continue to affect one another) — and that magic historically preceded religion, which in turn preceded science. Frazer distinguished magic, which seeks to compel impersonal forces, from religion, which supplicates personal gods.

Functionalist and sociological views

Bronisław Malinowski, based on fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands, argued that magic arises in situations of danger and uncertainty — such as open-sea fishing — where technical knowledge runs out, serving to reduce anxiety and organize effort. Émile Durkheim distinguished religion from magic on social grounds: religion unites its adherents into a moral community or "church", whereas magic is performed by individual practitioners for clients and has no such enduring community.

Modern approaches

Contemporary scholars tend to regard the magic/religion boundary as culturally constructed: practices labelled "magic" are often simply the rituals of other people, or unofficial practices disapproved of by religious authorities. Many religious rituals include techniques that earlier scholars would have classified as magical, and theologians have historically policed the boundary — for example, distinguishing licit prayer and sacrament from illicit sorcery — more sharply than the practices themselves seem to warrant.
