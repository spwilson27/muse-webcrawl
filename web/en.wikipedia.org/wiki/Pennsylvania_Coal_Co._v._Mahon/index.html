Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon

CourtSupreme Court of the United States
Decided11 December 1922
Citation260 U.S. 393
MajorityHolmes
DissentBrandeis
IssueRegulatory takings under the Fifth Amendment

The case concerned a Pennsylvania law restricting coal mining that caused houses on the surface to subside.

Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon, 260 U.S. 393 (1922), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision that founded the doctrine of regulatory takings. The Court held that a Pennsylvania statute, the Kohler Act, which prohibited anthracite coal mining that caused subsidence of surface structures, went so far in destroying the coal company's reserved mining rights that it amounted to a taking of property requiring just compensation under the Fifth Amendment.

Facts and opinion

The Mahons' predecessors had bought surface rights while the Pennsylvania Coal Company retained the right to mine beneath, with the surface owner waiving damages. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., for the majority, acknowledged that government "could hardly go on" if every regulatory diminution of value required payment, but wrote the famous rule: "while property may be regulated to a certain extent, if regulation goes too far it will be recognized as a taking."

Dissent and legacy

Justice Louis Brandeis dissented, arguing that the restriction merely prevented a public harm analogous to a nuisance and that values are inherently relative. Mahon's "too far" test remains the conceptual starting point of regulatory takings law, developed later in cases such as Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City (1978)—which, fittingly, involved the successor company of this case's plaintiff's industry peers—and Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992).
