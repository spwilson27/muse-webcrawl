Descartes' theorem

In geometry, Descartes' theorem states that for every set of four mutually tangent circles, the radii of the circles satisfy a particular quadratic equation. By solving this equation, one can construct a fourth circle tangent to three given, mutually tangent circles. The theorem is named for René Descartes, who stated it — without the modern algebraic form — in a letter of 1643 to Princess Elisabeth of the Palatinate. The four circles are sometimes called Descartes circles or "kissing circles", and configurations of them lead to the Apollonian gasket.

Statement

It is convenient to express the theorem in terms of the curvature (or bend) of each circle, defined as the signed reciprocal of its radius, k = ±1/r. The curvature is taken as negative if the circle is internally tangent to the others — that is, if it encloses them, like the outer circle of an annulus. If the curvatures of four mutually tangent circles are k1, k2, k3 and k4, then Descartes' theorem states:

(k1 + k2 + k3 + k4)2 = 2(k12 + k22 + k32 + k42).

Given three circles, this is a quadratic equation for the fourth curvature, and its two solutions correspond to the two circles tangent to all three — the small circle in the gap between them and the large enclosing circle. A straight line can be treated as a degenerate circle of curvature zero, giving related formulas for configurations of circles and lines.

History

The result was known in some form in ancient Greek geometry, as part of the study of tangent circles associated with Apollonius of Perga. Descartes communicated his version in correspondence in 1643 but did not publish a proof. The theorem was rediscovered several times, notably by Frederick Soddy, whose 1936 poem "The Kiss Precise" popularised the curvature formulation — hence the name "kissing circles". A complex-number version of the theorem, which also locates the centres of the circles, was developed in the early 2000s.
