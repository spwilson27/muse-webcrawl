Mid-20th century baby boom

The mid-20th-century baby boom was a marked increase in birth rates in many countries following the Second World War, generally dated from 1946 to 1964 in the United States. Those born during the period are known as baby boomers. Similar booms occurred in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and much of Western Europe, though with varying timing and intensity.

Causes

The boom reflected postponed marriages and childbearing during the Great Depression and the war, followed by economic prosperity, rising wages, suburbanization, and — in the United States — veterans' benefits under the G.I. Bill that supported home ownership and education. Total fertility rates in the US peaked at about 3.7 children per woman in the late 1950s.

Scale

Some 76 million babies were born in the United States during the boom years, forming an outsized demographic cohort. In France, the United Kingdom, and other countries the boom came in waves — an immediate post-war spike and a second rise into the 1960s — ending with the widespread adoption of the contraceptive pill and falling fertility in the mid-1960s and 1970s.

Impact

The boomers drove post-war consumer culture, the growth of suburbs and schools in the 1950s, youth culture in the 1960s and 1970s, and housing and financial markets thereafter. As the cohort reaches retirement age in the 2010s–2030s, it places major demands on pension systems and healthcare, a central issue in demographic and fiscal policy.
