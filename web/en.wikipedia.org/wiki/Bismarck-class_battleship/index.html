The Bismarck class was a pair of fast battleships built for Nazi Germany's Kriegsmarine shortly before the Second World War. The ships were the largest and most powerful warships built by Germany at the time, and among the largest battleships ever constructed by a European power. The class comprised Bismarck and Tirpitz, laid down in 1936 in the context of the Anglo-German Naval Agreement, though their true displacement far exceeded the treaty's nominal limits.

Design

The design drew heavily on experience from the First World War-era Baden class, with an emphasis on close-range protection, extensive subdivision, and a broad beam for stability. The main battery consisted of eight 38-centimetre guns in four twin turrets, two forward and two aft, supplemented by twelve 15 cm secondary guns and a heavy anti-aircraft suite. Armour protection included a main belt up to 320 mm thick. Three shafts driven by geared steam turbines gave a top speed of about 30 knots.

Service history

Bismarck's only operation, in May 1941 (Operation Rheinübung), saw her sink the British battlecruiser HMS Hood in the Battle of the Denmark Strait, before she herself was crippled by a torpedo from a Fairey Swordfish and sunk by British battleships on 27 May 1941. Tirpitz spent most of the war in Norwegian fjords as a fleet in being, threatening Arctic convoys; after repeated attacks she was capsized by RAF Lancaster bombers using Tallboy bombs at Tromsø on 12 November 1944.

Legacy

The wreck of Bismarck, discovered in 1989 by Robert Ballard at a depth of about 4,800 m, has been surveyed by several expeditions. The class remains one of the most studied battleship designs of the Second World War.
