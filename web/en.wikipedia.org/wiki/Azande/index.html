The Azande (also spelled Zande; singular: Zande) are an ethnic group of north-central Africa, living principally in the southeastern Central African Republic, the northeastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, and western South Sudan. They speak Zande, an Adamawa-Ubangi language. The Azande were brought into a single political system in the 18th and 19th centuries by ruling dynasties, most notably the Vungara clan, whose kingdoms expanded by conquest and assimilation.

History

Zande kingdoms expanded eastward during the 1800s under rulers such as Gbudwe, incorporating many neighbouring peoples who adopted the Zande language and culture. In the late nineteenth century the region was divided between the Belgian Congo, French Equatorial Africa, and the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. Colonial rule dismantled the kingdoms; in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, the "Zande Scheme" of the 1940s resettled the population for agricultural development and sleeping sickness control.

Culture and beliefs

The Azande are renowned in anthropology through E. E. Evans-Pritchard's classic ethnography Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande (1937), based on fieldwork in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. Evans-Pritchard showed how beliefs in witchcraft (mangu), oracles — above all the benge poison oracle administered to chickens — and magic formed a coherent, self-consistent system for explaining misfortune and guiding action. Witchcraft was believed to be an inherited substance in the belly, and accusations typically followed lines of social tension.

Society

Historically, Zande society was stratified between the aristocratic Avungara (Vungara) ruling clan, who supplied kings and chiefs, and commoner clans. Settlement was dispersed, with homesteads scattered in clearings among gardens of cassava, groundnuts, bananas and other crops; hunting, including of elephants for ivory, was traditionally important for men. Azande craftsmen were famed for ironwork, notably the multi-bladed throwing knife known as the kpinga, and for carved wooden figures called yanda associated with the Mani society.

Religion

Traditional belief recognises a supreme god, Mbori, alongside ancestor spirits (atore), though much religious practice centres on witchcraft beliefs, oracles and magic. Since the twentieth century, Christianity — Catholic and Protestant — has spread widely among the Azande, alongside and in tension with older beliefs, which continue to influence interpretations of illness and misfortune.
