Financial inclusion

Financial inclusion is the availability and equality of opportunities to access financial services. It refers to a process by which individuals and businesses can access appropriate, affordable, and timely financial products and services — including banking, payments, savings, credit, and insurance. The World Bank and the G20 treat financial inclusion as a key enabler of development and poverty reduction.

Measurement

The World Bank's Global Findex Database, published triennially since 2011, is the standard source on account ownership. It found that the share of adults worldwide with an account at a financial institution or mobile money provider rose from 51% in 2011 to 76% in 2021, with mobile money driving much of the recent growth in Sub-Saharan Africa. Around 1.4 billion adults remained unbanked in 2021, disproportionately women, the poor, rural residents, and informal workers.

Drivers and strategies

  • Mobile money — services such as M-Pesa, launched in Kenya in 2007, allow deposits, transfers, and payments via basic mobile phones and agent networks.
  • Digital payments and government transfers — paying wages, pensions, and benefits into accounts brings people into the formal system.
  • Policy programs — India's Jan Dhan Yojana (2014) opened hundreds of millions of basic accounts, forming, with Aadhaar identification and mobile connectivity, the "JAM trinity" for direct benefit transfers.
  • Agent banking and microfinance — local agents and institutions such as Grameen Bank extend services where branches are uneconomic.
  • Regulatory reforms — tiered know-your-customer rules, interoperable payment systems, and central bank digital currency experiments.

Benefits and challenges

Access to accounts helps households smooth consumption, invest in education and businesses, receive remittances cheaply, and build resilience to shocks; studies associate mobile money access in Kenya with measurable poverty reduction, particularly for women-headed households. Challenges include dormant accounts, low digital and financial literacy, the gender gap in phone ownership and account use, consumer protection risks, data privacy, and the danger that easy digital credit produces over-indebtedness. Inclusion focused only on account numbers, rather than active and beneficial usage, is a common criticism of headline targets.
