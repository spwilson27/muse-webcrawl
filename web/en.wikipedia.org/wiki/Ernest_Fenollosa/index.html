Ernest Fenollosa

Ernest Francisco Fenollosa (18 February 1853 – 21 September 1908) was an American art historian and philosopher who championed Japanese art in the Meiji era.

Ernest Francisco Fenollosa (18 February 1853 – 21 September 1908) was an American art historian, educator, and philosopher. Teaching in Tokyo during the Meiji period, he became the foremost Western advocate of traditional Japanese art at a moment when Japan itself was discarding it in favour of Westernisation, and he is credited with helping to preserve entire genres of Japanese painting and sculpture.

Life in Japan

Fenollosa was born in Salem, Massachusetts, the son of a Spanish immigrant pianist, and graduated from Harvard College in 1874. Invited to teach philosophy and political economy at the Imperial University of Tokyo, he arrived in Japan in 1878. Captivated by Japanese art, he studied painting under Kano Hōgai, collected avidly, and with his student Okakura Kakuzō travelled the country cataloguing temple treasures — famously participating in the 1884 unveiling of the hidden Yumedono Kannon at Hōryū-ji. He converted to Buddhism and took the name Teishin. The Japanese government appointed him Imperial Commissioner of Fine Arts, and he helped found the Tokyo School of Fine Arts and the Imperial Museum; his lectures and surveys underpinned Japan's first laws protecting cultural property.

Later career

Returning to America in 1890, Fenollosa became curator of the Department of Chinese and Japanese Art at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, building its collection — much of it his own — into one of the finest outside Japan. After a scandal over his divorce and remarriage he resigned in 1896 and returned to Japan to teach English literature in Tokyo. His later years were devoted to literature; he studied Noh drama under Umewaka Minoru and translated Chinese poetry with the help of Japanese scholars.

Legacy

Fenollosa died suddenly in London in 1908; his ashes were returned for burial at Mii-dera, Ōtsu. His unfinished manuscripts passed to the poet Ezra Pound, who edited and published his translations of Noh plays and of Chinese poetry — Cathay (1915) and The Chinese Written Character as a Medium for Poetry — works that profoundly influenced Anglo-American modernist literature. His monumental Epochs of Chinese and Japanese Art appeared posthumously in 1912.
