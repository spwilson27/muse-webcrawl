Digital image processing

Digital image processing is the use of a digital computer to process digital images through an algorithm. As a subcategory or field of digital signal processing, digital image processing has many advantages over analog image processing: it allows a much wider range of algorithms to be applied to the input data and can avoid problems such as the build-up of noise and distortion during processing. Since images are defined over two dimensions (perhaps more), digital image processing may be modeled in the form of multidimensional systems.

The generation and development of digital image processing are mainly affected by three factors: first, the development of computers; second, the development of mathematics (especially the creation and improvement of discrete mathematics theory); third, the demand for a wide range of applications in environment, agriculture, military, industry and medical science has increased.

History

Many of the techniques of digital image processing were developed in the 1960s at Bell Laboratories, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, the University of Maryland, and a few other research facilities, with application to satellite imagery, wire-photo standards conversion, medical imaging, videophone, character recognition, and photograph enhancement. The cost of processing was fairly high, however, with the computing equipment of that era.

That changed in the 1970s, when digital image processing proliferated as cheaper computers and dedicated hardware became available. Images then could be processed in real time, for some dedicated problems such as television standards conversion. As general-purpose computers became faster in the 2000s, digital image processing has become the most common form of image processing, having replaced analog methods in most applications.

Tasks

Digital image processing allows the use of much more complex algorithms, and hence, can offer both more sophisticated performance at simple tasks, and the implementation of methods which would be impossible by analog means. Common tasks include:

  • Image enhancement — improving the appearance of an image for human viewing, for example by adjusting contrast or removing noise
  • Image restoration — recovering an image degraded by known phenomena such as blur or noise
  • Geometric transformations — scaling, rotation, warping and perspective correction
  • Colour processing — colour balancing, quantisation and conversion between colour spaces
  • Image compression — reducing the storage or bandwidth an image requires
  • Segmentation — partitioning an image into meaningful regions or objects
  • Feature extraction and object recognition — the foundation of computer vision and medical image analysis

Techniques

Spatial and frequency domain methods

Operations can be applied directly to pixel values (spatial domain) or to a transform of the image such as the Fourier transform (frequency domain). Filtering in the frequency domain can suppress periodic noise patterns, while convolution kernels in the spatial domain implement blurring, sharpening, and edge detection. The two descriptions are linked by the convolution theorem.

Morphological processing

Mathematical morphology applies set-theoretic operations—erosion, dilation, opening and closing—using a structuring element. These are widely used for cleaning binary images, separating touching objects, and analysing shapes in microscopy and document analysis.

Neural networks

Since the 2010s, convolutional neural networks have achieved state-of-the-art results in denoising, super-resolution, segmentation and recognition. Learned methods require large training sets but can model complex degradations that defeat classical algorithms.

Applications

Applications include medical imaging (CT and MRI reconstruction, microscopy), remote sensing from satellites, industrial inspection, document scanning and optical character recognition, astronomy, forensics, and consumer photography, where in-camera processing performs exposure fusion, portrait effects and stabilization assistance in real time.
