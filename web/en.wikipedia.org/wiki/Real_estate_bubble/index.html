Real estate bubble

A real estate bubble or property bubble (or housing bubble for residential markets) is a type of economic bubble that occurs periodically in local or global real estate markets, typically following a land boom. A land boom is a rapid increase in the market price of real property such as housing until prices reach unsustainable levels relative to incomes and rents, and then decline.

Characteristics

Housing bubbles are characterized by rapid price appreciation driven by speculation and the expectation of continued increases, easy access to credit, and rising price-to-income and price-to-rent ratios. Economists identify bubbles using measures such as these ratios; unlike stock market bubbles, property bubbles usually develop over years and "pop" slowly, because housing is illiquid, expensive to trade, and owners are reluctant to sell at a loss.

Notable examples

The Japanese asset price bubble of the late 1980s saw urban land prices soar before collapsing in the early 1990s, ushering in Japan's "Lost Decade." The United States housing bubble of the mid-2000s, fueled by subprime lending and securitization, burst in 2007–2008 and triggered the global financial crisis. Other episodes include the Spanish property bubble (1997–2008) and the Irish property bubble.

Consequences

Because housing is both a household's largest asset and collateral for bank lending, bursting property bubbles can produce banking crises, negative equity, foreclosure waves, and long recessions.
