Melanism is the development of the dark-colored pigment melanin in the skin or its appendages. Melanism is the opposite of leucism and albinism, which are characterized by a lack of melanin. An animal with the phenotype of melanism is called a melanist or melanic. Melanism can be adaptive — conferred by natural selection — or maladaptive, as in some genetic disorders; in humans, excess melanin production and deposition is involved in conditions such as Addison's disease and some forms of neurofibromatosis.

Industrial melanism

The best-known case is the peppered moth (Biston betularia) in industrial England. Before the Industrial Revolution, pale moths predominated, camouflaged against lichen-covered bark; as soot blackened trees and killed lichens, the dark carbonaria form, once rare, came to dominate near industrial cities, because it was less visible to predators. The frequency of the dark form declined again after clean-air legislation reduced soot — a classic, directly observed example of evolution by natural selection.

Melanism in mammals and other animals

Melanic forms occur in many species: the black panther is a melanistic jaguar or leopard, the black squirrel is a melanistic gray squirrel, and melanistic wolves owe their color to a mutation inherited from domestic dogs. Adaptive hypotheses include camouflage, thermoregulation, disease resistance, and sexual selection.

Genetics

Melanism frequently involves the melanocortin-1 receptor gene (MC1R) and the agouti signalling protein pathway, which regulate the type and amount of melanin produced by melanocytes.
