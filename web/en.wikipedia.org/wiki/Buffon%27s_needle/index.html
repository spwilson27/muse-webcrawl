Buffon's needle

Buffon's needle
Field: Probability theory, geometric probability
Proposed by: Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon (1777)

Buffon's needle is a classic problem in geometric probability: suppose a needle of length l is dropped at random onto a plane ruled with parallel lines a distance d apart, where l ≤ d. What is the probability that the needle crosses one of the lines?

History

The problem was first posed in 1733 by the French naturalist Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon, and he presented a solution in his 1777 work Essai d'arithmétique morale. It is notable as one of the earliest problems in geometric probability.

Solution

The answer is P = 2l / (πd). Because the answer involves π, repeated random drops of a needle can be used to estimate π numerically, a method in the spirit of Monte Carlo simulation. If many needles are dropped and the fraction crossing a line is measured, π ≈ 2lN / (dh), where N is the number of drops and h the number of hits.

Experiments

The Italian mathematician Mario Lazzarini famously claimed in 1901 to have performed the experiment with 3,408 tosses and obtained π correct to six decimal places, a result later suspected of involving bias in choosing the ratio of needle length to line spacing.

Generalizations

Buffon's noodle problem, posed by Pierre-Gilles de Gennes, extends the question to a flexible curve: the expected number of line crossings depends only on the curve's length, not its shape.
