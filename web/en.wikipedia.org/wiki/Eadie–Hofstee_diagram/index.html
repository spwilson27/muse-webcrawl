Eadie–Hofstee diagram

FieldBiochemistry, enzyme kinetics

In biochemistry, an Eadie–Hofstee diagram (also known as an Eadie–Hofstee plot or Eadie plot) is a graphical representation of enzyme kinetics in which the reaction rate (velocity) is plotted as a function of the ratio of rate to substrate concentration. It is one of several linearisations of the Michaelis–Menten equation.

Derivation

The Michaelis–Menten equation relates the initial rate v of an enzyme-catalysed reaction to the substrate concentration [S]:

v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])

Rearranging gives the Eadie–Hofstee form:

v = −Km · (v / [S]) + Vmax

A plot of v against v/[S] therefore yields a straight line with slope −Km, y-intercept Vmax, and x-intercept Vmax/Km.

History

The plot derives from work by George Eadie in 1942 and was independently described by Barend Hofstee in 1952. Like other linear transformations of the Michaelis–Menten equation, it was historically used to estimate kinetic parameters by hand before non-linear regression became widely available.

Advantages and limitations

Unlike the double-reciprocal Lineweaver–Burk plot, the Eadie–Hofstee plot does not compress data points at high substrate concentrations, and deviations from linearity — for example those caused by multiple enzymes or allosteric effects — can be easier to see. However, because the dependent variable v appears on both axes, measurement errors in v are correlated between the axes, which can distort parameter estimates. Modern practice favours direct non-linear fitting of the Michaelis–Menten equation, with the plot retained mainly for display.
