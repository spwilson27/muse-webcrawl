Hero (Chinese: 英雄; pinyin: Yīngxióng) is a 2002 Chinese wuxia film directed by Zhang Yimou, starring Jet Li as the nameless protagonist with Tony Leung, Maggie Cheung, Zhang Ziyi, Chen Daoming and Donnie Yen. A visually celebrated retelling of the attempted assassination of the King of Qin, it tells its story in competing versions distinguished by bold colour palettes, and became one of the most successful Chinese-language films ever released internationally.

Plot

In ancient China during the Warring States period, a nameless prefect is summoned before the King of Qin, who is on the verge of unifying China by conquering the six other kingdoms. Nameless claims to have killed the king's three most feared assassins — Long Sky, Flying Snow and Broken Sword — and recounts how. The king, suspecting deception, tells his own version of events; Nameless then reveals the truth: the "duel" was staged so that he, a Zhao assassin sworn to avenge his homeland, could get within ten paces of the throne. But Broken Sword, persuaded that China's suffering can only end through unification, had written two characters in the sand — tianxia, "all under heaven" — and Nameless, converted by the idea, spares the king. He walks out of the palace to face a storm of arrows, dying a hero who chose peace over vengeance.

Production and style

The film is famous for its colour-coded flashbacks — red for the invented story, blue for the king's version, white for the truth, green for memory — filmed by cinematographer Christopher Doyle in locations including the poplar forests of Inner Mongolia and the lakes of Jiuzhaigou. Its set pieces include a duel in falling autumn leaves between Maggie Cheung and Zhang Ziyi, and a sword fight on the surface of a mirror-still lake. Composer Tan Dun's score features violin solos by Itzhak Perlman.

Reception

Hero was the first Chinese-language film to top the US box office when finally released there by Miramax in 2004, and was nominated for the Academy Award and Golden Globe for Best Foreign Language Film. Critics praised its visual splendour while some debated its politics — its sympathetic portrayal of the unifying, authoritarian Qin king drew both defence as historical nuance and criticism as apology for autocracy.
