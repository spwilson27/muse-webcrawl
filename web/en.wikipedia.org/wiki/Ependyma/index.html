Ependyma

The ependyma is the thin neuroepithelial lining of the ventricular system of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord. It is made up of a single layer of ependymal cells, a type of glial cell, which are joined by tight junctions forming a barrier between the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and the underlying nervous tissue.

Structure and function

Ependymal cells range from cuboidal to columnar in shape and bear numerous microvilli and, in many regions, motile cilia on their apical surfaces. The beating of these cilia helps circulate the cerebrospinal fluid through the ventricles and central canal. Unlike most epithelia, the ependyma lacks a basement membrane in many regions, and its cells have long basal processes contacting astrocytes and blood vessels.

Specialised ependymal derivatives include the choroid plexus, where ependymal cells overlie fenestrated capillaries and secrete cerebrospinal fluid, and tanycytes in the walls of the third ventricle, which are thought to transport substances between the CSF and the hypothalamic portal circulation. Ependymal cells in the subventricular zone also function as neural stem cells capable of generating new neurons and glia.

Clinical significance

Tumours arising from ependymal cells are called ependymomas; they occur most often in children, where they are among the more common malignant brain tumours, frequently arising in the fourth ventricle. Inflammation of the ependyma (ependymitis) can occur with ventriculitis, and loss or damage of ependymal cilia contributes to some forms of hydrocephalus.
