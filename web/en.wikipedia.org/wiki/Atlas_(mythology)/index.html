In Greek mythology, Atlas (Greek: Ἄτλας) is a Titan condemned to hold up the heavens, or celestial sphere, for eternity after the Titanomachy. Atlas also plays a role in the myths of two of the greatest Greek heroes: Heracles (the Roman Hercules) and Perseus.

Mythology

Atlas was the son of the Titan Iapetus and the Oceanid Asia or Clymene. After the Olympians defeated the Titans, Zeus condemned Atlas to stand at the western edge of the earth and hold up the sky on his shoulders. In the Odyssey, Atlas is the father of the nymph Calypso, and is described as one who "knows the depths of all the seas" and holds the pillars that keep heaven and earth apart.

Heracles and Perseus

As one of his twelve labours, Heracles was sent to fetch the golden apples of the Hesperides, Atlas's daughters. Atlas offered to fetch the apples himself if Heracles would hold up the sky meanwhile; on returning, Atlas tried to leave Heracles under the burden permanently, but Heracles tricked him into resuming it. In another tradition, Perseus turned Atlas to stone with the head of Medusa, transforming him into the Atlas Mountains of northwest Africa.

Legacy

Atlas gives his name to the Atlantic Ocean ("Sea of Atlas"), to collections of maps (through Gerardus Mercator's 16th-century usage), and to the first cervical vertebra, which supports the skull. The image of Atlas bearing the sphere — notably the Farnese Atlas statue — remains one of the most familiar in Western art.
