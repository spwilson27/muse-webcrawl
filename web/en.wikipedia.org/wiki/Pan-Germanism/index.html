Pan-Germanism

Pan-Germanism (German: Pangermanismus) is a pan-nationalist political idea and movement, originating in the early nineteenth century, that aimed to unify all German-speaking peoples — and often all Germanic peoples — in a single nation-state. It was one of several pan-movements of the era, alongside pan-Slavism, and it influenced German politics from the revolutions of 1848 through the Nazi period.

Origins

Thinkers such as Johann Gottlieb Fichte, in his Addresses to the German Nation (1808), and Ernst Moritz Arndt articulated cultural and linguistic German nationalism under Napoleonic occupation. The unification of 1871 created a German Empire that nevertheless excluded Austria's Germans, leaving the "German question" open between kleindeutsch and großdeutsch solutions.

Organisations and radicalisation

The Pan-German League (Alldeutscher Verband), founded in 1891, campaigned for colonial expansion, naval building, and the interests of ethnic Germans abroad. After the First World War, pan-German ideas fed into the völkisch movement and were taken up — and ultimately radicalised — by Nazism, whose programme of Lebensraum and the annexation of Austria and the Sudetenland in 1938 drew on pan-German arguments. The catastrophic end of the Second World War discredited the movement.
