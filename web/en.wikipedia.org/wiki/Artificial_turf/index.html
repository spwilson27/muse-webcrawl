Artificial turf


A modern artificial turf sports pitch with crumb-rubber infill.

Artificial turf is a surface of synthetic fibers made to look like natural grass, used most often for sports arenas originally designed for or normally played on grass, but also widely used for residential lawns and commercial landscaping. It is manufactured principally from synthetic materials such as nylon, polypropylene and polyethylene, and is favoured for its durability and low maintenance: it requires no irrigation or mowing, and withstands heavy use and extreme weather.

History

Artificial turf first gained prominence when AstroTurf was installed in the Houston Astrodome in 1966, after the indoor stadium's natural grass died under its translucent roof panels. The short-pile nylon surface spread rapidly through North American professional stadiums in the 1970s, and artificial pitches were briefly used in English professional football in the 1980s. Early surfaces were widely criticised for hardness, friction burns and increased injury risk.

Modern turf

Third-generation systems, developed in the 1990s, use longer polyethylene fibres with an infill of sand and granulated ("crumb") rubber, often recycled from tyres, to cushion impact and make ball behaviour more like natural grass. Such surfaces are now common in American football, field hockey (where water-based pitches are standard at elite level), rugby, and training and community football facilities, and have been approved for top-level matches under programmes such as FIFA Quality. Hybrid systems, in which natural grass is reinforced with stitched synthetic fibres, are used at many elite stadiums.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include year-round playability, lower maintenance costs, and no need for watering, fertiliser or pesticides. Disadvantages include higher surface temperatures in hot weather, the environmental impact of plastic manufacture and disposal, the spread of rubber infill as microplastic pollution, and continuing debate among players and researchers about injury rates, particularly for knee and ankle injuries. Some jurisdictions have considered or enacted restrictions on crumb-rubber infill.
