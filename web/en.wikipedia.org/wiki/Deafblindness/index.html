Deafblindness

[Image: Two people communicating using tactile sign language, hand over hand]

Deafblindness is the condition of little or no useful hearing and little or no useful sight. The degree of impairment varies widely: most people described as deafblind retain some residual vision, hearing, or both, while total deafblindness is comparatively rare. Because the distances senses are both impaired, deafblind people face distinctive challenges in communication, mobility, and access to information, and deafblindness is recognised as a distinct disability rather than simply the sum of deafness and blindness.

Causes

Deafblindness may be congenital or acquired. Congenital causes include Usher syndrome—the most common genetic cause, combining congenital hearing loss with progressive vision loss from retinitis pigmentosa—CHARGE syndrome, and congenital rubella syndrome. Acquired deafblindness most often results from ageing (combined age-related hearing and vision loss), illness such as meningitis, or injury.

Communication

Methods depend on the individual's residual senses and history. They include tactile signing (sign language received by touch), the deafblind manual alphabet (letters spelled onto the hand), Tadoma (feeling the speaker's lips and throat), print on palm, braille, and large print or hearing aids for those with partial senses. The best-known deafblind person in history, Helen Keller, learned language through finger spelling with her teacher Anne Sullivan.
