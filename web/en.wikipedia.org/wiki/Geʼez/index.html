Ge'ez

A Ge'ez manuscript of the Book of Enoch.

Ge'ez (ግእዝ, Gəʾəz) is an ancient South Semitic language that originated in Eritrea and the northern region of Ethiopia in the Horn of Africa. It later became the official language of the Kingdom of Aksum and remained the language of the Ethiopian imperial court. Today Ge'ez is used mainly as a liturgical language by the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Ethiopian Catholic Church, the Eritrean Catholic Church, and the Beta Israel Jewish community.

History

Ge'ez first appears in inscriptions of the kingdom of Aksum in the first centuries CE, written in a South Arabian-derived script that developed vowels in the 4th century, around the time Ethiopia adopted Christianity under King Ezana. A substantial literature followed, including Bible translations, royal chronicles, theological treatises and hagiographies. Ge'ez ceased to be a commonly spoken language around the 10th–12th centuries, displaced by Amharic, Tigrinya and Tigre, but it remained the written literary language of Ethiopia until the 19th century, much as Latin did in medieval Europe.

Phonology and grammar

Ge'ez is a typical Semitic language: its vocabulary is built from consonantal roots (usually triconsonantal) expanded by vowel patterns and affixes. It has seven vowels and a consonant inventory including ejective consonants characteristic of Ethiopian Semitic languages. Nouns inflect for gender, number and case (nominative and accusative), and the verb system distinguishes perfect and imperfect aspects with rich subject and object agreement.

Literature

Ge'ez literature includes the Kebra Nagast ("Glory of Kings"), the Ethiopian biblical canon — which preserves books such as Enoch and Jubilees lost elsewhere — chronicles, monastic rules, and the Fetha Nagast law code. The script itself, also called Ge'ez or Ethiopic, is an abugida still used to write Amharic, Tigrinya and other languages of the region.
