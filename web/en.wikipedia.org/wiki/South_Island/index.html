South Island

The South Island (Māori: Te Waipounamu) is the larger of the two main islands of New Zealand, separated from the North Island by Cook Strait. With an area of about 150,000 square kilometres, it is the world's twelfth-largest island, though it holds only about a quarter of New Zealand's population.

Geography

The Southern Alps run the length of the island, rising to Aoraki / Mount Cook at 3,724 metres, New Zealand's highest peak. West of the Alps, the West Coast receives very heavy rainfall and retains dense temperate rainforest, including Fiordland National Park with its fiords such as Milford Sound. East of the ranges lie the Canterbury Plains and the dry intermontane basins of Otago.

Population and cities

Christchurch is the largest city, followed by Dunedin, Nelson, Invercargill and Queenstown. The island's economy rests on agriculture, tourism, wine (notably Marlborough sauvignon blanc and Central Otago pinot noir), hydroelectricity and fishing.

History

Māori settled the island from around the 13th century; Ngāi Tahu is the principal iwi today. European settlement expanded rapidly after 1840, boosted by the Otago gold rush of the 1860s. The island was known in the 19th century as the "Middle Island", the smaller Stewart Island being then counted as the South Island.
