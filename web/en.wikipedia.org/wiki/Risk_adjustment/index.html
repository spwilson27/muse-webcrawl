Risk adjustment

Risk adjustment is a method of adjusting payments, premiums, or performance measures to account for differences in the risk profile of the people or entities being compared. It is most prominent in health insurance and health care financing, where it is used to compensate insurers and health plans that enrol sicker-than-average populations, thereby reducing the incentive to avoid high-risk patients (risk selection or "cream skimming").

In health insurance

In systems with competing insurers and community-rated premiums — such as the Dutch and Swiss health insurance markets, German sickness funds, and the marketplaces and Medicare Advantage programme in the United States — risk adjustment transfers money from plans with healthier enrollees to plans with sicker ones. Statistical models predict each enrollee's expected costs from characteristics such as age, sex, and diagnosed conditions. The U.S. Affordable Care Act's risk adjustment programme uses the HHS-HCC model, based on hierarchical condition categories; Medicare Advantage uses the CMS-HCC model, which also drives a large share of payment to private Medicare plans.

Methods

Models are typically regression-based: diagnosis codes from claims or medical records are grouped into condition categories, whose coefficients estimate incremental costs. Good risk adjustment balances predictive accuracy against incentives for upcoding (documenting diagnoses more intensively than the underlying illness warrants), a persistent concern in Medicare Advantage. Simpler approaches adjust only for demographics; morbidity-based models are more accurate but data-hungry. Related methods include risk equalisation, reinsurance, and risk corridors, sometimes combined with adjustment.

Other applications

The term is also used in provider profiling — adjusting hospital mortality or readmission rates for patient case mix before comparing quality — and in finance and project appraisal, where expected cash flows or discount rates are modified to reflect risk. In all cases the aim is fairness of comparison: without adjustment, plans, providers, or projects serving riskier populations appear falsely expensive or ineffective.
