Baby boomers, often shortened to boomers, are the demographic cohort following the Silent Generation and preceding Generation X. The generation is most often defined by the years 1946 to 1964, during which there was a marked, sustained increase in birth rates — the post–World War II baby boom — in the United States and much of the Western world.

Terminology and dates

The phrase "baby boom" describes the sharp rise in births that began as soldiers returned from the Second World War and continued through the 1950s before subsiding in the mid-1960s. Demographers and the U.S. Census Bureau generally use 1946–1964 for the American cohort. In 1946 roughly 3.4 million babies were born in the United States; births peaked at about 4.3 million in 1957 and remained above 4 million a year until 1965. At its height the cohort numbered about 76 million Americans, making it the largest living generation in the country until it was overtaken by millennials.

Characteristics

Baby boomers grew up amid postwar economic expansion, suburbanization, and the spread of television. In their youth they were associated with the civil rights movement, second-wave feminism, anti–Vietnam War protests, the counterculture of the 1960s and rock music. As a large cohort, boomers exerted an outsized cultural and economic influence at every stage of life: as children they drove school construction and consumer markets aimed at youth; as adults, housing and stock markets; and in old age, health care and retirement systems.

The generation is often divided into two subgroups: the "leading-edge" boomers (born 1946–1955), who came of age during the 1960s, and the "trailing-edge" boomers or "Generation Jones" (born 1956–1964), whose formative years were the 1970s and who reached adulthood during the economic malaise of that decade.

Retirement and later life

The oldest boomers reached the traditional retirement age of 65 in 2011. Their mass retirement has prompted recurring debate in the United States and other Western countries about the solvency of pension and social insurance systems, the transfer of wealth to younger generations, and intergenerational fairness in housing and employment.
