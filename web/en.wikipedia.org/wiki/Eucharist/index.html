The Eucharist (from Greek εὐχαριστία, eucharistia, "thanksgiving"), also called Holy Communion and the Lord's Supper, is a Christian rite that is considered a sacrament in most churches, and an ordinance in others. According to the New Testament, Jesus instituted the practice at the Last Supper, the night before his crucifixion, giving his disciples bread with the words "This is my body" and wine with the words "This is my blood." Christians generally recognize a special presence of Christ in the Eucharist, though they differ on the mode — one of the major doctrinal divisions of the Reformation.

History

Early Christians celebrated the rite in homes as part of a fellowship meal, as described in Acts and in Paul's first letter to the Corinthians, the earliest written account (c. AD 55). By the second century, writers such as Justin Martyr describe a Sunday assembly with readings, prayers, and the distribution of bread and wine over which thanks had been given; the Didache preserves still earlier thanksgiving prayers. Over the following centuries the rite developed into the structured liturgies of East and West — the Divine Liturgy and the Mass — and in the Middle Ages the doctrine of transubstantiation was defined in scholastic theology and affirmed at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215).

Views by tradition

  • Catholic: The bread and wine are changed in substance into the body and blood of Christ (transubstantiation), while the appearances remain; the Mass is also a sacrifice, re-presenting the one sacrifice of Calvary.
  • Orthodox: Affirm a real change (metabole) of the gifts, effected by the epiclesis (invocation of the Holy Spirit), while declining to define the mechanism philosophically.
  • Lutheran: Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with, and under" the forms of bread and wine (sacramental union).
  • Reformed/Presbyterian: A real but spiritual presence: believers genuinely feed on Christ by the Spirit, though the elements themselves remain bread and wine.
  • Anglican: Views range historically from near-Catholic to Reformed; the Articles of Religion reject transubstantiation while affirming a "heavenly and spiritual" feeding.
  • Baptist and many evangelical: An ordinance and memorial of Christ's death, without a change or special presence in the elements.

Practice

Traditions differ on who may receive (open versus closed communion), how often the rite is celebrated (daily, weekly, quarterly), the matter used (leavened or unleavened bread, wine or unfermented grape juice), and the age of first communion. Ecumenical dialogue, such as the 1982 Lima text Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry, has narrowed but not eliminated the differences.
