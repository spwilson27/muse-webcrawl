Avogadro's law

Avogadro's law (sometimes called Avogadro's hypothesis or principle) is an experimental gas law stating that equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain the same number of molecules. In modern terms, the volume of an ideal gas is directly proportional to the amount of substance (number of moles) when temperature and pressure are held constant: V ∝ n, or V/n = k.

History

The hypothesis was proposed in 1811 by the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro. It helped resolve longstanding confusion between atoms and molecules and allowed Stanislao Cannizzaro to establish a consistent scale of atomic weights, famously presented at the Karlsruhe Congress of 1860. Avogadro's specific claim that elementary gases are diatomic (e.g., H2, O2) was controversial for decades.

Consequences

Combined with Boyle's law, Charles's law, and Gay-Lussac's law, Avogadro's law leads to the ideal gas law, PV = nRT. It implies that one mole of any ideal gas occupies about 22.4 litres at standard temperature and pressure (0 °C, 1 atm), and underpins the concept of molar volume and Avogadro's constant, NA ≈ 6.022×1023 mol−1.

Limitations

The law is exact only for ideal gases; real gases deviate measurably at high pressures and low temperatures, deviations corrected by equations of state such as van der Waals'.
