Natural rubber, also called India rubber, latex, Amazonian rubber, or caoutchouc, is a polymer of the organic compound isoprene obtained principally from the milky latex of the rubber tree Hevea brasiliensis, native to the Amazon basin. The latex is harvested by tapping — cutting a groove in the bark so that latex flows into a cup — and is then coagulated, washed, and dried into sheets or blocks for industrial use. Natural rubber is prized for its elasticity, resilience, and abrasion resistance, and accounts for roughly 40–45% of world rubber consumption, the remainder being synthetic rubber.

History

Mesoamerican peoples used rubber for balls, containers, and waterproofing thousands of years before European contact; the word caoutchouc derives from an Indigenous Amazonian term meaning "weeping wood". Rubber's modern industry began with Charles Goodyear's discovery of vulcanization in 1839, which stabilised rubber against heat and cold by heating it with sulfur. Demand exploded with the bicycle and automobile industries, fuelling the brutal Amazon rubber boom. In 1876 Henry Wickham collected Hevea seeds in Brazil and sent them to Kew Gardens; seedlings were propagated in Ceylon and Singapore, founding the plantation industry of Southeast Asia, which soon eclipsed wild Amazonian production.

Production

Thailand and Indonesia are the largest producers, followed by Vietnam, India, China, and Malaysia. Most natural rubber is grown on smallholdings rather than large estates. The main commercial grades include ribbed smoked sheet (RSS) and technically specified rubber (TSR). The industry faces threats from South American leaf blight — the disease that prevents large-scale plantation cultivation in the tree's native continent — price volatility, and pressure to address deforestation and labour conditions.

Properties and uses

Natural rubber is an elastomer with outstanding tensile strength, tear resistance, and low heat build-up, making it indispensable for aircraft and heavy-truck tyres, where synthetics cannot fully substitute. Other uses include latex gloves and medical products, condoms, hoses, belts, footwear soles, adhesives, and foam. Proteins in natural latex can provoke latex allergy, a significant occupational and medical concern.
