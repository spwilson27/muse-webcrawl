Surgery theory

In mathematics, specifically in geometric topology, surgery theory is a collection of techniques used to produce one finite-dimensional manifold from another in a "controlled" way — by cutting out a piece of the manifold and replacing it with a piece of another manifold, matched along the boundary. It is named by analogy with surgery on a body.

Surgery on a circle produces either a pair of circles or a figure depending on the gluing

The basic operation

A surgery on an m-dimensional manifold replaces an embedded sphere times a disc, Sp × Dq, with a disc times a sphere, Dp+1 × Sq−1, glued along their common boundary. The classic example is surgery on a torus, which can produce a sphere or a connected sum of tori. The trace of a surgery is a cobordism between the manifolds before and after.

The surgery programme

Developed principally by William Browder, Sergei Novikov, C. T. C. Wall and Andrew Ranicki, surgery theory reduces classification problems for manifolds — such as the Poincaré conjecture in high dimensions and the Borel conjecture — to computations in algebra. The central tool is the surgery exact sequence, which relates the set of manifold structures on a space to homotopy-theoretic normal invariants and to the L-groups of the fundamental group's group ring, which measure the obstruction to completing a surgery to a homotopy equivalence. The s-cobordism theorem and the work of Stephen Smale on the h-cobordism theorem are landmarks of the subject.

Applications

Surgery theory was used to prove the generalised Poincaré conjecture in dimensions five and above and to classify exotic spheres — smooth structures on spheres not diffeomorphic to the standard one.
