Sit-down strike

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sit-down strike is a form of strike in which workers occupy their workplace — sitting down at their stations rather than walking out — and refuse to leave, thereby preventing the employer from operating the facility or bringing in replacement workers. It differs from a conventional strike, in which strikers leave and picket outside, and from the related sit-in protest tactic of the civil rights movement, though the two influenced each other.

History

Factory occupations occurred in Europe from the early twentieth century — notably in Italy's biennio rosso of 1920 and in France in June 1936, when a wave of sit-down strikes accompanied the Popular Front's victory. The tactic achieved its most famous successes in the United States in 1936–37. Rubber workers in Akron pioneered American sit-downs, and the Flint sit-down strike against General Motors (30 December 1936 – 11 February 1937) became the landmark event: members of the United Auto Workers held GM's Fisher Body plants in Flint, Michigan, for 44 days, resisting police and court injunctions, until GM recognised the UAW as bargaining agent. The victory triggered a wave of sit-downs across American industry and helped propel the CIO's organising drives.

Legality

In the United States, the Supreme Court ruled in NLRB v. Fansteel Metallurgical Corp. (1939) that sit-down strikers who seized and held an employer's property could lawfully be discharged, and the tactic consequently declined, though factory occupations have recurred in other countries and in later episodes such as the Republic Windows and Doors occupation in Chicago in 2008, which won workers their severance pay. Employers and courts generally treat occupations as trespass once a strike is declared unlawful, but the tactic remains powerful because it physically denies the employer the use of the plant and makes eviction politically costly.
