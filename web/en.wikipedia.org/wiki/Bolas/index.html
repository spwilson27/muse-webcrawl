Bolas

Bolas (from Spanish bola, "ball"; also known as a boleadora) is a type of throwing weapon made of weights on the ends of interconnected cords, used to capture animals by entangling their legs. It was originally used by the indigenous peoples of the South American pampas, Patagonia and the Andes, and later famously by the gauchos — South American cowboys — to catch running cattle, guanacos and rheas.

Design

A bolas consists of two or three (occasionally more) weights, typically stones or metal balls, each tied to its own length of cord, with the cords joined at a common central point. Weights intended for birds are light; those for cattle are heavy and may be sheathed in rawhide. The thrower grasps the central knot or one weight, whirls the weapon overhead, and releases it so that the weights spread out in flight and wrap around the target's legs, tripping and immobilising it.

History

Archaeological finds in Patagonia show bolas stones dating back thousands of years. Indigenous hunters used them for guanaco and rhea long before European contact. Spanish settlers adopted the weapon, and the gauchos made it an emblem of pampas horsemanship, using the boleadoras both for hunting and in warfare during the South American wars of independence. Variants exist in other cultures: the Inuit used a similar corded weapon (kiipooyaq) for birds, and the Ainu and Siberian peoples used related entangling devices.

Use in the modern world

Bolas are still used by some horsemen in Argentina and Uruguay for handling livestock. Modern law-enforcement devices inspired by the bolas project a kevlar cord with weights to entangle a fleeing suspect's legs, though their deployment remains controversial.
