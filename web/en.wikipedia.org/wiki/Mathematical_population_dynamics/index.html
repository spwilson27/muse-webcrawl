Mathematical population dynamics

Mathematical population dynamics is the branch of mathematical biology that models how populations of organisms change over time through birth, death, immigration and emigration. Its equations form the foundation of theoretical ecology and are applied in fisheries management, epidemiology, conservation biology and pest control.

Exponential and logistic growth

The simplest model, exponential growth, assumes a constant per-capita growth rate, so the population increases without bound. The logistic model, introduced by Pierre François Verhulst in the 19th century, adds a carrying capacity: growth slows as the population approaches the maximum its environment can sustain, producing an S-shaped curve.

Predator–prey models

The Lotka–Volterra equations, developed independently by Alfred J. Lotka and Vito Volterra in the 1920s, describe coupled oscillations between a prey population and its predator. Later models incorporate functional responses, refuges and age structure, giving richer behaviour including stable equilibria and limit cycles.

Structured and stochastic models

Matrix population models, such as the Leslie matrix, track individuals by age or stage class and are widely used in conservation assessments. Stochastic and individual-based models capture demographic randomness, which matters for small or endangered populations.

Applications

Population models guide harvest quotas in fisheries, culling and vaccination strategies in disease ecology, and reintroduction programmes for endangered species. They also underpin metapopulation theory, which studies networks of local populations linked by dispersal.
