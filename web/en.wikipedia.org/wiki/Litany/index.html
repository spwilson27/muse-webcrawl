Litany

A litany, in Christian worship, is a form of prayer consisting of a series of petitions, supplications or invocations, spoken or sung by a leader, to which the congregation responds with a short, fixed refrain. The word derives from the Greek litaneia (λιτανεία), meaning "supplication" or "entreaty", related to processions of prayer in times of need.

Structure

A typical litany opens with the Kyrie eleison ("Lord, have mercy") and a Trinitarian invocation, proceeds through a list of petitions — often addressed to God under various titles, to the Virgin Mary, to angels and saints, or asking deliverance from evils and intercession for needs — and closes with the Agnus Dei and concluding prayers. The response is typically uniform throughout, e.g.:

Leader: Saint Mary, Mother of God —
Congregation: pray for us.

Leader: Saint Michael the Archangel —
Congregation: pray for us.

In the Roman Catholic Church

Six litanies are officially approved for public recitation in the Latin Church: the Litany of the Saints (the oldest and model for the others), the Litany of Loreto (Marian, approved 1587), the Litany of the Holy Name of Jesus, the Litany of the Sacred Heart of Jesus, the Litany of the Most Precious Blood, and the Litany of St. Joseph. The Litany of the Saints is used at the Easter Vigil, ordinations, and religious professions, and traditionally in processions in times of calamity.

In Eastern Christianity

In the Byzantine rite, the equivalent are the ektenia (litanies) led by the deacon during the Divine Liturgy — the Litany of Peace, the Litany of Supplication and others — with the choir responding "Lord, have mercy" (Kyrie eleison) or "Grant this, O Lord".

In Anglican and Lutheran usage

The Litany in the Book of Common Prayer, first published in English by Thomas Cranmer in 1544 and included in the 1549 Prayer Book, is one of the earliest English-language services. It is sung or said in procession or at Morning Prayer. Lutheran liturgies also retain a litany, often sung during Lent and in times of public distress.

Figurative use

In secular usage, a "litany" has come to mean any long, repetitive list, especially of complaints or faults: "a litany of failures".
