Clearing house

A clearing house is a financial institution formed to facilitate the exchange (i.e., clearing) of payments, securities, or derivatives transactions. The clearing house stands between two clearing firms (also known as "member firms" or "participants") and its purpose is to reduce the risk of a member firm failing to honour its trade settlement obligations.

History

Clearing houses originated in banking, where cheques drawn on different banks were exchanged and settled at a central location; the Bankers' Clearing House in London was established in 1775. As futures and options exchanges developed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, clearing houses became central to derivatives markets, beginning with institutions such as the clearing house of the Chicago Board of Trade.

Functions

A clearing house for exchange-traded derivatives acts as a central counterparty (CCP): through novation it becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, so that participants no longer face each other directly. It nets obligations across many trades, collects margin from members, and maintains a default fund to cover losses if a member fails. By multilateral netting, a clearing house greatly reduces the number and value of settlements required each day.

Examples

  • CME Clearing, part of the CME Group
  • LCH, based in London
  • The Options Clearing Corporation (OCC)
  • The Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation (DTCC), whose subsidiaries clear U.S. securities
  • Eurex Clearing in Europe

Payment clearing

In payment systems, clearing houses such as automated clearing houses (ACH) batch and settle transfers between banks. In the United States, organisations such as The Clearing House and the Federal Reserve operate large-value and retail payment clearing systems.
