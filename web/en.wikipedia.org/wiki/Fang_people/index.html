Fang people

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Fang people, also known as Fãn or Pahouin, are a Bantu ethnic group found in Equatorial Guinea, northern Gabon, and southern Cameroon. They form the largest ethnic group in Equatorial Guinea and a major group in Gabon, where they constitute a substantial share of the population. The Fang speak the Fang language, a member of the Beti-Pahuin group of Bantu languages.

History

The Fang migrated into the equatorial forest region from the northeast, from around the savannas of what is now Cameroon, in waves over the 19th and early 20th centuries, driven by trade, conflict, and the search for new land. Their advance toward the coast brought them into contact with European traders and, later, Spanish, French, and German colonial administrations. Fang society was traditionally organised without centralised kingship, in segmentary clan and lineage structures that proved adaptable to migration.

Culture and religion

The Fang are renowned for their wood sculpture, above all the reliquary guardian figures (eyima bieri) mounted on bark containers holding the skulls of honoured ancestors. These serene, abstracted heads profoundly influenced early 20th-century European modernists, including Picasso and the Fauvists, after examples reached Paris. The traditional bwiti religion, centred on ancestor veneration and the visionary use of iboga, remains influential, though most Fang today are Christian, and in Gabon bwiti has developed syncretic forms. Fang oral literature, epic recitation accompanied by the mvet harp-zither, is recognised as a masterpiece of intangible heritage.

Notable people

The Fang have been prominent in regional politics since independence: Léon M'ba, the first president of Gabon, and his successor Omar Bongo were of Fang and related backgrounds, as was Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo, the long-serving president of Equatorial Guinea.
