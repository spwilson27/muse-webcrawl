Hyperventilation

Hyperventilation is irregular breathing that occurs when the rate or tidal volume of breathing eliminates more carbon dioxide than the body can produce. This leads to hypocapnia, a reduced concentration of carbon dioxide dissolved in the blood, and a rise in blood pH known as respiratory alkalosis. Hyperventilation can be voluntary or involuntary, and is distinct from hyperpnea, the appropriately increased ventilation that meets increased metabolic demand during exercise.

Causes

Hyperventilation may result from anxiety and panic attacks, pain, fever, hypoxemia, lung diseases such as pneumonia or pulmonary embolism, metabolic acidosis (as a compensatory response), sepsis, head injury, certain drugs such as salicylates, and mechanical over-ventilation of a patient. It also occurs normally at high altitude as the body compensates for low oxygen.

Symptoms

Falling carbon dioxide causes cerebral vasoconstriction, which can produce light-headedness, dizziness, visual disturbances, and in severe cases fainting. Respiratory alkalosis lowers ionized calcium, leading to tingling and numbness around the mouth and in the fingers, muscle cramps, and carpopedal spasm. Chest tightness, palpitations, and a sense of breathlessness — paradoxically, despite overbreathing — are also common, especially in hyperventilation syndrome associated with panic.

Management

Treatment addresses the underlying cause. For panic-related hyperventilation, reassurance and slow breathing exercises are recommended. The traditional practice of rebreathing into a paper bag, intended to restore carbon dioxide, is now discouraged because it can cause dangerous hypoxia if the diagnosis is wrong.
