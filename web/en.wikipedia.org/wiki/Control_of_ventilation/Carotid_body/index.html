Carotid body

The carotid body is a small cluster of chemoreceptor cells (glomus cells) and supporting sustentacular cells located in the adventitia of the bifurcation of the common carotid artery, where it divides into the internal and external carotid arteries. Together with the aortic bodies, the carotid bodies form the peripheral chemoreceptors, which monitor the composition of arterial blood.

Function

The carotid body senses changes in arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), carbon dioxide (PaCO2), and pH, as well as temperature and osmolarity. It is especially important as the principal sensor of hypoxia: when arterial oxygen falls, type I (glomus) cells depolarize, release neurotransmitters such as dopamine and ATP, and increase firing in the carotid sinus nerve, a branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve (cranial nerve IX). Signals reach the nucleus tractus solitarius in the medulla, which increases ventilation and sympathetic outflow.

Because it receives an exceptionally high blood flow for its size, the carotid body responds primarily to the partial pressure of dissolved oxygen rather than to total oxygen content; thus it is relatively insensitive to anemia but highly responsive to hypoxemia.

Physiology

Stimulation of the carotid body produces hyperventilation, bronchodilation, and increased heart rate and blood pressure. The carotid bodies become more sensitive during chronic hypoxia, as at high altitude, and they enlarge in people living at altitude and in some chronic lung diseases.

Pathology

Tumors of the carotid body, called carotid body tumors or chemodectomas, are a type of paraganglioma. They are usually benign, slow-growing, and painless neck masses. Surgical removal can be complicated by the tumor's rich vascularity and proximity to cranial nerves.
