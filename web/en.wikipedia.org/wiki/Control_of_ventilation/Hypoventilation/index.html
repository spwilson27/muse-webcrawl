Hypoventilation

Hypoventilation (also known as respiratory depression) occurs when ventilation is inadequate to perform needed gas exchange, causing an increase in the concentration of carbon dioxide (hypercapnia) and respiratory acidosis. It is defined by insufficient alveolar ventilation relative to metabolic carbon dioxide production, and is the opposite of hyperventilation.

Causes

Hypoventilation can arise from depression of the respiratory centers in the brainstem by drugs such as opioids, benzodiazepines, and alcohol; from neuromuscular diseases including myasthenia gravis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Guillain–Barré syndrome; from chest wall disorders such as severe kyphoscoliosis and obesity hypoventilation syndrome; and from advanced lung disease. Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, historically called "Ondine's curse", is a rare genetic disorder in which automatic control of breathing is impaired, especially during sleep.

Effects

Rising carbon dioxide and falling pH produce headache, drowsiness, confusion, and in severe cases coma (CO2 narcosis). Hypoventilation also causes hypoxemia, because alveolar oxygen is displaced by accumulating carbon dioxide. Chronic hypercapnia leads to compensatory renal retention of bicarbonate and can strain the right side of the heart, contributing to pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale.

Treatment

Management targets the underlying cause: opioid antagonists such as naloxone for drug-induced respiratory depression, non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation (such as BiPAP) for acute exacerbations, and in severe cases mechanical ventilation. Respiratory stimulants have a limited role.
