Thoracic diaphragm

The thoracic diaphragm, or simply the diaphragm, is a sheet of internal skeletal muscle in humans and other mammals that extends across the bottom of the thoracic cavity. The diaphragm separates the thoracic cavity, containing the heart and lungs, from the abdominal cavity and performs an important function in respiration: when the diaphragm contracts, the volume of the thoracic cavity increases, creating a vacuum that draws air into the lungs, and when it relaxes, air is expelled.

Structure

The diaphragm is a double-domed structure with a central tendon, to which muscle fibers converge from attachments around the lower rib cage, sternum (xiphoid process), and lumbar vertebrae. Its right dome sits slightly higher than the left because of the liver beneath it. Three major openings pass through or behind the diaphragm: the caval opening for the inferior vena cava, the esophageal hiatus for the esophagus and vagus nerves, and the aortic hiatus for the aorta, thoracic duct, and azygos vein.

It is innervated by the phrenic nerves, arising from cervical spinal roots C3, C4, and C5 — summarized by the mnemonic "C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive."

Function

The diaphragm is the principal muscle of quiet breathing, accounting for roughly 75% of the change in intrathoracic volume during inhalation. It also assists in actions that raise intra-abdominal pressure, including vomiting, defecation, urination, and childbirth, and it helps prevent acid reflux by exerting pressure at the esophageal sphincter.

Disorders

Diaphragmatic problems include hiatal hernia, in which part of the stomach protrudes through the esophageal hiatus; congenital diaphragmatic hernia, a birth defect allowing abdominal organs into the chest; and paralysis from phrenic nerve injury, which can cause breathlessness and is sometimes treated by surgical plication or diaphragm pacing. Rhythmic involuntary contractions of the diaphragm produce hiccups.
