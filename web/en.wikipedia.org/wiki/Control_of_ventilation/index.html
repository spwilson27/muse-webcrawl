Control of ventilation

The control of ventilation refers to the physiological mechanisms involved in regulating breathing. Breathing is normally an automatic, subconscious process controlled by respiratory centers in the brainstem, though it can also be modified voluntarily by the cerebral cortex.

Respiratory centers

Neuronal groups in the medulla oblongata — including the dorsal and ventral respiratory groups — generate the basic rhythm of breathing, while centers in the pons (the pontine respiratory group, historically called the pneumotaxic and apneustic centers) fine-tune the pattern. The principal muscles driven are the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles.

Chemical control

Central chemoreceptors near the medulla respond mainly to the pH of cerebrospinal fluid, which tracks arterial carbon dioxide levels; rising CO2 is the dominant stimulus increasing ventilation. Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies respond to falling arterial oxygen, rising CO2, and falling pH, and become especially important at high altitude or in chronic lung disease.

Other influences

Stretch receptors in the lungs mediate the Hering–Breuer reflex limiting over-inflation; irritant receptors respond to smoke and dust. Exercise, emotion, fever, and voluntary commands (speaking, singing, breath-holding) all modulate ventilation. Failure of the automatic system occurs in conditions such as congenital central hypoventilation syndrome ("Ondine's curse").
