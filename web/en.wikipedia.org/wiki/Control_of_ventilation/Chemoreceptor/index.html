Chemoreceptor

A chemoreceptor, also known as a chemosensor, is a specialized sensory receptor which transduces a chemical substance (endogenous or induced) to generate a biological signal. Chemoreceptors are fundamental to the senses of taste and smell, and to internal homeostatic mechanisms that monitor blood gases, pH, glucose, and osmolality.

Respiratory chemoreceptors

In the control of breathing, chemoreceptors fall into two groups:

  • Central chemoreceptors, located on the ventral surface of the medulla, respond to the pH of the cerebrospinal fluid. Because carbon dioxide crosses the blood–brain barrier and acidifies the cerebrospinal fluid, central chemoreceptors effectively track arterial CO2 and account for most of the ventilatory response to hypercapnia.
  • Peripheral chemoreceptors, principally the carotid bodies and aortic bodies, respond rapidly to arterial oxygen, carbon dioxide, and pH. They are the main sensors responsible for the hypoxic ventilatory response.

Taste and smell

The chemoreceptors of the tongue and oral cavity detect dissolved chemicals and generate the sense of taste, while olfactory chemoreceptors in the nasal epithelium detect airborne molecules and produce the sense of smell. In insects, chemoreceptors on antennae and mouthparts guide feeding, mating, and oviposition.

Other examples

Chemoreception also underlies the vomiting reflex triggered by the chemoreceptor trigger zone in the area postrema, the detection of pheromones in many animals, and bacterial chemotaxis, in which single-celled organisms swim toward attractants and away from repellents by modulating flagellar rotation.
