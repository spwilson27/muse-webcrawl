Freedom of information

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freedom of information is the right of the public to access information held by public bodies, and the body of laws — freedom of information laws, or "access to information" and "right to information" laws — that give effect to it. It is an extension of freedom of speech and freedom of the press: citizens are presumed entitled to government records except where a specific exemption applies, and agencies typically must respond to requests within a set time, with refusals subject to appeal to an information commissioner or the courts.

History

The world's first freedom of information law is generally considered to be Sweden's Freedom of the Press Act of 1766, influenced by the philosopher Anders Chydenius, which established the principle of public access (offentlighetsprincipen) still central to Swedish government. The United States enacted its federal Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) in 1966, effective 1967, after a decade of campaigning by Congressman John Moss; it was significantly strengthened in 1974 in the wake of Watergate. From the 1970s onward, access laws spread widely: more than 130 countries now have such laws or constitutional provisions, including India's Right to Information Act (2005) and the United Kingdom's Freedom of Information Act 2000.

Principles and practice

Modern access regimes share common features: a general right of access without needing to show a special interest; a duty to respond promptly; enumerated exemptions (national security, personal privacy, law enforcement, commercial confidentiality, deliberative processes); independent oversight by an ombudsman or information commissioner; and proactive publication duties. In practice, users include journalists, researchers, businesses, and civil-society groups; recurring problems include delays, heavy redactions, fees, and the growth of informal or unrecorded decision-making. International standards are promoted by Article 19 of the ICCPR, UN and regional bodies, and assessment tools such as the Global Right to Information Rating.
