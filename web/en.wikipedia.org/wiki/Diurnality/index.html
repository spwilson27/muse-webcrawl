Diurnality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diurnality is a form of plant and animal behaviour characterised by activity during the day and a period of sleeping or inactivity at night. Animals active during the day are called diurnal. The common opposite is nocturnality (activity at night), while crepuscular animals are active chiefly at dawn and dusk, and cathemeral animals distribute activity across the whole 24-hour cycle.

In animals

Many animal types are diurnal, including most primates (among them humans), many insects such as butterflies and honey bees, most birds, and numerous reptiles and mammals such as squirrels and elephants. Diurnality is often tied to vision: diurnal animals commonly have eyes optimised for bright light and colour discrimination rather than for extreme sensitivity in darkness. Activity patterns are governed by an internal circadian clock synchronised to the light–dark cycle, so diurnal animals kept in constant conditions continue to show a roughly 24-hour rhythm.

The trait has evolved and been lost many times. Most mammals are thought to descend from nocturnal ancestors (the "nocturnal bottleneck" hypothesis, linked to the age of dinosaurs), with diurnality re-evolving in several lineages. Some species are flexible: an animal usually described as nocturnal may become diurnal to avoid predators, competition, or heat.

In plants

In botany, diurnal describes flowers that open during the day and close at night, a pattern often adapted to daytime pollinators such as bees; the reverse pattern suits moth-pollinated plants. Day length also triggers physiological responses such as flowering (photoperiodism).
