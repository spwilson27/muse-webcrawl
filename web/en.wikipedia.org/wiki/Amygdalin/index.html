Amygdalin

NamesD-mandelonitrile-β-D-glucosido-6-β-D-glucoside
FormulaC20H27NO11
Molar mass457.43 g/mol
AppearanceWhite crystalline powder

Amygdalin is a naturally occurring chemical compound found in many plants, most notably in the seeds (kernels) of apricots, bitter almonds, apples, peaches, cherries and plums. It is classified as a cyanogenic glycoside because each amygdalin molecule includes a nitrile group which can be released as the toxic cyanide anion by the action of enzymes or acid.

Chemistry

Amygdalin consists of a benzaldehyde cyanohydrin moiety (mandelonitrile) linked to two glucose units. In the presence of the enzyme beta-glucosidase and water, amygdalin breaks down into glucose, benzaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide. This reaction is responsible for the bitter almond odour of crushed kernels and for the compound's toxicity when ingested in quantity.

History and medical claims

Amygdalin was isolated from bitter almond seeds in 1830 by the French chemists Pierre-Jean Robiquet and Antoine Boutron-Charlard. In the 1950s a semi-synthetic form, marketed as Laetrile, was promoted as a treatment for cancer and sometimes mislabelled "vitamin B17". Clinical studies, including a 1982 trial sponsored by the U.S. National Cancer Institute, found no anticancer activity, and its use is associated with a risk of cyanide poisoning. Regulatory agencies in many countries prohibit its sale as a medicine.

Toxicity

Because amygdalin releases hydrogen cyanide on digestion, eating large numbers of bitter apricot kernels or bitter almonds can cause symptoms of cyanide poisoning, including nausea, headache, dizziness and, in severe cases, respiratory failure. Health authorities advise limiting consumption of bitter apricot kernels.
