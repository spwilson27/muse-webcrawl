The wind triangle is a vector diagram used in air navigation to account for the effect of wind on an aircraft in flight. It consists of three vectors: the air vector (the aircraft's heading and true airspeed), the ground vector (the aircraft's track and ground speed), and the wind vector (wind direction and speed). Because the ground vector is the vector sum of the air vector and the wind vector, knowing any two sides of the triangle allows the third to be determined, graphically or with a flight computer such as the E6B.

Use in navigation

A pilot planning to fly a desired track (course) over the ground in the presence of wind must head the aircraft partly into the wind; the angle between heading and track is the drift angle (or wind correction angle). The wind also adds to or subtracts from the aircraft's speed over the ground, changing fuel consumption and estimated time of arrival. In flight, comparing the planned ground speed and track with the actual progress lets the navigator determine the actual wind aloft.

Elements

  • Heading — the direction the aircraft's nose points, measured in degrees from north.
  • True airspeed (TAS) — the aircraft's speed through the air mass.
  • Track (course over ground) — the actual path over the Earth's surface.
  • Ground speed — the rate of progress along the track.
  • Wind velocity — reported by the direction from which the wind blows and its speed, e.g. 270/25.

Example

An aircraft with a true airspeed of 120 knots wishing to track 090° (east) in a wind of 270/25 (a 25-knot tailwind component is absent here; a wind from the west pushes it eastward) — in practice the pilot plots the wind vector from the tip of the air vector to obtain the resultant ground vector, yielding a ground speed of about 145 knots on the desired track with little or no drift if the wind is directly behind. Crosswind components instead require a wind correction angle into wind and produce drift downwind if uncorrected.
