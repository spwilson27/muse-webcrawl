John Robin Warren AC (11 June 1937 – 23 July 2024) was an Australian pathologist who, with physician Barry Marshall, discovered in 1982 that the bacterium Helicobacter pylori is the cause of most peptic ulcers — overturning the long-held belief that ulcers were caused by stress, spicy food, and excess acid. The finding revolutionized ulcer treatment, allowing cure with antibiotics. Warren and Marshall shared the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Early life and career

Warren was born in Adelaide and studied medicine at the University of Adelaide, graduating in 1961. After training in clinical pathology he took a position at the Royal Perth Hospital in 1968, where he spent most of his career as a pathologist examining stomach biopsies.

The Helicobacter discovery

In 1979 Warren observed large numbers of small curved bacteria colonizing the lower stomach in about half of his biopsy specimens, and noticed they were consistently associated with inflammation (gastritis). In 1981 he was joined by trainee physician Barry Marshall, and together they studied biopsies from 100 patients, culturing the organism — later named Helicobacter pylori — and showing its association with gastritis and peptic ulceration. Their 1984 Lancet paper was initially met with skepticism; Marshall famously drank a culture of the bacterium in 1984, developed gastritis, and cured himself with antibiotics, fulfilling Koch's postulates.

Recognition and later life

By the mid-1990s antibiotic eradication therapy had become standard, transforming a chronic relapsing disease into a curable infection. Warren received numerous honors, including appointment as a Companion of the Order of Australia in 2007. He retired in 1999 and died in Perth on 23 July 2024, aged 87.
