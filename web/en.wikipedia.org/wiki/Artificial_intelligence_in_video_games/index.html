Artificial intelligence in video games

Game AI
ScopeBehaviour of non-player characters and game agents
Key techniquesFinite-state machines, behaviour trees, pathfinding (A*), steering behaviours, utility systems, Monte Carlo tree search
Notable systemsF.E.A.R. (goal-oriented action planning); The Sims (smart objects/utility); Halo (behaviour trees)

In video games, artificial intelligence (AI) is used to generate responsive, adaptive, or seemingly intelligent behaviours primarily in non-player characters (NPCs), similar to human-like intelligence. Game AI differs from academic artificial intelligence in emphasis: its goal is not optimal decisions but believable, entertaining behaviour that serves the player's experience within tight computational budgets.

Overview

From the earliest arcade games, "AI" in games has largely consisted of hand-authored rules and heuristics. The ghosts in Pac-Man (1980) each follow a distinct targeting rule, producing the impression of personalities. Sports and fighting games use difficulty scaling and reactive scripts, while real-time strategy games combine strategic heuristics with unit-level pathfinding. Because players expect challenge rather than perfection, designers often make agents deliberately fallible, and many games grant computer opponents compensating advantages—sometimes controversially described as the AI "cheating."

Techniques

Movement and pathfinding

Agents must navigate environments while avoiding obstacles and one another. Common approaches include waypoints and navigation meshes for route planning, the A* search algorithm for shortest paths, and steering behaviours (seek, flee, wander, flocking) for moment-to-moment motion.

Decision-making

Finite-state machines model an NPC as a set of states (patrol, chase, attack, flee) with transitions between them. Behaviour trees organise decisions hierarchically and became widespread after their prominent use in the Halo series. Utility-based systems score possible actions against an agent's needs—the approach popularised by The Sims, where "smart objects" advertise the desires they satisfy. Goal-oriented action planning, showcased in F.E.A.R. (2005), lets agents assemble sequences of actions to satisfy goals at runtime. Monte Carlo tree search and related methods are used in digital board games and some strategy titles.

Learning systems

Machine learning has seen selective use: the creatures in Black & White (2001) could be trained by reward and punishment, and the alien in Alien: Isolation (2014) combined a director AI with a learning hunter. Research systems based on reinforcement learning—such as OpenAI Five for Dota 2 and DeepMind's AlphaStar for StarCraft II—have defeated top human professionals, though such compute-intensive approaches remain rare in shipped consumer games. More recently, large language models have been used experimentally to drive free-form NPC dialogue.

Other uses

Beyond NPC behaviour, AI techniques support procedural content generation, animation blending, automated playtesting and difficulty adjustment, matchmaking, and anti-cheat detection.

Criticism and perception

Players frequently judge AI harshly when behaviour appears scripted or unfair, yet they also reward visible "smart" moments such as enemies flanking, fleeing, or communicating. Game designers note that the perceived intelligence of an agent often depends more on clear signalling of its intentions than on the sophistication of its algorithms.
