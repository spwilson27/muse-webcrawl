Battle of Blenheim

The Battle of Blenheim (German: Schlacht bei Höchstädt) was a major battle of the War of the Spanish Succession, fought on 13 August 1704 near the village of Blindheim (called Blenheim in English) in Bavaria. The Grand Alliance army under John Churchill, Duke of Marlborough, and Prince Eugene of Savoy decisively defeated the Franco-Bavarian army of Marshal Tallard and the Elector Maximilian II Emanuel. The victory saved Vienna from the Franco-Bavarian threat, knocked Bavaria out of the war, and shattered the reputation of French military invincibility built up over the reign of Louis XIV. It was one of the turning points of the war and among the most celebrated victories in British military history.

Background

By 1704 the war was going badly for the Alliance: a Franco-Bavarian army threatened Vienna itself. Marlborough conceived the audacious plan of marching his army some 400 km from the Low Countries to the Danube — concealing his true objective even from his Dutch allies — and joined Eugene after storming the Schellenberg heights at Donauwörth. The combined army then sought battle with Tallard's French army, reinforced by Marsin and the Elector of Bavaria, near the Danube villages between Blenheim and Lutzingen.

Battle

Marlborough's plan inverted convention: while Eugene pinned the Franco-Bavarian right in bitter fighting, Marlborough attacked the French flanks at the villages of Blenheim and Oberglau to draw in Tallard's reserves, then launched the decisive blow — a massed cavalry charge across the open centre, where Tallard had fatally weakened his line by crowding infantry into the villages and leaving his cavalry unsupported. After a hard contest in which French cavalry twice recoiled, Marlborough's squadrons broke through, rolled up the French centre, and drove thousands into the Danube. Tallard was captured; the infantry trapped in Blenheim village surrendered en masse. French losses exceeded 30,000 killed, wounded, drowned or captured.

Consequences

Bavaria was occupied and its Elector exiled; the threat to Vienna vanished, and the balance of the war shifted permanently. Marlborough's reputation soared — Queen Anne rewarded him with the Woodstock estate on which Blenheim Palace, named for the battle, was built. The battle also marked the eclipse of French battlefield dominance and the arrival of Britain as a great military power.
