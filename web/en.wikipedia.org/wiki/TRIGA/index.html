TRIGA (Training, Research, Isotopes, General Atomics) is a class of small nuclear research reactors designed and manufactured by General Atomics in San Diego, California. The first TRIGA prototype, the Mark I, achieved criticality on May 3, 1958, and operated until 1997. More than 70 TRIGA reactors have been installed in 24 countries, at universities, hospitals, laboratories, and government facilities, making it one of the most widely used research reactor designs in the world.

Design

TRIGA reactors are pool-type reactors: the core sits near the bottom of a large open tank of water, which serves as coolant, neutron moderator, and radiation shielding. The defining feature of the design is its fuel, uranium zirconium hydride (UZrH), in which low-enriched uranium is intimately mixed with zirconium hydride. The hydrogen in the fuel itself provides prompt moderation with a strong negative temperature coefficient: as the fuel heats up, the neutron spectrum hardens and reactivity falls almost instantaneously.

This physics gives TRIGA reactors a celebrated inherent safety characteristic. A TRIGA can be deliberately "pulsed" by rapidly ejecting a control rod, driving the reactor briefly to power levels thousands of times its steady-state rating — peak powers of several thousand megawatts for a few tens of milliseconds — after which the fuel's temperature feedback shuts the reaction down by itself, with no operator or engineered system intervention. This pulsing capability is used for transient irradiation experiments.

Steady-state TRIGA power levels range from about 100 kilowatts to 16 megawatts thermal (the largest being the TRIGA in Pitesti, Romania). Early models used uranium enriched to about 20 percent; fuel enriched to higher levels was used in some early units under the Atoms for Peace program, and many reactors have since been converted to low-enriched uranium.

History

The TRIGA was developed in 1956–1958 by a team at General Atomics led by physicist Edward Teller, working with a group of young physicists that included Freeman Dyson. Teller insisted the reactor be safe enough that, in his words, it could be given to a university and run by students — "even if a graduate student went mad and pulled out all the control rods, the reactor would shut itself down." The design emerged from the 1956 Atoms for Peace era effort to create a reactor for research and isotope production that did not require elaborate safety systems.

The first production model, the TRIGA Mark II, was widely exported. Notable installations include reactors in Austria, Bangladesh, Brazil, Finland, Germany, Indonesia, Italy, Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, Morocco, the Philippines, Romania, Slovenia, Thailand, Turkey, and Vietnam, as well as dozens in the United States.

Uses

TRIGA reactors are used for training reactor operators and nuclear engineering students, neutron activation analysis, production of radioisotopes for medicine and industry, neutron radiography, materials irradiation testing, and basic research in nuclear physics and chemistry. Because they require comparatively modest infrastructure, many remain in service decades after installation; several have operated for more than 50 years.
