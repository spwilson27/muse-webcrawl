Industrial espionage

Industrial espionage, also known as economic espionage or corporate spying, is a form of espionage conducted for commercial purposes instead of the purely national security purposes of conventional spying. It involves the theft or illicit acquisition of trade secrets, proprietary technology, customer lists, pricing and strategy documents, and other confidential business information.

Targets range from formulas and designs to bid prices and plans.

Methods

Techniques range from recruiting or planting insiders, bribery, burglary and "dumpster diving", through wiretapping and computer intrusion, to the exploitation of former employees. A distinction is often drawn between espionage and legitimate competitive intelligence, which gathers information from open sources within legal and ethical bounds.

State involvement

Some industrial espionage is conducted by states on behalf of domestic industry, or by states seeking technology for military and economic advantage. The United States enacted the Economic Espionage Act of 1996, criminalising the theft of trade secrets, with enhanced penalties where the theft benefits a foreign government or instrumentality. Similar legal regimes, and much controversy over state-sponsored cyber intrusions aimed at commercial targets, exist internationally.

Historical examples

Classic cases include the 18th-century smuggling of silk-production knowledge and silkworms from China, Samuel Slater memorising British textile mill designs and carrying them to America, and Cold War efforts by Soviet intelligence to acquire Western technology through front companies and covert purchase networks.
