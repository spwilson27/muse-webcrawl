Livery company

The livery companies of the City of London are historic trade associations, descended from medieval guilds, that once regulated their respective crafts and trades within the City. There are 111 livery companies, ordered by precedence established in 1515, beginning with the Mercers, Grocers, Drapers, Fishmongers, Goldsmiths, Merchant Taylors and Skinners — the "Great Twelve". Their members, called liverymen, historically elected the Lord Mayor and officers of the City of London, and freemen of the City still play a role in those elections.

History

London's trade guilds grew from religious and craft fraternities of the 12th and 13th centuries and gained royal charters from the 14th century onward. Companies took their name from their distinctive dress, or "livery", worn on ceremonial occasions. At their height they controlled entry to trades, set standards, ran apprenticeships, and provided welfare for members. Their regulatory power waned from the 18th century as trade outgrew the City, but the companies survived as charitable, educational and fellowship bodies.

Modern role

Today the companies are major charitable funders — collectively giving tens of millions of pounds annually — and many support schools, almshouses, universities and the armed forces. Some retain trade functions: the Goldsmiths' Company still operates the London Assay Office and hallmarking; the Fishmongers' Company inspects fish at Billingsgate; the Gunmakers prove firearms; and the Vintners and Dyers perform the annual Swan Upping census on the Thames. New companies continue to be created for modern professions, most recently in areas such as information technology, management consultancy and artificial intelligence.

Order of precedence (first twelve)

  1. Worshipful Company of Mercers
  2. Worshipful Company of Grocers
  3. Worshipful Company of Drapers
  4. Worshipful Company of Fishmongers
  5. Worshipful Company of Goldsmiths
  6. Worshipful Company of Merchant Taylors
  7. Worshipful Company of Skinners
  8. Worshipful Company of Haberdashers
  9. Worshipful Company of Salters
  10. Worshipful Company of Ironmongers
  11. Worshipful Company of Vintners
  12. Worshipful Company of Clothworkers

The Merchant Taylors and Skinners famously disputed sixth and seventh place; the Lord Mayor's 1484 ruling that they alternate each year is the origin of the expression "at sixes and sevens".
