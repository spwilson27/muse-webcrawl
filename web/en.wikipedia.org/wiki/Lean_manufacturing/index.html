Lean manufacturing is a production method aimed primarily at reducing times within the production system as well as response times from suppliers and to customers, by systematically eliminating waste. It is derived from the Toyota Production System (TPS) developed by Taiichi Ohno and Eiji Toyoda at the Japanese automobile manufacturer Toyota between the late 1940s and the 1970s. The term "lean" was popularised by the 1990 book The Machine That Changed the World, a study of the global automobile industry by researchers at MIT.

Principles

Lean thinking is commonly summarised in five principles: specify value from the customer's perspective; map the value stream; create flow; establish pull (produce only what is demanded, as in just-in-time production and kanban); and pursue perfection through continuous improvement (kaizen).

Toyota identified seven wastes (muda): transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, overprocessing, and defects; an eighth, unused employee creativity, is often added. Core tools include 5S workplace organisation, poka-yoke error-proofing, jidoka (automation with a human touch), standardised work, and value-stream mapping.

Adoption and criticism

Lean methods have spread from automotive manufacturing to aerospace, healthcare, software development (where they influenced agile methods and the "lean startup" movement), and services. Critics argue lean can become a cost-cutting programme that intensifies work, and that minimal inventories create fragility to supply disruptions.
