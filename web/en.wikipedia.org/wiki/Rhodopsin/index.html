Rhodopsin, also known as visual purple, is a protein encoded by the RHO gene in humans. It is the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that serves as the light-sensitive pigment of the rod cells in the retina, and is responsible for the first events in the perception of light. Rhodopsin is extremely sensitive — a rod cell can respond to a single photon — making it essential for vision in low-light (scotopic) conditions.

Structure

Rhodopsin consists of the protein opsin (348 amino acids in cattle and humans), a seven-transmembrane α-helical apoprotein, covalently bound via a protonated Schiff base to the chromophore 11-cis-retinal, derived from vitamin A. It was the first GPCR whose three-dimensional structure was determined by X-ray crystallography (bovine rhodopsin, in 2000), and it remains the structural archetype of the largest family of signalling receptors.

Function

When rhodopsin absorbs a photon, the 11-cis-retinal isomerises to all-trans-retinal, forcing a conformational change that produces metarhodopsin II, the active state. This activates the G protein transducin, which stimulates a phosphodiesterase that hydrolyses cyclic GMP; cGMP-gated sodium channels close and the rod hyperpolarises — the visual signal. After signalling, rhodopsin is phosphorylated by rhodopsin kinase and capped by arrestin, then regenerated with fresh 11-cis-retinal from the retinal pigment epithelium. The bleaching and regeneration cycle explains the time course of dark adaptation.

History

Franz Christian Boll observed in 1876 that the frog retina's pigment bleaches in light; Willy Kühne named it "Sehpurpur" (visual purple) and showed its regeneration in darkness.

Clinical significance

More than 150 mutations in the RHO gene cause autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, a progressive degeneration of photoreceptors leading to night blindness and loss of peripheral vision. Vitamin A deficiency impairs rhodopsin regeneration and likewise causes night blindness.
