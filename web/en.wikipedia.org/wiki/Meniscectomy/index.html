Meniscectomy

Meniscectomy is a surgical procedure involving the removal of all or part of a torn meniscus — the C-shaped fibrocartilage that cushions and stabilises the knee joint. It is one of the most commonly performed orthopaedic operations, usually done arthroscopically (through small incisions with the aid of a camera) as day surgery.

Meniscectomy

Arthroscopy of the knee

Type: orthopaedic surgery
Approach: arthroscopic (usual)
Alternatives: meniscal repair, physiotherapy

Types

In a partial meniscectomy, only the torn, unstable portion of the meniscus is trimmed away, preserving as much healthy tissue as possible; this is by far the most common form today. A total meniscectomy removes the entire meniscus and was standard practice before the meniscus's protective role was appreciated; it is now rare because total removal greatly increases contact stress on the articular cartilage and the long-term risk of osteoarthritis.

Indications and evidence

Meniscal tears occur from sports injuries in young patients and from degeneration in older adults. Surgery is generally considered when a tear causes mechanical symptoms such as locking or catching, or pain that fails to respond to conservative treatment. For degenerative tears in middle-aged patients, several randomised trials — most notably the FIDELITY trial (2013) — found arthroscopic partial meniscectomy no better than sham surgery or exercise therapy, prompting guidelines to favour physiotherapy first in that group. Where the tear is in the vascularised outer zone, especially in young patients, meniscal repair is preferred to removal because it preserves the joint.

Recovery

After arthroscopic partial meniscectomy, patients usually bear weight immediately and return to normal activities within a few weeks, guided by physiotherapy to restore strength and range of motion.
