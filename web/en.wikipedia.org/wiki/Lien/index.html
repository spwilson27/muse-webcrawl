Lien

Lien
FieldProperty law, secured transactions
NatureLegal claim or security interest in property
Common typesMechanic's lien, tax lien, judgment lien, maritime lien

A lien (pronounced "leen"; from Old French lien, "bond", from Latin ligamen) is a form of security interest granted over an item of property to secure the payment of a debt or the performance of some other obligation. The owner of the property, who grants the lien, is the lienor, and the person who has the benefit of the lien is the lienee.

Types

  • Consensual liens — created by agreement, such as mortgages and security interests under commercial law.
  • Mechanic's liens (construction liens) — statutory claims for contractors, subcontractors and suppliers who improve real property and are not paid.
  • Tax liens — imposed by governments for unpaid taxes; in the United States the federal tax lien arises by statute upon assessment.
  • Judgment liens — arising when a court judgment is recorded against a debtor's property.
  • Maritime liens — secret, non-possessory claims against vessels for necessaries, seamen's wages and salvage, enforceable in admiralty.
  • Common-law possessory liens — a right to retain goods until a related debt is paid, e.g. a repairer's lien.

Perfection and priority

Depending on the type, a lien may need to be "perfected" — typically by filing a public notice or taking possession — to be effective against third parties. Priority among competing liens often follows rules such as "first in time, first in right", subject to statutory exceptions (for example, purchase-money security interests and certain tax-lien superpriorities).

Enforcement and discharge

If the underlying obligation is not satisfied, many liens can be enforced by foreclosure or a forced sale of the property, applying the proceeds to the debt according to priority. A lien is discharged when the debt is paid or the lien expires, at which point the lienee may record a release or satisfaction.
