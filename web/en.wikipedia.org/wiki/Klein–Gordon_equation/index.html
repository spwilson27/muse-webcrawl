Klein–Gordon equation

The Klein–Gordon equation is the relativistic wave equation for spinless particles.

The Klein–Gordon equation (Klein–Fock–Gordon equation or sometimes Klein–Gordon–Fock equation) is a relativistic wave equation, related to the Schrödinger equation. It is second-order in space and time and manifestly Lorentz-covariant. It is a differential equation version of the relativistic energy–momentum relation, whose solutions describe free spinless particles (spin-0 fields) such as pions and the Higgs boson.

(□ + (mc/ħ)²) ψ = 0,   i.e.   (1/c²) ∂²ψ/∂t² − ∇²ψ + (mc/ħ)²ψ = 0

History

The equation was named after physicists Oskar Klein and Walter Gordon, who proposed it in 1926, although Vladimir Fock also published it independently the same year, and it had appeared earlier in the work of several others including Schrödinger himself. It was initially intended as a relativistic wave equation for electrons, but it gave incorrect fine-structure predictions for hydrogen and appeared to allow negative probabilities and negative energies, which led to its abandonment in favour of the Dirac equation. It was later reinterpreted in quantum field theory as the equation of motion for a scalar field.

Interpretation

Unlike the Dirac equation, which describes spin-1/2 particles, the Klein–Gordon equation describes scalar (spin-0) particles. Its conserved current can have a negative density, which blocked its interpretation as a single-particle probability amplitude. In quantum field theory this difficulty disappears: the field is an operator, the would-be negative-energy solutions correspond to antiparticles, and the conserved quantity is interpreted as electric charge rather than probability.

Free particle solutions

Plane-wave solutions of the form e−i(Et − p·x)/ħ satisfy the relativistic dispersion relation E² = p²c² + m²c⁴. For m = 0 the equation reduces to the ordinary wave equation. The general solution is a superposition of positive- and negative-frequency modes, which in quantum field theory become annihilation and creation operators for particles and antiparticles respectively.

Action

The equation follows from the action for a free real scalar field with Lagrangian density ℒ = ½ (∂μφ ∂μφ − m²φ²) (in natural units), via the Euler–Lagrange equations. Adding interaction terms yields models such as φ⁴ theory, and the real scalar field with a double-well potential plays a central role in the Higgs mechanism, where the scalar is promoted to a complex doublet.
