Paneer (pronounced [pəˈniːr]) is a fresh acid-set cheese common in the Indian subcontinent, made by curdling hot milk with a food acid such as lemon juice, vinegar or citric acid, then draining and pressing the curds. Because it is set by acid and heat rather than rennet, paneer is suitable for lacto-vegetarian diets, and because its proteins are already heat-denatured it does not melt when cooked — it can be fried, grilled or simmered in curries while keeping its shape.

Preparation

Whole milk (buffalo milk gives a firmer, richer result) is brought to the boil, acid is stirred in until the curds separate from the greenish whey, and the curds are strained through muslin, rinsed, and pressed under a weight for one to three hours into a firm block that can be sliced or cubed. Fresh paneer keeps only a few days refrigerated. The unpressed, loose form is called chhena in eastern India and is the base of sweets such as rasgulla and sandesh.

Culinary uses

Paneer is a staple protein in North Indian vegetarian cooking: palak paneer (with spinach), paneer butter masala, shahi paneer, mattar paneer (with peas), kadai paneer, paneer tikka (marinated and grilled in a tandoor or on skewers) and paneer bhurji (scrambled). In Indian restaurants worldwide it often serves as the vegetarian counterpart to chicken dishes.

History

The word derives from Persian panir ("cheese"). The antiquity of paneer in India is debated: acid-curdled milk products are described in early texts, while some food historians credit Portuguese influence in Bengal (for chhena sweets) or Afghan and Persian traditions in the north for the pressed form. It is comparable to other fresh acid-set cheeses — and, as a soft white cheese eaten fresh, distantly to Italy's ricotta, though ricotta is made from reheated whey rather than pressed whole-milk curd.

Nutrition

Paneer is rich in protein and calcium; whole-milk paneer contains roughly 18 g protein and 20 g fat per 100 g, varying with milk and pressing.
