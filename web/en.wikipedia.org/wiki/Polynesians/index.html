Polynesians

Polynesians are an ethnolinguistic group of closely related peoples who are native to Polynesia (islands in the Polynesian Triangle), an expansive region of Oceania in the Pacific Ocean. They trace their early prehistoric origins to Island Southeast Asia and form part of the larger Austronesian ethnolinguistic group, with an initial homeland in Taiwan. They speak the Polynesian languages, a subgroup of the Oceanic branch of the Austronesian family.

Origins

Polynesians are the product of the Austronesian expansion, one of the greatest maritime migrations in human history. Ancestors of the Polynesians, associated with the Lapita culture, moved from Island Southeast Asia into the Bismarck Archipelago by about 1500 BCE and reached the western Polynesian islands of Tonga and Samoa by around 900 BCE. After a long pause in West Polynesia, voyagers settled the rest of the Polynesian Triangle — bounded by Hawaiʻi in the north, Rapa Nui (Easter Island) in the southeast, and Aotearoa (New Zealand) in the southwest — between roughly 1000 and 1300 CE, using double-hulled voyaging canoes and sophisticated non-instrument navigation by stars, swells, and bird flight.

Genetic studies show Polynesians descend primarily from Austronesian-speaking peoples related to Indigenous Taiwanese and northern Philippine populations, with varying degrees of admixture from Papuan-related peoples acquired during the passage through Melanesia. Evidence of contact with South America — including the presence of the sweet potato in Polynesia before European contact and genetic signatures in eastern Polynesian populations — indicates Polynesian voyagers likely reached the Americas around the twelfth century CE.

Peoples and languages

Polynesian peoples include the Māori of New Zealand, Native Hawaiians, Samoans, Tongans, Tahitians, Cook Islands Māori, Tuvaluans, Niueans, Tokelauans, Wallisians and Futunans, the people of Rapa Nui, and the Marquesans, among others. Their languages — Samoan, Tongan, Tahitian, Hawaiian, Māori, Rapa Nui and others — are closely related and largely mutually derived from Proto-Polynesian. In the 21st century, large diaspora communities live in New Zealand, Australia, and the United States; Auckland, New Zealand, is sometimes called the largest Polynesian city in the world.

Culture

Traditional Polynesian societies were organised around extended kin groups and chiefly rank, with complex systems of tapu (sacred restriction), oral genealogies, and highly developed arts including tattooing (tatau), carving, weaving, and dance. Subsistence combined arboriculture and gardening of taro, yam, breadfruit, banana, and coconut with reef and deep-sea fishing and the husbandry of pigs, dogs, and chickens. European contact from the eighteenth century brought disease, depopulation, colonisation, and Christianisation, but the later twentieth century saw a cultural renaissance, including revitalisation of voyaging — symbolised by the canoe Hōkūleʻa's 1976 voyage from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti — and of endangered languages.
