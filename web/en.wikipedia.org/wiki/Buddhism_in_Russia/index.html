Buddhism in Russia is one of the country's recognised "traditional" religions, practised mainly by the Mongolic and Turkic peoples of Siberia and the Volga region — the Buryats, Kalmyks and Tuvans — who follow the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism. Russia is notable for containing Europe's only region where Buddhism is the historically dominant religion, the Republic of Kalmykia.

History

Buddhism reached Buryatia from Mongolia and Tibet in the 17th–18th centuries; in 1741 Empress Elizabeth issued a decree recognising the Buddhist church in the Russian Empire. The Kalmyks, who had migrated to the Volga steppe in the 17th century, likewise maintained temples and ties with Tibet.

Under Stalin in the 1930s, monasteries were destroyed and lamas persecuted; the Kalmyks were deported to Siberia in 1943 and allowed to return only in 1957. A single monastery, Ivolginsky datsan near Ulan-Ude, was permitted to reopen in 1945 as a controlled centre of Soviet Buddhism.

Revival

Since the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, Buddhism has revived strongly. Ivolginsky datsan is the seat of the Pandito Khambo Lama, head of the Buddhist Traditional Sangha of Russia, and is famous for preserving the undecayed body of Dashi-Dorzho Itigilov (died 1927). Kalmykia's capital Elista hosts the Golden Abode of Buddha Shakyamuni (2005), one of the largest Buddhist temples in Europe.
