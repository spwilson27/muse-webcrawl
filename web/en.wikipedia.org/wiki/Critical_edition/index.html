Critical edition

Critical editions print variant readings in an apparatus criticus below the text.

A critical edition is an edition of a text — typically a work surviving in multiple manuscripts or early printings — that has been prepared by collating the surviving witnesses and using textual criticism to establish the most accurate text possible, usually the one closest to the author's original. Critical editions are standard for classical, biblical, medieval and early modern literature, and for the scholarly editing of modern authors' works in history, philosophy and music.

Contents

A critical edition typically includes an introduction explaining the transmission of the text and the principles of the edition; the established text itself; and an apparatus criticus, a system of notes (usually at the foot of the page) recording variant readings in the witnesses, editorial emendations and their sources. Editions may also include a stemma codicum — a family tree of the manuscripts — commentary on interpretation, indices and concordances.

Method

The classical method, associated with Karl Lachmann, proceeds by recensio (surveying and classifying witnesses through their shared errors), examinatio (eliminating copies of surviving manuscripts), and emendatio (correcting the archetype where all witnesses err). Modern editors have debated this approach — Joseph Bédier's "best-text" method, the "copy-text" theory of W. W. Greg and Fredson Bowers, and genetic criticism of drafts each offer alternatives. In music, critical editions (such as the great collected-works series) similarly establish reliable performance texts from autographs and early editions.
