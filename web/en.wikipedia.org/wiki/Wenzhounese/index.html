Wenzhounese (Chinese: 温州话; Wenzhounese: yù), also known as the Oujiang dialect, is a variety of Wu Chinese spoken in and around the city of Wenzhou in southeastern Zhejiang province, China. It is famous within China for its extreme divergence from other varieties: it is mutually unintelligible not only with Mandarin but even with neighbouring Wu dialects such as Shanghainese, and it is often jokingly called "the devil's language" for its difficulty.

Characteristics

Wenzhounese preserves the three-way stop contrast of Middle Chinese (voiceless unaspirated, aspirated and voiced) and has eight tones. It retains many archaic lexical items and grammatical features lost elsewhere, including unusual word order and distinctive classifiers, while its phonology includes sounds such as a voiced dental fricative rare among Sinitic languages. The dialect differs considerably between urban Wenzhou and surrounding counties such as Rui'an and Yueqing.

Use and diaspora

Because of Wenzhou's tradition of emigration and entrepreneurship, Wenzhounese-speaking communities exist in many countries, notably in Italy, France, Spain and the United States, where the dialect is used in family and business life. Folklore holds that during wartime Chinese forces used Wenzhounese speakers for secure radio communication because enemy listeners could not understand the dialect, a story often repeated (though poorly documented). Use of the dialect among younger people in Wenzhou is declining under the influence of Mandarin-medium schooling.
