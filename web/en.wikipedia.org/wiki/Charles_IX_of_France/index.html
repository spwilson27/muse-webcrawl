Charles IX of France

Portrait of Charles IX by François Clouet.

Charles IX (27 June 1550 – 30 May 1574) was King of France from 1560 until his death in 1574. The third son of Henry II and Catherine de' Medici, he succeeded his elder brother Francis II at the age of ten, with his mother acting as regent. His reign was dominated by the French Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots, and by the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, in which thousands of Protestants were killed in Paris and the provinces.

Catherine's regency pursued a policy of religious compromise, embodied in the Edict of January 1562, but civil war broke out that year after the massacre of Protestant worshippers at Vassy. Charles was declared of age in 1563, though his mother remained dominant. Three civil wars followed in a decade, punctuated by peace edicts — Amboise (1563), Longjumeau (1568), and Saint-Germain (1570), which granted the Huguenots substantial concessions and led to the marriage of the king's sister Margaret to the Protestant Henry of Navarre in August 1572.

St. Bartholomew's Day massacre

Days after the wedding, a failed attempt on the life of the Huguenot leader Admiral Coligny alarmed the court. On the night of 23–24 August 1572, Charles, under pressure from his mother and councillors, authorized the killing of the Huguenot leadership in Paris; the targeted murders spiralled into a general massacre of Protestants in the capital and then in provincial cities, with total deaths estimated in the thousands to tens of thousands. Charles's responsibility and state of mind during the massacre have been debated ever since; he reportedly suffered remorse and deteriorating health thereafter. A fourth religious war followed, ended by the Edict of Boulogne in 1573.

Death and legacy

Charles died of tuberculosis at the Château de Vincennes in May 1574, aged 23, leaving no legitimate son; his only child by his wife Elisabeth of Austria was a daughter, Marie Elisabeth. He was succeeded by his brother, Henry, Duke of Anjou, who returned from Poland, where he had briefly been king, to reign as Henry III. Charles is also remembered as a patron of the Pléiade poets and as the king who, by the Edict of Roussillon (1564), fixed 1 January as the start of the year in France.
