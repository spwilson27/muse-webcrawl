Geometric progression

A geometric progression with ratio 1/2

In mathematics, a geometric progression, also known as a geometric sequence, is a sequence of non-zero numbers where each term after the first is found by multiplying the previous one by a fixed number called the common ratio. For example, 2, 6, 18, 54, … is a geometric progression with common ratio 3, and 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, … has common ratio 1/2.

General form

If the first term is a and the common ratio is r, the n-th term is a rn−1. The sum of the first n terms is a(rn − 1)/(r − 1) for r ≠ 1. When |r| < 1, the infinite geometric series converges to a/(1 − r) — the fact behind Zeno's paradoxes and the classic example 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ⋯ = 1.

Properties

Each term is the geometric mean of its neighbours. Geometric sequences grow (or decay) exponentially, in contrast to the linear growth of an arithmetic progression. They appear in compound interest, radioactive decay, population models, and the analysis of algorithms.

History

Geometric progressions appear in ancient Egyptian and Babylonian mathematics; Euclid's Elements treats them in Books VIII and IX, and the Rhind papyrus contains a famous problem involving a geometric progression of houses, cats, mice and grain.
