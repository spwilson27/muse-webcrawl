Value (economics)

In economics, value is the importance, worth or usefulness of a good or service. Classical economics drew a sharp distinction between value in use (a thing's utility) and value in exchange (what it can command in trade, reflected in its price) — a distinction Adam Smith illustrated with the paradox that water, though supremely useful, is cheap, while diamonds are dear though of little practical use. Explaining what determines exchange value was the central problem of the theory of value.

Theories of value

Labour theory of value. Adam Smith, David Ricardo and later Karl Marx held that the exchange value of a commodity is rooted in the labour required to produce it. Marx refined the idea into "socially necessary labour time" and used it as the foundation of his analysis of surplus value and profit in Capital.

Marginal utility theory. In the "marginal revolution" of the 1870s, William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras replaced objective cost-based explanations with subjective ones: value arises from the utility of the marginal unit to the consumer, which resolves the water–diamond paradox because price reflects scarcity-weighted marginal, not total, utility.

Modern view. Neoclassical economics generally treats value as determined jointly by supply (costs) and demand (preferences) at the margin, with prices conveying information about relative scarcity; Alfred Marshall compared the two to the blades of a pair of scissors. Related concepts include market value, intrinsic value in finance, economic value added, and, outside market analysis, ethical notions of value addressed by philosophy and by criticisms of commodification.
