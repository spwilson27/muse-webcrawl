Primus inter pares

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Primus inter pares is a Latin phrase meaning first among equals. It is typically used as an honorary title for someone who is formally equal to other members of their group but is accorded unofficial respect, traditionally of a purely honorary nature, and often given a special role or office in a formal organisation, such as a prime ministry, chairmanship, archbishopric, or presidency.

History

The phrase derives from the title princeps senatus in the Roman Republic, the member of the Senate listed first on the roll, who was asked to speak first on the topic under discussion. Augustus adopted the related title princeps civitatis ("first citizen of the state") to present his preeminence as resting on authority and prestige rather than on formal monarchical power.

Usage

The concept describes, among others:

  • The Prime Minister of the United Kingdom in relation to the Cabinet, whose office historically grew out of chairing fellow ministers.
  • The Archbishop of Canterbury among the bishops of the Anglican Communion.
  • The Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople among the primates of the Eastern Orthodox Church, whose primacy is one of honour.
  • The chair of the United States Federal Reserve among the members of the Federal Open Market Committee.
  • The Chief Justice of the United States, who presides over the Supreme Court but has only one vote like the associate justices.
