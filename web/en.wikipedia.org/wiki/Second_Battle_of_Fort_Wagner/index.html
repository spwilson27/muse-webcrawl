The assault of the 54th Massachusetts on Fort Wagner

The Second Battle of Fort Wagner, fought on 18 July 1863, was an unsuccessful assault by the Union Army on Fort Wagner (Battery Wagner), a Confederate stronghold on Morris Island guarding the southern approaches to Charleston Harbor, South Carolina. The battle is best remembered for the valour of the 54th Massachusetts Infantry, one of the first African-American regiments in the United States Army, whose sacrifice helped transform Northern opinion about Black soldiers.

Background

An earlier attack on 11 July (the First Battle of Fort Wagner) had failed, and Union commander Quincy Gillmore spent the following week bombarding the fort with artillery and naval gunfire. Fort Wagner, built of sand and earth with palmetto-log bombproof shelters, absorbed the punishment remarkably well. Its roughly 1,700-man garrison under Brigadier General William B. Taliaferro waited behind intact parapets.

The assault

At dusk on 18 July, after the heaviest bombardment yet, about 5,000 Union troops attacked along the narrow beach. The assault was led by the 54th Massachusetts under Colonel Robert Gould Shaw, followed by brigades under George Crockett Strong and Haldimand Putnam. Confined to a narrow front between the sea and a marsh, and raked by musketry and artillery, the attackers reached the parapet and fought hand to hand, but could not take the fort and were driven back with heavy loss.

Colonel Shaw was killed on the parapet, and the 54th lost about 270 of some 600 men engaged, including Captain Lewis Douglass, son of Frederick Douglass, who survived. Sergeant William H. Carney, though wounded several times, kept the regimental colours from touching the ground and later became the first African American to earn the Medal of Honor for an action of this period. Confederate losses were comparatively light, about 174.

Aftermath and legacy

Gillmore resorted to siege operations, digging parallels toward the fort; after weeks of bombardment and the threat of the parallels reaching its ditch, the Confederates evacuated Fort Wagner and Morris Island on the night of 6–7 September 1863.

The 54th Massachusetts' conduct at Fort Wagner powerfully advanced the acceptance of Black troops in the Union Army; recruitment surged, and more than 180,000 African Americans ultimately served. The battle was depicted in the 1989 film Glory, and the Robert Gould Shaw Memorial in Boston commemorates Shaw and his men.
