A scrip (or chit in India) is any substitute for legal tender. It is often a form of credit. Scrips have been created for a number of reasons, including company payment for employees in company towns, where regular money may be in short supply, and as a means of fundraising. Scrip behaved as a token currency that was usually redeemable only at a specific store or set of stores, or for a specific purpose.

The term derives from the word "subscription receipt", and in North America and the United Kingdom has frequently been applied to the fractional shares issued when a stock dividend is paid. Land scrip was a right to acquire public land in the United States, issued as compensation for military service; in Canada, Métis scrip was issued by the government in the 19th and early 20th centuries in an attempt to extinguish Indigenous land claims.

Company scrip

In the United States, company scrip was a feature of coal mining, logging and other isolated industrial communities. Employers paid workers in scrip redeemable only at the company store, which often charged inflated prices; the practice kept workers in debt to the company ("I owe my soul to the company store", as the song "Sixteen Tons" puts it). A number of US states and other jurisdictions eventually regulated or banned payment in scrip, and the British Truck Acts had earlier required wages to be paid in cash.

Other forms

During the Great Depression, hundreds of American municipalities and organisations issued emergency clearing-house certificates and depression scrip to relieve cash shortages. In education, schools have used scrip as rewards; fundraising scrip programmes sell gift cards at face value with a percentage donated to the sponsoring organisation. Land scrip, military bounty scrip, dividend scrip and casino chips are further examples of substitutes or claims denominated in money but issued by non-state bodies.
