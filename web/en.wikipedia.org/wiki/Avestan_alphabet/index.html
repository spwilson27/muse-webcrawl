Avestan alphabet

The Avestan alphabet (known in Middle Persian as dēn dibīrih, "religious writing") is a writing system developed during Iran's Sasanian era (226–651 CE) to render the Avestan language, the liturgical language of Zoroastrianism, in written form. It was used to compile the canon of the Avesta at that period.

Characteristics

Unlike the consonantal Pahlavi scripts from which it derived, the Avestan alphabet is a full alphabet with distinct letters for vowels: it comprises 37 consonant and 16 vowel characters in its developed form, allowing the precise phonetic recording of a language previously transmitted orally for over a millennium. Letters are not joined within words, and the script is written right to left. A related cursive-derived script, Pazend, was later used for Middle Persian Zoroastrian texts rendered in Avestan letters.

History

According to tradition, the Sasanian king Shapur II (309–379) or a later monarch commissioned the writing down of the orally preserved Avesta; scholars place the invention of the alphabet around the 4th century CE. Manuscripts in the script survive from Zoroastrian communities in Iran and India (the Parsis). The alphabet was encoded in the Unicode Standard in 2009 (version 5.2).
