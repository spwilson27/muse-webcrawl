Shigella

GenusShigella
FamilyEnterobacteriaceae
Type speciesShigella dysenteriae
DiseaseShigellosis (bacillary dysentery)

Shigella is a genus of Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, non-motile, rod-shaped bacteria closely related to Escherichia coli. It is the causative agent of shigellosis (bacillary dysentery) in humans and other primates. The genus is named after the Japanese bacteriologist Kiyoshi Shiga, who discovered Shigella dysenteriae in 1897 during a severe dysentery epidemic in Japan.

Species

Four species (or serogroups) are recognised: S. dysenteriae (group A), S. flexneri (group B), S. boydii (group C), and S. sonnei (group D). S. flexneri is the most common cause of shigellosis in the developing world, while S. sonnei predominates in industrialised countries. S. dysenteriae type 1 produces Shiga toxin and can cause severe epidemic dysentery.

Pathogenesis

Shigella has an exceptionally low infectious dose — as few as 10 to 100 organisms. After ingestion, the bacteria invade the epithelial cells of the colon via M cells, spread from cell to cell using actin-based motility, and provoke an intense inflammatory response that ulcerates the mucosa. The result is frequent small-volume bloody, mucoid stools with abdominal cramps and tenesmus. Complications include dehydration, toxic megacolon, reactive arthritis, and, with S. dysenteriae, haemolytic uraemic syndrome.

Epidemiology and treatment

Shigellosis is estimated to cause well over a hundred million infections and many tens of thousands of deaths annually, mostly among young children in low-income settings, and spreads by the faecal–oral route through contaminated food, water, and hands. Rehydration is the mainstay of treatment; antibiotics shorten illness and bacterial shedding, but resistance — including to fluoroquinolones and azithromycin — is increasingly common. No licensed vaccine is yet available.
