Amyloid

Amyloids are aggregates of proteins characterized by a fibrillar morphology and a cross-β-sheet quaternary structure, in which β-strands run perpendicular to the long axis of the fibril. Many proteins can form amyloid, and such aggregates are associated with more than 30 human diseases, collectively called amyloidoses, including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and type 2 diabetes. The name derives from the Latin amylum (starch), because the deposits were originally mistaken for starch when Rudolf Virchow coined the term in 1854.

Amyloid fibrils show a characteristic unbranched, rope-like morphology.

Structure

Amyloid fibrils are typically 7–13 nanometres in diameter, composed of two or more protofilaments twisted together. Their cross-β structure gives characteristic X-ray fibre diffraction reflections at about 4.7 Å (strand spacing) and 10 Å (sheet spacing). Amyloid binds dyes such as Congo red — producing apple-green birefringence under polarized light — and thioflavin T, properties used in histological diagnosis.

Diseases

In Alzheimer's disease, the amyloid-β peptide aggregates into extracellular plaques; in systemic amyloidoses, precursor proteins such as immunoglobulin light chains (AL amyloidosis) or transthyretin (ATTR amyloidosis) deposit in organs including the heart, kidneys, and nerves. Prion diseases involve the amyloid-like aggregation of the prion protein. Not all amyloid is pathological: functional amyloids occur in bacteria (curli), fungi, and in human melanosomes.

Formation

Amyloid formation generally follows nucleation-dependent kinetics, with a lag phase, rapid elongation by monomer addition to fibril ends, and secondary processes such as fibril fragmentation and surface-catalysed nucleation that generate new aggregates. Soluble oligomeric intermediates are thought to be important toxic species in several diseases.
