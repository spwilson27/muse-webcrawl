Ophiolite

An ophiolite is a section of oceanic crust and the underlying upper mantle that has been uplifted and exposed above sea level, typically emplaced onto continental crust during plate collisions. The name comes from the Greek ophis (snake), referring to the green, scaly appearance of the altered rocks such as serpentinite.

An idealised ophiolite sequence, from top to bottom, consists of pelagic sediments, pillow basalts, a sheeted dyke complex, gabbros, and ultramafic peridotites representing the mantle. This stratigraphy matches the structure of oceanic lithosphere inferred from marine geophysics, making ophiolites key evidence in the development of plate tectonics.

Famous examples include the Troodos Massif in Cyprus, the Semail ophiolite in Oman (one of the largest and best preserved), and the Bay of Islands complex in Newfoundland.
