Life estate

In common law and statutory law, a life estate (or lifetime estate) is the ownership of immovable property for the duration of a person's life. It is an estate in land that lasts for the life of the person to whom it is granted (the life tenant). Although ownership is technically temporary, because it ends at death, the life tenant is treated as the owner for many purposes while the estate lasts. When the estate ends, the property passes to a remainderman (if the grant names one) or reverts to the grantor or the grantor's estate (a reversion). The concept descends from feudal land law and is found in England and Wales, the United States, Canada, Australia and other common-law jurisdictions; civil-law systems have related institutions such as the usufruct.

Types

The measuring life is usually the life tenant's own, but an estate pur autre vie ("for another's life") is measured by someone else's lifetime — for example, "to A for the life of B". Life estates are created expressly by deed or will ("to A for life, then to B"), by implication, or by operation of law. Historically the most important legal life estates were dower (a widow's life interest in one-third of her husband's lands) and curtesy (a widower's interest), now largely abolished or replaced by elective-share statutes and homestead rights. The fee tail was a related device for keeping land in a family line.

Rights and duties of the life tenant

A life tenant may possess and use the property, take its rents and profits, and may sell or mortgage their own life interest — but the buyer acquires only an estate pur autre vie that still ends at the original tenant's death. The life tenant owes duties to those who will take after them, chiefly the duty not to commit waste: voluntary (affirmative) waste such as destroying buildings or cutting timber beyond estovers; permissive waste, allowing the property to fall into disrepair; and (in some jurisdictions) ameliorative waste, radically changing the property even for the better. The life tenant must ordinarily pay property taxes, insurance and interest (but not principal) on mortgages, and keep the property in reasonable repair. Under the doctrine illustrated by cases such as Melms v. Pabst Brewing Co., drastic changes in neighbourhood character may excuse acts that would otherwise be waste.

Uses and valuation

Life estates are used in estate planning to let a surviving spouse live in a home for life while guaranteeing that the property ultimately passes to children, to provide for an elderly relative, or in Medicaid planning (e.g. "lady bird" enhanced life estate deeds in some US states, which preserve the grantor's power to sell or revoke). For tax and damages purposes, life estates are valued actuarially using mortality tables — for example in the IRS tables under section 7520 of the US Internal Revenue Code — with the remainder interest valued as the complement.
