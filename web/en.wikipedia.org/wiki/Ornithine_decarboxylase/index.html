The enzyme ornithine decarboxylase (EC 4.1.1.17, ODC) catalyzes the decarboxylation of ornithine (a product of the urea cycle in eukaryotes) to form putrescine. This reaction is the committed and rate-limiting step in the synthesis of polyamines such as spermidine and spermine, which are essential for cell growth, proliferation and differentiation.

Reaction and mechanism

ODC is a pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)-dependent decarboxylase. PLP forms a Schiff base with a lysine residue in the active site and then with the substrate; loss of carbon dioxide from ornithine produces putrescine (1,4-diaminobutane). The enzyme functions as a dimer, with active sites formed at the interface of the two subunits.

Regulation

ODC has one of the shortest half-lives of any mammalian enzyme (roughly 10–30 minutes) and is exquisitely regulated at transcriptional, translational and post-translational levels. Its degradation by the proteasome is unusual in being ubiquitin-independent: it is targeted by the protein antizyme, whose own synthesis is stimulated by polyamines through a programmed ribosomal frameshift. An antizyme inhibitor (AZIN1) can sequester antizyme and stabilise ODC. Growth factors and oncogenes such as MYC strongly induce ODC, and elevated polyamine synthesis is a hallmark of many cancers.

Clinical significance

The irreversible inhibitor eflornithine (α-difluoromethylornithine, DFMO) is a "suicide inhibitor" of ODC used to treat African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness), because the parasite depends heavily on polyamines for growth, and is also used topically to reduce facial hair growth. DFMO has been investigated and used as a chemopreventive and maintenance agent in cancers such as neuroblastoma and colorectal adenomas. Rare gain-of-function variants in ODC1 cause a developmental disorder (Bachmann–Bupp syndrome) characterised by alopecia, developmental delay and polyamine accumulation.
