Natural theology

Paley's watchmaker analogy is the best-known argument of natural theology.

Natural theology is a type of theology that seeks to provide arguments for theological topics — principally the existence and attributes of God — based on reason and the observation of nature, without recourse to supernatural revelation such as scripture or religious experience. It contrasts with revealed theology, which takes sacred texts as authoritative. Natural theology has been a major strand of Western philosophy from classical antiquity through the Enlightenment.

Classical arguments

  • Cosmological arguments — infer a first cause or necessary being from the existence and contingency of the universe; classically formulated in Thomas Aquinas's "Five Ways".
  • Teleological (design) arguments — infer a designer from apparent order and purpose in nature; famously expressed by William Paley's watchmaker analogy in Natural Theology (1802).
  • Ontological arguments — Anselm of Canterbury's a priori argument from the concept of a greatest conceivable being.
  • Moral arguments — infer God from the existence of objective moral values or conscience.

History

Greek philosophers such as Plato and Aristotle reasoned about a prime mover and divine order from nature alone. Medieval scholastics integrated these arguments with Christian doctrine. Natural theology flourished in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Britain, where "physico-theology" celebrated divine design in anatomy and astronomy; the Bridgewater Treatises of the 1830s continued this tradition. David Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779) mounted a celebrated critique of the design argument, Immanuel Kant argued that theoretical reason could not prove God's existence, and Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection later removed the need for a designer to explain biological adaptation. In the twentieth century, Karl Barth famously rejected natural theology from within Protestant theology, while other thinkers — and later proponents of "fine-tuning" arguments from cosmology — have continued to defend forms of it.
