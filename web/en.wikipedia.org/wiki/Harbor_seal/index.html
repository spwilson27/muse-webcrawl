The harbor seal (Phoca vitulina), also called the common seal, is a true seal (family Phocidae) found along temperate and Arctic marine coastlines of the Northern Hemisphere. It is the most widely distributed species of pinniped in the world, occurring in coastal waters of the northern Atlantic and Pacific Oceans and adjacent seas such as the Baltic and North Seas.

Description

Individual harbor seals possess a unique pattern of spots, either light on a dark background or dark on a light background, and are variably coloured from brownish-grey to tan. Adults reach 1.6–1.9 m (5.2–6.2 ft) in length and weigh 55–170 kg (120–370 lb), males being slightly larger than females. They have V-shaped nostrils, a rounded, earless head and no external ear flaps, distinguishing them from eared seals (sea lions and fur seals). Their bodies and flippers are short, with flattened, clawed digits.

Distribution and habitat

Five subspecies are generally recognised, including P. v. vitulina of the eastern Atlantic, P. v. richardii of the eastern Pacific, and the rare freshwater Ungava seal of northern Quebec lakes. Harbor seals favour sheltered coastal haul-out sites — sandbanks, estuaries, rocky islets and beaches — close to their feeding areas, and typically remain faithful to familiar resting spots, foraging within a few tens of kilometres.

Behaviour and life history

Harbor seals are generally solitary at sea but gather in groups on land during pupping and moulting seasons. They feed opportunistically on fish such as herring, hake, sand lance and flatfish, as well as squid and crustaceans, diving typically to depths of 20–100 m for a few minutes, though dives beyond 500 m have been recorded. Females give birth to a single pup each year after an approximately 11-month pregnancy including delayed implantation; pups are born well developed, can swim within hours and are weaned after about four to six weeks on extremely fat-rich milk. Females live 30–35 years, males 20–25.

Population and conservation

The global population is estimated at roughly 350,000–500,000 individuals and the species is listed as Least Concern, though regional populations — notably in the Baltic Sea and parts of Alaska — have suffered serious declines from disease outbreaks (phocine distemper virus killed tens of thousands in the North Sea in 1988 and 2002), pollution and bycatch. Harbor seals are protected in the United States under the Marine Mammal Protection Act and in Europe under the Habitats Directive.
