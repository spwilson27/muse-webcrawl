The Consolidated B-24 Liberator is an American heavy bomber designed by Consolidated Aircraft of San Diego, California. It was used extensively in World War II. Designed in early 1939 as a modernized successor to the B-17 Flying Fortress, it was the most produced American military aircraft of all time — over 18,482 built — and one of the most widely used multi-role aircraft of the war.

Design

The B-24 was designed around its high-aspect-ratio Davis wing, which gave it outstanding range and payload for the era. It featured a distinctive twin tail and a deep fuselage housing a cavernous bomb bay with rolling bomb bay doors. Powered by four Pratt & Whitney R-1830 Twin Wasp engines, it could carry 8,800 lb of bombs over a combat radius of about 1,100 miles.

Operational history

The B-24 served in every theater of the war: it was the workhorse of the Eighth Air Force's daylight bombing campaign over Germany, of anti-submarine patrols over the Atlantic (closing the "Mid-Atlantic gap" with very-long-range PB4Y variants), of the strategic bombing of Japan, and of the famous low-level Operation Tidal Wave raid on the Ploiești oil refineries in Romania on 1 August 1943 — one of the most costly air raids in American history.

The maritime patrol version, the PB4Y-2 Privateer, and the C-87 Liberator Express transport derivative served in large numbers. The RAF, RCAF, RAAF and other Allied air forces operated the type, including the RAF Coastal Command anti-submarine squadrons.

Survivors

Only a handful of B-24s survive in museums; the best-known airworthy example is Diamond Lil, operated by the Commemorative Air Force.
