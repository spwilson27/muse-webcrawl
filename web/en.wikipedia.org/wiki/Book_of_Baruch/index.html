The Book of Baruch is a deuterocanonical book of the Bible, attributed to Baruch ben Neriah, the scribe and companion of the prophet Jeremiah. It is included in the canon of the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches but regarded as apocryphal by Protestants and absent from the Hebrew Bible. The book is a composite work, probably assembled between the 2nd and 1st centuries BCE, set in Babylon after the destruction of Jerusalem in 587/586 BCE.

Contents

Baruch consists of several distinct sections: a prose introduction in which the exiles confess their sins and send the book with a collection for the Temple in Jerusalem; a prayer of confession and petition (1:15–3:8); a poem in praise of Wisdom, identified with the Torah (3:9–4:4); a poem of consolation promising Jerusalem's restoration (4:5–5:9); and, in many editions, the Letter of Jeremiah against idolatry, appended as chapter 6 (numbered separately in some traditions).

Authorship and canon

Although Baruch was a historical figure, scholars generally regard the book as pseudepigraphal, reflecting the theology of a later age. It survives in Greek in the Septuagint and was cited by early Church Fathers such as Irenaeus and Clement of Alexandria, sometimes as part of Jeremiah. The Council of Trent affirmed its place in the Catholic canon in 1546.
