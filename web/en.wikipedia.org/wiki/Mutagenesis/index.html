Mutagenesis

Mutagenesis is a process by which the genetic information of an organism is changed by the production of a mutation. It may occur spontaneously in nature, or as a result of exposure to mutagens. It can also be achieved experimentally using laboratory procedures.

Natural mutagenesis

Spontaneous mutations arise from errors in DNA replication, spontaneous chemical changes in bases such as depurination and deamination, and the activity of transposable elements. Most such changes are corrected by DNA repair systems; those that remain contribute to genetic variation, evolution, and, in some cases, disease, including cancer.

Induced mutagenesis

Exposure to mutagens increases mutation rates above the spontaneous background. Physical mutagens include ultraviolet and ionising radiation; chemical mutagens include base analogues, alkylating agents, and intercalating dyes; biological mutagens include certain viruses and transposons. The Ames test uses bacteria to screen chemicals for mutagenic — and therefore potentially carcinogenic — activity.

Laboratory techniques

In the laboratory, mutagenesis is a fundamental tool of genetics and molecular biology. Random mutagenesis of whole genomes, followed by screening or selection, can reveal gene function. Site-directed mutagenesis, developed in the 1970s and recognised by the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to Michael Smith, allows precise changes at chosen positions in a DNA sequence; error-prone PCR, cassette mutagenesis, and CRISPR-based editing extend this precision. In plant breeding, radiation and chemical mutagenesis since the 1920s have produced thousands of crop varieties with improved traits.
