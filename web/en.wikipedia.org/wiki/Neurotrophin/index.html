Neurotrophin

Neurotrophins are a family of proteins that induce the survival, development, and function of neurons. They belong to a class of growth factors, secreted proteins that can signal particular cells to survive, differentiate, or grow. Growth factors such as neurotrophins that promote the survival of neurons are known as neurotrophic factors.

The term neurotrophin usually refers to four structurally related proteins in mammals: nerve growth factor (NGF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), neurotrophin-3 (NT-3), and neurotrophin-4 (NT-4). They act through two kinds of receptors: the high-affinity Trk receptor tyrosine kinases, and the low-affinity p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR), a member of the tumour necrosis factor receptor family.

The neurotrophic hypothesis

Neurotrophins were discovered through the work of Rita Levi-Montalcini, Viktor Hamburger, and Stanley Cohen, who showed that developing neurons compete for limiting amounts of target-derived NGF: neurons that obtain enough survive, while the rest die by apoptosis. This "neurotrophic hypothesis" explained how neuronal numbers are matched to their targets during development. Levi-Montalcini and Cohen shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of growth factors.

Neurotrophins are secreted as proneurotrophins that can be cleaved to mature forms; notably, proneurotrophins binding p75NTR in complex with sortilin-family receptors can trigger cell death — the opposite of the survival signal from mature neurotrophins through Trk receptors.

Functions in the adult nervous system

In adults, BDNF is released in an activity-dependent manner and is essential for long-term potentiation and memory formation; altered BDNF signalling has been linked to depression and neurodegenerative disease. NGF acting on nociceptors sensitises pain pathways, and anti-NGF antibodies have been developed as analgesics. Neurotrophin imbalances are also implicated in Alzheimer's disease, Huntington's disease, and peripheral neuropathies.
