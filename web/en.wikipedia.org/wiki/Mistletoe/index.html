Mistletoe

Mistletoe is the common name for obligate hemiparasitic plants in the order Santalales, especially the European Viscum album and the American Phoradendron species. Mistletoes attach to the stems or branches of a host tree or shrub by a specialised root-like structure, the haustorium, from which they draw water and mineral nutrients, while photosynthesizing for themselves — hence "hemiparasitic".

Biology

Mistletoe seeds are coated in a sticky substance, viscin, and are spread chiefly by birds — notably the mistle thrush in Europe and cedar waxwings and phainopeplas in North America — which digest the berries and deposit the seeds on branches, where they germinate and penetrate the bark. Most mistletoes have leathery evergreen leaves and inconspicuous flowers; their fruits are berries that are toxic to humans to varying degrees. Heavy infestations weaken and can kill host trees, yet mistletoes are also keystone resources in many woodlands, providing food and nesting sites for birds and insects.

Culture

Mistletoe figures prominently in European folklore. Pliny the Elder described the Druids cutting mistletoe from oak with a golden sickle in a ritual of sacrifice and blessing. In Norse myth, the god Baldr was killed by a dart of mistletoe, the one thing that had not sworn an oath not to harm him. The plant's evergreen persistence in winter made it a symbol of fertility and life; the custom of kissing under the mistletoe at Christmas, with a berry plucked for each kiss, became established in England in the 18th and 19th centuries.
