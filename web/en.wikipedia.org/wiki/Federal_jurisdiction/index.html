Federal jurisdiction

Federal jurisdiction in the United States is the legal authority of federal courts to hear cases. Article III of the Constitution defines the outer bounds of the judicial power of the United States, and Congress may grant the lower federal courts less than that full scope.

Legal basisU.S. Constitution, Article III; 28 U.S.C.
Main typesFederal question, diversity, admiralty
CourtsDistrict courts, courts of appeals, Supreme Court

Types

Federal question jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1331) covers cases arising under the Constitution, federal laws or treaties. Diversity jurisdiction (§ 1332) covers disputes between citizens of different states where the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000. Other grants include admiralty, bankruptcy, patent, federal crimes and cases involving the United States as a party.

Concurrent and exclusive jurisdiction

Many federal claims may also be heard in state courts (concurrent jurisdiction), and defendants may remove qualifying cases to federal court. Some matters — patents, federal crimes, bankruptcy — are exclusively federal.

Limits

Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction: they require standing, a live case or controversy, and are barred by doctrines such as sovereign immunity, the Eleventh Amendment, abstention and the Rooker–Feldman doctrine. Subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be waived.
