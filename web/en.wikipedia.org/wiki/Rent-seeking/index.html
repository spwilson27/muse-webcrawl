Rent-seeking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rent-seeking is the act of growing one's existing wealth by manipulating the social or political environment without creating new wealth. It occurs when an entity seeks to gain added wealth without any reciprocal contribution of productivity. Rent-seeking activities have negative effects on the rest of society because they result in the misallocation of resources.

The term was coined by Anne Osborn Krueger in her 1974 paper "The Political Economy of the Rent-Seeking Society", while the underlying concept traces to Gordon Tullock's 1967 work on tariffs, monopolies and theft. The term rent here refers to economic rent — income earned beyond the minimum needed to keep a factor of production in its current use — rather than to payment for the use of land or property.

Examples

Common examples of rent-seeking include:

  • Lobbying the government for tax exemptions, subsidies, or grants without an intent to use them productively.
  • Obtaining a government-enforced monopoly, quota, or tariff that restricts competition.
  • Occupational licensing campaigns by incumbent professionals that raise barriers to entry for newcomers.
  • Regulatory capture, in which a regulated industry influences the agency meant to oversee it.

A classic example is a firm that lobbies for import restrictions on a competing foreign product: the resulting higher prices transfer wealth from consumers to the protected firm, while the resources spent on lobbying are a deadweight loss to society.

The Tullock paradox

Gordon Tullock observed that the sums actually spent on rent-seeking are surprisingly small compared with the value of the rents sought — the so-called Tullock paradox. Proposed explanations include risk aversion among rent-seekers, restrictions on donations to politicians, and the fact that politicians may be unable to credibly commit to delivering favours.

Consequences

Rent-seeking diverts talent and capital from productive enterprise into unproductive political competition. In developing economies it has been linked to slower growth, corruption, and the persistence of inefficient state-granted privileges. The related concept of directly unproductive profit-seeking (DUP) activities, developed by Jagdish Bhagwati, covers rent-seeking, tariff evasion, and similar practices.
