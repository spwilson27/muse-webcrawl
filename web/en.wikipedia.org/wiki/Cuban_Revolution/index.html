The Cuban Revolution (Spanish: Revolución cubana) was the military and political overthrow of the government of Cuban dictator Fulgencio Batista by the 26th of July Movement and its allies, culminating on 1 January 1959 when Batista fled the country and the rebel army took power. The revolution continued as the new government under Fidel Castro carried out a sweeping programme of socialist reform, nationalization and alignment with the Soviet Union.

Background

Batista seized power in a 1952 coup, cancelling elections and presiding over a corrupt, repressive regime closely tied to American business and organized-crime interests. On 26 July 1953, Fidel Castro led a failed attack on the Moncada Barracks in Santiago de Cuba; imprisoned, then exiled to Mexico, he organized a guerrilla expedition.

The guerrilla war

In December 1956 Castro and 81 followers landed in Cuba from the yacht Granma. Most were killed, but a remnant — including Raúl Castro, Che Guevara and Camilo Cienfuegos — established a guerrilla base in the Sierra Maestra. Over 1957–58 the rebels won rural support, while urban opposition and Batista's brutality eroded the regime. After the failure of Batista's summer 1958 offensive (Operation Verano), the rebels advanced; Guevara's victory at Santa Clara in late December 1958 decided the war.

Aftermath

The revolutionary government expropriated land and foreign-owned property, prompting the United States embargo and the failed Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961. Cuba's alliance with the USSR led to the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962. The revolution reshaped Cuban society and became an inspiration — and a cautionary tale — for movements across Latin America.
