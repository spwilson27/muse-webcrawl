Korean martyrs

The Korean Martyrs are the victims of religious persecution against the Catholic Church in Korea during the 19th century. Between 1801 and 1866, in successive waves of persecution under the Joseon dynasty, thousands of Korean Catholics — together with French missionary priests and bishops — were executed for refusing to renounce their faith and perform Confucian ancestral rites. Estimates of the total number of martyrs range from about 8,000 to 10,000.

Background

Uniquely in Church history, Catholicism in Korea began not with foreign missionaries but with Korean scholars: envoys to Beijing in the 1780s brought back Jesuit books, and a lay community baptised its own members before the first priest arrived. The Joseon state, built on Neo-Confucian orthodoxy, treated the new faith as a subversive heterodoxy — above all because converts refused the ancestral rites (jesa) owed to parents and king.

Persecutions and canonisation

Major persecutions struck in 1801 (Sinyu), 1839 (Gihae), 1846 (Byeongo), and 1866 (Byeongin), the last triggered partly by Korea's confrontation with France. Among the most famous victims is Saint Andrew Kim Taegon, the first Korean-born Catholic priest, executed in 1846 at age 25.

In 1984, during the first papal visit to Korea, Pope John Paul II canonised 103 martyrs in Seoul — an unprecedented canonisation outside Rome — including Andrew Kim, the lay apostle Paul Chong Hasang, and ten French missionaries of the Paris Foreign Missions Society. A further 124 martyrs were beatified by Pope Francis in 2014. Their feast day, the Memorial of Saints Andrew Kim Tae-gŏn, Paul Chŏng Ha-sang, and Companions, is kept on 20 September.
