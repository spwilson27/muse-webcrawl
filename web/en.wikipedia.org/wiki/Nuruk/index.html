Nuruk

OriginKorea
TypeFermentation starter
Main ingredientsWheat, rice or barley; wild microorganisms
Used inMakgeolli, cheongju, soju and other Korean alcoholic drinks

Nuruk (Korean: 누룩) is a traditional Korean fermentation starter used to make alcoholic beverages such as makgeolli, cheongju and soju. It is made from grains — most often wheat, but also rice or barley — which are moistened, shaped into cakes or left loose, and allowed to ferment in the open air so that wild moulds, yeasts and bacteria colonise them. The dried nuruk is later mixed with steamed rice and water to ferment into rice wine.

Function

Unlike the purified yeast cultures used in most Western brewing, nuruk contains a whole community of microorganisms. Moulds such as Aspergillus oryzae and Rhizopus produce enzymes, especially amylases, that break down rice starch into sugars (saccharification), while yeasts convert those sugars to alcohol and lactic acid bacteria add acidity and flavour. Because saccharification and fermentation proceed simultaneously, Korean rice brewing differs from Western beer brewing, in which malting and mashing precede fermentation.

Production and history

Traditionally nuruk was made in late summer: ground wheat was kneaded with water, pressed into round cakes, hung or laid out to ferment for several weeks, then dried and stored for use through the year. Regional and household variations in the microbial community give different nuruk distinct characters. Nuruk has been used in Korea for many centuries, with references in historical texts from the Goryeo and Joseon periods, and it is comparable to other Asian fermentation starters such as Chinese jiuqu and Japanese koji, though nuruk relies on naturally occurring mixed microbes rather than a single cultivated mould. Modern commercial nuruk is produced under controlled conditions, and researchers study its microbiome to standardise quality while preserving the flavour complexity of traditional Korean liquors.
