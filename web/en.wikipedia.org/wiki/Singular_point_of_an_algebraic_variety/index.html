Singular point of an algebraic variety

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In algebraic geometry, a point of an algebraic variety is called singular if the variety fails to be locally well-behaved there — intuitively, if it has a cusp, a self-intersection, or another "pinched" feature rather than looking like a smooth manifold near the point. Points that are not singular are called regular or smooth, and a variety all of whose points are regular is a nonsingular variety. The set of singular points is called the singular locus.

Definition via the Jacobian

For a variety defined by polynomial equations f1 = … = fk = 0 in affine n-space, a point P is singular when the Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives at P has rank less than the codimension of the variety; equivalently, the local ring at P is not a regular local ring. For a plane curve given by a single equation f(x, y) = 0, the singular points are those at which f and both first partial derivatives vanish simultaneously.

Examples

The cuspidal cubic y2 = x3 has a cusp at the origin: both partials vanish there and the curve is not smooth. The nodal cubic y2 = x3 + x2 crosses itself at the origin, which is an ordinary double point (a node). In higher dimensions, the vertex of a cone and the pinch point of the Whitney umbrella are standard examples of singular points.

Invariants and classification

Singular points of curves can be studied through invariants such as the multiplicity (the lowest degree of a non-zero term in the local equation), the delta invariant, and the Milnor number; for a plane curve singularity these determine the genus correction in the degree–genus formula. In surface theory, rational double points (Du Val singularities) and more generally the ADE classification of simple singularities connect algebraic geometry with Lie theory and the geometry of resolution.

Resolution of singularities

A central theme of algebraic geometry is resolution of singularities: replacing a singular variety by a nonsingular one that maps birationally onto it, typically by repeatedly blowing up along the singular locus. Hironaka's theorem (1964) guarantees that resolution is possible for varieties over fields of characteristic zero, a result for which he received the Fields Medal. Resolution in positive characteristic remains an active research area, established in low dimensions but open in general.
