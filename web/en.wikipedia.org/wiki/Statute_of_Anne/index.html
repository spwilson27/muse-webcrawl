Statute of Anne

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Statute of Anne, passed by the Parliament of Great Britain in 1710 (8 Anne c. 19) and in force from 10 April 1710, was the first statute to provide for copyright regulated by the government and the courts, rather than by private parties. Styled “An Act for the Encouragement of Learning”, it granted authors (or their assigns, typically booksellers) exclusive printing rights for fourteen years, renewable once if the author survived, with twenty-one years for books already in print; copies were to be deposited in nine libraries.

The Act broke the Stationers' Company's perpetual printing monopoly (maintained since 1557 through licensing) and located the right in authors — though the trade treated the authorial right as an asset to be bought. Its limits were enforced in the “battle of the booksellers”: Millar v. Taylor (1769) upheld a perpetual common-law copyright, but Donaldson v. Beckett (1774) decided the statute prevailed — copyright became a limited statutory monopoly in the public interest, the settlement underlying modern copyright's balance of incentive and Public domain. Anne's statute is thus the direct ancestor of copyright law throughout the common-law world.
