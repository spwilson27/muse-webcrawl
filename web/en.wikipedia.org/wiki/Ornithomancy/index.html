Ornithomancy (from Greek ornis, "bird", and manteia, "divination"; also modernly termed aeromancy in some usage) is the practice of reading omens from the actions of birds. It was one of the principal forms of divination in the ancient world. In Greek the word for a bird of omen, especially a bird of prey, was oiōnos, and the seer who interpreted birds was an oiōnopolos or oiōnistes.

Greek practice

In Homer's epics, bird signs are crucial at moments of decision: an eagle carrying a serpent or a goose is read as a prophecy of success or disaster. The direction of flight mattered — a bird flying to the right (east) was favourable, to the left unfavourable — as did the species, the cries, and whether the bird appeared singly or in flocks. The seer Calchas, who guided the Greeks at Troy, was famed as the greatest of augurs. According to Aeschylus, Prometheus claimed to have taught mortals how to distinguish favourable from unfavourable birds.

Roman augury

The Romans formalised bird divination as augury, an official state ritual performed by the college of augurs before public undertakings. Auspices were taken from the flight and feeding of birds; signs from the sky were divided into regions (the templum) and classified with great precision. The legendary foundation of Rome turned on such a contest: Romulus and Remus each watched for birds, and Remus saw six vultures while Romulus saw twelve, giving Romulus the right to found the city. The practice of taking the auspices persisted throughout the Republic and into the Empire.

Other cultures

Bird omens were observed in many societies, from the Etruscans (whose disciplina influenced Roman augury) to peoples of Central and South Asia, Africa and the Americas. In Hindu tradition the flight of particular birds has omen value, and in Norse mythology the ravens of Odin brought knowledge from the world of men.
