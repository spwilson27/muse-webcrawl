Dashi (出汁, だし) is a family of stocks used in Japanese cuisine. Dashi forms the base for miso soup, clear broth, noodle broth, and many simmering liquids, and is the foundation of umami flavour in Japanese cooking.

Types

  • Kombu dashi — steeped from dried kelp; delicate and vegetarian.
  • Katsuo dashi — from katsuobushi, shavings of fermented, smoked and dried skipjack tuna (bonito).
  • Awase dashi — the commonest, combining kombu and katsuobushi; the glutamates of the kelp and inosinates of the fish multiply each other's umami effect.
  • Niboshi (iriko) dashi — from dried baby sardines or anchovies; robust, used for everyday miso soup.
  • Shiitake dashi — from dried shiitake mushrooms; used in Buddhist vegetarian (shōjin) cooking.

Preparation

Unlike long-simmered Western stocks, dashi is brief: kombu is steeped in water at about 60 °C (or soaked cold) to extract glutamates without bitterness, then bonito flakes are added off the boil and strained within a minute. The first extraction (ichiban dashi) is used for clear soups; a second, weaker extraction (niban dashi) serves for miso soup and simmered dishes. Instant dashi granules and liquid concentrates are now used in most homes.

Chemistry and history

In 1908 Kikunae Ikeda identified glutamic acid from kombu dashi as the source of the taste he named umami, now recognised as a basic taste; Shintaro Kodama found inosinic acid in katsuobushi in 1913. Dashi-based seasoning has been documented in Japanese cookery for centuries, spreading widely in the Edo period as dried bonito and kelp trade routes (the kitamaebune shipping from Hokkaido kelp beds) developed.

Uses

Dashi underpins miso soup, suimono clear soups, noodle broths for soba and udon, nimono simmered dishes, chawanmushi, tamagoyaki (dashimaki), takoyaki and okonomiyaki batters, and dipping sauces such as mentsuyu served with food packed in bento boxes.
