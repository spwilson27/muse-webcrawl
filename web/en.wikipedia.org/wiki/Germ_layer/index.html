Germ layer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A germ layer is a primary layer of cells that forms during embryonic development. In most animals, gastrulation organises the embryo into three germ layers — the ectoderm (outer), mesoderm (middle) and endoderm (inner) — from which all tissues and organs arise. Animals with three layers are called triploblastic; simpler animals such as cnidarians are diploblastic, with only ectoderm and endoderm.

Derivatives

The ectoderm gives rise to the epidermis and its appendages (hair, nails, sweat glands), the entire nervous system, the sensory epithelia of the eye, ear and nose, and the enamel of the teeth. The mesoderm forms muscle, bone, cartilage, blood and blood vessels, the heart, kidneys, gonads, the dermis and the lining of the body cavities. The endoderm lines the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts and gives rise to the liver, pancreas, thyroid and other glandular organs. The neural crest — a migratory ectodermal population — is sometimes called a fourth germ layer because of its many derivatives, including peripheral neurons, melanocytes and much of the facial skeleton.

Development

Germ layers are established during gastrulation, when cells of the blastula rearrange and internalise through the primitive streak (in amniotes) or blastopore. Their fates become progressively restricted by signalling pathways such as Nodal, BMP and Wnt. The germ layer concept, developed in the nineteenth century by workers including Christian Pander, Karl Ernst von Baer and Robert Remak, was a founding insight of embryology and remains basic to developmental biology and to the interpretation of teratomas and stem-cell differentiation.
