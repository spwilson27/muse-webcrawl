Ice storm

An ice storm, also known as a glaze event or a silver storm, is a type of winter storm characterized by freezing rain. Precipitation falls as liquid rain but freezes on contact with exposed surfaces, coating everything in a layer of clear ice called glaze. The United States National Weather Service defines an ice storm as a storm that results in an accumulation of at least 0.25 inches (6.4 mm) of ice on exposed surfaces.

Formation

Ice storms form when a layer of warm air is trapped between two layers of cold air. Snow falling from the upper cold layer melts completely in the warm layer, then enters a shallow layer of below-freezing air near the ground. If that surface layer is thin, the droplets do not have time to refreeze into sleet; instead they become supercooled and freeze on impact with the ground, trees, vehicles, and structures.

Effects

Glaze ice is exceptionally destructive. A half-inch of ice can add hundreds of pounds of weight to tree limbs and power lines, snapping branches and bringing down utility poles, which causes widespread and sometimes long-lasting power outages. Ice-covered roads become nearly impassable, and the weight of ice can collapse roofs and towers. Even thin accumulations make sidewalks and road surfaces extremely slippery for pedestrians and drivers.

Notable ice storms

  • The North American ice storm of 1998 devastated eastern Canada and the northeastern United States, leaving millions without power — some for weeks — and causing billions of dollars in damage; it remains one of Canada's worst natural disasters.
  • The January 2009 ice storm across the central United States cut power to more than two million customers in states including Kentucky, Missouri, and Arkansas.
  • The December 2013 North American storm complex coated parts of Ontario, the Great Lakes, and New England in ice just before Christmas.
  • The February 2021 Texas winter storm combined ice accumulations with extreme cold and grid failures.

Measurement

Ice accretion is measured on flat surfaces and reported in inches or millimetres; forecasters also use damage-based indices, such as the Sperry–Piltz Ice Accumulation Index, to estimate likely utility damage from forecast ice amounts, wind, and temperature.
