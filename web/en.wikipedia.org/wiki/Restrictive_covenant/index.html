Restrictive covenant

A restrictive covenant is a type of covenant — a deed restriction — that limits what can be built or done on a piece of property. In property law, restrictive covenants "run with the land": they bind not only the original parties but also successors in title. They are commonly used by developers and homeowners' associations to prescribe matters such as building height, architectural style, setbacks, fencing, or permitted uses.

Enforceability

For a restrictive covenant to run with the land at common law, it generally must be in writing, intend to run, touch and concern the land, and there must be privity of estate; in equity, notice to the purchaser is central. Courts enforce covenants through injunctions rather than damages.

Racially restrictive covenants

In the United States, many covenants in the early and mid-20th century prohibited the sale or occupancy of property by people of particular races or religions and were an instrument of residential segregation. In Shelley v. Kraemer (1948), the U.S. Supreme Court held that judicial enforcement of racially restrictive covenants violated the Equal Protection Clause, and the Fair Housing Act of 1968 made such covenants illegal, though they remain in many old deeds as unenforceable relics.
