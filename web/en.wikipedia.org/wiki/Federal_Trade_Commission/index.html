Federal Trade Commission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) is an independent agency of the United States government whose principal mission is promoting consumer protection and antitrust law — the elimination and prevention of anticompetitive business practices, such as coercive monopoly. It was established on September 26, 1914, when President Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Trade Commission Act into law.

Mandate

Section 5 of the FTC Act prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce" and "unfair methods of competition." The FTC shares civil antitrust enforcement with the Department of Justice Antitrust Division, reviewing mergers under the Clayton Act and Hart-Scott-Rodino premerger notification program. Unlike the DOJ, the FTC proceeds through its own administrative adjudication or in federal court, and cannot impose criminal penalties.

Consumer protection work

The Bureau of Consumer Protection polices deceptive advertising, fraud, identity theft, telemarketing (including the Telemarketing Sales Rule and the National Do Not Call Registry), and privacy and data security. Specific statutes the FTC enforces include the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) and the Fair Credit Reporting Act. The FTC Act's prohibition on unfair practices has been used to challenge false advertising since amendments in 1938 (the Wheeler-Lea Act) expressly extended its reach to practices harming consumers.

Organization

The Commission consists of five members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for seven-year terms; no more than three may be of the same political party, and the President designates the chair. Its work is carried out by the Bureaus of Consumer Protection, Competition, and Economics, with regional offices across the country.
