The Rules of the Game (French: La Règle du jeu) is a 1939 French comedy-drama film directed by Jean Renoir, featuring an ensemble cast including Nora Gregor, Paulette Dubost, Mila Parély, Marcel Dalio, Julien Carette, Roland Toutain, Gaston Modot, and Renoir himself. A biting satire of French high society on the eve of the Second World War, it was initially a commercial and critical failure but is now widely regarded as one of the greatest films ever made, frequently appearing near the top of critics' polls, including the British Film Institute's Sight and Sound poll.

Plot

Aviator André Jurieux lands at Le Bourget after a record transatlantic flight and is devastated that Christine, the Austrian-born wife of the Marquis Robert de la Chesnaye, did not come to meet him — he flew for her sake. Robert, meanwhile, is ending an affair with Geneviève. To resolve the tangle, Robert invites everyone — Jurieux, Christine's friend Octave (played by Renoir), Geneviève, and others — to his country estate, La Colinière, for a weekend of hunting and a masquerade. Below stairs, parallel intrigues play out among the servants. A famous hunting sequence, in which the guests mechanically slaughter rabbits and pheasants, exposes the cruelty beneath the elegance; in the climax, the gamekeeper Schumacher mistakes Jurieux for his wife's suitor and shoots him dead, an accident the Marquis smoothly explains away.

Production and style

Renoir adapted the plot loosely from Alfred de Musset's play Les Caprices de Marianne. The film is celebrated for its deep-focus cinematography, long takes, and fluid camera movements through the chateau's rooms, techniques that allow multiple layers of action to unfold simultaneously and that influenced generations of directors from Orson Welles to Robert Altman.

Reception and restoration

Preview audiences rioted and the film was banned by the French government in October 1939 as demoralizing; after the war it was thought lost until a reconstruction from surviving materials premiered in 1959, prompting its reappraisal as a masterpiece.
