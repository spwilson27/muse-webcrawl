Heartland theory

The Heartland theory is a geopolitical hypothesis proposed by the British geographer Sir Halford Mackinder in his 1904 paper "The Geographical Pivot of History", presented to the Royal Geographical Society. Mackinder argued that the vast interior of Eurasia — the "pivot area", renamed the Heartland in his 1919 book Democratic Ideals and Reality — was the key to world power, because it was rich in resources and inaccessible to sea power.

The theory

Mackinder divided the world into the World-Island (the joined continents of Europe, Asia and Africa), the offshore islands (including the British Isles and Japan), and the outlying islands (the Americas and Australia). The Heartland — roughly Eastern Europe across Siberia and Central Asia, drained by rivers flowing to the Arctic or to inland seas — was, he argued, a natural fortress that railways would let a land power mobilise across, reversing the historical advantage of maritime nations.

He summarised the theory in a famous dictum in 1919: "Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland; who rules the Heartland commands the World-Island; who rules the World-Island commands the World."

Influence

The theory influenced German Geopolitik through Karl Haushofer (though Haushofer's influence on Nazi strategy is debated), and later shaped Cold War thinking: Nicholas Spykman's rival "Rimland" thesis held that the coastal fringes of Eurasia were the decisive zone, an idea reflected in the Western strategy of containment of the Soviet Union. The NATO–Warsaw Pact confrontation in Central Europe was often interpreted through a Heartland lens.

Criticism

Critics note that the Heartland's harsh climate, sparse population and poor infrastructure limited its real power; that air power, missiles and nuclear weapons undermined the fortress idea; that the Soviet Union controlled the Heartland yet lost the Cold War; and that Mackinder's scheme understated the economic dynamism of maritime trading states. The theory nonetheless remains a standard starting point in the study of geopolitics, and is still invoked in discussions of Central Asia, the Arctic and great-power competition.
