Visual acuity

Visual acuity (VA) commonly refers to the clarity of vision — the ability of the eye to distinguish fine detail — but it can also encompass colour vision, contrast sensitivity, and peripheral awareness. Visual acuity depends on optical factors (the sharpness with which the retina receives an image), neural factors (the health of the retina, visual pathways and visual cortex), and factors such as illumination, contrast, and exposure time.

Measurement

Distance acuity is most often measured with a Snellen chart, whose lines of letters (optotypes) decrease in size; the result is recorded as a Snellen fraction such as 20/20 (6/6 in metres), meaning the observer can read at 20 feet what a person with standard vision reads at 20 feet. 20/20 is considered normal but is not perfect vision — many young adults achieve 20/15 or better. Research and clinical trials prefer charts with a logarithmic progression of sizes, such as the ETDRS chart, with results expressed in logMAR units. Near acuity is tested at reading distance, commonly with Jaeger or N-notation charts.

Physiological limits

The maximum resolution of the human eye is set by the optics of the eye (diffraction and aberrations), the density of photoreceptors — cones in the fovea are spaced about 2.5 micrometres apart — and neural sampling, giving a limit near 1 arcminute of visual angle for standard optotypes, corresponding to 20/10 or slightly better.

Disorders and correction

Reduced acuity most often results from refractive errors — myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism and presbyopia — which are corrected with glasses, contact lenses, or refractive surgery. Other causes include cataract, macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma and neurological disease. Legal blindness in the United States is defined as best-corrected acuity of 20/200 or worse in the better eye.
