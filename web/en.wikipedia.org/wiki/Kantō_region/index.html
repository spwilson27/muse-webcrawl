Kantō region

The Kantō region (関東地方, Kantō-chihō) is a geographical region of Honshū, the largest island of Japan. It consists of seven prefectures around Tokyo: Gunma, Tochigi, Ibaraki, Saitama, Tokyo, Chiba and Kanagawa. With more than 43 million residents — roughly one third of Japan's population — it forms the most populous metropolitan region in the world, the Greater Tokyo Area.

Geography

Much of the region is the Kantō Plain, Japan's largest lowland, drained by the Tone, Arakawa and Tama rivers and bordered by mountains to the north and west and the Pacific Ocean to the east. The plain's volcanic-ash (Kantō loam) soils and its position over several tectonic plate boundaries make the region earthquake-prone: the Great Kantō earthquake of 1923 devastated Tokyo and Yokohama, killing over 100,000 people.

History

Kantō ("east of the barrier") was the power base of the Kamakura shogunate (1185–1333) and then of Tokugawa Ieyasu, whose shogunate ruled from Edo — modern Tokyo — from 1603 to 1868. The Meiji Restoration made Tokyo the imperial capital, and after reconstruction from the 1923 earthquake and Second World War firebombing, the region became the political, financial and industrial core of Japan.

Economy and transport

Kantō generates a share of Japan's GDP exceeding that of most nations, hosting the Keihin and Keiyō industrial zones, the ports of Tokyo, Yokohama and Chiba, and two international airports (Haneda and Narita). Its rail network, centred on JR East and private railways, is the busiest commuter system on earth, and the Tōkaidō and other Shinkansen lines radiate from Tokyo Station.
