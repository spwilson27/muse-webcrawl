Host cell

A host cell is a living cell in which a virus, parasite or other organism replicates. In virology, the term typically refers to a cell that a virus invades and uses to produce copies of itself; in cell biology it may also refer to a cell used to grow or maintain another organism in culture.

In virology

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites: they cannot reproduce on their own and must enter a suitable host cell. The virus attaches to receptors on the cell surface, penetrates or is taken into the cell, and then uses the cell's machinery — ribosomes, enzymes and energy supplies — to synthesise viral proteins and replicate its genome. New virus particles (virions) are assembled and released, often destroying the host cell in the process, as in the lytic cycle of bacteriophages.

The range of cells a virus can infect — its host range and tissue tropism — is determined largely by the receptors it binds. Some viruses remain dormant in the host cell as a provirus integrated into the host genome (lysogeny), replicating passively whenever the cell divides.

In biotechnology

Host cells are widely used in biotechnology as "factories" for producing proteins, vaccines and other products. Common hosts include the bacterium Escherichia coli, yeasts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and cultured animal cells such as Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells. Viruses for vaccine production are often grown in host cells such as embryonated eggs or continuous cell lines.
