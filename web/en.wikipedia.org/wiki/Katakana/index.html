Katakana

Katakana (片仮名、カタカナ) is a Japanese syllabary, one component of the Japanese writing system along with hiragana, kanji and in some cases the Latin script (rōmaji). The word katakana means "fragmentary kana", as its characters are fragments simplified from more complex kanji. Katakana and hiragana are both kana systems; each has 46 basic characters representing the same syllables, but with different forms.

Usage

In modern Japanese, katakana is most often used for transcription of foreign-language words — for example, "television" is written テレビ (terebi) — and for loanwords generally (gairaigo). It is also used for onomatopoeia, scientific and technical terms such as species names, the names of Japanese companies written for emphasis, and in telegrams and some annotation contexts. Conversely, words written in katakana in older texts may now be written in hiragana.

History

Katakana developed in the Heian period as a shorthand: Buddhist monks simplified fragments of man'yōgana characters as reading marks to indicate pronunciation and grammatical elements when annotating Chinese texts. Its angular shapes contrast with the cursive forms of hiragana, which developed in court circles. Standardization came with the script reforms of 1900 and the post-1945 reforms that fixed the modern 46-character inventory.

Small versions of the vowels and ャ・ュ・ョ form contracted sounds; the chōonpu (ー) lengthens a vowel; dakuten marks add voicing (e.g. カ→ガ, ハ→バ) and handakuten gives パ行. Extended combinations such as ファ and ウィ were developed to represent foreign sounds not native to Japanese.
