Amoebozoa

Amoebozoa is a major taxonomic group of eukaryotes containing about 2,400 described species of amoeboid organisms, most of which move and feed using blunt, lobe-shaped pseudopodia (lobopodia). It includes familiar organisms such as Amoeba proteus and Chaos, the slime moulds (myxogastrids and dictyostelids), the testate amoebae (Arcellinida), and anaerobic forms such as Entamoeba. Together with the opisthokonts (animals, fungi and their relatives), Amoebozoa forms the supergroup Amorphea.

Characteristics

Most amoebozoans are single-celled, naked or shelled (testate), and feed by phagocytosis, engulfing bacteria, algae and detritus. Their pseudopods are typically tubular or lobose, with granular endoplasm streaming controlled by actin–myosin systems. Many species have complex life cycles: the cellular slime mould Dictyostelium discoideum, a major model organism, aggregates into a multicellular fruiting body when starved, while plasmodial slime moulds such as Physarum polycephalum form giant multinucleate plasmodia.

Classification

Modern classifications divide Amoebozoa principally into Lobosa — amoebae with lobose pseudopods and no flagellate stages, including Tubulinea and Discosea — and Conosa, which includes the slime moulds (Mycetozoa), the anaerobic Archamoebae such as Entamoeba and Pelomyxa, and the Variosea. Molecular phylogenetics has repeatedly reshaped the group's internal taxonomy.

Ecology and medical importance

Amoebozoans are abundant in soil and freshwater, where they are key predators of bacteria and nutrient recyclers. Some are pathogens: Entamoeba histolytica causes amoebic dysentery and liver abscesses in humans, and the amoebozoan-affiliated free-living amoebae of other groups cause rare brain infections. Fossil vase-shaped microfossils suggest the group had appeared by the Neoproterozoic, some 800 million years ago.
