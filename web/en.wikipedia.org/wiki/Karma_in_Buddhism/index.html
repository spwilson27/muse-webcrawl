Karma (Sanskrit: कर्म; Pali: kamma) in Buddhism is the principle that intentional actions of body, speech and mind condition future experience. The Buddha famously defined karma as volition: "It is intention, monks, that I call karma; having intended, one acts through body, speech and mind" (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63).

Doctrine

Buddhist karma is a natural causal process rather than a system of reward and punishment imposed by a deity. Wholesome (kusala) actions rooted in generosity, loving-kindness and wisdom produce pleasant results (vipāka); unwholesome actions rooted in greed, hatred and delusion produce suffering. Not everything a person experiences is attributed to karma—early texts list other causes, including physical and environmental factors—and karma is one strand within the broader framework of dependent origination.

Karma and rebirth

Karma is the mechanism that links lives in the cycle of saṃsāra: the moral quality of one's actions influences the realm and circumstances of rebirth. Buddhism's doctrine of non-self (anattā) raises the philosophical question of what, if anything, carries karma between lives; schools answer through analogies such as one flame lighting another, and, in Yogācāra, through the concept of a storehouse consciousness.

Practice

The doctrine motivates ethical conduct, the cultivation of merit through generosity and moral discipline (sīla), and the dedication or transfer of merit to others, a widespread practice in Theravāda and Mahāyāna traditions. Liberation (nirvāṇa) entails the ending of karma-producing action altogether.
