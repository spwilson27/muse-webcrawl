The Hampton Court Maze is a hedge maze planted at Hampton Court Palace in the London Borough of Richmond upon Thames, England. It is the oldest surviving hedge maze in the United Kingdom and is generally considered the most famous maze in the world. Planted in the 1690s for William III and Mary II, it forms part of the palace's Wilderness garden.

History

The maze was planted between about 1690 and 1695 by the royal gardeners George London and Henry Wise as part of the formal gardens laid out for William and Mary. It was originally planted with hornbeam hedges, although the hedges seen today are largely yew, replanted over the centuries as the original planting aged and sections were renewed.

The maze covers roughly a third of an acre, and its paths run to about half a mile in total. Its design is trapezoidal, following the shape of the Wilderness, the area of informal woodland garden north of the palace's formal Privy Garden.

Design

Unlike many later puzzle mazes, Hampton Court is a relatively simple design with a single winding route broken up by a number of misleading junctions and dead ends. A well-known traditional method of solving it is to keep one hand — conventionally the right hand — in continuous contact with the hedge, though the many openings in the hedges mean this does not always lead directly to the centre. On average, visitors take around twenty minutes to reach the middle.

In literature

The maze's fame was cemented by Jerome K. Jerome's comic novel Three Men in a Boat (1889), in which Harris attempts to navigate it using a plan and leads a crowd of fellow visitors hopelessly astray, eventually having to be rescued by the keeper. The passage remains one of the best-known comic set pieces in English literature and is still quoted in guidebooks to the palace.
