Thomas Becket (c. 1119/1120 – 29 December 1170), also known as Saint Thomas of Canterbury and Thomas à Becket, was Archbishop of Canterbury from 1162 until his murder in 1170. He is venerated as a saint and martyr by both the Catholic Church and the Anglican Communion. He engaged in a conflict with Henry II, King of England, over the rights and privileges of the Church and was murdered by followers of the king in Canterbury Cathedral.

Early life and chancellorship

Born in Cheapside, London, to a prosperous Norman merchant family, Becket was educated in London and Paris and entered the service of Archbishop Theobald of Canterbury, who sent him to study canon law in Bologna and Auxerre. In 1155 Henry II appointed him Lord Chancellor; Becket became the king's close friend and administered the kingdom with conspicuous splendour, leading troops in the Toulouse campaign of 1159.

Archbishop and conflict

When Becket reluctantly accepted the archbishopric in 1162, expecting him to be compliant, Henry was dismayed: Becket underwent an abrupt conversion to an ascetic, zealous defence of Church independence. The quarrel came to a head over the Constitutions of Clarendon (1164), by which Henry sought to subject criminous clergy to royal courts. Becket fled into exile in France for six years. A fragile reconciliation in 1170 allowed his return, but his excommunication of bishops who had crowned the young king in his absence reignited the conflict. According to tradition, Henry uttered words interpreted by four knights — Reginald FitzUrse, Hugh de Morville, William de Tracy and Richard le Breton — as a wish to be rid of Becket; they rode to Canterbury and murdered him in the cathedral on 29 December 1170.

Aftermath and legacy

The murder shocked Christendom. Becket was canonised by Pope Alexander III in 1173, and Canterbury became one of Europe's greatest pilgrimage destinations, famously the goal of the pilgrims in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales. Henry II performed public penance at Becket's tomb in 1174. The shrine was destroyed in 1538 on the orders of Henry VIII. Becket's story has been retold in T. S. Eliot's Murder in the Cathedral and Jean Anouilh's Becket.
