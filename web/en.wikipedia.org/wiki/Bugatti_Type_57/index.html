The Bugatti Type 57 and its later variants were grand touring cars built by the French manufacturer Bugatti from 1934 to 1940. Designed by Jean Bugatti, son of founder Ettore Bugatti, the Type 57 was powered by a 3,257 cc twin-cam straight-eight engine derived from the Type 49 but with dual overhead camshafts. It is regarded as one of the finest pre-war automobiles and the archetypal classic Bugatti road car.

Design

The Type 57 combined a refined chassis with an advanced engine: a dual overhead camshaft straight-eight with gear-driven camshafts, producing around 135 hp in standard form. Most cars received factory coachwork named after Alpine passes and places — including the Galibier four-door saloon, the Ventoux coupe, the Stelvio cabriolet, and the Atalante coupe — although independent coachbuilders also bodied the chassis. A lowered, supercharged version, the Type 57S/SC, offered substantially higher performance.

Variants

The range included the original Type 57, the lowered Type 57S, supercharged Type 57C and 57SC models, and the racing Type 57G "Tank," which won the 1936 French Grand Prix and the 1937 24 Hours of Le Mans. Jean Bugatti himself was killed in 1939 while testing a Type 57-based car, a loss that deeply affected the company shortly before the Second World War ended production.

Type 57SC Atlantic

The most famous variant is the Type 57SC Atlantic, with flowing teardrop bodywork and a distinctive dorsal fin seam — a feature inherited from the earlier Aerolithe show car, whose magnesium-alloy body panels had to be riveted rather than welded. Only a handful of Atlantics were built, and surviving examples are among the most valuable automobiles in the world; one sold privately for a reported sum exceeding $30 million, and another anchors the Mullin Automotive Museum collection.

Legacy

With roughly 710 examples built, the Type 57 was Bugatti's most successful pre-war model. Its engineering and styling have influenced later Bugatti revivals, and the Atlantic in particular is routinely cited among the most beautiful cars ever made. The modern Chiron and its La Voiture Noire one-off pay homage to the Type 57SC Atlantic.
