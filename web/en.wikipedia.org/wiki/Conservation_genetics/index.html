Conservation genetics

Conservation genetics is an interdisciplinary subfield of population genetics that aims to understand the dynamics of genes in populations for the purpose of avoiding extinction and preserving or restoring biodiversity. It applies genetic methods to the conservation and management of endangered species, both in the wild and in captive breeding programmes in zoos and seed banks.

Key concepts

Conservation geneticists study genetic diversity, inbreeding, and gene flow. Small populations lose diversity through genetic drift and suffer inbreeding depression — reduced fitness caused by mating between relatives. The effective population size (Ne) is usually far smaller than the census size, so much of the field concerns how to maintain evolutionary potential: the capacity of a population to adapt to environmental change.

The classic "50/500 rule" suggested an effective size of 50 to avoid short-term inbreeding depression and 500 to retain long-term adaptive potential, though later authors have argued for higher values (100/1000). Other central topics include:

  • Genetic rescue — introducing migrants to increase diversity and fitness, as when Florida panthers were supplemented with Texas cougars.
  • Outbreeding depression — reduced fitness that can occur when genetically distant populations are crossed.
  • Taxonomic and management units — using genetics to define evolutionarily significant units (ESUs) and management units for legal protection.
  • Forensics and monitoring — DNA barcoding and environmental DNA (eDNA) to identify species, track poaching and estimate population sizes non-invasively.

Methods

Early work relied on allozymes and then microsatellites and mitochondrial DNA. Modern conservation genetics uses genome-wide SNP data, whole-genome sequencing, ancient DNA from museum specimens, and environmental DNA sampling. These tools can reconstruct demographic history, detect hybridization, identify the source of confiscated wildlife products, and guide captive breeding by minimizing kinship among breeding pairs.

History

The field coalesced in the late 1970s and 1980s, spurred by Michael Soulé's work on conservation biology and studies of cheetahs, whose strikingly low genetic diversity was documented in the 1980s. Landmark applications include the genetic management of the black-footed ferret, California condor and giant panda recovery programmes.
