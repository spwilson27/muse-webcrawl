Nuclear weapons testing

Nuclear weapons testing is the practice of detonating nuclear explosive devices to verify their design, measure their yield and effects, and demonstrate military capability. Between 1945 and 2017, more than 2,000 nuclear test explosions were conducted worldwide by eight countries.


An atmospheric nuclear test explosion at a Pacific proving ground.

History

The first nuclear test, codenamed Trinity, was conducted by the United States on 16 July 1945 near Alamogordo, New Mexico, yielding about 20 kilotons. The Soviet Union followed in 1949 (RDS-1), the United Kingdom in 1952 (Hurricane), France in 1960, and China in 1964. India conducted a test in 1974 and weaponised tests in 1998, as did Pakistan in 1998. North Korea conducted six tests between 2006 and 2017.

Types of tests

Tests have been conducted in the atmosphere, underwater, underground, and in outer space. Atmospheric tests spread radioactive fallout widely; the 1954 Castle Bravo shot at Bikini Atoll, at 15 megatons the largest United States test, contaminated a Japanese fishing vessel, the Lucky Dragon No. 5, and Marshall Islanders, fuelling worldwide opposition. The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty banned tests in the atmosphere, underwater, and in space for its signatories, driving testing underground.

Effects and legacy

Testing exposed military personnel, test-site workers, and downwind populations to radiation, and contaminated sites including Semipalatinsk in Kazakhstan, Novaya Zemlya in Russia, Mururoa in French Polynesia, and the Nevada Test Site. Compensation programmes and environmental remediation have followed in several countries.

Treaties and moratoria

The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT), opened for signature in 1996, bans all nuclear explosions, but has not entered into force because several key states have not ratified it. Nevertheless, all recognised nuclear-weapon states have observed testing moratoria since the 1990s; North Korea is the only country to have tested in the twenty-first century. The CTBT's International Monitoring System detects explosions worldwide using seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound, and radionuclide stations.
