Strategic sourcing is an institutional procurement process that continuously improves and re-evaluates the purchasing activities of a company. In the services industry, strategic sourcing refers to a service solution, sometimes offered by a third party, that is designed to help companies optimise their purchasing functions.

The term was popularised in the early 1980s through the work of consulting firms and builds on Peter Kraljic's influential 1983 Harvard Business Review article, "Purchasing Must Become Supply Management", which introduced a portfolio matrix classifying purchases by profit impact and supply risk.

Overview

Strategic sourcing differs from conventional purchasing in that it considers the total cost of ownership rather than the purchase price alone, aligns sourcing decisions with corporate strategy, and treats key suppliers as long-term partners. It is generally applied to high-value, high-impact categories of spend.

The sourcing process

A widely used model comprises seven steps:

  1. Profile the category — analyse the spending category, its usage and internal demand.
  2. Select the sourcing strategy — based on portfolio analysis of supply risk and value.
  3. Generate the supplier portfolio — identify and pre-qualify potential suppliers.
  4. Select the implementation path — requests for proposal, auctions, or direct negotiation.
  5. Negotiate and select suppliers — evaluate bids against total cost and service criteria.
  6. Operational integration — integrate suppliers into ordering, payment and logistics systems.
  7. Track and benchmark — monitor supplier performance and market conditions, restarting the cycle as needed.

Criticism

Critics note that aggressive strategic sourcing can over-emphasise cost reduction at the expense of resilience, quality and innovation, and that consolidated supplier bases may increase exposure to disruption. These concerns have driven complementary approaches such as category management and supply-risk management.
