History of evolutionary thought

The history of evolutionary thought traces ideas about the mutability of species from ancient philosophers through Lamarck and Darwin to the modern synthesis and molecular evolution.

Ancient and medieval ideas

Greek thinkers such as Anaximander speculated that life arose from the sea, and Empedocles imagined the chance combination of parts; Lucretius echoed some of these notions. Yet the dominant framework — Plato's essentialism and Aristotle's fixed scale of nature — held species to be immutable for two millennia, reinforced by medieval natural theology.

The Enlightenment and early transformism

Linnaeus's taxonomy (1735) organised life while generally assuming fixity, though he later allowed hybridisation. Buffon hinted at degeneration from common ancestors; Erasmus Darwin versified transmutation. In 1809 Jean-Baptiste Lamarck published the first systematic theory of evolution, based on use and disuse and the inheritance of acquired characters, while Georges Cuvier's studies of extinction and comparative anatomy pushed the other way, favouring catastrophism.

Darwin and Wallace

Charles Darwin, influenced by Lyell, Malthus and his Beagle voyage observations, drafted natural selection by 1838 but published only when Alfred Russel Wallace independently arrived at the theory in 1858. On the Origin of Species (1859) assembled overwhelming evidence for descent with modification by natural selection. The theory was widely accepted for common descent, though selection itself entered an "eclipse" around 1900.

The modern synthesis and beyond

Mendel's rediscovered genetics (1900), population genetics by Fisher, Haldane and Wright, and the work of Dobzhansky, Mayr and Simpson forged the modern synthesis of the 1930s–40s. The discovery of DNA's structure (1953), neutral theory (Kimura), punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge and Gould) and evo-devo have since deepened and extended evolutionary theory into the extended evolutionary synthesis debated today.
