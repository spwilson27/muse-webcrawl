Litmus test (politics)

In politics, a litmus test is a question posed to a candidate for high office — frequently a judicial nomination — whose answer is taken to decide the nominator's or a constituency's support. The phrase derives from the chemical litmus test, in which litmus paper changes colour decisively in acid or alkali.

Use in the United States

The term became prominent in debates over U.S. Supreme Court nominations from the 1980s onward, particularly over the issue of abortion after Roe v. Wade (1973). Presidents and senators have often denied applying any litmus test, while interest groups on all sides accuse opponents of imposing one. Litmus tests are also said to be applied to candidates on gun rights, taxation, environmental regulation, and loyalty to a party leader.

Criticism

Critics argue that single-issue litmus tests narrow debate, discourage otherwise qualified nominees, and push candidates toward evasive answers at confirmation hearings. Defenders reply that a candidate's position on a fundamental question is legitimate information for voters and appointing officials.
