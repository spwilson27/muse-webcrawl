The Ziggurat of Ur (Sumerian: É-temen-ní-gùru) is a Neo-Sumerian ziggurat in what was once the city of Ur near Nasiriyah, in present-day Dhi Qar Governorate, Iraq. Built in the Bronze Age by King Ur-Nammu of the Third Dynasty of Ur around the 21st century BCE, it is one of the best-preserved structures of ancient Mesopotamia and, with the ziggurats of Eridu and Uruk, among the most famous.

Construction

The ziggurat was built in stages: a massive mudbrick core faced with fired bricks set in bitumen. The base measures about 64 by 46 metres; inscriptions stamped on the bricks name Ur-Nammu as builder of the first stage, and his son Shulgi is credited with completing the upper storeys. Herodotus' description and cuneiform evidence suggest the structure originally rose in three terraces to more than 30 metres, crowned by a shrine of the moon god Nanna, the patron deity of Ur. A triple staircase — one flight against the facade and two flanking it — climbs to the first terrace in a design unique among ziggurats.

Later history

The ziggurat was already ancient when the Neo-Babylonian king Nabonidus restored it in the 6th century BCE. Over the millennia the upper stages eroded and the baked-brick casing was robbed, leaving a mound that early travellers sketched long before excavation. Under Saddam Hussein in the 1980s the facade of the first stage and the monumental staircase were partially reconstructed; the monument stands near the Tallil air base and suffered minor damage from shelling in the 1991 Gulf War.

Excavation

The site was excavated in the 1920s and 1930s by Sir Leonard Woolley for the British Museum and the University of Pennsylvania, in the same campaigns that uncovered the Royal Cemetery of Ur. Woolley's investigations of the ziggurat revealed its construction sequence, foundation deposits and the later restorations.

Significance

The stepped silhouette of the ziggurat — an artificial mountain raising the temple toward the gods — has often been linked with the biblical Tower of Babel tradition, though that story is usually associated with the Etemenanki of Babylon. The Ziggurat of Ur remains the most complete surviving example and an icon of Sumerian civilisation.
