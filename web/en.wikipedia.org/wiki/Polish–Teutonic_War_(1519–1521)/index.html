The Polish–Teutonic War of 1519–1521 (Polish: Wojna pruska, "the Prussian War"; German: Reiterkrieg, "the Horsemen's War") was fought between the Kingdom of Poland and the Teutonic Knights, the last of the Polish–Teutonic Wars. It was precipitated by Grand Master Albert of Brandenburg-Ansbach's refusal to swear homage to King Sigismund I of Poland, as required by the Second Peace of Thorn (1466), and by Albert's search for allies — including Imperial and Muscovite support — against Poland.

Course of the war

War broke out at the end of December 1519. Polish forces under the command of Grand Crown Hetman Mikołaj Firlej advanced into the Order's territory, besieging Marienwerder (Kwidzyn) and Preußisch Holland (Pasłęk) in early 1520. Albert, reinforced by some 10,000–15,000 German mercenaries raised with Imperial funds, counter-attacked, taking several towns and marching on Danzig (Gdańsk), but failed to capture it in November 1520; the city's defences and the arrival of Polish reinforcements forced his withdrawal. Campaigning was hampered by lack of money on both sides — the mercenaries' pay fell into arrears — and a four-year truce was concluded at Toruń on 5 April 1521 through Imperial and papal mediation.

Aftermath

The truce deferred the question of homage to arbitration that never resolved it. Instead, Albert, advised by Martin Luther among others, dissolved the Order's state in Prussia, converted to Lutheranism, and on 8 April 1525 concluded the Treaty of Kraków: the Teutonic Order's Prussian territory became the secular Duchy of Prussia, held as a fief of the Polish Crown, and on 10 April 1525 Albert performed the Prussian Homage to Sigismund in Kraków's market square — a landmark event in Polish history.
