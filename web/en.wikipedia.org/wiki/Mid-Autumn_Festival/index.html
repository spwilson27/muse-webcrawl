The Mid-Autumn Festival, also known as the Moon Festival or Mooncake Festival, is a harvest festival celebrated in Chinese culture. It is held on the 15th day of the 8th month of the Chinese lunisolar calendar, with a full moon at night, corresponding to mid-September to early October of the Gregorian calendar. On this day, many believe that the moon is at its brightest and fullest size, coinciding with harvest time in the middle of autumn.

The festival is one of the most important holidays in Chinese culture; its popularity is on par with that of Chinese New Year. The history of the festival dates back over 3,000 years. Similar festivals are celebrated in other cultures in East and Southeast Asia, including Tết Trung Thu in Vietnam, Chuseok in Korea (held on the same day) and Tsukimi in Japan.

Origins and mythology

Moon worship in China can be traced to the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE) and earlier. Ancient emperors worshipped the harvest moon in autumn in the hope of a plentiful crop the following year. The term "mid-autumn" (中秋) first appeared in the Rites of Zhou, a classic of the Zhou dynasty. Celebration became popular during the Tang dynasty, and by the Song dynasty elaborate festivities were widespread.

The best-known legend associated with the festival is that of Chang'e, wife of the archer Hou Yi, who swallowed an elixir of immortality and flew to the moon, where she remains as the Moon Goddess, accompanied in later tellings by the Jade Rabbit. Offerings are made to Chang'e during the festival.

Traditions

Families gather to share a meal, give thanks for the harvest, and pray for good fortune. Carrying brightly lit lanterns, lighting lanterns on towers, and floating sky lanterns are popular customs; in some regions, dragon and lion dances are performed. In southern China and Vietnam, children parade with colourful lanterns. Making and sharing mooncakes is among the most central traditions.

Mooncakes

Mooncakes are rich pastries traditionally filled with sweet-bean, egg yolk, meat or lotus-seed paste, often imprinted with characters for longevity or harmony. During the Yuan dynasty, legend has it — though this is generally regarded as folklore — that mooncakes were used to conceal messages coordinating a revolt against Mongol rule. Modern varieties include baked, snow-skin and ice-cream mooncakes, elaborately boxed and given as gifts.

Dates

The festival falls on the 15th day of the 8th lunar month: in recent years, 29 September 2023, 17 September 2024, and 6 October 2025. It is a public holiday in mainland China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macau and Vietnam.
