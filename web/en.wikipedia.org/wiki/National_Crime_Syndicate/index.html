The National Crime Syndicate was the name given by the press and historians to a confederation of American organised crime groups that operated from the late 1920s into the post-war era. It is traditionally said to have been established at the Atlantic City Conference of May 1929, where leading underworld figures met to divide territory, settle disputes, and plan for the end of Prohibition.

The Syndicate brought together the Italian-American Mafia families with Jewish-American and other criminal organisations in a loose national alliance. Its enforcement arm was popularly known as Murder, Inc., a group of contract killers based in Brooklyn that was exposed in the early 1940s.

Origins

Prohibition (1920–1933) made bootlegging immensely profitable and forced criminal gangs into both violent competition and practical cooperation. Figures such as Charles "Lucky" Luciano, Meyer Lansky, Frank Costello, and Johnny Torrio advocated a corporate model of crime: territorial agreements, shared ventures in gambling and liquor, and a governing board — the Mafia's Commission — to arbitrate disputes instead of gang wars.

Structure and activities

The Syndicate was never a single corporation; it functioned as a network coordinating bootlegging during Prohibition and, after repeal, illegal gambling, loan-sharking, labour racketeering, narcotics, and Las Vegas casino investment. Its significance in popular history was cemented by the Kefauver Committee hearings of 1950–51, which publicised the idea of a nationwide criminal conspiracy, and by the Apalachin meeting of 1957, where a gathering of mobsters was raided by police.

Historiography

Some historians argue the "National Crime Syndicate" was a real coordinating body; others contend it was a convenient label used by journalists and law enforcement for a looser set of alliances among largely autonomous groups. The degree of centralisation remains debated, though the existence of inter-group cooperation and of the Commission is well documented.
