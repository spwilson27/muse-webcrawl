Implied reader

The implied reader is a concept in literary theory, developed most fully by the German critic Wolfgang Iser in The Implied Reader (1972) and The Act of Reading (1976). It denotes the reader that a text presupposes and constructs through its structures — the role a real reader is invited to occupy in order for the work to realise its effects. The implied reader is neither a specific historical person nor an ideal, perfectly competent reader, but a textual structure: the network of expectations, knowledge, and responses that the work itself lays out.

Reader-response criticism studies how texts structure the role of their reader.

Theory

Iser argued that literary texts are inherently indeterminate: they contain gaps (Leerstellen, "blanks") that the reader must fill in by connecting perspectives, anticipating developments, and revising expectations. The implied reader is the position from which these gaps can be filled coherently. Two aspects define the concept: the reader's role as laid down by the text, and the process by which a real reader adopts — or resists — that role.

Related concepts

The implied reader is the counterpart of the implied author, the image of the author that the text projects, a term coined by Wayne C. Booth. It is also related to Umberto Eco's "model reader" and to the narratee, the figure addressed by the narrator within the text. Critics have debated how far the implied reader constrains interpretation: Stanley Fish argued that interpretive communities, rather than texts, determine readings, while others use the concept to defend the idea that some interpretations are more faithful to a work than others.
