Feminist art

Feminist art is art created with an awareness of the social and political significance of gender, generally associated with the feminist art movement that emerged in the late 1960s and 1970s alongside second-wave feminism. Feminist artists sought to change the world not only through art but by challenging the structures of the art world itself—its institutions, histories, and criteria of value—from which women had been largely excluded.

History

Linda Nochlin's 1971 essay "Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?" catalyzed feminist art history by attributing women's absence from the canon to institutional barriers rather than innate talent. In 1971 Judy Chicago and Miriam Schapiro founded the Feminist Art Program at the California Institute of the Arts, which produced the collaborative installation Womanhouse (1972). Chicago's The Dinner Party (1974–1979), a monumental triangular banquet table with place settings for 39 notable women, became an icon of the movement. In New York, groups such as the Art Workers' Coalition, Women Artists in Revolution, and the A.I.R. Gallery (founded 1972, the first women's cooperative gallery in the U.S.) protested the underrepresentation of women in exhibitions such as the Whitney Annual.

Themes and media

Feminist artists often validated media traditionally dismissed as "craft" or decorative—textiles, needlework, ceramics—and turned to performance, video, and installation to represent female experience, the body, domestic labor, and violence against women. Notable figures include Miriam Schapiro, Faith Ringgold, Cindy Sherman, Barbara Kruger, Ana Mendieta, Carolee Schneemann, Yoko Ono, Hannah Wilke, Mary Kelly, and the Guerrilla Girls, an anonymous collective founded in 1985 that exposed sexism and racism in the art world with posters citing gallery and museum statistics.

Later developments

From the 1980s onward, feminist art diversified: postmodern and theory-informed work questioned essentialism about "the female experience," and Black, Latina, and postcolonial feminists—including Faith Ringgold, Adrian Piper, Lorna Simpson, and Carrie Mae Weems—pressed the movement toward intersectional perspectives. Major retrospectives such as WACK! Art and the Feminist Revolution (2007) reassessed the movement's legacy.
