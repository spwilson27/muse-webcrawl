The Second Battle of St Albans was fought on 17 February 1461 during the Wars of the Roses. A Lancastrian army under Queen Margaret of Anjou defeated the Yorkist forces of Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, and recovered custody of King Henry VI, who had been Warwick's prisoner. It was the second battle fought at St Albans; the first, in 1455, had opened the wars.

Background

After the Yorkist victory at Northampton in July 1460, Warwick held Henry VI captive in London, and the Act of Accord named Richard, Duke of York, heir to the throne. Margaret of Anjou raised an army in the north; York rode out to meet it and was killed at the Battle of Wakefield on 30 December 1460. Margaret's army then marched south toward London, living off the land and gaining a fearsome reputation, while Warwick positioned his army across her route at St Albans, fortifying with ditches, caltrops and primitive defences, and posting detachments to watch several approaches.

The battle

Margaret's army, guided by local knowledge, approached St Albans from an unexpected direction, entering the town via Dunstable and defeating a Yorkist detachment of archers in the market square after hard fighting in the streets. Warwick's main body, drawn up north of the town around Bernards Heath, found itself outmanoeuvred: its fortified line faced the wrong way, contingents arrived piecemeal, and the Burgundian mercenaries' handguns and other devices proved of little effect. Under pressure and the threat of being cut off, Warwick's cohesion collapsed; many men fled or changed sides. Warwick escaped with part of the army toward Chipping Norton.

Henry VI was found by the Lancastrians, seated under a tree where his captors had left him — contemporary accounts describe him singing. The king knighted several Lancastrian followers, and two captured Yorkist lords, Lord Bonville and Sir Thomas Kyriell, were executed the next day despite a promise of safety, to Margaret's enduring discredit.

Aftermath

The victory opened the road to London, but the city, alarmed by reports of looting and negotiating between the factions, shut its gates and sent supplies to the Lancastrian army while refusing it entry. Meanwhile Edward, Earl of March, advanced from the west fresh from his victory at Mortimer's Cross. Margaret withdrew north to Dunstable and then York, allowing Edward to enter London, where he was proclaimed King Edward IV on 4 March 1461. The Lancastrian triumph at St Albans was thus short-lived: six weeks later Edward crushed Margaret's army at Towton, the bloodiest battle of the wars.
