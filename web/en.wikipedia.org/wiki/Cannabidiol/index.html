Cannabidiol (CBD) is a phytocannabinoid, one of over 100 identified cannabinoids in the cannabis plant. It accounts for up to 40% of the plant's extract. Unlike tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), CBD is not intoxicating and does not produce a "high".

Medical uses

The strongest evidence for CBD's efficacy is in the treatment of certain childhood epilepsy syndromes, such as Dravet syndrome and Lennox–Gastaut syndrome. A purified CBD oral solution (marketed as Epidiolex in the US and Epidyolex in the EU) was approved by the FDA in 2018 for these conditions. Clinical evidence for most other promoted uses — anxiety, insomnia, pain, and inflammation — remains limited or inconclusive, and regulatory agencies have warned against unproven therapeutic claims for over-the-counter CBD products.

Pharmacology

CBD has low affinity for the CB1 and CB2 cannabinoid receptors; its effects are thought to involve multiple targets, including the serotonin 5-HT1A receptor, TRPV1 channels, and inhibition of the enzyme FAAH and adenosine uptake. It is metabolised in the liver by cytochrome P450 enzymes and can interact with many medications.

History and legality

CBD was isolated in 1940 and its structure elucidated by Raphael Mechoulam in 1963. The 2018 US Farm Bill removed hemp (cannabis with less than 0.3% THC) from the Controlled Substances Act, spurring a large consumer market in CBD oils, edibles, and cosmetics, though the FDA has approved only the prescription product and restricts adding CBD to food.
