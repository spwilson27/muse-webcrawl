Fordlândia ("Ford-land") is a district and historical company town in the municipality of Aveiro, in the Brazilian state of Pará. It is located on the east bank of the Tapajós River, some 300 kilometres (190 mi) south of the city of Santarém. It was established by American industrialist Henry Ford in 1928 as a prefabricated industrial town intended to be inhabited by 10,000 people to secure a source of cultivated rubber for the Ford Motor Company's automobile production.

History

Ford negotiated with the government of Pará for a concession of land in exchange for a share of profits and infrastructure investment. Ford intended the town to be a self-sufficient American-style community, with Cape Cod-style housing, a hospital, schools, a golf course and strict rules reflecting his own values — including prohibition of alcohol and tobacco — imposed on Brazilian workers who had their own customs and expectations. Tensions culminated in a workers' riot in December 1930 over changes to food service.

Failure

The plantation itself failed for ecological reasons: rubber trees planted densely in rows, unlike their scattered distribution in the wild forest, were devastated by South American leaf blight and pests, and the site's hilly, infertile soil proved unsuitable. Ford never visited the town. Operations shifted downstream to Belterra, but with the development of synthetic rubber the project lost its purpose. In 1945 Ford sold the holdings back to the Brazilian government at a large loss, having spent roughly $20 million. Fordlândia became a byword for grandiose failure; its water tower, warehouses and ruins remain, and a small population still lives in the district.
