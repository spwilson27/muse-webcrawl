Sylow theorems

In abstract algebra, the Sylow theorems are fundamental results in the theory of finite groups, proved by the Norwegian mathematician Ludwig Sylow in 1872. They give detailed information about subgroups of prime-power order that a given finite group must contain — a partial converse to Lagrange's theorem, which says only that subgroup orders divide the group order.

Definitions

Let G be a finite group of order pnm, where p is prime and p does not divide m. A subgroup of order pn is called a Sylow p-subgroup of G. Subgroups whose order is a power of p are called p-subgroups or p-groups when finite.

The theorems

First theorem. For every prime power pk dividing the order of G, there exists a subgroup of G of order pk. In particular, Sylow p-subgroups always exist.

Second theorem. All Sylow p-subgroups of G are conjugate to one another: if P and Q are Sylow p-subgroups, then Q = gPg−1 for some g in G. Hence any two have the same cardinality and structure up to isomorphism-in-place.

Third theorem. The number np of Sylow p-subgroups satisfies np ≡ 1 (mod p) and np divides m (the p-free part of |G|). Moreover, np is the index of the normalizer of any Sylow p-subgroup.

Applications

The Sylow theorems are the standard first tool for analyzing groups of small order: counting arguments with np often force the existence of a normal subgroup, proving that a group is not simple. For example, groups of order pq (distinct primes) and groups of order p2q are frequently shown non-simple or classified entirely. The theorems also underpin classification results and the study of simple groups, and analogous statements (Sylow's theorems for infinite groups, Hall's theorems for solvable groups) extend the ideas.

Proof ideas

Standard proofs use group actions: the first theorem is proved by considering the action of a p-subgroup by left multiplication and a counting argument on cosets, or via Cauchy's theorem generalized by induction. The conjugacy statement follows from the action of G on its family of Sylow p-subgroups by conjugation, and the counting statement follows from the orbit–stabilizer theorem since all orbits under that action force the number to be ≡ 1 mod p.
