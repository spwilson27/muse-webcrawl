Banlieue

Banlieue
LanguageFrench
Literal meaningSuburb; from ban (proclamation/jurisdiction) + lieue (league)
ConnotationOften working-class, immigrant-populated housing estates

Banlieue (French pronunciation: [bɑ̃ljø]) is the French word for a suburb of a large city. Since the late 20th century, in media and political discourse the term — especially in the plural, les banlieues — has acquired a strong secondary connotation referring to the deprived housing estates (cités) on the outskirts of French cities, home to large working-class and immigrant populations and associated in public debate with unemployment, crime and social unrest. This usage contrasts with wealthy suburbs such as Neuilly-sur-Seine or Le Vésinet, which are also technically banlieues.

Background

Post-war France built vast high-rise estates, the grands ensembles, in suburban communes to solve the housing crisis; deindustrialisation from the 1970s left many of their residents — including many families of North and sub-Saharan African origin — in concentrated poverty. Designated priority zones (ZUS, later QPV under the politique de la ville) receive targeted state intervention and the urban renewal agency ANRU has demolished and rebuilt many tower blocks.

Unrest and representation

The banlieues became a national political issue through episodes such as the 1983 March for Equality (the "Marche des Beurs"), the 1990s disturbances, and above all the riots of autumn 2005, which began in Clichy-sous-Bois after the deaths of two teenagers fleeing police and led to a state of emergency. French cinema and rap music — films such as La Haine (1995) and artists from these estates — have shaped the cultural image of the banlieue in France and abroad.
