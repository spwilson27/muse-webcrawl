Prestel

Prestel (a contraction of "press telephone") was an interactive videotex system developed in the United Kingdom by the Post Office and launched publicly in 1979. It delivered pages of text and simple block graphics to television sets and dedicated terminals over ordinary telephone lines, letting users browse news, travel timetables, financial prices and shopping catalogues, and — unusually for its time — send messages and download software. Prestel was the world's first large-scale public videotex service and a direct ancestor of the online services that followed.

Development

The system grew out of work by Sam Fedida at the Post Office Research Centre at Martlesham Heath in the early 1970s, combining technologies behind the teletext services Ceefax and ORACLE with two-way telephone communication. After trials from 1976, Prestel opened in London, Birmingham and Edinburgh in September 1979. Information providers — eventually more than a thousand of them — edited their own pages remotely; among the best-known services were the travel agent Thomas Cook's holiday database and the Micronet 800 service for home-computer users, launched in 1983.

Technology

Prestel pages were frames of 24 rows by 40 columns, using the alphamosaic graphics of the teletext standard: characters could be drawn from block-mosaic glyphs in seven colours. Subscribers used an adapted television or terminal with a modem (typically 1200/75 bit/s — fast down, slow up) and navigated by typing page numbers preceded by an asterisk. Charges combined a subscription, connect-time fees and per-page prices for premium content, billed through the telephone account.

Use and decline

The service found niches in travel agencies, agriculture, finance and education, and its Micronet arm pioneered online chat, downloadable games and software ("telesoftware"). But household take-up disappointed: terminals were expensive, and by the late 1980s Prestel had tens of thousands rather than the millions of users once forecast. Competing systems — France's Minitel above all — achieved greater penetration. Prestel passed to British Telecom at privatisation, was sold to a management buy-out in 1994 and was progressively wound down as the World Wide Web made videotex obsolete.

Legacy

Prestel demonstrated consumer demand for interactive information, electronic mail and online transactions years before the web, and its page-number addressing, gateway services and community of information providers foreshadowed later online platforms. Its technical standards fed into the European videotex family that included Germany's Bildschirmtext and, by influence, the design of teletext worldwide.
