Fibrin

Fibrin (also called Factor Ia) is a fibrous, non-globular protein involved in the clotting of blood. It is formed by the action of the protease thrombin on fibrinogen, which causes it to polymerize. The polymerized fibrin, together with platelets, forms a hemostatic plug or clot over a wound site.

When a tissue or blood vessel is injured, fibrinogen is converted at the wound into fibrin by thrombin, an enzyme generated by the coagulation cascade. Fibrin monomers assemble into long fibres that form a mesh, trapping platelets and blood cells to produce a clot that stops bleeding.

Structure and formation

Thrombin cleaves small fibrinopeptides from the ends of fibrinogen molecules, exposing binding sites that allow the resulting fibrin monomers to polymerise spontaneously into half-staggered protofibrils and then fibres. The clot is then stabilised when factor XIIIa introduces covalent cross-links between fibrin strands, greatly increasing the clot's mechanical strength and resistance to breakdown.

Role in disease

Excessive fibrin formation contributes to thrombosis, including deep vein thrombosis and the arterial clots that cause myocardial infarction and stroke. Conversely, fibrinogen deficiency (afibrinogenemia) or impaired fibrin formation causes bleeding disorders. Degradation of fibrin by plasmin — fibrinolysis — produces fragments including D-dimer, a clinically important marker used in the diagnosis of thrombotic disease.

Applications

Purified fibrinogen and thrombin are combined in fibrin glue (fibrin sealant), a surgical adhesive used to control bleeding and seal tissues.
