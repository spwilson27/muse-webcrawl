Syncytin

Syncytin
TypeFamily of envelope-derived fusogenic proteins
OriginCaptured retroviral env genes (endogenous retroviruses)
Human membersSyncytin-1 (ERVW-1), Syncytin-2 (ERVFRD-1)
FunctionCell–cell fusion in placental development
LocationPlacenta (syncytiotrophoblast)

Syncytins are a family of proteins found in placental mammals that mediate the fusion of trophoblast cells to form the syncytiotrophoblast, the multinucleated layer that interfaces with maternal blood in the placenta. Remarkably, syncytins are encoded by env genes captured from ancient endogenous retroviruses that infected the germlines of mammalian ancestors; their viral fusogenic machinery was co-opted ("exapted") for reproduction.

Human syncytins

Humans have two principal syncytins. Syncytin-1, identified in 2000, is the envelope protein of the endogenous retrovirus ERVWE1 (family W) and binds the neutral amino-acid transporter ASCT2 (SLC1A5) as its receptor. Syncytin-2, characterised in 2003, derives from ERVFRDE1, uses the receptor MFSD2A, and additionally carries an immunosuppressive domain that may help shield the fetus from the maternal immune system — another inheritance from retroviral envelope biology. Reduced syncytin expression and defective trophoblast fusion are associated with pre-eclampsia and intrauterine growth restriction, and abnormal syncytin expression has been reported in some cancers and neurological conditions.

Evolutionary significance

Different mammalian lineages independently captured different retroviral envelope genes as syncytins — primates, rodents (syncytin-A and -B), lagomorphs, carnivores, ruminants (which use them in their distinctive placental structures) and even a viviparous lizard lineage have their own versions. This convergent domestication is a striking example of convergent evolution and of the constructive role of viruses in host evolution: the invasive, fused placenta characteristic of eutherian mammals may have been enabled repeatedly by viral genes. The oldest known syncytin dates to over 100 million years ago, roughly coinciding with the radiation of placental mammals.
