The siege of Sarajevo was a prolonged blockade of Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia and Herzegovina, during the Bosnian War. Lasting from 2 April 1992 to 29 February 1996 — 1,425 days, longer than the siege of Leningrad — it was the longest siege of a capital city in the history of modern warfare. Bosnian Serb forces of the Army of Republika Srpska surrounded the city and subjected it to shelling and sniper fire from the surrounding hills, while the outnumbered Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina defended it.

Life under siege

The city's roughly 350,000–450,000 trapped residents endured years without reliable water, electricity, gas or heating; food arrived through a UN airlift — the longest-running humanitarian airlift in history — and through a hand-dug tunnel beneath the airport runway connecting Sarajevo to Bosnian-held territory. Snipers and shelling made everyday movement dangerous: the main boulevard was nicknamed Sniper Alley, and the shelling of markets and queues, including the Markale massacres of 1994 and 1995, killed dozens of civilians at a time. Despite this, Sarajevans sustained a defiant cultural life of concerts, theatre and newspapers, famously symbolised by cellist Vedran Smailović playing in the ruins.

End of the siege

The second Markale massacre in August 1995 helped trigger NATO's Operation Deliberate Force bombing campaign against Bosnian Serb positions. The Dayton Agreement of November–December 1995 ended the war, and the siege was formally lifted on 29 February 1996 as Bosnian government forces took control of the suburbs. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia later convicted several VRS commanders, including Stanislav Galić and Dragomir Milošević, of conducting a campaign of terror against Sarajevo's civilians.
