Yan tan tethera

Yan tan tethera is the first line (and common name) of a set of traditional shepherds' counting numerals once used across Northern England — in Cumberland, Westmorland, Yorkshire, Lancashire, and neighbouring counties — to count sheep, and also for counting stitches in knitting and other tally work. The numerals descend from Brythonic Celtic counting systems related to Welsh, surviving in English dialects long after the Celtic languages themselves vanished from the region.

The numbers

Versions vary by locality; a typical Cumberland sequence runs:

Number12345678910
Wordyantantetherametherapimpsetheraletherahoveradoveradick

Counting commonly proceeded to twenty (a "score"), sometimes marked on a stick or by shifting a pebble from hand to hand, and the rhythm of the words — often chanted as yan, tan, tethera, methera, pimp — made them memorable and easy to pace. Related rhymes appear in children's counting-out games, and the composer Harrison Birtwistle used "Yan Tan Tethera" as the title of a mechanical pastoral opera (1984).

Origins

The system's resemblance to Welsh numerals (un, dau, tri, pedwar, pump) and to Breton and Cornish forms points to a Common Brittonic ancestor. Linguists regard the scores as evidence that a Cumbric language akin to Welsh persisted in the northwest into the early medieval period, and that its number words survived as a technical vocabulary among shepherds for centuries afterward.
