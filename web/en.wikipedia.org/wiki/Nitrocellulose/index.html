Nitrocellulose

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Nitrocellulose (guncotton) fibres

Nitrocellulose (also known as cellulose nitrate) is a highly flammable compound formed by nitrating cellulose through exposure to a mixture of nitric and sulfuric acids. Depending on the degree of nitration, it ranges from lacquer-grade material to guncotton, a powerful explosive. Trade names have included pyroxylin, collodion, and Xyloidine.

History

The French chemist Théophile-Jules Pelouze reported nitrated paper and cotton in 1838, and Christian Friedrich Schönbein is generally credited with the discovery of guncotton in 1846, recognising its potential as a propellant more powerful than black powder. Early manufacture was dangerous — unstable residual acid caused devastating factory explosions — until Frederick Abel's pulping and washing process (patented 1865) made production acceptably safe. Nitrocellulose later became the basis of smokeless powder (Poudre B, 1884; ballistite; cordite) and of celluloid, the first commercial plastic, as well as early photographic and motion-picture film stock.

Properties

Nitrocellulose is a white or slightly yellow, fibrous material resembling cotton wool. It ignites readily and burns extremely rapidly with a bright flame; highly nitrated grades (above ~12.5% nitrogen) can detonate. It dissolves in ether–alcohol mixtures to form collodion, and in acetone and esters for lacquers. Its notorious flammability led to the replacement of nitrate film by acetate "safety film" in the mid-20th century.

Uses

Modern applications include smokeless propellants and explosives, lacquers and wood finishes, nail polish, printing inks, leather dressings, membranes for molecular biology (blotting), and the classic magician's flash paper. Old nitrate film archives require special storage because decomposing stock can spontaneously combust.
