Harry Beck

Henry Charles Beck (4 June 1902 – 18 September 1974) was an English technical draughtsman who created the present London Underground Tube map in 1931. Beck drew up the diagram in his spare time while working as an engineering draughtsman at the London Underground Signals Office. He believed that passengers riding underground were not concerned about geographical accuracy and were more interested in how to get from one station to another and where to change trains.

Born: 4 June 1902, Leyton, London

Died: 18 September 1974 (aged 72), Southampton

Occupation: Technical draughtsman, graphic designer

Known for: London Underground Tube map (1933)

The Tube map

Before Beck, Underground maps were drawn geographically, becoming cluttered and hard to read as the network sprawled. Beck, drawing on the conventions of electrical circuit diagrams, redrew the network using only horizontal, vertical, and 45-degree diagonal lines, with evenly spaced stations and interchanges clearly marked. The Underground's publicity department initially rejected the design as too radical, but Beck persisted, and a trial pocket map issued in 1933 proved an immediate success with the public. The design has remained in use, with modifications, ever since.

Beck received little payment beyond a modest fee and for decades no credit; the map became a design icon recognised around the world, and in 2006 it was voted Britain's second-favourite design icon in a BBC poll, behind Concorde. Beck's name is now credited on every copy of the Tube map.

Later work

Beck continued to revise the map until about 1960, when the publicity office commissioned a new version from Harold Hutchison without consulting him, a slight that distressed him deeply. He also produced a diagram for the Paris Métro (never adopted), railway maps for British Railways regions, and taught typography and colour design at the London School of Printing and Kindred Trades.
