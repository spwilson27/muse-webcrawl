Galvanic anode

A galvanic anode, or sacrificial anode, is the main component of a galvanic cathodic protection system used to protect buried or submerged metal structures from corrosion. It is made from a metal alloy with a more "active" (more negative) electrode potential than the metal of the structure being protected. The difference in potential between the two metals causes the galvanic anode to corrode preferentially, so that the protected structure is consumed far more slowly — the anode is sacrificed to protect the structure.

Materials

The three most commonly used anode materials are zinc, aluminium alloys and magnesium. Zinc anodes are widely used on ships' hulls, ballast tanks and seawater pipelines; aluminium alloy anodes (typically aluminium–zinc–indium) offer higher capacity per unit weight and are common on offshore structures; magnesium anodes, with the highest driving voltage, are used mainly for buried pipelines and tanks in soil, and in domestic water heaters.

Design and maintenance

Anodes are cast in shapes suited to their application — slabs and bracelets for pipelines and hulls, rods for water heaters — and are sized so their mass provides the required protective current for a design life. Because they are consumed in service, sacrificial anodes must be inspected periodically and replaced when wasted. Galvanic systems need no external power, in contrast to impressed-current cathodic protection, but their low driving voltage limits their use in high-resistivity environments.
