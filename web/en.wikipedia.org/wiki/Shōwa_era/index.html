The Shōwa era (昭和時代, Shōwa jidai) is the period of Japanese history corresponding to the reign of Emperor Shōwa (Hirohito) from 25 December 1926 until his death on 7 January 1989. At 62 years, it is the longest era of a Japanese emperor in recorded history. The era's name, drawn from the Chinese Book of Documents, means "enlightened peace."

The Shōwa era encompasses dramatic upheaval: the rise of militarism and ultranationalism in the 1930s, the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Pacific War, Japan's defeat and occupation by the Allied Powers (1945–1952), democratization under a new constitution, and the postwar "economic miracle" that made Japan the world's second-largest economy.

Early Shōwa and militarism

The era began amid financial crisis and political instability. The Shōwa financial crisis of 1927, assassinations of civilian politicians, and the Mukden Incident of 1931 — which led to the occupation of Manchuria — marked the ascendancy of the military. Japan withdrew from the League of Nations, and in 1937 full-scale war with China began.

Pacific War

In December 1941 Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, opening the Pacific War against the United States and its allies. After initial expansion across Southeast Asia and the Pacific, Japan suffered a series of defeats. In August 1945 the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, together with the Soviet declaration of war, preceded Japan's surrender on 2 September 1945.

Occupation and recovery

Under the Allied occupation led by General Douglas MacArthur, Japan adopted a pacifist constitution (1947), land reform, and democratization. The San Francisco Peace Treaty (1951) restored sovereignty in 1952. Rapid industrial growth from the 1950s to the early 1970s transformed Japan; symbols of recovery included the 1964 Tokyo Olympics and the opening of the Shinkansen.

Late Shōwa

The oil shocks of the 1970s slowed growth, and the 1980s saw the rise of an asset price bubble. Emperor Hirohito died on 7 January 1989 and was succeeded by Akihito, beginning the Heisei era.
