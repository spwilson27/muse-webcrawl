Creole language

A creole language, or simply creole, is a stable natural language that develops from the mixing of different languages into a new one within a fairly brief period of time: often, a pidgin evolved into a full-fledged language. While the concept is similar to that of a mixed or hybrid language, creoles are often characterized by a tendency to systematize their inherited semantics (frequently relexifying much of the vocabulary), without the extreme morphological irregularities and broken transmission found in some other contact languages.

Formation

Creoles typically arose in colonial settings — plantations, trading posts and ports — where speakers of many languages needed a common medium. When children grow up speaking a simplified contact language (a pidgin) as a target of acquisition, they expand it into a complete language with native speakers, a process called nativization. The language supplying most vocabulary is called the lexifier; the others are substrate languages, which may influence grammar and phonology.

Examples

Well-known creoles include Haitian Creole (French-based, spoken by over ten million people and an official language of Haiti), Jamaican Patois and other English-based Caribbean creoles, Cape Verdean Creole (Portuguese-based), Papiamento in the ABC islands, Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea, Louisiana Creole, and Mauritian Creole. Some scholars also classify languages such as Afrikaans as creoles or "semi-creoles", though this is debated.

Creole studies

The field of creole studies examines how creoles form and what they reveal about language acquisition and change. Derek Bickerton's language bioprogram hypothesis proposed that creole grammars reveal children's innate default grammar; other scholars emphasise gradual formation, substrate influence or ordinary processes of language change. Where a creole coexists with its lexifier, a post-creole continuum of varieties may develop, from basilect (deepest creole) through mesolect to acrolect (closest to the standard).

Status

Creoles were long stigmatised as "broken" versions of European languages, but linguists regard them as full languages with their own grammars. Several have gained official recognition, standardised orthographies and literatures.
