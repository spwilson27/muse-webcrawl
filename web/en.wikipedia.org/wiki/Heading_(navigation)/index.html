Heading (navigation)

In navigation, the heading of a vessel, aircraft or vehicle is the direction in which its nose (or bow) is pointing at any given moment. Heading is expressed in degrees, measured clockwise from north — either true north or magnetic north, depending on the reference used — from 0° to 360°.

Heading versus course

Heading is not the same as course. The course is the intended or actual direction of travel over the ground. Because wind and currents can push a vessel or aircraft sideways, the direction in which the craft is pointing (its heading) may differ from the direction in which it is actually moving (its course over ground, or track). The angular difference between the two is caused by drift, and navigators compensate by steering a heading offset into the wind or current — a technique known as crabbing in aviation.

Types of heading

  • True heading — measured relative to the geographic (true) north pole.
  • Magnetic heading — measured relative to magnetic north; the difference from true heading is the magnetic variation for the locality.
  • Compass heading — the heading as read from a magnetic compass, which additionally differs from magnetic heading by deviation, errors caused by the craft's own magnetic field.
  • Grid heading — measured relative to grid north on a map projection.

Measurement

Heading is traditionally measured with a compass — magnetic, gyrocompass or fluxgate — and in modern craft by GPS-derived attitude and heading reference systems (AHRS) and inertial navigation systems. In flight, heading is a primary parameter controlled by the pilot and assigned by air traffic control, conventionally spoken as three digits (e.g. "heading two seven zero").
