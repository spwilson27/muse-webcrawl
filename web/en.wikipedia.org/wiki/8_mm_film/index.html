8 mm film

8 mm film is a motion picture film format in which the film strip is 8 millimetres (0.31 in) wide. It exists in two main versions: Standard 8 (also called Regular 8), introduced by Eastman Kodak in 1932, and Super 8, introduced in 1965. Both were designed primarily for home movies, making amateur filmmaking affordable for the first time.

Standard 8

Standard 8 film was manufactured by splitting 16 mm film lengthwise. It came on 25-foot spools that were exposed on one half, flipped, and exposed on the other, then slit and spliced after processing to give 50 feet of finished film.

Super 8

Super 8 used a single 8 mm-wide strip in a convenient drop-in cartridge, with smaller perforations that allowed a nearly 50% larger image area and better picture quality. Sound versions carried a magnetic stripe. Super 8 became the dominant amateur and educational format until home video displaced it in the 1980s.

Legacy

The format remains in niche use by film students and experimental filmmakers, and Kodak revived Super 8 camera production in the 2010s. The most famous 8 mm footage is arguably Abraham Zapruder's film of the assassination of President John F. Kennedy in 1963.
