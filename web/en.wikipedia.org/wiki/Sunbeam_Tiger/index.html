Sunbeam Tiger

ManufacturerRootes Group
Production1964–1967
AssemblyWest Bromwich, England (Jensen Motors)
Engine260 cu in (4.3 L) Ford V8 (Mk I); 289 cu in (4.7 L) Ford V8 (Mk II)
DesignerCarroll Shelby (prototype development)

The Sunbeam Tiger is a high-performance V8 version of the British Rootes Group's Sunbeam Alpine convertible, produced from 1964 to 1967. Following the formula proven by the AC Cobra, Rootes fitted the modest four-cylinder Alpine with a Ford small-block V8, creating a fast and relatively affordable Anglo-American sports car.

Development

Rootes's American west-coast manager Ian Garrad, seeking a performance image for the Alpine, arranged for Carroll Shelby — who had performed the same V8 transplant on the AC Ace to create the Cobra — to build a prototype, while a second was built by racing driver Ken Miles. Shelby's car, using a Ford 260 cu in V8, was chosen for production. Because Rootes lacked capacity, final assembly was contracted to Jensen Motors at West Bromwich, which built the cars from bodies supplied by Pressed Steel.

Models

The Tiger Mk I (1964–1966) used the 260 cu in (4.3 L) Ford V8 producing 164 hp, with a four-speed manual gearbox and a stronger Salisbury rear axle; rack-and-pinion steering replaced the Alpine's steering box. Later Mk I cars (Mk IA) adopted the Alpine Series V body with curved door tops. The rarer Tiger Mk II (1967) received the 289 cu in (4.7 L) engine with 200 hp, an oil cooler, and distinctive eggcrate grille and wheel-arch extensions. Total production was about 7,128 cars, of which only around 600 were Mk IIs.

Competition and legacy

Tigers competed in rallies and sports-car racing in Europe and the United States, with class successes including the 1964 Geneva Rally, and drag-racing and SCCA competition in America. Production ended in 1967 after Chrysler's takeover of Rootes, since the car's Ford engine could not be replaced by a suitable Chrysler unit. The Tiger is remembered as one of the classic "hairy-chested" British sports cars and period models are highly collectible; it is also fondly known to television audiences as the car driven by Maxwell Smart in the opening of Get Smart.
