Devotio

In ancient Roman religion, devotio was a ritual act of self-sacrifice in which a Roman commander vowed his own life — and the lives of the enemy — to the gods of the underworld in exchange for victory in battle. Having pronounced the formula, the commander charged into the enemy ranks seeking death, believing that his sacrifice would doom the opposing army.

The Decii

The most famous examples involve members of the gens Decia. At the Battle of Veseris in 340 BC, the consul Publius Decius Mus, told by a dream that the army whose general died would prevail, devoted himself and the Samnite enemy to the di Manes and Tellus, then rode alone into the enemy line and was killed; the Romans won the battle. His son, also Publius Decius Mus, repeated the act at the Battle of Sentinum in 295 BC against the Samnites and Gauls, and a grandson was said to have done the same at Asculum in 279 BC against Pyrrhus of Epirus.

The rite

According to Livy, the pontifex maximus dictated the prayer while the commander stood on a spear, wearing the toga praetexta with his head veiled. The vow offered the devoted man, the enemy legions and their auxiliaries to the gods below. If the devoted commander survived, his act was considered unfulfilled and an expiatory offering — including a buried statue — was required instead.
