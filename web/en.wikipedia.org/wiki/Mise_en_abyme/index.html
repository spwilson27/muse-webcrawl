Mise en abyme

Mise en abyme
TypeNarrative technique
Coined byAndré Gide (1893)
OriginHeraldry (French: abyme, centre of a shield)

Mise en abyme (French: [miz ɑ̃n‿abim]; also mise en abîme) is a formal technique in Western art and literature in which an image contains a smaller copy of itself, in a sequence appearing to recur infinitely; "recursive" in theory, though in practice the series terminates after two or three levels. The term is derived from heraldry, where it describes a coat of arms appearing in miniature at the centre of a larger shield.

In literary criticism, mise en abyme denotes a nested narrative — a story within a story that mirrors, comments on, or duplicates the enclosing work. The concept was introduced into criticism by André Gide in an 1893 journal entry, in which he observed his fondness for works containing a transposed, smaller-scale version of their own subject, citing examples in painting such as memoirs pictures by Memling and Quentin Matsys, and in literature the play within the play in Shakespeare's Hamlet.

Origins

The phrase comes from heraldry: an escutcheon placed en abyme sits at the centre (the "abyss" or heart) of the shield. Gide borrowed the term in 1893, and it was later taken up systematically by the critic Lucien Dällenbach in his 1977 study Le récit spéculaire ("The Mirror in the Text"), which classified types of internal duplication in narrative.

In literature

Examples include the play "The Mousetrap" within Hamlet, which restages the murder at the heart of the plot; the interpolated tales of Don Quixote; and Edgar Allan Poe's "The Oval Portrait". In the twentieth century the device became a hallmark of self-reflexive and postmodern fiction, as in the works of Jorge Luis Borges, whose story "The Circular Ruins" concerns a man who dreams another man into existence and discovers that he himself is being dreamed. Frames can also nest without mirroring exactly, as in the Arabian Nights, where Scheherazade tells stories containing further stories.

In visual art and film

In painting, the convex mirror in Jan van Eyck's Arnolfini Portrait (1434) reflects the whole scene from the opposite viewpoint, and Velázquez's Las Meninas (1656) places a mirror and the painter himself inside the picture. A modern commercial example is the Droste effect, named after the Dutch cocoa brand whose packaging showed a nurse carrying a tray bearing the same packet, apparently ad infinitum. In cinema, mise en abyme occurs in films about filmmaking and in recursive dream narratives; critics also apply the term to the play of mirrors and doubles in works by directors such as Jean-Luc Godard.

Related concepts

Mise en abyme is closely related to, but distinct from, the Droste effect (a purely visual recursion), infinite regress, self-reference, and metafiction. Not every story within a story qualifies: the embedded element must in some way mirror the structure or theme of the whole.
