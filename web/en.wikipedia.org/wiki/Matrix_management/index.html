Matrix management

Matrix management is an organizational structure in which some individuals report to more than one supervisor or leader — relationships described as solid-line or dotted-line reporting. More broadly, it may also describe the management of cross-functional, cross-business groups and other work models that do not maintain strict vertical business units or silos grouped by function and geography.

Matrix management, developed in the United States in the 1970s, gave rise to matrix organizations, and had its wider applications beginning in the 1980s.

Overview

There are different types of matrix management, including strong, weak, and balanced. Here are the general characteristics:

  • Weak matrix management — This type of matrix management gives more managerial authority to the functional manager (FM), and the role of the project manager (PM) is more like that of a coordinator or expediter. The functional manager has oversight on the project, and the PM has more of a support role.
  • Balanced matrix management — The PM and FM share authority equally. The PM is given more time and power to oversee the project. The staff reports to both managers.
  • Strong matrix management — In this case, most of the power and authority lie with the PM. The project manager has a full-time role, controls the budget, and talks directly to top-level administrators. This style gives the project manager more influence within the company among staff.

Context

Typically, a person has two managers in a matrix management system: a direct manager and a project or product manager. The direct manager is in charge of hiring, firing, training the employee, and making final decisions on promotions, bonuses, vacation, as well as workflow. The project or product manager coordinates the employee's work across multiple projects and products, and decides, with input from the direct manager, which project the employee should be prioritized on.

For example, engineering groups in a technology company might be organized into various sub-teams. The members of each sub-team report to their respective managers (e.g., the engineering manager), but they're also assigned to specific products, and each product has a product manager. The engineering manager and the product manager must collaborate to ensure that each of the various products is properly staffed.

History

The terminology was in common use by the 1970s, with the structure taking hold at companies such as Digital Equipment Corporation under Ken Olsen. Matrix management was also prominent in the aerospace industry, where complex projects required integration of many functional specialties.
