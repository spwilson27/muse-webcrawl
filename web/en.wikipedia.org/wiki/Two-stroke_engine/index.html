Two-stroke engine

A two-stroke (or two-cycle) engine is a type of internal combustion engine that completes a power cycle with two strokes (up and down movements) of the piston during one revolution of the crankshaft — one power stroke per revolution, compared with one per two revolutions in a four-stroke engine. This gives a high power-to-weight ratio and mechanical simplicity, at the cost of greater fuel consumption and exhaust emissions.

Operation

In the common crankcase-scavenged design, the underside of the piston compresses the incoming fuel–air mixture in the sealed crankcase. As the piston rises it compresses the charge in the cylinder and uncovers the intake port; as it falls after ignition it uncovers the exhaust port and then the transfer port, letting the pressurised fresh mixture sweep the burnt gases out (scavenging) before the cycle repeats. Because the crankcase serves as a pump, lubrication is usually provided by oil mixed with the fuel ("premix").

Advantages and disadvantages

Two-strokes are light, simple, cheap and produce power every revolution, suiting handheld tools, motorcycles, outboard motors and large marine diesels (the biggest two-stroke diesels are among the most thermally efficient engines ever built). Their drawbacks — some fresh charge escaping unburned through the exhaust, oil burning in the combustion chamber, and poor idling — have driven them from most road vehicles under modern emissions rules, though direct-injection designs mitigate the losses.
