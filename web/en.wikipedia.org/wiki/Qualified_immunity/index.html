Qualified immunity

In United States law, qualified immunity is a legal principle that grants government officials performing discretionary functions immunity from civil suits, unless the plaintiff shows that the official violated "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known". Most often applied to police officers sued under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, it is distinct from absolute immunity, which protects officials such as judges, prosecutors and the President.

History

The doctrine was introduced by the Supreme Court in Pierson v. Ray (1967) as a good-faith defence, and given its modern form in Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982), which made immunity turn on objective legal standards rather than an official's subjective intent. In Saucier v. Katz (2001) and later cases the Court refined the two-step inquiry: whether a constitutional right was violated, and whether it was clearly established at the time.

Criticism and reform

Qualified immunity is one of the most debated doctrines in American law. Critics — including judges across the ideological spectrum — argue that the "clearly established" standard, glossed to require precedent with closely matching facts, makes accountability for misconduct nearly impossible and was invented without basis in the 1871 statute's text. Defenders argue it shields officials from personal liability for reasonable split-second decisions and prevents courts from second-guessing them. After the murder of George Floyd in 2020, several states, including Colorado and New Mexico, abolished or limited the defence in state law, though federal reform bills stalled in Congress.
