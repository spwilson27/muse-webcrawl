Caprinae

The subfamily Caprinae, also sometimes referred to as the family Capridae, is part of the ruminant family Bovidae and consists of mostly medium-sized bovids. A member of this subfamily is called a caprine. Caprines include the domestic sheep and goats, their wild relatives the ibexes, chamois, tahrs, and serows, as well as the muskox and the takin. Prominent members include the mountain goat of North America, the Barbary sheep, and the goral. Most caprines are adapted to mountainous terrain, with stocky builds, gripping hooves, and strong hindquarters for climbing.

Characteristics

Caprines are generally sturdy, short-legged ruminants; both sexes usually bear horns, which in males can be massive ridged structures used in head-butting contests during the rut. Their teeth and digestive system are adapted to coarse mountain forage. Many species form herds with seasonal altitudinal migration. The group likely evolved in Asia during the Miocene and spread through mountain ranges of Eurasia and North America.

Classification

Tribes and genera commonly included in Caprinae include:

  • Caprini — Capra (goats and ibexes), Ovis (sheep), Hemitragus (tahr), Pseudois (bharal), Ammotragus (Barbary sheep), Arabitragus, Nilgiritragus
  • Ovibovini — Ovibos (muskox), Budorcas (takin)
  • Naemorhedini — Naemorhedus (gorals), Capricornis (serows), Oreamnos (mountain goat), Rupicapra (chamois)

The boundaries of the subfamily and the relationships among these groups have been revised repeatedly with molecular data; some classifications raise caprines to family rank (Capridae) or merge them differently within Bovidae.

Domestication

Two caprines were among the first animals domesticated by humans: the domestic goat, descended chiefly from the bezoar ibex (Capra aegagrus), and the domestic sheep, descended from the Asiatic mouflon (Ovis gmelini). Both were domesticated in the Fertile Crescent roughly 10,000 years ago and remain economically vital worldwide for meat, milk, wool, and hides.
