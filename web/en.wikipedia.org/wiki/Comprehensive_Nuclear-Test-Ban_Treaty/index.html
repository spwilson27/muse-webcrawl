The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT) is a multilateral treaty that bans all nuclear test explosions, whether for military or civilian purposes, in all environments. It was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in September 1996 and opened for signature that month. The treaty has been signed by more than 185 states and ratified by over 175, but it has not entered into force, because several of the 44 "Annex 2" states whose ratification is required—including the United States, China, and Russia (which revoked its ratification in 2023)—have not ratified.

Verification

A global International Monitoring System of seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound, and radionuclide stations, overseen by the CTBTO Preparatory Commission in Vienna, detects nuclear explosions; the system successfully identified all announced North Korean tests. On-site inspections are provided for once the treaty enters into force. The treaty builds on the earlier Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963, which banned atmospheric, underwater, and outer-space tests among its parties.

History

Moves toward a comprehensive ban began in the 1950s amid concern over radioactive fallout, but negotiations repeatedly stalled over verification and linkage to disarmament. After the Cold War, the indefinite extension of the Non-Proliferation Treaty (1995) lent momentum to talks at the Conference on Disarmament, whose failure to reach consensus led supporters to bring the text directly to the UN General Assembly in 1996. Most nuclear-weapon states observe testing moratoria despite the treaty's pending legal status.
