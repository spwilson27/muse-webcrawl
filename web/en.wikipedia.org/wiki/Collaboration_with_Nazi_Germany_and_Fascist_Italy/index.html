Collaboration with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy

Collaboration with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy describes the cooperation of governments, military forces, police, and civilian populations in occupied and Axis-aligned countries with the German and Italian occupiers during the Second World War. Collaboration ranged from pragmatic accommodation by administrations seeking to protect their populations, through economic and industrial cooperation, to ideological alignment and active participation in persecution, deportation, and military operations alongside the Axis.

Forms of collaboration

  • State collaboration — governments such as Vichy France under Marshal Pétain maintained formal relations with Germany and enacted cooperation policies under the armistice.
  • Military collaboration — foreign volunteers and formations fought with the Wehrmacht and Waffen-SS, including the French Charlemagne Division, the Dutch and Flemish legions, and units recruited in the occupied Soviet territories.
  • Police collaboration — local police forces in many countries assisted in roundups and deportations of Jews and other targeted groups.
  • Economic collaboration — firms and administrations supplied labour, raw materials, and industrial output to the occupiers.
  • Ideological collaboration — fascist movements and parties, such as Nasjonal Samling in Norway and the Arrow Cross in Hungary, sought power through Axis support.

By country

France

The Vichy regime pursued collaboration as official policy after the 1940 armistice; its officials organised, among other measures, the Vel' d'Hiv roundup of July 1942, in which French police arrested over 13,000 Jews in Paris for deportation. The Milice, a collaborationist paramilitary force, fought the Resistance alongside the Gestapo.

Norway and the Low Countries

Vidkun Quisling's Nasjonal Samling government in Norway gave the word "quisling" to the language as a synonym for traitor. In the Netherlands, the NSB under Anton Mussert cooperated with the occupation, while Dutch civil servants and police were heavily involved in implementing German measures; the Netherlands suffered the highest Jewish death rate in Western Europe. Belgium saw collaboration by both Flemish (VNV, DeVlag) and Walloon (Rexist) movements.

Eastern Europe and the Balkans

In Croatia, the Ustaše regime of the Independent State of Croatia ran its own concentration camps, including Jasenovac. In occupied Soviet territories, local auxiliary police (Schutzmannschaft) participated in mass shootings. Attitudes and levels of collaboration varied widely, and many who cooperated also faced coercion; historians distinguish collaboration from the broader grey zone of accommodation under occupation.

Post-war reckoning

After liberation, collaborationists faced purges (épuration) ranging from legal trials and executions — Pétain was sentenced to death (commuted), Quisling and Pierre Laval were executed — to extrajudicial reprisals. Debate over the scale of complicity and resistance shaped national memory for decades, notably in France's reckoning with the Vichy past.
