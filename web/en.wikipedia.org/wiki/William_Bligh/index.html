William Bligh (9 September 1754 – 7 December 1817) was an officer of the Royal Navy and a colonial administrator. He is best known for the mutiny on HMS Bounty in 1789, in which his master's mate Fletcher Christian led the crew against him, and for his extraordinary open-boat voyage to safety afterwards.

The Bounty and the open-boat voyage

Bligh had sailed with Captain James Cook on his third voyage and was an accomplished navigator and hydrographer. Commanding the Bounty on a mission to carry breadfruit plants from Tahiti to the West Indies, he was set adrift with 18 loyal men in the ship's launch on 28 April 1789. Without charts, he navigated some 3,600 nautical miles to Timor — the greatest feat of open-boat navigation in history — losing only one man.

Later career

Acquitted at his court martial, Bligh fought at the Battle of Copenhagen (1801) under Nelson. As Governor of New South Wales from 1806, his attempt to stamp out the rum trade provoked the Rum Rebellion of 1808, the only successful armed takeover of an Australian government. He was later promoted to rear-admiral and vice-admiral, dying in London in 1817.
