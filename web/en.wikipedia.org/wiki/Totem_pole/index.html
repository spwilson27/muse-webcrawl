Totem pole

Totem poles (Haida: gyaahlgálang) are monumental carvings, a type of Northwest Coast art, consisting of poles, posts or pillars carved with symbols or figures. They are usually made from large trees, mostly western red cedar, by First Nations and Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest coast, including the Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka'wakw, Nuu-chah-nulth and Coast Salish peoples.

Meaning and function

The figures carved on a pole—known as crests—represent beings or events in the oral histories of the owning family or clan, and serve as a public record of lineage, rights, and privileges. Poles are not religious idols; types include house frontal poles, house posts supporting roof beams, memorial poles raised in honour of a deceased chief, mortuary poles containing remains, and "shame poles" erected to embarrass debtors or wrongdoers.

History

Carving reached monumental scale in the 19th century, when metal tools obtained through the fur trade and wealth from that trade, combined with the potlatch system, enabled hereditary chiefs to raise poles at great feasts. Colonial suppression of the potlatch in Canada (1885–1951) led to a sharp decline; many poles were removed to museums. Since the mid-20th century a carving revival led by artists such as Bill Reid, Robert Davidson and Tony Hunt has raised new poles, and some museum-held poles have been repatriated.

Carving and raising

A pole is carved lying horizontally from a single cedar log, hollowed at the back on tall examples to prevent cracking and reduce weight, then raised by a crew with ropes and scaffolding during a potlatch at which the carver is paid and the pole's stories are publicly validated.
