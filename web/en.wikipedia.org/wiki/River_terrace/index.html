River terrace

River terraces are elongated, step-like landforms that flank the sides of floodplains and river valleys. Each terrace consists of a flat surface, called a tread, bordered downslope by a steeper slope called a riser. Terraces represent former floodplains that were abandoned when the river cut down to a lower level, and flights of terraces record the history of a valley's incision.

Formation

Terraces form when a river that previously deposited sediment, or flowed at a stable level, begins to incise its channel. Causes of incision include a fall in base level (commonly sea level), tectonic uplift of the land, and changes in climate that alter the balance between a river's discharge and its sediment load. During glacial periods, rivers often carried abundant coarse sediment and aggraded their valleys; in warmer interglacial periods, reduced sediment supply relative to discharge promoted downcutting, leaving the old valley floor stranded as a terrace.

Two main types are recognized. Fill terraces develop when a thick fill of alluvium is partially excavated, leaving remnants of the fill surface. Strath terraces are cut into bedrock, with only a thin veneer of gravel on the flat bedrock surface (the strath). Paired terraces occur at matching heights on both sides of a valley, indicating vertical incision, whereas unpaired terraces result when the river migrates laterally as it cuts down.

Significance

Because each terrace tread was once a floodplain, the sequence of terraces in a valley is a record of landscape evolution and environmental change. Gravels capping the terraces may contain datable material—such as volcanic ash, buried soils, or archaeological artifacts—that allows geologists to reconstruct the timing of incision. The terraces of the River Thames in England, for example, have yielded a long record of Pleistocene climate change and early human occupation.

Terraces are also practically important: their flat, well-drained surfaces above flood level have long been favored for settlement, agriculture, and transport routes.
