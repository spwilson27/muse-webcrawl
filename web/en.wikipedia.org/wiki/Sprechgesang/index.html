Sprechgesang

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Notation for Sprechstimme, using crossed noteheads

Sprechgesang (German: [ˈʃpʁɛçɡəˌzaŋ], "spoken singing") is an expressionist vocal technique between singing and speaking. Though sometimes used interchangeably with Sprechstimme, Sprechgesang is more closely associated with the rhythmic, pitched speaking style of Engelbert Humperdinck's opera Königskinder (1897), while Sprechstimme is associated with the composers of the Second Viennese School, especially Arnold Schoenberg.

Technique

In Sprechgesang the performer realises notated rhythms and approximate pitches with the speaking voice rather than the sustained singing voice. The effect is a heightened, declamatory delivery that preserves the contour of a melodic line without fully singing its pitches. Schoenberg, in the foreword to Pierrot Lunaire (1912), instructed that the performer should transform the written melody into a speech melody, taking account of the given pitches but abandoning them by falling or rising immediately after sounding them.

History

The device has antecedents in melodrama, where spoken text is accompanied by music, and in the heightened declamation of earlier German opera. Humperdinck used notated speech-song in Königskinder. Schoenberg employed Sprechstimme in his Gurre-Lieder (1911) and most famously in Pierrot Lunaire, Op. 21, a cycle of 21 melodramas for speaker and chamber ensemble. Alban Berg used the technique extensively in Wozzeck and Lulu, distinguishing several gradations between speech and song. Kurt Weill and other composers of the Weimar era adopted similar effects for cabaret and theatre music.

Notation

Sprechgesang is commonly notated with small crosses (×) through the stems of notes, or with notes whose heads are replaced by crosses. The rhythm is given exactly, while pitches are intended as relative contours rather than exact intervals.

Related styles

Similar effects occur in the parlando of Italian opera, in recitative, and in later twentieth-century works. In popular music, talk-singing and the delivery associated with artists such as Rex Harrison in My Fair Lady are sometimes loosely compared to Sprechgesang, and the term has been applied to spoken-sung vocals in rock music.
