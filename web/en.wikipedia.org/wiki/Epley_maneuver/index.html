Epley maneuver

The Epley maneuver, or canalith repositioning procedure, is a maneuver used to treat benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) of the posterior semicircular canal. The maneuver was developed by Dr. John Epley and first reported in 1992. It seeks to move the free-floating calcium carbonate crystals (canaliths) out of the posterior semicircular canal of the inner ear and back into the utricle, where they no longer cause vertigo.

Procedure

The standard modified Epley maneuver for the right ear proceeds as follows, each position being held for about 30 seconds or until nystagmus and vertigo subside:

  1. The patient sits upright on an examination table with the head turned 45° toward the affected side.
  2. The patient is quickly lowered backward into a supine position with the head hanging slightly off the end of the table (the Dix–Hallpike position), reproducing vertigo.
  3. The head is rotated 90° to the opposite side.
  4. The patient rolls onto the side opposite the affected ear while the head is turned a further 45° so that the face points partly downward.
  5. The patient is helped back up into a sitting position.

The sequence is run in mirror image for the left ear. Guidelines historically recommended post-procedure restrictions (such as sleeping propped up), but evidence for their benefit is weak, and they are no longer routinely advised.

Effectiveness

Systematic reviews find the Epley maneuver effective, with short-term resolution rates of BPPV in roughly 70–80% of patients after one or two applications, and numbers-needed-to-treat of around two to three compared with sham treatment. It can be performed in primary care and taught for self-treatment at home with appropriate instruction. Recurrence of BPPV is common, and the maneuver can be repeated. Contraindications and caution include severe neck disease, unstable cardiovascular conditions, and recent retinal detachment; for horizontal-canal BPPV, other maneuvers such as the Lempert ("barbecue roll") are used instead.
