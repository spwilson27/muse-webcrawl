Atypical pneumonia

Atypical pneumonia, also known as walking pneumonia, is any type of pneumonia not caused by one of the pathogens most commonly associated with the disease. Its clinical presentation contrasts with that of "typical" bacterial pneumonia, which is usually lobar, caused by organisms such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, and presents with high fever, productive cough and consolidation signs. Atypical pneumonia tends to have a more gradual onset, a dry cough, prominent systemic symptoms and patchy interstitial infiltrates on imaging.

Interstitial infiltrates on a chest X-ray of atypical pneumonia.

Causes

The classic "atypical" organisms are bacteria that lack a peptidoglycan cell wall or live intracellularly: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydophila pneumoniae, Chlamydophila psittaci (psittacosis) and Legionella pneumophila (Legionnaires' disease), as well as Coxiella burnetii (Q fever) and Francisella tularensis. Many respiratory viruses — influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, adenovirus and coronaviruses — also produce an atypical picture. Because the organisms often lack cell walls or reside inside cells, they do not respond to beta-lactam antibiotics.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically develop over several days and include low-grade fever, non-productive cough, headache, sore throat, myalgias and malaise. Extrapulmonary features may accompany specific pathogens: erythema multiforme and haemolytic anaemia with Mycoplasma, gastrointestinal symptoms and confusion with Legionella, and exposure histories such as contact with birds (psittacosis) or parturient animals (Q fever). Patients are often only mildly ill — hence "walking pneumonia".

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on clinical suspicion supported by chest imaging, serology, urinary antigen testing (for Legionella and pneumococcus) and nucleic-acid amplification tests. First-line treatment is usually a macrolide such as azithromycin; respiratory fluoroquinolones or doxycycline are alternatives. Severe cases, especially Legionnaires' disease, require prompt therapy and supportive care.

History

The distinction arose in the 1930s and 1940s as clinicians recognised pneumonias that did not follow the classic pneumococcal course; Hobart Reimann described "primary atypical pneumonia" in 1938, and the Cold War-era Eaton agent was eventually identified as Mycoplasma pneumoniae.
