Myomectomy

Myomectomy, sometimes also called fibroidectomy, is the surgical removal of uterine leiomyomas, also known as fibroids. In contrast to a hysterectomy, the uterus remains preserved and the woman retains her reproductive potential. Myomectomy is performed to relieve symptoms caused by fibroids — such as heavy menstrual bleeding, pelvic pressure, urinary frequency, and infertility — while preserving the uterus.

Fibroids may be subserosal, intramural, or submucosal; the surgical approach depends on their size and location.

Indications

Myomectomy is indicated for women with symptomatic uterine fibroids who wish to preserve fertility or avoid hysterectomy. Fibroids can interfere with conception or pregnancy when they distort the uterine cavity, and their removal may improve pregnancy rates in selected cases.

Approaches

  • Abdominal myomectomy (laparotomy) — performed through an incision in the lower abdomen; suitable for large or numerous fibroids.
  • Laparoscopic myomectomy — a minimally invasive approach using small incisions and a camera; robot-assisted laparoscopy is an increasingly common variant. Tissue may be removed by morcellation, though concerns about spreading undiagnosed uterine sarcoma have led regulators to restrict power morcellation.
  • Hysteroscopic myomectomy — used for submucosal fibroids protruding into the uterine cavity; a resectoscope is passed through the cervix without incisions.
  • Vaginal and cervical myomectomy — for prolapsed pedunculated fibroids presenting through the cervix.

Technique

Key principles include preoperative planning with imaging, reducing blood loss (for example with vasopressin injection, tourniquets, or preoperative GnRH analogues), careful enucleation of each fibroid from its pseudocapsule, and meticulous multilayer closure of the uterine wall to minimise the risk of rupture in a subsequent pregnancy. Pregnancy after myomectomy is often recommended to be delivered by caesarean section if the uterine cavity was entered.

Risks and outcomes

Risks include haemorrhage (sometimes requiring transfusion or, rarely, hysterectomy), postoperative adhesions, and recurrence of fibroids; cumulative recurrence rates of roughly 10–50% over five years are reported, depending on the number of fibroids removed. Compared with hysterectomy, myomectomy carries a somewhat higher risk of blood loss but preserves fertility and the uterus.

Alternatives

Alternatives to myomectomy include hysterectomy, uterine artery embolization, focused ultrasound treatment, and medical management with hormonal or non-hormonal drugs.
