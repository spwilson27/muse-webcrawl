The Courtauld Institute of Art, commonly referred to as The Courtauld, is a self-governing college of the University of London specialising in the study of the history of art and conservation. Founded in 1932 through the philanthropy of the industrialist and art collector Samuel Courtauld, Viscount Lee of Fareham and Sir Robert Witt, it is among the world's leading centres for art-historical teaching and research. Its public gallery, The Courtauld Gallery, is housed with the institute in Somerset House in London.

The Courtauld occupies the north wing of Somerset House on the Strand.

History

Samuel Courtauld, who had built one of Britain's finest collections of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist painting, endowed the institute to establish art history as an academic discipline in Britain; the Courtauld's first home was Home House, Portman Square, which also displayed his collection. The institute moved to Somerset House in 1989. Its directors have included Anthony Blunt (1947–1974) — later disgraced as a Soviet spy — and it has been closely associated with the Warburg Institute tradition of iconographic scholarship.

The Courtauld Gallery

The gallery displays the collection bequeathed by Samuel Courtauld, including Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère, Van Gogh's Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear, and major works by Cézanne, Gauguin, Renoir, Monet and Seurat, together with the Princes Gate Collection of Old Master paintings and drawings assembled by Count Antoine Seilern. The gallery reopened in November 2021 after a major redevelopment ("Courtauld Connects").

Teaching and research

The Courtauld offers a BA in History of Art, MA programmes (including curating and the history of art), postgraduate research degrees, and renowned courses in the conservation of easel paintings and of wall paintings. Its library and photographic libraries (the Conway and Witt Libraries) are major research resources. Alumni include many directors of national museums and galleries, leading curators and art historians, which has led to the informal description of its graduates' prominence as the "Courtauld mafia".
