Anonymous source

An anonymous source is a person who provides information to a journalist or other investigator on condition that their identity is not disclosed. Anonymous sources are a longstanding feature of investigative reporting, enabling whistleblowers and insiders to reveal wrongdoing, corruption or information of public interest that they could not safely disclose under their own names.

The use of anonymous sources is also controversial. Critics argue that unattributed claims cannot be checked by readers and may be used to plant self-serving or false information, while journalists respond that anonymity is often the only way to obtain information that powerful institutions wish to conceal.

Terminology

Journalists distinguish several levels of attribution. Information given on the record may be quoted and attributed by name. Information on background may be used but attributed only generally, for example to "a senior official". Deep background information may be used but not attributed at all, and information given off the record is not intended for publication, though conventions differ between countries and news organisations. The most famous anonymous source in American journalism, "Deep Throat" of the Watergate scandal, was revealed in 2005 to have been FBI associate director Mark Felt.

Protection of sources

Many jurisdictions protect journalists from being compelled to reveal their sources through shield laws or case law recognising a reporter's privilege. In the United States there is no federal shield law, although most states provide some protection. Journalists have on occasion gone to jail for contempt of court rather than reveal a source, as in the case of Judith Miller in 2005. Internationally, source protection is recognised as an element of freedom of the press under instruments such as Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Criticism and standards

News organisations typically require editors to know the identity of anonymous sources and to approve their use, and style guides urge that anonymity be granted only when a source has information that cannot be obtained otherwise and faces a genuine risk. Anonymous sourcing has been implicated in reporting failures, including aspects of the reporting on Iraqi weapons of mass destruction before the 2003 invasion of Iraq, leading major newspapers to tighten their policies.
