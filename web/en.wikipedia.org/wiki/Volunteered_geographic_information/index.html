Volunteered geographic information

Volunteered geographic information (VGI) is the harnessing of tools to create, assemble, and disseminate geographic data provided voluntarily by individuals. The term was coined by geographer Michael Goodchild in 2007 as a special case of the broader phenomenon of user-generated content. VGI underlies projects such as OpenStreetMap, in which millions of contributors map roads, buildings, and points of interest, often using consumer GPS devices and aerial imagery.

Motivation and examples

Contributors are motivated by altruism, local knowledge, professional interest, reputation, and recreation. Beyond OpenStreetMap, VGI includes geotagged photographs on platforms such as Flickr and Panoramio, citizen-science observations like eBird bird sightings, crisis mapping during disasters (for example, the crowd-sourced mapping of the 2010 Haiti earthquake), and community reporting systems for potholes, hazards, or public services.

Quality and criticism

Because contributions come from non-professionals, VGI quality varies. Research comparing OpenStreetMap to commercial and government datasets has often found volunteer data comparable or superior in completeness for road networks in well-mapped areas, while coverage is uneven globally and demographic biases in the contributor base leave some places and features under-represented. Issues include vandalism, inconsistent tagging, licencing, and the absence of formal quality assurance — mitigated in practice by community review and versioning. Goodchild's related concept of "citizens as sensors" frames VGI as a vast, distributed network of human observers complementing remote sensing.

Relation to other concepts

VGI overlaps with crowdsourcing, participatory GIS, neogeography, and citizen science. Some researchers distinguish VGI from "contributed" geographic information produced incidentally, such as location traces from mobile phones, which raise distinct privacy questions.
