Aperture masking interferometry

Aperture masking interferometry
FieldObservational astronomy
PurposeHigh angular resolution imaging beyond atmospheric seeing
Related techniquesSpeckle interferometry; long-baseline interferometry

Aperture masking interferometry (or simply aperture masking) is a form of speckle interferometry in observational astronomy in which a mask containing a pattern of small holes is placed over the aperture of a single telescope. The mask converts the telescope into an array of small apertures whose light interferes, producing an interferogram from which an image can be reconstructed at the telescope's diffraction limit — typically far sharper than the resolution allowed by atmospheric seeing. The technique dates from work by Roger Jennison in radio astronomy in the 1950s and was developed for optical use by John E. Baldwin and colleagues at Cambridge in the 1980s.

Method

By sampling only small sub-apertures, each pair of holes forms a baseline measuring one spatial frequency of the source. With a non-redundant arrangement of holes, each baseline is unique, and the closure phase — the sum of fringe phases around a triangle of holes — is immune to atmospheric phase errors, allowing the recovery of true image phases. The method sacrifices most of the telescope's light, so it is best suited to bright objects such as nearby stars, binary systems, and the dusty envelopes of evolved stars. Aperture masks have been fitted to large ground-based telescopes such as Keck and the VLT, and a masking mode (the NIRISS aperture masking interferometer) flies on the James Webb Space Telescope, where it enables high-contrast imaging of exoplanets and disks.
