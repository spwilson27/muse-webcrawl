Brutalist architecture

Brutalist architecture is an architectural style that emerged during the 1950s in the United Kingdom, among the reconstruction projects of the post-war era. Brutalist buildings are characterised by minimalist constructions that showcase the bare building materials and structural elements over decorative design. The style commonly makes use of exposed, unpainted concrete or brick, angular geometric shapes and a predominantly monochrome colour palette; other materials, such as steel, timber and glass, are also featured.

Origins and name

The term derives from the French béton brut ("raw concrete"), used by Le Corbusier for buildings such as the Unité d'habitation in Marseille (1952). The Swedish architect Hans Asplund coined nybrutalism ("New Brutalism"), and the critic Reyner Banham popularised the English term for the work of Alison and Peter Smithson. Despite the popular association with the word "brutal", the style's name has no connotation of harshness in its original usage.

Characteristics

Brutalist buildings often repeat modular elements, express their structure and services honestly — stair towers, water tanks and ducts are externalised — and use board-marked concrete (béton brut) imprinted with the timber formwork. Many are large-scale public buildings: universities, libraries, council offices, theatres and social housing blocks.

Reception and revival

Long reviled as ugly and associated with urban decay, many Brutalist landmarks were demolished, including Birmingham Central Library and Preston bus station (the latter later listed). Since the 2010s the style has undergone a critical reappraisal, with preservation campaigns, listing decisions and popular books (the "SOS Brutalism" initiative) defending works such as London's Barbican and National Theatre.
