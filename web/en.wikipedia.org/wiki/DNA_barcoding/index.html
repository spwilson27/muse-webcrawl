DNA barcoding

DNA barcoding is a method of species identification using a short section of DNA from a specific gene or genes. The premise of DNA barcoding is that by comparison with a reference library of such DNA sections (also called "sequences"), an individual sequence can be used to uniquely identify an organism to species, just as a supermarket scanner uses the familiar black stripes of the UPC barcode to identify an item in its stock against its reference database.

One of the most commonly used barcode regions for animals is a 648-bp segment of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene, proposed by Paul Hebert and colleagues in 2003. For plants, the chloroplast genes rbcL and matK are standard; for fungi, the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region is used.

Applications

  • Identifying species from fragments, larvae, gut contents or processed products where morphology is unavailable.
  • Detecting food fraud, such as mislabeled fish in markets and restaurants.
  • Quarantine and biosecurity screening of invasive species and pests.
  • Verifying the contents of herbal medicines and other commercial products.
  • Assessing biodiversity, including from environmental DNA (eDNA) in water and soil samples.

Reference libraries

The Barcode of Life Data System (BOLD) is the principal repository for barcode sequences, housing millions of records linked to voucher specimens. The Consortium for the Barcode of Life (CBOL) coordinates global barcoding efforts.

Advantages and limitations

Barcoding can identify specimens that are damaged, immature, or morphologically indistinguishable, and can reveal cryptic species. However, it may fail to discriminate very recently diverged species, can be confounded by hybridization and introgression, and depends on the completeness and accuracy of reference libraries. Related techniques include metabarcoding, which barcodes whole communities from bulk samples using high-throughput sequencing.
