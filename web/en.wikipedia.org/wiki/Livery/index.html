Livery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Livery: from household uniform to aircraft paint scheme

A livery is, in its oldest sense, a distinctive uniform or insignia worn by the servants, retainers or officials of a household, company or sovereign, displaying the colours or arms of the master. The word derives from the French livrée, "delivered" — the allowance of food, clothing or badges handed out to dependents. In medieval and early modern England, the giving of livery badges to armed retainers ("livery and maintenance") became a politically potent practice that statutes repeatedly tried to restrict, and the great London guilds are still known as livery companies, their senior members as liverymen.

Modern senses

By extension, "livery" now denotes any standardised identifying design applied to vehicles and their operators. A livery yard or livery stable keeps horses for hire or boards them for owners. Aircraft carry an airline livery — the complete paint and branding scheme applied across a fleet — and racing cars, trains, buses and taxis likewise wear the livery of their operator or sponsor. Historic examples range from the scarlet-and-gold of British royal coachmen, still seen on state occasions, to the chequered "Battenburg" markings of British emergency vehicles. The term also survives in "livery of seisin", the feudal ceremony by which possession of land was publicly delivered to a new tenant.
