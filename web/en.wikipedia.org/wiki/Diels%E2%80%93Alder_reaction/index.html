Diels–Alder reaction

The Diels–Alder reaction is a chemical reaction between a conjugated diene and a substituted alkene, commonly termed the dienophile, to form a substituted cyclohexene derivative. It is the prototypical example of a pericyclic reaction with a concerted mechanism, and one of the most powerful methods in organic synthesis for building six-membered rings with control of stereochemistry.

History

Otto Paul Hermann Diels and Kurt Alder first documented the reaction in 1928, describing the addition of cyclopentadiene to quinone and related systems. For this work they received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1950.

Mechanism and selectivity

The reaction proceeds in a single concerted step through a cyclic transition state: the diene contributes four π-electrons and the dienophile two, so it is classified as a [π4s + π2s] cycloaddition, thermally allowed under the Woodward–Hoffmann rules. The reaction is stereospecific — the geometry of the dienophile is preserved in the product — and typically favours the endo product, in which substituents on the dienophile point towards the diene's π system (the Alder endo rule). The reverse process, the retro-Diels–Alder reaction, is used to unmask dienes and in mass spectrometry. Variants include the hetero-Diels–Alder reaction, which forms heterocycles, and the intramolecular Diels–Alder, widely used in total synthesis of natural products.
