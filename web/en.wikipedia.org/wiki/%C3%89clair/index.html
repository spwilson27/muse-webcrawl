Éclair

An éclair is a long, thin French pastry made of choux dough, filled with cream and topped with icing. The dough is piped into an oblong shape, baked until crisp and hollow, filled — classically with chocolate or coffee-flavoured pastry cream, or with whipped cream — and finished with fondant icing, commonly chocolate.

Etymology

The word éclair is French for "flash of lightning"; the pastry is said to be named either for the way it glistens with icing or because it is eaten quickly — "in a flash".

History

The éclair is generally credited to the early 19th century and is often attributed to the chef Antonin Carême, who refined many French pastries after the Revolution. Earlier forms of filled choux pastries, such as the pain à la Duchesse, preceded it. By the later 19th century the éclair was a staple of Parisian pâtisseries, and it spread internationally, becoming a standard bakery item in many countries.

Preparation

Choux pastry is piped onto baking trays, baked until puffed and dry, then cooled. The shells are filled from the ends or from beneath with crème pâtissière and dipped in fondant or ganache. Modern pâtissiers produce éclairs in a wide range of flavours, from salted caramel to matcha and fruit.
