Pietism

Pietism was an influential movement within Lutheranism that combined orthodox doctrine with an emphasis on personal devotion, heartfelt conversion, Bible study, and holy living. It arose in late 17th-century Germany in reaction to what its leaders saw as the cold formalism and intellectualism of established Lutheran orthodoxy.

Origins

The movement is usually dated from Philipp Jakob Spener's Pia Desideria (1675), which proposed small devotional gatherings (collegia pietatis), greater lay involvement in spiritual life, practical Christianity, and reform of theological education. Spener's disciple August Hermann Francke built Pietist institutions at Halle — schools, an orphanage, a publishing house — that became a centre of education, missions, and social work.

Influence

Pietism spread through Scandinavia and, via emigration, to North America. It influenced the Moravian Church under Count Zinzendorf, and through the Moravians it shaped John Wesley and the Methodist movement. Pietism encouraged foreign missions (the Danish-Halle mission to India), Bible distribution, hymnody, and lay piety, and it left a lasting mark on evangelical Christianity. Critics within orthodoxy complained of separatism and subjectivism, and "pietist" is sometimes used pejoratively for excessive moralism.
