The Divine Comedy (Italian: Divina Commedia) is an Italian narrative poem by Dante Alighieri, begun around 1308 and completed in 1321, shortly before the author's death. It is widely considered the pre-eminent work of Italian literature and one of the greatest works of world literature. The poem describes Dante's journey through the three realms of the Christian afterlife: Hell (Inferno), Purgatory (Purgatorio) and Paradise (Paradiso), guided first by the Roman poet Virgil and then by Beatrice, the embodiment of divine love.

Structure and journey

The poem has 100 cantos — an introductory canto plus 33 for each realm — written in terza rima, a three-line stanza form Dante invented. In the Inferno, Dante descends through nine concentric circles of Hell, where sins are punished by poetic symmetry (contrapasso): flatterers wallow in filth, the violent boil in blood, and traitors, worst of all, are frozen in ice at the centre, where Satan chews Judas, Brutus and Cassius. In Purgatorio he climbs a mountain island where souls are cleansed through penance. In Paradiso he rises through the celestial spheres to the Empyrean and a final, fleeting vision of God.

Significance

Dante wrote not in Latin but in his Tuscan vernacular, demonstrating that it could carry the highest poetry — a choice that helped make Tuscan the basis of standard Italian. The poem is at once theology, philosophy, satire and autobiography: Dante scores old political scores, placing enemies in Hell and honouring friends, and weaves in classical figures alongside Florentine neighbours. Its imaginative geography of the afterlife shaped Western ideas of Hell for centuries, and its influence runs from Chaucer and Milton to T.S. Eliot; like the Iliad of Homer, to whom Dante pays homage by making Virgil his guide, it became a foundation text of European literature.
