Nitration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electrophilic aromatic nitration of benzene

Nitration is a general class of chemical processes for introducing a nitro group (–NO2) into an organic compound. Most famously, aromatic nitration converts benzene and its derivatives into nitroarenes using "mixed acid" — a combination of concentrated nitric acid and sulfuric acid — and underpins the manufacture of explosives, dyes, pharmaceuticals, and agrochemicals.

Electrophilic aromatic nitration

In mixed acid, nitric acid is protonated and loses water to generate the nitronium ion (NO2+), a powerful electrophile. The aromatic ring attacks the nitronium ion, forming a resonance-stabilized carbocation intermediate (the sigma complex or arenium ion); a base then removes the ring proton, restoring aromaticity and yielding the nitroarene. Substituents on the ring govern the rate and the regiochemistry (ortho/para versus meta) of further nitration.

Applications

Nitrobenzene, the prototypical product, is produced on a vast industrial scale, mostly to be reduced to aniline for polyurethane manufacture. Successive nitration of toluene gives trinitrotoluene (TNT). Treatment of glycerol or cellulose with mixed acid gives nitroglycerin and nitrocellulose, respectively — technically nitrate esters rather than true C-nitro compounds, but made by closely related "nitration" processes. Nitro groups are also valuable intermediates because they can be reduced to amines, key building blocks in synthesis.

Safety and other nitrations

Nitrations are strongly exothermic and can run away if cooling fails; industrial processes use continuous-flow reactors and careful dosing to manage the hazard. Beyond aromatic substitution, nitration chemistry includes addition of nitrogen oxides to alkenes, nitration of alkanes at high temperature (the basis of nitromethane production), ipso nitration, and biological nitration pathways. The term should not be confused with nitrification, the microbial oxidation of ammonia in soils.
