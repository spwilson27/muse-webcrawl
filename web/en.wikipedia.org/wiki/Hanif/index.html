Hanif

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hanif (Arabic: حنيف, romanized: ḥanīf; plural: حنفاء, ḥunafāʾ) is an Arabic term referring in the Qur'an to a person who follows a pure monotheism, turning away from idolatry. The Qur'an particularly associates the term with Abraham (Ibrahim), who is described as a hanif and a muslim — neither Jew nor Christian, but devoted to the one God before the Torah and the Gospel.

In the Qur'an

The word occurs about a dozen times in the Qur'an. Abraham is its principal exemplar: "Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was one inclining toward truth, a Muslim [submitting to God]" (Q 3:67). Believers are commanded to follow the millat Ibrāhīm ḥanīfan — "the creed of Abraham, the hanif" — and to set one's face toward religion as a hanif (Q 30:30). The concept thus presents Islam as a restoration of Abraham's original, uncorrupted monotheism.

Pre-Islamic usage

Islamic tradition describes certain pre-Islamic Arabs as hunafa who rejected the polytheism of Mecca in search of the religion of Abraham; figures named in the sources include Waraqa ibn Nawfal, a cousin of Khadija, and the inquirer Zayd ibn Amr. Scholars debate the term's origins and historical usage: it may derive from a Syriac root used for "gentile" or "pagan", later revalued in the Qur'an to denote the upright monotheist, and the actual religious practice of the supposed pre-Islamic hanifs is difficult to reconstruct.
