Classic of Filial Piety

The Classic of Filial Piety, also known by its Chinese name Xiaojing (孝經), is a Chinese classic treatise giving advice on filial piety: the virtue of reverence and obedience owed by children to their parents and by subjects to their ruler. Traditionally attributed to a conversation between Confucius and his disciple Zengzi (Zeng Shen), the short text — some 1,800 characters in eighteen short chapters — was probably compiled in the early Han dynasty from earlier materials, and it became one of the most widely read texts in imperial China, serving for centuries as a primer for schoolchildren.

Content

The text presents filial piety (xiao) as the root of all virtue and the foundation of social and political order. Confucius explains that filial duty begins with caring for one's own body — "our bodies, hair and skin are received from our parents, and we must not dare to injure them" — and culminates in establishing oneself in the Way and making one's name known so as to glorify one's parents. Successive chapters apply the doctrine to the Son of Heaven, the feudal princes, the great officers, the scholar-officials, and commoners, and discuss mourning rites, service to the ruler, and the ruler's use of filial piety in government. The text extends the family ethic to the state: a man who is filial at home will be loyal in office.

History and influence

The Xiaojing was cited in Han sources and received an imperial commentary attributed to Emperor Xuanzong of Tang (742 CE), whose text, with the Song-dynasty commentary of Xing Bing, became standard. Under the Song, the work was carved on stone steles (the famous Kaicheng Stone Classics) and used in the civil examination curriculum. Although not one of the Five Classics or Four Books, it counted among the Thirteen Classics of the Confucian canon. Its ideas influenced law — unfilial conduct was one of the "Ten Abominations" in imperial Chinese legal codes — and spread throughout East Asia, shaping ethics in Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Notable English translations include those by James Legge (1879) and Henry Rosemont Jr. and Roger Ames (2009), who translated it as The Chinese Classic of Family Reverence.
