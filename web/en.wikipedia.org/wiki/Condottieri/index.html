Condottieri (singular condottiero or condottiere) were Italian captains in command of mercenary companies during the late Middle Ages and the Renaissance. In Renaissance Italy, the condottieri were effectively professional soldiers whose services were hired by the Italian city-states and the Papacy, who fought a series of wars among themselves from the eleventh to the fifteenth centuries and with overseas powers.

Origins

The word derives from condotta, the contract between a captain and his employer specifying pay, numbers, and duration of service. From the thirteenth century, Italian communes increasingly hired foreign mercenary companies — such as Werner von Urslingen's Great Company and the White Company of the Englishman Sir John Hawkwood — rather than relying on citizen militias. By the fourteenth century Italian captains predominated, and the great companies became professional, disciplined forces centred on heavy cavalry (men-at-arms) supported by infantry and crossbowmen.

Heyday and criticism

Successful condottieri could amass wealth and even principalities: Francesco Sforza seized Milan in 1450 and founded a dynasty. Venice honoured Bartolomeo Colleoni with Verrocchio's famous equestrian monument. Contemporaries such as Machiavelli attacked mercenary arms as untrustworthy and ruinous, and battles between condottieri armies — fought for limited objectives and ransomable prisoners — were sometimes criticised as bloodless manoeuvre, though campaigns in fact brought sieges, sackings, and heavy losses.

Decline

The French invasion of 1494 began the Italian Wars; artillery, massed infantry, and the growing scale of national armies outclassed the condottieri system, which faded during the sixteenth century as states built standing forces.
