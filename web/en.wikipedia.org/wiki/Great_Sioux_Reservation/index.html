Great Sioux Reservation

The original Great Sioux Reservation under the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868).

The Great Sioux Reservation was a large Native American reservation established for the Lakota Sioux (Teton Sioux) by the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868. It originally encompassed all of present-day South Dakota west of the Missouri River (the West River region), including the sacred Black Hills, together with additional hunting grounds in neighbouring territories — in all, some 60 million acres where the Sioux were guaranteed "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation".

Reduction of the reservation

The discovery of gold in the Black Hills in 1874 triggered an influx of miners and, after the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, the United States seized the Black Hills under an 1877 act, taking some 7.3 million acres. In 1889 the remaining reservation was broken up by the Sioux Act into five smaller reservations — Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Standing Rock, Cheyenne River and Lower Brule — and roughly nine million acres of the remainder were opened to non-Indian settlement, a process completed in 1890 alongside the admission of South Dakota to the Union.

The Black Hills claim

The seizure of the Black Hills became one of the most famous Native American land claims. In 1980 the US Supreme Court ruled in United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians that the taking had been illegal and awarded compensation, which with interest has grown to more than a billion dollars. The Sioux nations have refused to accept the money, maintaining that the Black Hills were never sold and demanding the return of the land — a position summed up in the slogan "The Black Hills are not for sale." Legislation to return federal lands in the hills, such as the Bradley Bill of the 1980s, has repeatedly been proposed but not enacted.
