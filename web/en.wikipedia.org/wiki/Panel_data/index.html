Panel data

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Panel data, also called longitudinal data, are multi-dimensional data collected by observing the same subjects — individuals, households, firms, countries, or other units — repeatedly over time. Panel data combine the features of cross-sectional data (many units at one time) and time series data (one unit over many times), and they are a staple of econometrics and the social sciences.

Structure

A panel is balanced if every unit is observed in every time period, and unbalanced otherwise. Examples include the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) in the United States, the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP), the British Household Panel Survey and its successor Understanding Society, and firm-level datasets such as Compustat.

Analysis

Panel data allow researchers to control for unobserved, time-invariant differences between units. The fixed effects model gives each unit its own intercept, eliminating bias from any constant omitted characteristics; the random effects model treats unit effects as random draws and is more efficient when those effects are uncorrelated with the regressors — an assumption tested by the Hausman test. First-differencing and within-group demeaning are equivalent estimation strategies for fixed effects. Dynamic panels, in which lagged outcomes appear as regressors, require instrumental-variable estimators such as Arellano–Bond.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages include greater variability and less collinearity than pure time series, the ability to study dynamics and transitions (such as entries into poverty or unemployment), and control of unobserved heterogeneity. Limitations include attrition — units dropping out of the panel over time — measurement problems, and the complexity of modelling dependence within units, which typically requires cluster-robust standard errors.
