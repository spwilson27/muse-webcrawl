Ambrotype

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ambrotype (from Greek ἄμβροτος, "immortal") is a positive photograph on glass made by means of the wet plate collodion process. It was patented by James Ambrose Cutting in 1854 and was widely used for portraiture in the 1850s and early 1860s, competing with the daguerreotype and later largely displaced by the cheaper tintype and paper prints from negatives.

Process

An ambrotype is an underexposed collodion negative on a plate of glass. When the plate is backed with a dark material—black velvet, lacquer, or painted backing—the dark areas of the scene, which are nearly transparent in the negative, appear dark, while the image areas coated with light-scattering silver appear milky and light, so the picture reads as a positive. The image is laterally reversed, like a daguerreotype, because it is a unique direct-positive object rather than a print from a negative. Plates were usually varnished for protection and mounted in hinged cases, often with hand-tinting applied to cheeks, jewelry, and clothing.

Invention and popularity

The underlying idea—that an underexposed collodion negative could appear positive against a dark ground—had been described by Frederick Scott Archer, inventor of the collodion process, and others before Cutting's 1854 United States patents on the sealed, balsam-mounted version, for which he coined the name from his own middle name, Ambrose. In Britain the process was commonly called a "collodion positive".

Ambrotypes were cheaper and faster to produce than daguerreotypes, required shorter exposures, and lacked the mirror-like reflectivity that could make daguerreotypes hard to view. They were produced in the same standard sizes as daguerreotypes—from whole-plate down to small gem formats—and were a staple of portrait studios. During the American Civil War, ambrotypes of soldiers were made in large numbers alongside tintypes, which used the same principle on thin iron plates and were cheaper still.

Decline

By the mid-1860s the ambrotype declined as paper photographs—albumen prints and cartes de visite made from reusable glass negatives—became dominant, and as the tintype took over the inexpensive portrait market. Ambrotypes remain collectible, and the wet collodion process has seen a modern revival among fine-art photographers.
