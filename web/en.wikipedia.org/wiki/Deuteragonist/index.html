Deuteragonist

In Greek drama, the deuteragonist was the second actor, after the protagonist.

In literature, the deuteragonist (from Ancient Greek δευτεραγωνιστής, "second actor") is the second most important character in a narrative, after the protagonist and before the tritagonist. The deuteragonist often acts as a constant companion to the protagonist or as someone who continues actively aiding the protagonist's cause.

History

The term originates in ancient Greek theatre, where drama originally had a single actor plus a chorus. According to Aristotle's Poetics, Aeschylus introduced the second actor (the deuteragonist), allowing dialogue between characters rather than only between the actor and the chorus; Sophocles later added a third actor, the tritagonist.

Role in narrative

The deuteragonist may be a sidekick, a love interest, a family member, or a secondary viewpoint character. Unlike the antagonist, the deuteragonist usually supports the protagonist, though in some works the deuteragonist is themselves an antagonist or an antihero. In ensemble works the line between protagonist and deuteragonist can blur, and modern fiction frequently develops deuteragonists into protagonists of their own subplots or sequels.

Examples

Classic examples include Dr. Watson to Sherlock Holmes, Sancho Panza to Don Quixote, and Samwise Gamgee to Frodo Baggins in The Lord of the Rings. In film, deuteragonists often share near-equal screen time with the protagonist, as in buddy films.
