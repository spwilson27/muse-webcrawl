Demining

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Demining, or mine clearance, is the process of removing land mines from an area. In military operations, the object is to rapidly clear a path through a minefield so that forces can advance; this is often called breaching. In humanitarian demining, the goal is to clear land completely to a standard safe for civilian use, removing all mines and unexploded ordnance. Humanitarian demining is a central component of mine action, which also includes victim assistance, risk education, stockpile destruction, and advocacy.

Methods

Manual demining remains the most reliable method. Deminers systematically scan ground with metal detectors and, on detecting a signal, carefully excavate with prodders and trowels, working in marked lanes while wearing body armour and visors. Because modern mines contain very little metal, detection is slow and difficult.

Other methods include:

  • Mine detection dogs and rats, trained to smell explosive compounds; organisations such as APOPO train African giant pouched rats ("HeroRATs") for this work.
  • Mechanical clearance, using flails, tillers, and armoured vehicles that detonate or destroy mines in the ground.
  • Explosive methods, such as mine-clearing line charges used in military breaching.
  • Ground-penetrating radar and dual-sensor detectors that combine radar with metal detection to distinguish mines from metallic clutter.

Humanitarian mine action

The Ottawa Treaty (Mine Ban Treaty) of 1997, championed by the International Campaign to Ban Landmines, obliges signatory states to clear mined areas under their control. The United Nations coordinates mine action through UNMAS, and organisations such as The HALO Trust and the Mines Advisory Group employ thousands of local deminers in countries including Cambodia, Angola, Mozambique, Afghanistan, and Ukraine. Clearance is expensive and slow: a land mine may cost only a few dollars to lay but hundreds or thousands of dollars to clear safely.

Naval mine clearance

At sea, naval mines are cleared by minesweepers and minehunter vessels, which use sweeps, sonar, and remotely operated vehicles to locate and neutralise mines, protecting shipping lanes after conflicts.
