Aladdin (Arabic: علاء الدين, ʿAlāʾ ad-Dīn) is the hero of the Middle Eastern folk tale "Aladdin and the Wonderful Lamp" (also translated "Aladdin and the Magic Lamp"). It is one of the best-known tales of the One Thousand and One Nights (Arabian Nights), although it does not appear in the oldest Arabic manuscripts: it was added by the French translator Antoine Galland in the early 18th century, who reported hearing it in Paris in 1709 from the Syrian storyteller Hanna Diyab. It is now regarded as an "orphan tale" of the collection, of probable Syrian origin.

The story

Aladdin, the lazy son of a poor tailor in a city in China, is recruited by a sorcerer from the Maghreb who pretends to be his uncle. The sorcerer sends the boy into a trapped cave to retrieve an oil lamp, but Aladdin is sealed inside when he refuses to hand the lamp over first. Trapped, he rubs a magic ring the sorcerer had lent him and summons a jinn who frees him; later, rubbing the lamp summons a far more powerful jinn who grants his wishes. With the lamp's help Aladdin becomes rich, marries the sultan's daughter (named Badroulbadour in Galland), and builds a magnificent palace.

The sorcerer returns and steals the lamp — and with it the palace and princess — by tricking Aladdin's wife into exchanging the old lamp for a new one. Aladdin uses the ring's jinn to recover his wife and palace, kills the sorcerer, and later defeats the sorcerer's vengeful brother, living happily ever after.

Adaptations

The tale has been adapted countless times in pantomime, opera, film, and animation, most famously in Disney's 1992 animated film Aladdin, which moved the setting to the fictional Arabian city of Agrabah and made the lamp's genie a comic figure voiced by Robin Williams; a live-action remake followed in 2019. The name Aladdin and the image of a genie emerging from a lamp are fixtures of popular culture worldwide.
