Ghent Altarpiece

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The Ghent Altarpiece opened, its tiers of saints, angels and the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb glowing in gold and jewel colours]
The Ghent Altarpiece opened, its tiers of saints, angels and the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb glowing in gold and jewel colours

The Ghent Altarpiece, also known as the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb, is a large polyptych altarpiece in St Bavo's Cathedral, Ghent, Belgium, completed in 1432. Begun by Hubert van Eyck and finished by his brother Jan van Eyck, it is a founding masterpiece of Early Netherlandish painting and of oil painting itself — and the most frequently stolen artwork in history.

The work

Twelve panels open to reveal the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb — Christ as the Lamb of God on an altar, adored by processions of saints, martyrs, hermits and knights in a paradisiacal landscape — beneath God the Father enthroned between the Virgin and John the Baptist, flanked by music-making angels and Adam and Eve. Closed, it shows the Annunciation and donors Jodocus Vijd and his wife kneeling before statues of the two Saint Johns. Its microscopic detail (jewels, fabrics, individual hairs, distant cities) announced what oil paint could newly do.

Thefts and restorations

Panels were hidden from iconoclasts, looted by French revolutionary troops (restored 1815), sold in part to Berlin, seized by the Nazis for the Altaussee salt mine, and recovered by the Monuments Men in 1945. In 1934 two lower panels were stolen; one (the Just Judges) has never been found — the copyist Jef Van der Veken painted the replacement, admitting on his deathbed complicity of knowledge. A decade-long conservation (2012–19) removed overpaint and revealed the Lamb's original, startlingly humanoid face, restored to view in 2020.
