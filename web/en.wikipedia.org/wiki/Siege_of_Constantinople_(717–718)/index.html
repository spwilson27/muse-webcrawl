Siege of Constantinople (717–718)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Siege of Constantinople of 717–718 was a major land and naval attack on the capital of the Byzantine Empire by the Umayyad Caliphate. The failure of the siege, masterminded by Caliph Sulayman and led by his brother Maslama ibn Abd al-Malik against Emperor Leo III, marked a decisive turning point in the Arab–Byzantine wars and halted Muslim expansion toward Europe from the east.

Background

After the failed first Arab siege of 674–678, the Umayyads prepared a far larger effort. In the midst of Byzantine civil turmoil, Leo III seized the throne in March 717. Maslama crossed into Anatolia with a huge army — Arabic sources give figures up to 120,000 men — while a fleet of some 1,800 ships under Sulayman ibn Mu'ad sailed to blockade the city.

The siege

The Arabs invested Constantinople in the summer of 717, building double siege lines facing the Theodosian Walls. Byzantine use of Greek fire devastated the Arab fleet, and the great chain across the Golden Horn prevented a naval assault. The winter of 717–718 was exceptionally harsh: the besiegers suffered famine and disease, and attacks by the Bulgars under Khan Tervel, allied with Byzantium, inflicted further heavy losses. A relief fleet from Egypt and North Africa, partly manned by Christian crews who defected, was ambushed and destroyed.

Aftermath

On 15 August 718, after thirteen months, Maslama abandoned the siege. The remnants of the Arab fleet were destroyed by storms on the return voyage. The victory secured the survival of the Byzantine Empire and is often compared in significance to the Battle of Tours (732) in the west.
