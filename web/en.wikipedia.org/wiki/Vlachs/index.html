Vlachs

Vlachs is a historical term of Germanic origin (from *walhaz, "foreigner", the same root as "Welsh" and "Walloon") used for the Romance-speaking peoples of Southeast Europe — descendants of Romanised populations south of the Danube and in the wider Balkan and Carpathian region. In medieval and early modern sources the name was also applied loosely to transhumant shepherds regardless of language. In modern usage "Vlach" most often refers to the Balkan Romance groups: the Aromanians, Megleno-Romanians, and Istro-Romanians, and sometimes to Romanians themselves, particularly in Greek, Serbian, and other neighbouring languages (e.g., Greek Vlachoi).

History

After Rome's withdrawal from Dacia (271 CE) and the Slavic settlements of the 6th–7th centuries, Latin-speaking populations persisted in the Balkan mountains and plains. Byzantine, Hungarian, and Slavic chronicles from the 10th–12th centuries mention Vlachs across Thessaly (called "Great Vlachia"), Macedonia, the Haemus range, and north of the Danube. Vlachs were famous as transhumant pastoralists, muleteers, and soldiers. The Second Bulgarian Empire was founded in 1185 by the brothers Peter and Asen, described in some sources as Vlachs, and Kaloyan corresponded with the Pope as ruler of "Bulgarians and Vlachs". North of the Danube, Vlach polities developed into the principalities of Wallachia (whose very name derives from "Vlach") and Moldavia.

Groups and languages

The Eastern Romance languages spoken by Vlach communities are Romanian (including Moldavian), Aromanian, Megleno-Romanian, and Istro-Romanian. Aromanian and Megleno-Romanian communities are concentrated in Greece, Albania, North Macedonia, and Bulgaria; Istro-Romanians survive in a few villages in Croatia's Istria. Many Balkan Vlach communities have assimilated linguistically into surrounding nations, and identity — Aromanian in particular — remains a subject of debate in Greece and elsewhere.
