Aptamer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aptamers are short, single-stranded nucleic acid molecules (DNA or RNA) — or, in some cases, peptides — that bind to a specific target molecule with high affinity and specificity because of their three-dimensional folded shape. The word combines the Latin aptus ("fitted") with the Greek meros ("part"). Aptamers are sometimes described as "chemical antibodies", because they can rival monoclonal antibodies in binding performance while being produced entirely by chemical synthesis.

Selection: SELEX

Aptamers are generated by an in vitro evolution process called SELEX (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential Enrichment), developed independently in 1990 by the laboratories of Larry Gold (Craig Tuerk and Gold) and Jack Szostak (Andrew Ellington and Szostak). Starting from a vast random library of up to 1015 different sequences, researchers pass the pool over the target, wash away weak binders, amplify the survivors by PCR, and repeat for many rounds with increasing stringency. The enriched sequences are then sequenced and characterised. Variations such as cell-SELEX select aptamers against whole living cells, and modern automated and microfluidic methods have greatly shortened the process.

Properties

Aptamers typically bind targets with dissociation constants in the nanomolar to picomolar range. Targets can include proteins, peptides, small molecules, metal ions, viruses, and whole cells. Compared with antibodies, aptamers are smaller, can be chemically synthesised with little batch variation, are generally non-immunogenic, tolerate chemical modifications that improve their stability against nucleases (such as 2'-fluoro or 2'-O-methyl sugars and locked nucleic acids), and can be selected against toxic or non-immunogenic targets that cannot raise antibodies. Their main disadvantages are rapid clearance by the kidneys (mitigated by conjugation to polyethylene glycol) and susceptibility to degradation when unmodified.

Applications

The first aptamer drug, pegaptanib (Macugen), an anti-VEGF RNA aptamer for wet age-related macular degeneration, was approved by the US FDA in 2004. A second, avacincaptad pegol (Izervay), a complement C5 inhibitor aptamer for geographic atrophy, was approved in 2023. Beyond therapeutics, aptamers are used as biosensors (in aptamer-based assays and field-effect sensors for detecting pathogens, toxins, and biomarkers), in targeted drug delivery (aptamer–drug conjugates and aptamer-decorated nanoparticles), in purification, and as research reagents. In nature, riboswitches function as aptamer domains that bind metabolites to regulate gene expression, suggesting aptamer-like binding is an ancient molecular strategy.
