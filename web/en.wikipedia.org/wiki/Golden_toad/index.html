Golden toad

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A male golden toad, brilliant orange, perched on a mossy log in cloud forest
A male golden toad, brilliant orange, perched on a mossy log in cloud forest

The golden toad (Incilius periglenes, formerly Bufo periglenes) was a species of true toad endemic to a tiny area of cloud forest near Monteverde in Costa Rica, famous for the males' brilliant orange colour. Discovered by science in 1964, it vanished within a quarter-century — last seen in 1989 — and became an icon of the global amphibian decline and of extinction in the climate-change era.

Biology

Males were unmistakable: fluorescent orange; females, larger, were black with scarlet-spotted yellow markings — extreme sexual dichromatism. The toads bred explosively in temporary rain pools in the elfin cloud forest of the Cordillera de Tilarán, hundreds converging for a few weeks each April–May, males wrestling for the fewer females; most tadpoles died as pools dried, and in good years thousands of toadlets left the water.

Extinction

The population crashed in 1987 (of ~43,500 eggs in studied pools, almost none survived a dry El Niño year) and never recovered — one male seen in 1988, one in 1989, none ever again; the IUCN declared it extinct in 2004. Explanations braid climate-driven dryness, the chytrid fungus devastating amphibians worldwide, and UV or contaminant cofactors; the golden toad's story — a species science knew for 25 years, on one protected mountaintop — remains conservation's memento mori.
