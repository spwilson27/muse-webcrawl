Pentameter

Pentameter (from Greek: πεντάμετρος, "five measures") is a poetic meter. It is a line of verse with five metrical feet. In English poetry, the most common form is iambic pentameter, in which each foot is an iamb — an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable — giving a line of ten syllables with alternating stress. In classical Greek and Latin poetry, "pentameter" most often refers to the dactylic pentameter used together with the hexameter in the elegiac distich.

Iambic pentameter

Iambic pentameter was established as the dominant meter of English verse during the early modern period. Geoffrey Chaucer used a form of it in The Canterbury Tales; the meter was refined by Edmund Spenser, Christopher Marlowe, and William Shakespeare, whose plays are written largely in blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter. John Milton used blank verse for the epic Paradise Lost, and the meter remained central to English poetry through the Romantics and beyond.

An example of strict iambic pentameter is John Keats's line from "Ode to a Nightingale", which can be scanned as five iambs:

"To cease upon the midnight with no pain"

In practice, poets vary the pattern with substitutions — for example, a trochee in the first foot, pyrrhic and spondaic feet, feminine endings, and caesuras (pauses within the line) — so that actual lines of iambic pentameter often depart from the strict template while maintaining five beats.

Related forms

  • Heroic couplet — rhymed pairs of iambic pentameter lines, the dominant form of Augustan poetry as practised by Alexander Pope and John Dryden.
  • Blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter, used for drama and epic.
  • Sonnet — in its Petrarchan and Shakespearean forms, a fourteen-line poem in iambic pentameter.

Dactylic pentameter

In classical prosody, the elegiac pentameter consists of two halves (hemiepeis) of two and a half dactylic feet each; despite the name, it is not five full feet. Combined with a preceding hexameter line, it forms the elegiac couplet used by Greek and Roman poets such as Ovid for elegy, epigram, and lament.

Other pentameters

Pentameter lines can be built from other feet: trochaic pentameter (five trochees) is rarer in English; anapestic and dactylic pentameters produce lines of fifteen syllables and are uncommon. The strict five-beat structure, together with the flexibility to vary the foot type, has made pentameter the workhorse line of many European poetic traditions.
