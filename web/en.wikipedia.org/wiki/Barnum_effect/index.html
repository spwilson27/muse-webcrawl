Barnum effect

The Barnum effect, also called the Forer effect, is a psychological phenomenon in which individuals accept vague, general personality descriptions as uniquely applicable to themselves. It helps explain the perceived accuracy of horoscopes, fortune-telling, cold reading, and some personality tests.

Forer's experiment

In 1948 psychologist Bertram R. Forer gave his students a "personalised" personality sketch and asked them to rate its accuracy; the average rating was 4.3 out of 5. In fact, every student had received the identical text, assembled from horoscope statements such as "You have a great need for other people to like and admire you." The effect is named after showman P. T. Barnum, associated with the phrase "there's a sucker born every minute" and whose attractions supposedly had "something for everybody".

Factors

The effect is stronger when the description is positive or flattering, when the subject believes it was individually prepared, and when it is attributed to a credible authority. Statements work best when they are vague, two-sided ("at times you are extroverted, while at other times you are reserved"), and widely true of most people.
