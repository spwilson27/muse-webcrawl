Chondrocyte

Chondrocytes (from Greek χόνδρος, chondros = cartilage + κύτος, kytos = cell) are the only cells found in healthy cartilage. They produce and maintain the cartilaginous matrix, which consists mainly of collagen and proteoglycans. Chondrocytes develop from mesenchymal stem cells through the process of chondrogenesis, passing through an intermediate stage called the chondroblast.

Structure and function

Mature chondrocytes sit in small cavities called lacunae within the cartilage matrix. Because cartilage is avascular, chondrocytes receive nutrients by diffusion and have a low metabolic rate, relying largely on anaerobic glycolysis. They secrete the extracellular matrix — principally type II collagen and the proteoglycan aggrecan — and also the enzymes that slowly turn it over.

Development

During endochondral ossification, chondrocytes in the growth plate proliferate, hypertrophy, and eventually undergo apoptosis as the cartilage template is replaced by bone. Regulation of chondrocyte differentiation involves signalling molecules such as Indian hedgehog, parathyroid hormone-related protein, and the transcription factor SOX9, which is essential for chondrogenesis.

Clinical significance

Loss or dysfunction of articular chondrocytes underlies osteoarthritis, in which the cartilage matrix degrades faster than it is repaired. Autologous chondrocyte implantation is a surgical technique in which a patient's own chondrocytes are cultured and re-implanted to repair cartilage defects.
