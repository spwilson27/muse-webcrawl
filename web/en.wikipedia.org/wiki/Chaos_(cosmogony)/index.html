Chaos (cosmogony)

Chaos (Ancient Greek: χάος, romanized: Kháos) is the mythological void state preceding the creation of the universe (the cosmos) in Greek creation myths. In Hesiod's Theogony, Chaos was the first thing to exist: "at the very beginning Chaos came into being", from which came Gaia (Earth), Tartarus (the Abyss), Eros (Love), Erebus (Darkness), and Nyx (Night). The word originally denoted a chasm or gaping void rather than disorder, though the modern meaning of "chaos" as complete disorder derives from it.

Hesiod

In Hesiod's Theogony (c. 730–700 BC), Chaos stood at the very beginning of the ordered universe. From Chaos came Erebus and black Night; from Night, in union with Erebus, came Aether (Brightness) and Day. Hesiod's Chaos has often been interpreted as a gaping emptiness, like a canyon or the space between heaven and earth.

Later interpretations

Later Greek and Roman writers, including Hyginus and Ovid, personified Chaos as a goddess and a shapeless mass — Ovid in the Metamorphoses describes it as "a rude and undeveloped mass" containing the discordant seeds of all things. The concept parallels creation-from-chaos (chaoskampf) themes elsewhere: the Babylonian Tiamat, the Norse Ginnungagap, and the formless waters in Genesis.

In philosophy and science

Anaximander's apeiron and later philosophical notions of a formless origin echo the idea. In modern usage, "chaos" shifted to mean disorder, giving rise to terms like chaos theory, which studies deterministic systems highly sensitive to initial conditions.
