Sex allocation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sex allocation is the allocation of resources to male versus female reproduction in sexual species. In dioecious species the concept principally concerns the sex ratio of offspring, while in hermaphrodites it concerns the division of resources between male and female function. Sex allocation theory is a well-developed branch of evolutionary biology, regarded by many as one of its most successful predictive frameworks.

Fisher's principle

R. A. Fisher argued in 1930 that, in a large randomly mating population, natural selection favours equal investment in sons and daughters. If one sex becomes rarer, parents producing that sex gain a fitness advantage because their offspring face less competition for mates; this frequency-dependent selection drives the population toward an equilibrium at which the total investment in each sex is equal. When sons and daughters cost the same to produce, this predicts a 1:1 sex ratio.

Departures from equality

Many species show systematically biased sex ratios, explained by extensions of the theory. W. D. Hamilton showed that local mate competition — when brothers compete for mates in isolated groups — favours strongly female-biased ratios. Under the Trivers–Willard hypothesis, mothers in good condition may benefit from biasing investment toward the sex with the higher variance in reproductive success. In sequential hermaphrodites, sex allocation takes the form of the timing of sex change, as analysed by Eric Charnov in his influential 1982 monograph The Theory of Sex Allocation.

Evidence

Sex allocation theory has been tested extensively in parasitoid wasps, fig wasps, plants, fish and mammals, and has enjoyed unusual quantitative success: for example, the precise female-biased ratios of fig wasps vary with the number of foundresses in the manner predicted by local mate competition theory.
