Photoevaporation


Photoevaporation is the dispersal of gas from a protoplanetary disk or planetary atmosphere by high-energy radiation.

Photoevaporation is the process whereby high-energy radiation — extreme ultraviolet (EUV), far ultraviolet (FUV) or X-ray photons — heats gas so that it escapes the gravitational pull of a star or planet. The term is used most often in the study of protoplanetary disks and exoplanet atmospheres.

Protoplanetary disks

A young star's own ultraviolet and X-ray emission heats the surface layers of its surrounding disk; gas heated to around 10,000 K flows away as a thermal wind. Nearby massive stars can also erode disks externally — the tadpole-shaped "proplyds" imaged by the Hubble Space Telescope in the Orion Nebula are disks being photoevaporated by the Trapezium stars. Photoevaporation is thought to set the lifetime of planet-forming disks (typically a few million years) and may help open the inner disk cavities seen in "transition disks", limiting the time available for gas-giant formation and migration.

Planetary atmospheres

Stellar XUV radiation can drive hydrodynamic escape of the upper atmospheres of close-in exoplanets. Photoevaporation is a leading explanation for the "radius valley" (or Fulton gap) in the size distribution of small exoplanets: planets that receive high irradiation are stripped of their hydrogen–helium envelopes and shrink to bare rocky cores, while more distant or more massive planets retain them. Extended escaping atmospheres have been observed directly, for example in Lyman-alpha transits of the hot Neptune GJ 436b.
