Finance

Finance is the study and discipline of money, currencies and capital assets. It is interrelated with economics but narrower in scope: where economics studies the production, consumption, and distribution of goods and services, finance focuses on how individuals, businesses, and governments allocate resources over time under conditions of risk and uncertainty, and on the systems and markets — banks, exchanges, and other institutions — that make that allocation possible. A key concept is the time value of money: a unit of money today is worth more than the same unit in the future because of its earning potential, and future cash flows are valued by discounting them at an appropriate rate of return.

Branches

  • Personal finance — budgeting, saving, investing, insurance, mortgages, and retirement planning for individuals and households.
  • Corporate finance — how firms raise capital (equity and debt), decide which projects to fund (capital budgeting), and manage working capital and payouts such as dividends.
  • Public finance — government revenue (taxation), expenditure, borrowing, and debt management.
  • Investment management — portfolio construction, asset pricing, and the analysis of securities such as stocks and bonds.

Financial markets and instruments

Financial markets — money markets, bond markets, stock markets, and derivatives markets — match savers with borrowers and allow risk to be transferred. Instruments include shares, bonds, loans, options, futures, and swaps. Prices in these markets aggregate information and expectations; theories such as the efficient-market hypothesis and the capital asset pricing model describe how returns relate to risk, while behavioural finance studies systematic departures caused by human psychology.

History

Finance has ancient roots in Mesopotamian and Roman lending and in medieval bills of exchange, but its modern form grew with the first joint-stock companies and stock exchanges in 17th-century Amsterdam, the development of banking and insurance, and, in the 20th century, the mathematical formalisation of the field by scholars such as Harry Markowitz (portfolio theory), William Sharpe, and Fischer Black and Myron Scholes (option pricing). Periodic crises — 1929, the 2007–08 global financial crisis — have repeatedly reshaped regulation of the industry.

Risk and regulation

Because financial institutions are highly interconnected and leveraged, finance is heavily regulated to protect depositors and investors and to contain systemic risk. Central banks, securities regulators, prudential standards such as Basel III capital requirements, and deposit-insurance schemes are central parts of this framework.
