Rumen

The rumen, also known as the paunch, is the largest compartment of the stomach in ruminant animals such as cattle, sheep, goats, and deer. It is the primary site of microbial fermentation of ingested feed, allowing ruminants to digest fibrous plant material that monogastric animals cannot.

Anatomy and function

The rumen occupies much of the left side of the abdominal cavity and, in an adult cow, can hold well over 100 litres. Its interior is lined with small projections called papillae that increase surface area for absorbing volatile fatty acids produced by fermentation. Together with the reticulum, with which it shares a continuous cavity, the rumen mixes and churns digesta through rhythmic contractions.

Microbiology

The rumen hosts an enormous population of bacteria, protozoa, fungi, and archaea. These microbes break down cellulose and hemicellulose into volatile fatty acids — chiefly acetate, propionate, and butyrate — which supply most of the animal's energy. Microbial cells themselves are later digested in the abomasum and small intestine, providing protein. Fermentation also produces gases, mainly carbon dioxide and methane, which the animal expels by eructation (belching).
