The Apennine Mountains (Italian: Appennini) are a mountain range consisting of parallel smaller ranges extending some 1,200 km along the length of peninsular Italy, from the Maritime Alps near the French border in the north-west to the toe of Italy in Calabria, with related ranges continuing in northern Sicily. They form the backbone of the Italian peninsula, running generally close to the Adriatic coast in the north and centre and more centrally further south.

Geography

The range is conventionally divided into the Northern, Central, and Southern Apennines. The highest point is Corno Grande (2,912 m), the summit of the Gran Sasso d'Italia massif in Abruzzo, which also carries the Calderone glacier, one of the southernmost in Europe. Other notable peaks include Monte Vettore in the Sibillini range, Monte Amaro in the Maiella, and Monte Cimone, the highest in the north. Major rivers of Italy — the Tiber, Arno, and Volturno among them — rise in the Apennines.

Geology and nature

The Apennines were built by the collision of the African and Eurasian plates and the subduction of the intervening oceanic and continental crust; the range is still seismically active, and destructive earthquakes (L'Aquila 2009; central Italy 2016) are a recurring feature. Extensive beech and fir forests cover much of the range, protected in national parks such as Abruzzo, Lazio and Molise, Gran Sasso, Majella, and Pollino, which shelter Marsican brown bears, Apennine wolves, and chamois.
