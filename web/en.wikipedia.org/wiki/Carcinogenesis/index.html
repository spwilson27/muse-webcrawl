Carcinogenesis

Carcinogenesis, also called oncogenesis or tumorigenesis, is the formation of a cancer, whereby normal cells are transformed into cancer cells. The process is characterized by changes at the cellular, genetic, and epigenetic levels and abnormal cell division. Cell division is a physiological process that occurs in almost all tissues and under a variety of circumstances; normally the balance between proliferation and programmed cell death is maintained, but mutations in DNA disrupt this order.

Mechanism

Cancer is fundamentally a disease of regulation of tissue growth. In order for a normal cell to transform into a cancer cell, the genes that regulate cell growth and differentiation must be altered. The affected genes are divided into two broad categories: oncogenes, which promote cell growth and reproduction, and tumor suppressor genes, which inhibit cell division and survival. Malignant transformation can occur through the formation of novel oncogenes, the inappropriate over-expression of normal oncogenes, or by the under-expression or disabling of tumor suppressor genes.

Multistage model

Experimental carcinogenesis is classically described in three stages. Initiation results from irreversible genetic damage caused by a carcinogen, such as a chemical mutagen, ionizing radiation or an oncogenic virus. Promotion is the clonal expansion of initiated cells by agents that are not themselves mutagenic, such as chronic inflammation or certain hormones. Progression involves further genetic changes, acquisition of invasiveness and the capacity to metastasize.

Causes

Agents capable of causing cancer are called carcinogens. They include tobacco smoke constituents such as benzo[a]pyrene, asbestos fibres, aflatoxins, ultraviolet and ionizing radiation, and viruses such as human papillomavirus, hepatitis B and C viruses, and Epstein–Barr virus. Genetic susceptibility and errors of DNA replication also contribute; most cancers arise from an accumulation of mutations over many years, which explains why cancer incidence rises steeply with age.

Hallmarks

Douglas Hanahan and Robert Weinberg summarized the acquired capabilities of cancers as the hallmarks of cancer: self-sufficiency in growth signals, insensitivity to anti-growth signals, evasion of apoptosis, limitless replicative potential, sustained angiogenesis, and tissue invasion and metastasis, later extended to include reprogrammed energy metabolism and evasion of immune destruction.
