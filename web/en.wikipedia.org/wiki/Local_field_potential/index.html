The local field potential (LFP) is an electrophysiological signal recorded with an electrode placed in neural tissue. It consists of the low-frequency component (typically below ~300 Hz) of the extracellular voltage, and reflects the summed and synchronised electrical activity — mainly synaptic currents, but also action potentials, afterpotentials and glial currents — of the population of neurons surrounding the electrode tip. Because it aggregates the input activity of a local neuronal population, the LFP is widely used as a measure of network activity complementary to single-unit spike recordings.

Biophysics

Extracellular potentials arise from transmembrane currents: a region of net current inflow (a sink, e.g., at excitatory synapses) and outflow (a source) constitutes a current dipole, and the potentials of many aligned dipoles — such as those formed by the parallel dendrites of cortical pyramidal neurons — sum constructively, producing a measurable field. Tissue acts as a low-pass spatial and temporal filter, so the LFP emphasises slow, synchronised synaptic events over fast, asynchronous spiking. Its spatial reach is debated but generally estimated at a few hundred micrometres, depending on the coherence of the underlying activity.

Interpretation

Analysis of the LFP commonly focuses on frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma) and phenomena such as hippocampal theta rhythm, sharp-wave ripples, cortical slow oscillations and event-related potentials. Current source density (CSD) analysis of multi-contact probe recordings is used to localise sinks and sources within cortical layers. Challenges in interpretation include volume conduction from distant sources, reference-electrode contamination, and the mixing of inputs from multiple afferent pathways.

Applications

  • Studying sensory processing, sleep, memory consolidation and oscillations in animal models
  • Brain–machine interfaces and neuroprosthetic decoding
  • Human recordings from deep-brain stimulation electrodes (e.g., subthalamic nucleus LFPs in Parkinson's disease) guiding adaptive stimulation
  • Related clinical signals such as the electrocorticogram (ECoG) and EEG, which are larger-scale analogues
