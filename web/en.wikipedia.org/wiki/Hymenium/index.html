Hymenium

The hymenium is the tissue layer on the hymenophore of a fungal fruiting body where the cells develop into basidia or asci, which produce spores. In some species all of the cells of the hymenium develop into basidia or asci, while in others some cells develop into sterile cells called cystidia (basidiomycetes) or paraphyses (ascomycetes). Cystidia are often important for species identification.

The hymenium lines the spore-bearing surfaces of fungal fruiting bodies.

Position

The position of the hymenium is traditionally the first characteristic used in the classification and identification of mushrooms. In gilled mushrooms (agarics), the hymenium covers the surface of the gills; in boletes it lines the tubes; in tooth fungi it covers the downward-pointing spines; and in puffballs it lines internal cavities. In ascomycetes such as morels and truffles, the hymenium lines the pits or the outer surface of the fruiting body, where the asci discharge their ascospores.

Structure

Beneath the hymenium lies the subhymenium, a layer of supporting hyphae, and below that the trama, the flesh of the fruiting body. The basidia of basidiomycetes typically bear four spores on slender projections called sterigmata, while the asci of ascomycetes typically contain eight ascospores formed by meiosis followed by a mitotic division.
