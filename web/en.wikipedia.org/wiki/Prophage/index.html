Prophage

A prophage is a bacteriophage (phage) genome that has been inserted into a bacterial host's chromosome (or exists as an extrachromosomal plasmid within the cell) and is replicated passively along with the host's DNA without destroying the host. The bacterium carrying a prophage is called a lysogen, and the state is known as lysogeny. On receiving certain signals — classically, damage to the host's DNA — the prophage can be induced: it excises itself, replicates, and directs the production of new virus particles that lyse the cell, entering the lytic cycle.

Mechanism

Temperate phages (such as the classic phage lambda of Escherichia coli) choose between lytic growth and lysogeny upon infection. In lysogeny, the phage's integrase enzyme mediates site-specific recombination between the phage attachment site (attP) and the bacterial attachment site (attB), inserting the viral genome into the chromosome. A phage-encoded repressor protein (the cI repressor in lambda) then shuts down the phage's lytic genes and confers immunity against superinfection by the same phage. When the host's SOS response is triggered — for example by ultraviolet light or certain antibiotics — the bacterial RecA protein stimulates cleavage of the repressor, the prophage excises (sometimes imprecisely, carrying adjacent host genes with it — specialised transduction), and lytic replication begins.

Effects on the host

Prophages are not silent passengers: many carry genes that change the host's phenotype, a phenomenon called lysogenic conversion. Notorious examples include:

  • The diphtheria toxin gene, carried by a prophage in Corynebacterium diphtheriae.
  • The cholera toxin genes, encoded by the CTXφ prophage in Vibrio cholerae.
  • Shiga toxins of enterohaemorrhagic E. coli, encoded on lambdoid prophages — which is why DNA-damaging antibiotics can worsen such infections by inducing the prophage.
  • Botulinum toxins in some strains of Clostridium botulinum.

Whole-genome sequencing shows that prophage remnants and intact prophages make up a substantial fraction of many bacterial genomes (up to about 20% in some strains), making them major agents of horizontal gene transfer and bacterial evolution.
