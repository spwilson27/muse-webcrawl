Drug legalization
A pro-legalisation rally

Drug legalization is the process of removing legal prohibitions against the production, sale, possession and use of currently prohibited drugs, typically replacing them with a system of legal regulation — licensing, taxation, age limits, quality control and advertising restrictions — of the kind applied to alcohol and tobacco. It is the maximal form of drug liberalization, and is distinct from decriminalization, which removes criminal penalties for personal possession while keeping supply illegal.

Arguments in favour

Supporters argue that prohibition creates a lucrative illegal market controlled by organised crime, generating violence — from gang conflict in consumer countries to the drug wars of producer and transit states such as Mexico and Colombia. They contend that legal regulation would assure product purity (reducing deaths from adulterants such as fentanyl), raise tax revenue, free police and prison resources, remove the stigma and criminal records that fall disproportionately on minorities and the poor, and allow health services to reach users more effectively. Civil-liberties arguments hold that competent adults should be free to decide what to consume.

Arguments against

Opponents argue that legal availability increases consumption — especially among adolescents — with consequent addiction, mental-health harms (notably psychosis associated with high-potency cannabis), impaired driving and workplace costs. They note that alcohol and tobacco, though legal and regulated, still cause enormous public-health burdens, and that commercialisation creates industries with incentives to expand use and lobby against controls. International treaties (notably the 1961 Single Convention) also constrain national legalisation.

Experience in practice

Cannabis is the principal drug for which full legalisation has been implemented: Uruguay (2013), Canada (2018), numerous US states since 2012, and — in more restricted forms — Malta, Luxembourg and Germany. Portugal's 2001 decriminalisation of all drugs for personal use, combined with expanded treatment, is widely cited as an evidence case for reform short of legalisation. Oregon's Measure 110 (2020) decriminalised small amounts of all drugs but was substantially rolled back in 2024 amid concerns about public drug use and overdose deaths. Heroin-assisted treatment and supervised consumption sites, as in Switzerland and the Netherlands, represent regulated medical models for otherwise illegal drugs.
