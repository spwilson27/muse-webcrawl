The Hakka (客家; Hakka: Hak-kâ) are a Han Chinese subgroup whose ancestral homes lie principally in the hill country of northeastern Guangdong, western Fujian and southern Jiangxi. The name Hakka means "guest families", reflecting their history of migration from northern China in successive waves beginning in late antiquity.

History

Driven south by war and disorder over many centuries, Hakka settlers often arrived after the best land was taken, settling in upland areas and developing a reputation for industry, clannishness and distinctive fortified architecture — including the great earthen tulou of Fujian and the walled villages of Guangdong. Competition with earlier-settled Cantonese neighbours erupted in the Punti–Hakka clan wars of the 19th century. Hakka emigrants spread across Southeast Asia, Taiwan, and beyond.

Culture

Hakka culture is noted for its language, cuisine (such as salt-baked chicken, stuffed tofu and preserved mustard greens), folk songs, and the historic prominence of Hakka women, who traditionally did not bind their feet. Prominent Hakka figures include Hong Xiuquan, Sun Yat-sen, Deng Xiaoping and Lee Kuan Yew.
