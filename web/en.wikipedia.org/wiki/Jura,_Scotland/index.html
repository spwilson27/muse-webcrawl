Jura (Scottish Gaelic: Diùra) is an island in the Inner Hebrides of Scotland, adjacent to and northeast of Islay. With an area of 36,692 hectares (142 sq mi) and a population of only about 230, Jura is far more sparsely populated than Islay, and is one of the least densely populated islands of Scotland. The main settlement is the village of Craighouse on the east coast.

Geography

Jura is dominated by three steep-sided conical quartzite hills, the Paps of Jura, which rise to 785 metres at Beinn an Òir and are visible from much of the Hebrides and the Northern Ireland coast. The island is largely covered by blanket bog and heath, and is famous for its large population of red deer — several thousand deer outnumber the human population many times over. Between Jura's northern tip and the island of Scarba lies the Gulf of Corryvreckan, home to one of the world's largest whirlpools.

History and culture

The island's name is generally thought to derive from the Old Norse for "deer island". Jura was historically held by the MacDonald Lords of the Isles and later passed to the Campbells. In the 20th century the island gained literary fame as the place where George Orwell wrote most of his novel Nineteen Eighty-Four while living at the remote farmhouse of Barnhill in 1946–1948. The KLF also drew attention to Jura in 1994, when they burned one million pounds in an abandoned boathouse on the island as an artistic act.

Economy

Jura's economy centres on the Isle of Jura whisky distillery in Craighouse, founded in 1810 and rebuilt in the 1960s, along with deer stalking, fishing, fish farming and tourism. The island is reached by a small vehicle ferry from Islay across the Sound of Islay, with Islay itself connected by ferry and air to the mainland.
