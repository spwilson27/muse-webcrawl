Lophophore

The lophophore (from Greek lophos, crest or tuft, and -phore, bearing) is a characteristic feeding organ found in three groups of animals: the brachiopods, the bryozoans (moss animals) and the phoronid worms. It consists of a ring or horseshoe-shaped crown of hollow, ciliated tentacles surrounding the mouth, used for suspension feeding and gas exchange.

Structure and function

The tentacles are hollow extensions of the coelom, covered with cilia arranged in bands. Beating of the lateral cilia drives a current of water across the tentacles; food particles are captured and passed along frontal cilia and food grooves to the mouth. The lophophore can usually be retracted into the body or protective case when the animal is threatened, and in bryozoans it is everted by muscular pressure on the body wall.

Taxonomic significance

The three lophophore-bearing groups were long united as the "Lophophorata", and while their exact relationships were debated, molecular evidence now places brachiopods, phoronids and bryozoans together within the larger clade Lophotrochozoa, alongside molluscs and annelids. In brachiopods the lophophore is supported by a brachidium and occupies much of the mantle cavity between the shells.
