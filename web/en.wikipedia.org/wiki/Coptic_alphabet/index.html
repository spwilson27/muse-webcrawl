The Coptic alphabet is the script used for writing the Coptic language, the latest stage of the ancient Egyptian language. The repertoire of glyphs is based on the Greek alphabet, augmented by letters derived from the Egyptian Demotic script for sounds not present in Greek. Most Coptic letters therefore descend from Greek uncials, while the additional letters — usually six or seven depending on dialect, such as ϣ (shai), ϥ (fai), ϧ (khai), ϩ (hori), ϫ (janja), ϭ (chima) and ϯ (ti) — come from Demotic.

History

"Old Coptic" texts from the first centuries AD experimented with writing Egyptian in Greek letters plus a few Demotic signs. The standard Coptic alphabet crystallized with the large-scale translation of the Bible into Coptic in the 3rd and 4th centuries AD. It was the first alphabetic writing system used for the Egyptian language, replacing hieroglyphic, hieratic and Demotic scripts. After the Arab conquest of Egypt, Coptic gradually yielded to Arabic as a spoken language, but it survived as the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church, a role it retains today.

Dialects and letters

The main literary dialects — Sahidic, Bohairic, Akhmimic, Fayyumic and others — use slightly different letter inventories and pronunciations; Bohairic, the liturgical dialect of the modern church, uses the full alphabet. Coptic also employs supralinear strokes and the jin kim dot conventions to mark syllabic consonants and glottal features in modern printed texts.

Unicode

Coptic was initially encoded within the Greek block in Unicode; since Unicode 4.1 (2005) it has its own block (U+2C80–U+2CFF), with additional letters in Greek Extended, enabling proper digital typesetting of liturgical and scholarly texts.
