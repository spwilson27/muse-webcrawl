José de la Cruz Porfirio Díaz Mori (15 September 1830 – 2 July 1915) was a Mexican general and politician who served seven terms as President of Mexico, in total for 31 years, from 1876 to 1880 and continuously from 1884 to 1911. His long rule, known as the Porfiriato, brought Mexico political stability, economic modernisation, and extensive foreign investment, but at the cost of authoritarian government, repression, and deepening inequality. His declaration in 1908 that Mexico was ready for democracy, followed by his decision to run for yet another term, precipitated the Mexican Revolution that overthrew him in 1911.

Early life and military career

Díaz was born in Oaxaca City, of mixed Spanish and Mixtec heritage, and studied law at the Instituto de Ciencias y Artes under the influence of Benito Juárez. Abandoning the seminary and then legal studies for the army, he rose as a Liberal general during the Reform War (1858–1861) and the French intervention (1862–1867). He fought at the Battle of Puebla on 5 May 1862 and achieved distinction at Miahuatlán and La Carbonera, and in 1867 he captured Puebla and then Mexico City, helping restore the republic of Juárez.

Rise to power

After losing presidential elections to Juárez and then Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada, Díaz rebelled under the Plan de Tuxtepec (1876), proclaiming "no re-election" (Sufragio efectivo, no reelección). Victorious at Tecoac, he took the presidency, stepping aside formally for Manuel González in 1880–1884 — a term widely seen as his proxy — and then returning to the presidency in 1884, which he held, through repeated re-elections, until 1911.

The Porfiriato

Díaz's regime rested on the slogan "order and progress" (paz, orden y progreso), influenced by positivist advisers known as the científicos. The government built some 19,000 km of railways, telegraph lines, ports, and mines, largely with British and American capital; exports and state revenues grew substantially, and Mexico City was modernised with drainage, electric lighting, and grand avenues. Political order was enforced by the rural constabulary (the rurales), rigged elections, a controlled press, and the co-optation or elimination of opponents — the policy summed up as pan o palo ("bread or the club"). Land laws transferred vast communal and "vacant" lands to survey companies and haciendas, dispossessing many villages; by 1910 a small elite controlled most of the country's land, and debt peonage was widespread, notably on Yucatán's henequen plantations. Strikes at Cananea (1906) and Río Blanco (1907) were suppressed with bloodshed.

Fall and exile

In a 1908 interview with the American journalist James Creelman, Díaz declared himself a democrat and Mexico ready for an opposition party. Francisco I. Madero took him at his word, campaigned for the presidency, and was jailed; after another Díaz re-election, Madero issued the Plan de San Luis Potosí calling for revolt from 20 November 1910. Revolutionary forces took Ciudad Juárez in May 1911; by the Treaty of Ciudad Juárez, Díaz resigned on 25 May 1911 and sailed for Europe aboard the Ypiranga, reportedly remarking that Madero had "unleashed a tiger". He died in exile in Paris on 2 July 1915 and is buried in Montparnasse Cemetery; his remains have never been repatriated, though there have been periodic proposals.

Legacy

Post-revolutionary Mexico officially condemned Díaz as a dictator, yet his state papers and era laid the infrastructural foundations of the modern country. Historians remain divided between emphasising the Porfiriato's growth and stability and its authoritarianism and social costs; the revolutionary slogan "effective suffrage, no re-election" was later enshrined in the Constitution of 1917.
