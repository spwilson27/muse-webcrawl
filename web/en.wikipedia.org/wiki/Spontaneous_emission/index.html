Spontaneous emission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of spontaneous emission: an excited atom emitting a photon in a random direction without external stimulus]
An excited atom emits a photon spontaneously.

Spontaneous emission is the process in which a quantum mechanical system (such as an atom) transitions from an excited energy state to a lower energy state and emits a quantised carrier of energy, such as a photon. It is the everyday glow of excited matter: fluorescence, phosphorescence (delayed by metastable states), the light of nebulae, and the background hiss inside every laser.

Classical physics could not explain it — an orbiting electron should radiate continuously and spiral in, and nothing 'triggers' a given emission. Einstein parametrised it with his A coefficient (1917) without a mechanism; the mechanism came with quantum electrodynamics (Dirac, 1927): the electron couples to the vacuum fluctuations of the electromagnetic field (Zero-point energy), which stimulate the transition even with no photons present — 'spontaneous' emission is thus emission stimulated by the vacuum. The rate is set by the transition's dipole matrix element and the density of photon states (Fermi's golden rule), and can be engineered: cavities suppress or enhance it (the Purcell effect), a control central to quantum optics and to making single-photon sources.

Timescales

Allowed electric-dipole transitions decay in nanoseconds; forbidden transitions (weak matrix elements) live far longer — the green 557.7 nm line of atomic oxygen in the aurora takes about a second, and hydrogen's 21-cm spin-flip transition has a mean life of about ten million years, yet is astronomy's most useful line because the universe contains so much hydrogen.
