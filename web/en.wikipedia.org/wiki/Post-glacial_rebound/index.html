Post-glacial rebound

Post-glacial rebound (also called isostatic rebound or glacial isostatic adjustment, GIA) is the rise of land masses after the removal of the huge weight of ice sheets during the last glacial period. The Earth's crust, depressed under the load of ice several kilometres thick, slowly rebounds as the viscous mantle flows back beneath it.

Effects

Rebound is fastest just after deglaciation and continues for tens of thousands of years. Present-day uplift reaches about 1 cm per year around Hudson Bay in Canada and in the Gulf of Bothnia in Fennoscandia, where raised beaches and harbours that have become landlocked document centuries of uplift; Stockholm's old port facilities and Finnish lake outlets have been notably affected. Areas beyond the former ice margins, which were bulged upward (the peripheral forebulge), are now subsiding, contributing to relative sea-level rise on the U.S. Atlantic coast.

Study

Measurements of rebound from raised shorelines, tide gauges, GPS, and satellite gravimetry (GRACE) constrain the viscosity structure of the Earth's mantle and are an important correction in studies of modern sea-level change and ice-sheet mass balance.
