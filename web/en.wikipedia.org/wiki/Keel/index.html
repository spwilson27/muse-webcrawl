Keel

A fin keel with a ballast bulb on a modern sailing yacht.

The keel is the longitudinal structural element at the lowest point of a ship's or boat's hull, running along the centreline from bow to stern. Often called the backbone of the vessel, the frames and hull plating are attached to it. In sailing vessels the term also denotes a hydrodynamic foil beneath the hull that provides ballast and resists lateral drift (leeway).

Structural keel

Historically fashioned from a single massive timber, the structural keel supports the hull's shape and strength. "Laying the keel" is the ceremonial start of a ship's construction. In steel ships the keel is a plate or bar integrated with the bottom structure.

Sailing keels

  • Full (long) keel — runs most of the hull length; stable and protective of the rudder and propeller but less manoeuvrable.
  • Fin keel — a shorter, deeper fin, often with a ballast bulb; common on modern racing yachts.
  • Bilge (twin) keels — two shallower keels letting a boat sit upright when dried out.
  • Canting keel — can be swung to windward to increase righting moment on high-performance racers.
  • Lifting keel / centreboard — retractable foils for shallow water.

Ballast in the keel lowers the centre of gravity, giving the vessel stability against the heeling force of the wind.
