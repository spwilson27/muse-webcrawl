Morihei Ueshiba (植芝 盛平, December 14, 1883 – April 26, 1969) was a Japanese martial artist and the founder of the martial art of aikido. He is often referred to by aikido practitioners as Ōsensei ("Great Teacher"). The son of a landowner from Tanabe, Ueshiba studied a number of martial arts as a young man and served in the Japanese Army during the Russo-Japanese War. Discharged in 1907, he moved to Hokkaidō in 1912 as the head of a pioneer settlement; there he encountered and studied with Sokaku Takeda, the founder of Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu.

Ueshiba left Hokkaidō in 1919 to join the Ōmoto-kyō movement, a Shinto sect, in Ayabe, where he served as a martial arts instructor and opened his first dojo. He accompanied the head of the Ōmoto-kyō group, Onisaburo Deguchi, on an expedition to Mongolia in 1924, where they were captured by Chinese troops and returned to Japan. The following year, Ueshiba had a profound spiritual experience, stating that "a golden spirit sprang up from the ground, veiled my body, and changed my body into a golden one." After this experience, his martial arts technique became gentler and more spiritual in nature.

Biography

Early years

Morihei Ueshiba was born in Tanabe, in what is now Wakayama Prefecture, on December 14, 1883, the fourth child and only son of Yoroku Ueshiba, a wealthy farmer and member of the municipal council. As a child, Ueshiba was rather weak and sickly, which led his father to encourage him to take up sumo and swimming. His interest in martial arts began after he witnessed his father being attacked by followers of a rival politician. After leaving school, Ueshiba worked briefly for a tax office before becoming a merchant. In 1901 he moved to Tokyo, where he opened a small stationery business and studied Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū jujutsu under Tokusaburō Tozawa. He later joined the military and trained in bayonet techniques.

Hokkaidō

In 1912, Ueshiba led a group of settlers from Tanabe to the remote Shirataki area of Hokkaidō. Life there was harsh; in 1915 he met the Daitō-ryū master Sokaku Takeda at the Hisada Inn in Engaru and began intensive study. Ueshiba trained with Takeda for several years and received a teaching license (kyōju dairi) in 1922. That same year, having learned of his father's death and returned to Ayabe, he began teaching what he then called "aiki-bujutsu."

Development of aikido

Throughout the 1920s and 1930s, Ueshiba taught in Tokyo, where his dojo (the Kobukan) attracted many students, and demonstrated his art before military and police audiences. He settled in Iwama, Ibaraki Prefecture, during the Second World War, and after the war aikido began to spread internationally. His son Kisshomaru Ueshiba became the second Doshu, and after Morihei's death in 1969 the leadership passed through the Ueshiba family.

Philosophy

Ueshiba's mature art was shaped by his Ōmoto-kyō spirituality. He taught that aikido was not a method of fighting but a way to reconcile the world and unify humankind: "Aikido is the principle of eternal growth... the spirit of loving protection of all life." He held that the true goal of budo was love and harmony rather than victory over an opponent.
