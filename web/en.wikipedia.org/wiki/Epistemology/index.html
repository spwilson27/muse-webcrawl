Epistemology is the branch of Philosophy concerned with knowledge: its nature, sources, structure and limits. Epistemologists ask what knowledge is, how it differs from mere true belief, how beliefs are justified, and whether sceptical challenges — the possibility that we are deceived about everything — can be answered. The classical analysis defined knowledge as justified true belief, a definition famously challenged by Edmund Gettier in 1963.

Sources of knowledge

Perception, memory, introspection, testimony and reasoning are the standard candidate sources. Rationalists such as Descartes and Leibniz emphasised a priori reasoning independent of experience; empiricists such as John Locke, George Berkeley and David Hume held that knowledge derives ultimately from sense experience. Immanuel Kant sought a synthesis, arguing that the mind actively structures experience through innate categories.

Scepticism and the Gettier problem

Descartes' method of doubt imagined a deceiving demon to test which beliefs survive radical scepticism. Hume argued that induction — inference from past to future — cannot be rationally justified. In 1963 Gettier published counterexamples in which a belief is true and justified yet intuitively not knowledge, prompting decades of work on a fourth condition or alternative accounts such as reliabilism.
