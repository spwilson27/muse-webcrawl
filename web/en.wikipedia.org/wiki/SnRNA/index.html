Small nuclear RNA (snRNA) is a class of small RNA molecules found within the splicing speckles and Cajal bodies of the cell nucleus in eukaryotic cells. The length of an average snRNA is approximately 150 nucleotides. They are transcribed by either RNA polymerase II or RNA polymerase III. Their primary function is in the processing of pre-messenger RNA (hnRNA) in the nucleus, above all in splicing — the removal of introns from primary transcripts.

SnRNPs and the spliceosome

snRNAs always function packaged with specific proteins as small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs, pronounced "snurps"). Each snRNP contains one snRNA (U1, U2, U4, U5 or U6), a ring of seven Sm proteins (or Lsm proteins for U6), and particle-specific proteins. Combinations of snRNPs assemble stepwise on each intron to form the spliceosome, a dynamic molecular machine whose RNA components catalyse the two transesterification reactions of splicing. U1 recognises the 5′ splice site, U2 the branch point, U5 aligns the exons, and the U2–U6 fold forms the catalytic core — leading to the view that the spliceosome is fundamentally a ribozyme.

A second, minor spliceosome processes a rare class of introns (formerly "AT-AC" introns) using U11, U12, U4atac and U6atac snRNAs alongside shared U5.

Other roles and disease

Related small RNAs include snoRNAs (small nucleolar RNAs), many derived from introns, which guide chemical modification of rRNAs and, in the case of U3 and U7, participate in rRNA processing and histone mRNA 3′-end formation respectively. Mutations in snRNA genes cause human disease: variants in RNU4-2 (encoding U4) were identified in 2024 as a frequent cause of neurodevelopmental disorders (ReNU syndrome), and mutations in minor-spliceosome snRNAs cause conditions such as microcephalic osteodysplastic primordial dwarfism type I. Autoantibodies against snRNP proteins (anti-Sm, anti-U1 RNP) are diagnostic markers in lupus and mixed connective tissue disease.
