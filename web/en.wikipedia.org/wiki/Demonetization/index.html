Demonetization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For the Indian policy of 2016, see 2016 Indian banknote demonetisation.

Demonetization is the act of stripping a currency unit of its status as legal tender. It occurs whenever there is a change in national currency: the current form of money is pulled from circulation and retired, often to be replaced with new notes or coins. The opposite of demonetization is remonetization, in which a form of payment is restored as legal tender.

Reasons

Governments demonetize currency units for various reasons: to fight inflation or counterfeiting, to combat tax evasion and the black economy, to undermine criminal or terrorist financing, to facilitate a currency union or reform (such as the changeover to the euro), or to withdraw worn-out or obsolete denominations. A demonetization may be a routine replacement of banknote series, announced well in advance, or a sudden measure kept secret until announcement to prevent holders of illicit cash from laundering it.

Notable examples

  • India (2016): On 8 November 2016, the Government of India announced the demonetisation of all ₹500 and ₹1,000 banknotes — about 86% of currency in circulation by value — with the stated aims of curbing black money, counterfeit currency, and corruption, and promoting digital payments. Long queues formed at banks, and the cash-dependent informal economy suffered significant disruption; most of the withdrawn value was ultimately deposited back into banks.
  • United States (1870s): The term is closely associated with the demonetization of silver under the Coinage Act of 1873, which ended the bimetallic standard and provoked the "Free Silver" movement and William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" campaign.
  • Euro changeover (2002): Legacy currencies such as the Deutsche Mark and French franc were demonetized as euro notes and coins replaced them.
  • Zimbabwe and North Korea carried out abrupt currency reforms that wiped out savings and caused severe hardship.

Economic effects

Sudden demonetization can cause a liquidity crunch, disrupt trade, and impose heavy costs on the poor and on informal sectors, while its benefits depend on whether unaccounted wealth is held in cash and whether the measure is accompanied by wider reforms. Gradual, pre-announced demonetizations, by contrast, are routine central-bank operations with minimal disruption.
