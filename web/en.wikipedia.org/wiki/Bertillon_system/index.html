Bertillon system

A Bertillon identification card, showing front and profile photographs.

The Bertillon system was a system of physical identification of criminals developed by the French police officer Alphonse Bertillon in the late nineteenth century. Based on anthropometry — the measurement of parts of the human body that were believed to remain unchanged in adults — it was the first scientific system used by police to identify repeat offenders, before being superseded by fingerprinting.

Method

Bertillon's method, introduced at the Paris Prefecture of Police in the early 1880s, recorded a standard set of measurements for each person arrested: height, arm span, sitting height, length and breadth of the head, length of the right ear, length of the left foot, and the lengths of the middle and little fingers and the forearm, among others. These were entered on a card together with a pair of standardized photographs — the origin of the modern "mug shot" — and a verbal description procedure known as the portrait parlé.

Decline

The system was widely adopted in Europe and the United States, but it was laborious and prone to measurement error. Its authority collapsed after the 1903 case of Will West and William West at Leavenworth prison — two unrelated men with nearly identical Bertillon measurements who could be distinguished by their fingerprints. By the 1910s and 1920s, dactyloscopy (fingerprint identification) had displaced anthropometry in most police forces, though Bertillon's standardized photography survived.
