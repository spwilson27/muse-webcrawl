Songtsen Gampo (Tibetan: སྲོང་བཙན་སྒམ་པོ, Wylie: Srong btsan sgam po; c. 605 – 650) was the founder of the Tibetan Empire and, by tradition, is credited with the introduction of Buddhism to Tibet. The 33rd king of the Yarlung dynasty, he unified the Tibetan Plateau and founded the empire that would dominate Central Asia for two centuries.

Reign

Songtsen Gampo succeeded his father Namri Songtsen around 618. Through a series of campaigns he subdued the neighbouring kingdoms of Zhangzhung and Supi and unified the plateau under the Yarlung dynasty. He moved the capital to Lhasa, which he developed as the political and religious centre of the empire.

Under Songtsen Gampo, Tibet became a major military power. His armies campaigned against Nepal, the kingdoms of the Western Regions, and Tang China. Following hostilities in the 630s, relations with the Tang court were formalised through marriage alliances.

Marriages and diplomacy

Two of Songtsen Gampo's marriages are especially celebrated in Tibetan tradition. The Nepalese princess Bhrikuti brought the statue of Akshobhya Buddha to Tibet, while the Chinese princess Wencheng, whom he married in 641, brought the revered Jowo statue of the Buddha, which is still enshrined in the Jokhang Temple in Lhasa. These marriages symbolised Tibet's diplomatic ties to both Nepal and China and are credited with bringing Buddhism, along with arts, crafts, and technology, to Tibet.

Administration and culture

Songtsen Gampo is credited with sending his minister Thonmi Sambhota to India to devise a script for the Tibetan language, modelled on Indian scripts. He promulgated a legal code, organised the empire's administration into districts, and promoted Buddhism through the construction of temples, including the Jokhang and Ramoche temples in Lhasa. Later tradition also attributes to him the building of the 108 temples said to pin down a demoness lying across the land.

Legacy

In Tibetan Buddhist tradition Songtsen Gampo is regarded as an emanation of the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara, and together with Trisong Detsen and Ralpacan he is counted among the three Dharma Kings of Tibet. His reign marks the beginning of Tibet's imperial era and of Buddhism's long history on the plateau.
