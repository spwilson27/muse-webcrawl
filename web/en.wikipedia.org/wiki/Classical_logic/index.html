Classical logic

Classical logic (or standard logic or Frege–Russell logic) is the intensively studied and most widely used class of deductive logic. Classical logic consists of propositional logic and first-order logic, whose semantics rest on the principle of bivalence: every statement is either true or false, with no third possibility.

Truth tables define the classical logical connectives under bivalent semantics.

Properties

Classical logic is characterized by principles that non-classical logics reject or restrict, including:

  • Law of excluded middle: for every proposition P, P or not-P.
  • Non-contradiction: not both P and not-P.
  • Explosion (ex contradictione quodlibet): from a contradiction, anything follows.
  • Monotonicity of entailment: adding premises cannot invalidate an argument.
  • Idempotency and the involution of negation (double negation elimination).

History and alternatives

The system was developed from Aristotle's syllogistic through the work of Boole, Frege, Russell, and Whitehead into modern mathematical logic. In the 20th century, alternatives arose: intuitionistic logic rejects excluded middle; paraconsistent logics reject explosion; relevance logic, fuzzy logic, and many-valued logics restrict other classical principles. "Classical" thus functions largely as the baseline against which these non-classical logics are defined.
