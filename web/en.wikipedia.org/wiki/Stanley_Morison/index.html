Stanley Morison

Born: 6 May 1889, Wanstead, Essex, England
Died: 11 October 1967 (aged 78), London, England
Occupation: Typographer, printing historian
Known for: Times New Roman typeface; typographic adviser to The Times and the Monotype Corporation

Stanley Arthur Morison (6 May 1889 – 11 October 1967) was an English typographer, printing executive and historian of printing. As typographic adviser to the Monotype Corporation and to The Times newspaper, he shaped much of twentieth-century British printing practice, and he is best remembered as the designer, with Victor Lardent, of the Times New Roman typeface (1931), one of the most widely used typefaces in the world.

Early life

Morison was self-educated after leaving school at fourteen. A conscientious objector during the First World War, he was imprisoned for his beliefs. After the war he worked in publishing and design, including at the Pelican Press and the Cloister Press, and in 1922 co-founded the Fleuron Society to promote fine printing; its journal The Fleuron, which he edited, became influential in raising British typographic standards.

Monotype and The Times

From 1923 Morison served as typographical adviser to the British branch of the Monotype Corporation, directing a programme of revivals of historic typefaces — including Garamond, Bembo, Baskerville and Poliphilus — that transformed book printing. In 1929 he became adviser to The Times. After famously criticising the paper's printing in a memorandum, he was commissioned to redesign it; the result was Times New Roman, first used on 3 October 1932. The face was designed for legibility and economy of space in newspaper columns, and was later released for general use, becoming the default typeface of innumerable documents and computer systems.

Writing and scholarship

Morison was a prolific author on the history of writing and printing. His works include Four Centuries of Fine Printing (1922), The English Newspaper (1932), First Principles of Typography (1936) — an influential statement of his doctrine that typography should serve the reader with clarity and consistency — and the posthumously completed Politics and Script (1972). His study of John Bell and the origins of the modern newspaper influenced histories of the press. He served as editor of the History of The Times and was a member of the committee that oversaw the design of the Festival of Britain lettering in 1951.

Legacy

Morison's insistence on typographic discipline, historical scholarship and fitness for purpose made him the dominant figure in British typography between the wars. Times New Roman remains a global standard, and his writings continue to be cited by type designers and printing historians.
