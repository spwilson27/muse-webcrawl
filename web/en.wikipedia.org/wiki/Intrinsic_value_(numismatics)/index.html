Intrinsic value (numismatics)

Intrinsic value (numismatics)
Also calledMelt value; bullion value
DefinitionMarket value of the metal contained in a coin
Contrasts withFace value; numismatic (collector) value

In numismatics, the intrinsic value of a coin is the market value of the metal it contains, also called its melt value or bullion value. It contrasts with the coin's face value (its denomination as money) and with its numismatic value (what collectors will pay for its rarity, condition and historical interest). A coin trades near its intrinsic value when it is a common-date bullion coin; a rare coin in high grade may be worth many times its metal content, while a base-metal circulation coin's face value usually exceeds its small melt value.

Calculation

Intrinsic value is computed from the coin's weight, its fineness (purity) and the current spot price of the metal: for example, a pre-1965 US dime contains 0.0723 troy ounces of pure silver (2.25 g of 90% silver), so its melt value tracks the silver price directly. Dealers typically buy common silver coins ("junk silver") at a small discount to melt and sell at a premium.

Historical consequences

When a coin's intrinsic value rises above its face value, coins disappear from circulation — the behaviour captured by Gresham's law ("bad money drives out good"). This repeatedly forced debasement or recoinage in the ancient and medieval worlds, drove gold coins out of circulation in bimetallic systems when mint ratios diverged from market ratios, and ended US silver coinage in 1964–65 when silver prices rose. Modern echoes include the melting or hoarding of copper pennies where legal, and prohibitions some mints impose on melting current coinage. Commodity-money systems rest on intrinsic value, whereas fiat money has negligible intrinsic value and circulates by legal and social convention.
