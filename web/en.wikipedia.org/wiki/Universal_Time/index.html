Universal Time (UT or UT1) is a time standard based on Earth's rotation. While originally based on mean solar time at the prime meridian at Greenwich, it is now measured by the rotation angle of the Earth relative to distant quasars and other celestial reference objects. It is approximately the same as Greenwich Mean Time (GMT) in everyday use, though GMT is no longer precisely defined by the astronomical observations that define UT.

Universal Time is reckoned from the Greenwich meridian

Versions

Several versions of Universal Time exist. UT0 is the raw time obtained from observing the diurnal motion of stars or radio sources at a particular observatory, uncorrected for the wobble of Earth's poles. Correcting for polar motion gives UT1, the principal form, which is directly proportional to the true rotation angle of the Earth and is needed by astronomers and navigators. UT1 varies irregularly because Earth's rotation speeds up and slows down under the influence of tides, winds, and core motions. UT2 applied a further smoothing for known seasonal variations and is now rarely used.

Relation to UTC

Since 1972 civil time in most countries has been based on Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), an atomic time scale kept within 0.9 seconds of UT1 by the insertion of leap seconds. The difference between UT1 and UTC, called DUT1, is monitored by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service and broadcast in time signals so that users needing true Earth-rotation time — for celestial navigation, satellite tracking, and pointing telescopes — can recover it.

History

The term "Universal Time" was adopted at the International Meridian Conference of 1884, which chose Greenwich as the prime meridian, and was formally defined by the International Astronomical Union in 1928, replacing the ambiguous use of GMT with a day beginning at midnight. The introduction of atomic time in the 1950s exposed the irregularity of Earth-based time and led to the modern system of UTC.
