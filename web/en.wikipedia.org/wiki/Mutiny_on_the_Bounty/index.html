Mutiny on the Bounty

The mutiny on the Bounty occurred in the South Pacific Ocean on 28 April 1789. Disaffected crewmen, led by acting-Lieutenant Fletcher Christian, seized control of the ship HMS Bounty from her captain, Lieutenant William Bligh, and set him and eighteen loyalists adrift in the ship's open launch. The mutineers variously settled on Tahiti or on Pitcairn Island. Bligh survived his voyage and reached England in April 1790, upon which an Admiralty court-martial acquitted him of responsibility for the loss of the ship.

Background

Bounty had been commissioned to sail to Tahiti and transport breadfruit plants to the British colonies in the West Indies, where they were intended as a cheap food source for enslaved workers. The ship sailed from Spithead on 23 December 1787 with a crew of 46 men under Bligh's command. The voyage was arduous; after ten months at sea, including a month-long failed attempt to round Cape Horn, the ship finally arrived in Tahiti in October 1788.

The crew spent five months in Tahiti collecting and potting breadfruit plants. During this time many of the men formed relationships with local women, and discipline aboard the ship relaxed considerably.

The mutiny

Bounty sailed from Tahiti on 5 April 1789. In the early hours of 28 April, about 1,300 miles west of Tahiti near the island of Tofua, Fletcher Christian and several of his followers seized Bligh in his cabin and brought him on deck. Despite strong words on both sides, Bligh and his loyalists were forced into the ship's 23-foot open launch and set adrift.

Bligh's voyage

Remarkably, Bligh navigated the launch some 3,600 nautical miles to Timor, arriving on 14 June 1789 — a feat of navigation widely regarded as extraordinary, accomplished without charts or a compass. No lives were lost during the voyage, although one man was killed by hostile islanders at Tofua.

Aftermath

After returning to Tahiti briefly, Christian and eight other mutineers, together with Tahitian men and women, sailed in search of a safe haven and settled on the uninhabited Pitcairn Island in January 1790, where they burned the Bounty. The community on Pitcairn was marked by violence and alcoholism; by 1808, when the American ship Topaz rediscovered the settlement, John Adams was the sole surviving mutineer. Their descendants still live on Pitcairn today.

Mutineers who remained on Tahiti were captured by HMS Pandora in 1791; four died when the ship wrecked on the Great Barrier Reef, and of the ten prisoners who reached England, three were hanged.

In popular culture

The mutiny has been the subject of numerous books and films, including the 1932 novel Mutiny on the Bounty by Charles Nordhoff and James Norman Hall, and film adaptations in 1935, 1962, and 1984 (The Bounty).
