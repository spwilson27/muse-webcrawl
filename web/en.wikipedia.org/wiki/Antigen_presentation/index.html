Antigen presentation

Antigen presentation is a vital immune process essential for T cell activation. It involves the display of peptide fragments of proteins, bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, on the surface of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), where they can be inspected by T cell receptors.


[Hero image placeholder: diagram of MHC class I and class II processing pathways]

MHC class I pathway

Intracellular (endogenous) proteins are degraded by the proteasome; peptides are transported into the endoplasmic reticulum by TAP transporters, loaded onto MHC class I molecules, and displayed at the surface to CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. Viruses and tumours that downregulate this pathway may be detected instead by natural killer cells.

MHC class II pathway

Extracellular proteins taken up by professional APCs are degraded in endolysosomal compartments; peptides replace the invariant chain's CLIP segment (with HLA-DM catalysis) on MHC class II molecules and are presented to CD4+ helper T cells. Cross-presentation allows dendritic cells to route exogenous antigens onto MHC class I as well.

Significance

Antigen presentation underlies vaccination, transplant rejection (via direct and indirect allorecognition) and many autoimmune diseases, and its evasion is a hallmark of some cancers.
