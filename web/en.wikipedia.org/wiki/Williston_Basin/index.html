The Williston Basin is a large intracratonic sedimentary basin in eastern Montana, western North and South Dakota, southern Saskatchewan and southwestern Manitoba in Canada. It is known for its rich deposits of petroleum, potash and lignite coal. Named for the city of Williston, North Dakota, the basin is roughly circular, measuring about 770 km (480 mi) across, with its depocentre near the North Dakota–Saskatchewan border region.

Geology

The basin began subsiding in the Ordovician period atop Precambrian basement and accumulated up to about 4,900 m (16,000 ft) of Paleozoic, Mesozoic and Cenozoic sedimentary rocks, including limestones, dolomites, shales, sandstones and thick evaporites. Its structural centre lies in northwestern North Dakota, and the basin is bounded by uplifts and arches such as the Cedar Creek Anticline and the Transcontinental Arch.

Petroleum

Oil was first discovered in the basin in 1951 near Tioga, North Dakota. Major production came from Ordovician Red River, Silurian Interlake and Devonian–Mississippian formations. From the 2000s, horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing in the Devonian–Mississippian Bakken Formation (and the underlying Three Forks) triggered a major oil boom in North Dakota, making the state the second-largest oil producer in the United States by the early 2010s. The Bakken is estimated to hold billions of barrels of technically recoverable oil.

Other resources

The basin hosts some of the world's largest potash deposits in the Devonian Prairie Evaporite Formation of Saskatchewan, extensive lignite coal in the Paleocene Fort Union Formation, and significant uranium and salt resources.
