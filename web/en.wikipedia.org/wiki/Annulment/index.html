Annulment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Annulment is a legal procedure within secular and religious legal systems for declaring a marriage null and void. Unlike divorce, it is usually retroactive, meaning that an annulled marriage is treated as if it never existed, or in some legal systems as if it were invalid from the beginning.

Grounds

Grounds for annulment vary by jurisdiction but commonly include fraud or misrepresentation, concealment (for example of an existing marriage, a sexually transmitted disease, or impotence), lack of free consent, underage marriage, incest or bigamy, and non-consummation. Because annulment treats the marriage as never having been valid, children of annulled marriages are protected in many jurisdictions by statutes preserving their legitimacy.

Religious annulment

In the Roman Catholic Church, a decree of nullity is granted by an ecclesiastical tribunal when a marriage is found to have lacked, from the outset, an essential element required for a valid sacramental marriage — for example, a grave defect of consent or an undisclosed impediment. A declaration of nullity does not deny that a relationship existed, only that it did not constitute a valid marriage in the Church's understanding. Some other faiths have analogous procedures.

Annulment versus divorce

Divorce terminates a valid marriage; annulment declares that a valid marriage never came into being. Historically, annulment was sought when divorce was unavailable or socially unacceptable, most famously by Henry VIII, whose request for an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon precipitated the English Reformation.
