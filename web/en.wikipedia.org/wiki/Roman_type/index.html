Roman type


Class: Serif, upright letterforms
Contrasts with: Italic type, blackletter
Prototype: Nicolas Jenson's roman, Venice, c. 1470

In Latin-script typography, roman type refers to the upright letterforms that form the default style of a typeface, in contrast to italic type (slanted, based on cursive handwriting) and blackletter. Roman type itself descends from the combination of the inscribed capitals of ancient Rome and the Carolingian minuscule handwriting revived by Italian Renaissance humanists, who mistakenly believed these letterforms to be authentically Roman.

History

The first roman types were cut in the 1460s and 1470s in Italy. Conrad Sweynheym and Arnold Pannartz used an early semi-roman at Subiaco and Rome; Nicolas Jenson in Venice around 1470 produced the roman type long regarded as the definitive model, and Aldus Manutius's punchcutter Francesco Griffo refined the form in the 1490s — the same workshop that introduced italic type in 1501 as a distinct, space-saving style. From Italy the roman spread through France (Garamond, Granjon) and the Netherlands; in northern Europe it coexisted with blackletter for centuries, roman generally being used for Latin and blackletter for vernacular texts, before roman became the universal default.

Usage

Today "roman" denotes the regular upright member of a type family — neither bold nor italic — and is the standard body-text style in Western typography. In some countries the term for roman type in general is Antiqua (as in German usage opposing Antiqua to Fraktur). Serif roman faces such as Times New Roman, Garamond, and Caslon take their names from this tradition.
