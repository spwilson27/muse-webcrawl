Metabolic alkalosis is a metabolic condition in which the pH of tissue is elevated beyond the normal range (7.35–7.45). This is the result of decreased hydrogen ion concentration, leading to increased bicarbonate (HCO3−), or alternatively a direct result of increased bicarbonate concentrations. The condition typically cannot last long if the kidneys are functioning properly.

Signs and symptoms

Mild cases of metabolic alkalosis often cause no symptoms. Moderate or severe alkalosis may cause confusion, dizziness, headache, muscle twitching and cramps, tingling in the extremities (paresthesia), nausea and vomiting. Because alkalosis shifts potassium into cells and lowers ionized calcium, affected patients can develop tetany-like symptoms. Breathing may become shallow as the body attempts to compensate by retaining carbon dioxide.

Causes

Metabolic alkalosis is usually divided into chloride-responsive and chloride-resistant causes. Chloride-responsive causes include loss of hydrogen ions from the gastrointestinal tract — most classically through protracted vomiting or nasogastric suction of gastric contents, which are rich in hydrochloric acid — and the use of loop or thiazide diuretics, which increase renal excretion of hydrogen and potassium. Chloride-resistant causes include primary hyperaldosteronism, Cushing syndrome, severe potassium depletion, and exogenous alkali loads such as excessive antacid use or the milk-alkali syndrome.

Pathophysiology

For metabolic alkalosis to persist, the kidneys must fail to excrete the excess bicarbonate. This maintenance phase is driven by volume depletion, chloride depletion, hypokalemia, and elevated aldosterone, all of which increase renal bicarbonate reabsorption and hydrogen ion secretion. Compensatory hypoventilation raises arterial carbon dioxide, partially offsetting the rise in pH; expected PaCO2 rises by roughly 0.7 mmHg for each 1 mEq/L rise in bicarbonate.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on arterial blood gas analysis showing an elevated pH with an elevated bicarbonate concentration, supported by serum electrolyte measurement. Urinary chloride concentration helps distinguish saline-responsive alkalosis (urine chloride below about 25 mEq/L) from saline-resistant forms. The underlying cause is sought through history, medication review, blood pressure measurement, and, where indicated, renin and aldosterone levels.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. Saline-responsive alkalosis is corrected with intravenous sodium chloride and potassium repletion; vomiting is controlled and offending diuretics stopped. In chloride-resistant cases, mineralocorticoid antagonists, potassium-sparing agents, or surgery for an aldosterone-producing adenoma may be required. In rare severe cases, acetazolamide or carefully administered dilute hydrochloric acid has been used.
