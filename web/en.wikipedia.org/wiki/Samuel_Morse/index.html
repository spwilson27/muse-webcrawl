Samuel Finley Breese Morse (April 27, 1791 – April 2, 1872) was an American inventor and painter. After a career as one of the leading portrait and history painters of his generation, he developed a single-wire electric telegraph system and, with Alfred Vail, the signalling code that bears his name, transforming long-distance communication worldwide.

Art career

The son of the geographer and minister Jedidiah Morse, he graduated from Yale in 1810 and studied art in London under Washington Allston, exhibiting at the Royal Academy. Returning to the United States, he won major commissions — including the vast Gallery of the Louvre (1831–33) and portraits of John Adams and the Marquis de Lafayette — and helped found the National Academy of Design in New York, serving as its president. Bitterly disappointed at being passed over for the Capitol Rotunda murals in 1837, he gradually abandoned painting.

The telegraph

On the voyage home from Europe in 1832 aboard the packet Sully, conversations about electromagnetism set Morse on the idea of an electric recording telegraph. Working with Leonard Gale and Alfred Vail, he demonstrated the system in 1837–38 and, after years of lobbying, obtained a $30,000 congressional appropriation in 1843 to build an experimental line between Washington and Baltimore. On May 24, 1844, the line opened with the biblical message "WHAT HATH GOD WROUGHT," tapped out in the dot-and-dash code developed principally with Vail, whose numeric-letter system evolved into American Morse code; the refined alphabetic code later standardised internationally differed in detail and is associated with Friedrich Gerke's revision.

Morse's patents were contested in lengthy litigation — notably against the rival systems of Charles Wheatstone's partners and Royal Earl House — but the U.S. Supreme Court in O'Reilly v. Morse (1854) upheld his core claims while rejecting his sweeping claim to all electromagnetic telegraphy. He spent his later years wealthy and honoured internationally, though his legacy is shadowed by his vocal pro-slavery and nativist politics. His estate, Locust Grove in Poughkeepsie, New York, is a National Historic Landmark.
