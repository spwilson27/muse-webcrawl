The Shōbōgenzō (正法眼蔵, "Treasury of the True Dharma Eye") is the masterwork of the Japanese Zen master Eihei Dōgen (1200–1253), founder of the Sōtō school of Zen in Japan. Composed between 1231 and 1253, it is a collection of essays (fascicles) on Buddhist practice and philosophy, regarded as one of the greatest works of Japanese religious literature.

Contents

The work survives in several recensions, most importantly the 75-fascicle and 12-fascicle versions, together with additional drafts. Famous fascicles include Genjōkōan ("Actualizing the Fundamental Point"), Bendōwa ("On the Endeavor of the Way"), Uji ("Being-Time"), Busshō ("Buddha Nature"), Sansuigyo ("The Mountains and Waters Sutra"), and Ikka-myōju ("One Bright Pearl"). A separate work of the same title, the Mana Shōbōgenzō, is a collection of 300 kōans compiled by Dōgen in Chinese.

Thought

Writing in vernacular Japanese rather than classical Chinese — unusual for scholastic Buddhist texts of the period — Dōgen reinterprets doctrine through striking, poetic language. Central themes include the unity of practice and enlightenment (shushō-ittō): zazen is not a means to enlightenment but its very expression; the identity of impermanence and Buddha-nature; and the notion of "being-time," in which all beings are time itself. His famous line in Genjōkōan — "To study the Buddha Way is to study the self; to study the self is to forget the self" — epitomises the work.

Influence

Long kept within the Sōtō school and little read outside it, the Shōbōgenzō was rediscovered in the 20th century and is now studied worldwide both as philosophy and as literature. Major English translations include those by Kazuaki Tanahashi and the Sōtō Zen Text Project (Bielefeldt, Griffith Foulk, and others).
