Brumby

A brumby is a free-roaming feral horse in Australia. Descended from horses that escaped or were released from early European settlements, brumbies are found in many parts of the country, with the largest populations in the Northern Territory and Queensland, and the best-known herds in the Snowy Mountains of New South Wales and Victoria, where they feature in Australian folklore.

Origin of the name

The word first appeared in print in 1880 in Melbourne. Explanations include derivation from James Brumby, a soldier and farrier who arrived in New South Wales in 1791 and whose horses reportedly ran wild; or from an Aboriginal word such as Pitjara baroomby, meaning wild.

History

Horses first arrived in Australia with the First Fleet in 1788. Escaped stock, abandoned animals and deliberate releases — including losses during the Boer War and First World War remount trade — built large feral populations across the continent. Banjo Paterson's poem "Brumby's Run" and Elyne Mitchell's Silver Brumby children's novels made the brumby an icon of the high country.

Management controversy

With population estimates ranging from 400,000 to over a million, brumbies damage fragile alpine wetlands, compete with livestock and erode waterways, leading to aerial culling programmes that have repeatedly provoked public controversy — notably the 2000 Guy Fawkes River cull and debates over Kosciuszko National Park, where heritage-protection legislation for brumbies was enacted in New South Wales in 2018 and later population-reduction targets adopted.
