Nulla poena sine lege

Nulla poena sine lege (Latin for "no penalty without law") is a legal principle stating that a person cannot be punished for an act unless that act was prohibited by law in force at the time it was committed, and unless the penalty itself is prescribed by law. Together with its companion maxim nullum crimen sine lege ("no crime without law"), it expresses the principle of legality in criminal law.

Components

The principle is often analysed into four sub-principles: nulla poena sine lege praevia (no retroactive punishment), sine lege scripta (the law must be written), sine lege certa (the law must be sufficiently certain and foreseeable), and sine lege stricta (criminal law must be interpreted strictly, without analogy to the accused's detriment).

In modern law

The principle is enshrined in Article 7 of the European Convention on Human Rights, Article 15 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and the constitutions of many states; it also appears in Article 11 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Its retroactivity limb was famously debated at the Nuremberg trials, where the defence argued that crimes against peace and humanity had not been codified when committed; the tribunal held that the maxim is a principle of justice rather than a limitation of sovereignty, and that the acts were criminal under existing treaties and customary law.

History

The formula is associated with the German jurist Paul Johann Anselm Ritter von Feuerbach, who popularized the Latin maxims in his 1801 textbook of criminal law, though antecedents appear in Enlightenment thought and earlier legal traditions.
