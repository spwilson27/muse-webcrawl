Water supply network

ComponentsSource, treatment works, pumping stations, reservoirs and water towers, transmission and distribution mains, service connections
PurposeDeliver potable water at adequate pressure and quantity to consumers
RelatedWater purification; sewerage

A water supply network or water supply system is a system of engineered hydrologic and hydraulic components that provide water supply. A water supply system typically includes a drainage basin and raw water source, a water purification facility, storage facilities such as reservoirs and water towers, pumping stations, and a network of transmission mains and distribution pipes delivering water to homes, businesses, industry and fire hydrants.

Components

Raw water collection and treatment

Water is collected from surface reservoirs, rivers, or wells and aqueducts and conveyed to treatment works, where it is purified to drinking-water standards. Treated ("finished") water typically passes first through clear-water storage at the treatment plant.

Storage and pumping

Service reservoirs and elevated tanks store treated water to balance hourly fluctuations in demand, maintain pressure, and provide reserves for firefighting and emergencies. Pumping stations lift water into elevated storage or higher pressure zones; in flatter, pumped systems, pressure is maintained directly by pumps with variable-speed drives.

Distribution

The distribution network consists of trunk mains feeding progressively smaller pipes arranged as grids or loops (to provide redundancy) or as branching "dead-end" layouts in rural areas. Typical materials include ductile iron, PVC, HDPE and steel. Service pipes connect premises to the street main; meters record consumption for billing. Networks lose significant volumes through leakage — "non-revenue water" — which utilities combat with pressure management, district metering and active leak detection.

Water quality and pressure in the network

Maintaining a disinfectant residual (usually chlorine or chloramine) throughout the network suppresses bacterial regrowth, but long residence times can produce disinfection by-products and taste complaints. Networks are normally designed to maintain pressures sufficient for household use and firefighting while avoiding the excess pressure that aggravates leakage and burst frequency.

History

Pressurised piped supply developed in the nineteenth century: London, Paris and American cities built steam-pumped systems with cast-iron mains, and the combination of filtration, chlorination and universal piped distribution essentially eliminated cholera and typhoid in industrialised cities. Roman aqueducts and qanat systems were notable pre-industrial conveyance networks, though they generally delivered water to public fountains rather than individual pressurised taps.
