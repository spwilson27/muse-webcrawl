Andean music is an umbrella term for the traditional and folk music of the Andean highlands of South America, principally Peru, Bolivia and Ecuador, built from indigenous Quechua and Aymara musical heritage blended after the Spanish conquest with European instruments and harmony.

Instruments

The signature sounds are wind instruments: the quena, an end-notched flute traditionally of wood or bone; the zampoña (panpipes, also called siku), played in interlocking pairs by large ensembles; and recorders such as the tarka and pinkillo. The charango — a small ten-string lute originally made from armadillo shell — and the Spanish guitar provide string accompaniment, with the bombo legüero drum and violin, harp and mandolin in many regional styles.

Genres and history

Central genres include the huayno, the most widespread Andean dance-song, the cueca, the festive carnavalito and Afro-Bolivian saya. The zarzuela melody "El Cóndor Pasa" (1913) by Daniel Alomía Robles became internationally famous through Simon & Garfunkel's 1970 version. In the 1960s–70s Andean folk groups, often in exile from the Chilean and Argentine dictatorships, popularised the music worldwide as part of the nueva canción movement (Inti-Illimani, Quilapayún, Los Kjarkas, Illapu), while groups such as Los Incas had already introduced the characteristic panpipe-and-charango sound to European and North American audiences.
