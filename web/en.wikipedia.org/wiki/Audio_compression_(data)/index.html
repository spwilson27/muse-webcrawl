Audio compression (data)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Data compression of audio — not to be confused with dynamic range compression — is a form of data compression designed to reduce the size of digital audio files and the bandwidth needed to stream them. Audio compression algorithms are implemented as codecs and fall into two families: lossless codecs, which permit exact reconstruction of the original data, and lossy codecs, which discard information the ear is unlikely to miss in order to achieve much smaller sizes.

Lossless compression

Lossless audio codecs such as FLAC, Apple Lossless (ALAC), and Monkey's Audio exploit the statistical redundancy and predictability of audio waveforms, typically reducing file size by 30–60 percent with bit-perfect reconstruction. They are favored for archiving and by audiophiles, and are increasingly used for high-resolution streaming.

Lossy compression

Lossy codecs — including MP3, AAC, Vorbis, and Opus — use psychoacoustic models to remove sounds masked by louder or nearby-frequency sounds, quantization noise shaping, and joint stereo coding. CD audio at 1,411 kbit/s can be reduced to 128–320 kbit/s with generally good perceived quality. Lossy compression made portable digital music players, downloads, and streaming practical, but repeated transcoding between lossy formats compounds quality loss ("generation loss").

Techniques

Modern perceptual codecs transform audio into the frequency domain (e.g., with the modified discrete cosine transform), analyze it against models of human hearing, and allocate bits where they matter most perceptually. Speech codecs such as AMR, SILK, and the speech mode of Opus use models of vocal production (linear predictive coding) to achieve intelligible speech at very low bit rates.
