New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court holding that the First Amendment requires a public official suing for defamation over statements about their official conduct to prove 'actual malice' — that the statement was made with knowledge of its falsity or reckless disregard for the truth.

Background

In 1960 The New York Times published a full-page fundraising advertisement, 'Heed Their Rising Voices', describing protests by civil rights demonstrators in Montgomery, Alabama, and soliciting funds for Martin Luther King Jr.'s defence. The ad contained minor factual errors. L. B. Sullivan, a Montgomery city commissioner, sued for libel and won $500,000 in an Alabama court despite not being named in the ad.

Decision

The Supreme Court unanimously reversed. Justice Brennan's opinion held that debate on public issues should be 'uninhibited, robust, and wide-open', and that erroneous statements are inevitable in free debate; a rule compelling critics of officials to guarantee the truth of every factual assertion would chill speech.

Legacy

The actual malice standard was later extended to public figures generally. The decision is regarded as a cornerstone of American press freedom, enabling vigorous reporting on government and public affairs. In recent years some justices have called for the case to be reconsidered.
