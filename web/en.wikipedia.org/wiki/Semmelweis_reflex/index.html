Semmelweis reflex

The Semmelweis reflex (or Semmelweis effect) is the tendency to reject new evidence or knowledge because it contradicts established norms, beliefs, or paradigms — a reflex-like rejection of ideas that conflict with a community's existing commitments, especially within science and medicine.

Origin of the term

The term commemorates Ignaz Semmelweis, a Hungarian physician working in Vienna in the 1840s. Semmelweis observed that maternal deaths from puerperal fever were far higher in a maternity clinic staffed by doctors and medical students than in one staffed by midwives. He concluded that the physicians were carrying "cadaverous particles" from autopsies to their patients, and in 1847 he instituted hand disinfection with chlorinated lime, which cut mortality dramatically.

Despite the results, his ideas were rejected or ridiculed by much of the medical establishment, partly because they implied that doctors themselves were causing deaths, and partly because they lacked a theoretical foundation before germ theory. Semmelweis was dismissed from his post, suffered a breakdown, and died in an asylum in 1865 — shortly before the work of Pasteur and Lister vindicated antiseptic practice.

Usage

The phrase is used more broadly for institutional resistance to innovation, where the rejection is driven by commitment to a paradigm rather than by an assessment of the evidence itself. It is related to concepts such as paradigm shift, confirmation bias, and the Planck principle.
