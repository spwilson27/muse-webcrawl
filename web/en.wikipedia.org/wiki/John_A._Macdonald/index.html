Sir John Alexander Macdonald (11 January 1815 – 6 June 1891) was the first prime minister of Canada, serving from 1867 to 1873 and from 1878 until his death in 1891. The dominant figure of Canadian Confederation, he had a political career that spanned almost half a century. Macdonald was born in Scotland; when he was a boy his family immigrated to Kingston in the Province of Upper Canada (today in eastern Ontario). As a lawyer, he was involved in several high-profile cases and quickly became prominent in Kingston, which elected him in 1844 to the legislature of the Province of Canada.

By 1857, he had become premier under the colony's unstable political system. In 1864, when no party could form a stable government, Macdonald agreed to a proposal from his political rival, George Brown, that the parties unite in a Great Coalition to seek federation and political reform. Macdonald was the leading figure in the subsequent discussions and conferences, which resulted in the British North America Act and the birth of Canada as a nation on 1 July 1867. Macdonald was the first prime minister of the new Dominion, and he served as prime minister for 19 years in total, a length of service surpassed only by William Lyon Mackenzie King.

Early life and legal career

Macdonald was born in Glasgow to Hugh Macdonald, an unsuccessful merchant, and Helen Shaw; the family emigrated in 1820 to Kingston, Upper Canada. Educated at local schools in Kingston, he was articled to a lawyer at 15, admitted to the bar in 1836, and prospered, building a large practice and business interests. He served in the militia during the Rebellions of 1837–38 and gained notice defending accused rebels and, in 1842, Nils von Schoultz, leader of an American filibustering raid. Personal tragedies marked his life: his infant son John Alexander died in 1848 and his first wife Isabella, chronically ill, in 1857; his drinking, notorious throughout his career, intensified in times of stress.

Rise in the Province of Canada

Elected to the Legislative Assembly for Kingston in 1844 as a Conservative, Macdonald held cabinet posts from 1847–48 and again from 1854, becoming co-premier of the Province of Canada (with Étienne-Paschal Taché, then George-Étienne Cartier) and the province's dominant English-speaking politician. He helped create the Liberal-Conservative Party, used patronage skilfully to hold together coalitions, and resisted both annexationist sentiment and "rep by pop" deadlock-breaking schemes until the constitutional impasse of 1863–64 made federation the path forward.

Confederation

Macdonald chaired the Charlottetown (September 1864) and Quebec (October 1864) Conferences and led the Canadian delegation to London (1866–67), where the Seventy-Two Resolutions were refined into the British North America Act 1867. Preferring a strong central government on the British model — and fearing the American Civil War's lesson about states' rights — he shaped a federal union with broad federal powers. Knighted for his role, he became the Dominion's first prime minister at Confederation on 1 July 1867.

First premiership (1867–1873)

Macdonald's first government expanded Canada westward: the purchase of Rupert's Land (1869) triggered the Red River Resistance under Louis Riel, settled by the Manitoba Act (1870); British Columbia joined in 1871 on the promise of a transcontinental railway, and Prince Edward Island in 1873. He created the North-West Mounted Police (1873) and negotiated the Treaty of Washington (1871) with Britain and the United States. In 1873 the Pacific Scandal — revelations that his party had taken campaign funds from railway magnate Hugh Allan in exchange for the Pacific railway charter — forced his government's resignation; Alexander Mackenzie's Liberals won the 1874 election.

Return to power and the National Policy

Macdonald returned to office in 1878 on the "National Policy": high protective tariffs to build Canadian manufacturing, rapid completion of the Canadian Pacific Railway, and promoted immigration to settle the West. The CPR — finished in 1885, driven through the Rockies partly with loans authorised amid the North-West Rebellion of 1885 — bound the country physically together; Macdonald's government controversially refused clemency to Louis Riel, who was hanged in November 1885, a decision that alienated many French Canadians. His Indian policy, including the residential school system expanded under his governments and the use of food withholding on the Plains during the 1880s, has drawn severe historical condemnation for its role in the dispossession and suffering of Indigenous peoples, a record that has driven modern re-assessment of his commemoration, including the removal of statues in several cities.

Later years, death, and legacy

Macdonald won further elections in 1882, 1887, and in March 1891 campaigned on the slogan "The Old Flag, The Old Policy, The Old Leader" against Liberal proposals for free trade (unrestricted reciprocity) with the United States. He suffered a severe stroke in May 1891 and died in office in Ottawa on 6 June 1891; he was buried at Cataraqui Cemetery in Kingston. Regarded for generations as the pre-eminent Father of Confederation and the architect of Canada's survival as a separate state, his reputation has been substantially debated since the early 21st century as attention centred on his government's Indigenous policies; he remains the second-longest-serving prime minister in Canadian history.
