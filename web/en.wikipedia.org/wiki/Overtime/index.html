Overtime

Overtime is the amount of time someone works beyond normal working hours. The term is also used for the compensation received for such work: many jurisdictions require overtime pay at a premium rate — commonly "time-and-a-half", i.e. 150% of the regular hourly wage — for hours worked beyond a statutory threshold.

Regulation

In the United States, the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 requires covered employers to pay non-exempt employees at least one and a half times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek; salaried "exempt" employees (executive, administrative, professional) are generally excluded, and the salary threshold for exemption has been periodically revised. In the European Union, the Working Time Directive caps the average working week at 48 hours including overtime, with opt-outs in some member states. Japan regulates overtime through labour agreements (the "36 agreement") and has introduced statutory caps, partly in response to karōshi (death from overwork).

Effects

Long working hours are associated with fatigue, errors, cardiovascular disease and other health effects; the WHO and ILO estimated in 2021 that working 55 or more hours per week is associated with a substantially higher risk of stroke and heart disease. Employers use overtime to handle demand fluctuations without hiring, but sustained overtime raises costs and attrition.

Alternatives

Alternatives include time off in lieu (TOIL), annualized hours, flexible working, and — in sports — the unrelated use of "overtime" for extra playing time beyond regulation in a tied game (see Overtime (sports)).
