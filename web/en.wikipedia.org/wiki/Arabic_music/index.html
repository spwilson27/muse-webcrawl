Arabic music

Arabic music (Arabic: الموسيقى العربية) is the music of the Arab world, encompassing traditions from the Arabian Peninsula, the Levant, Egypt, Iraq, and North Africa. Though regional styles vary greatly, they share a melodic system based on the maqam — melodic modes built from scales that use intervals foreign to Western music, notably quarter tones — and a rich theory of rhythmic cycles called iqa'at.

Instruments and ensembles

The core instruments of the classical ensemble (takht) are the oud (short-necked lute), qanun (plucked zither), nay (end-blown flute), violin, and percussion such as the riq, daf, and tabla. The voice is pre-eminent; the improvisational vocal and instrumental solo called taqsim is a hallmark of performance practice.

History

Music flourished at the Abbasid court of Baghdad, where theorists such as al-Kindi and al-Farabi wrote foundational treatises, and at Córdoba in al-Andalus. In the 20th century, Egypt — especially Cairo's recording and film industries — dominated popular Arabic music through stars such as Umm Kulthum, Mohamed Abdel Wahab, and Fairuz in Lebanon. Modern Arabic pop blends maqam melody with electronic production, while classical and folk traditions continue in schools and festivals.
