Empedocles

Empedocles

Born: c. 494 BC, Akragas, Sicily

Died: c. 434 BC (aged about 60)

Era: Pre-Socratic philosophy

School: Pluralist school

Main interests: Cosmology, ontology, epistemology

Notable ideas: Four classical elements (roots); Love and Strife as cosmic forces

Empedocles (Greek: Ἐμπεδοκλῆς; c. 494 – c. 434 BC) was a Greek pre-Socratic philosopher and a citizen of Akragas (modern Agrigento), a Greek city in Sicily. He is best known for originating the cosmogonic theory of the four classical elements — earth, water, air, and fire — which became the standard dogma for much of the next two thousand years.

Life

Little is known with certainty about Empedocles' life. He came from a wealthy and distinguished family and was active in the politics of Akragas, reportedly helping to overthrow the tyrant Thrasydaeus in 470 BC and refusing an offered kingship. Later tradition embellished his biography with legends: that he was a wonder-worker and healer, and the famous (almost certainly apocryphal) story that he threw himself into the crater of Mount Etna hoping his disappearance would make people believe he had become a god — a tale mocked by the poet Horace.

Philosophy

The four roots

Responding to the Eleatic argument of Parmenides that being is one and unchanging, Empedocles proposed that all things are composed of four eternal and unchanging "roots" (rhizomata): earth, air, fire, and water. Apparent change is merely the mixing and separation of these roots in different proportions — a famous analogy compares the process to a painter mixing pigments.

Love and Strife

The mixing and separation of the roots are driven by two opposing cosmic forces: Love (Philotēs), which draws the elements together into unity, and Strife (Neikos), which pulls them apart. The universe passes through an eternal cycle in which Love and Strife alternately dominate; when Love reigns completely, all is a single harmonious sphere.

Works

Empedocles wrote two poems in hexameter verse, On Nature and Purifications, of which substantial fragments survive — roughly 450 lines, about a quarter of the original. The Strasbourg Papyrus, identified in the 1990s, added further fragments of On Nature. Purifications presents religious teachings including reincarnation and a prohibition on eating meat and beans.

Zoogony and perception

Empedocles described a remarkable account of the origin of living things in which separate limbs and organs combined by chance, with only viable combinations surviving — an idea sometimes seen as a distant forerunner of natural selection. His theory of vision held that effluences from objects enter the pores of the eye, and he is credited with the discovery that air is a material substance, demonstrated with the klepsydra (water thief).
