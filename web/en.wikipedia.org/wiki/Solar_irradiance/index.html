Solar irradiance

Solar irradiance is the power per unit area received from the Sun in the form of electromagnetic radiation, measured in watts per square metre (W/m²). Irradiance at the top of Earth's atmosphere, averaged over the solar cycle, is called the solar constant, with a modern satellite-measured value of about 1,361 W/m². Solar irradiance is the dominant driver of Earth's climate and is essential data for solar energy engineering.

Total and spectral irradiance

Total solar irradiance (TSI) integrates radiation over all wavelengths. Continuous satellite measurements since 1978 (by instruments on missions such as SORCE and TSIS) show TSI varying by about 0.1% over the 11-year solar cycle, rising slightly with solar activity because bright faculae outweigh dark sunspots. Spectral solar irradiance describes the distribution across wavelengths; ultraviolet radiation varies proportionally far more than the total and affects stratospheric ozone and heating.

Irradiance at the surface

On average, about 340 W/m² arrives at the top of the atmosphere when averaged over the whole globe and day-night cycle; roughly 30% is reflected back to space, and about 160–170 W/m² of solar radiation is absorbed at the surface. At ground level, irradiance is divided into direct normal irradiance (DNI), received straight from the solar disc; diffuse horizontal irradiance (DHI), scattered by the atmosphere; and global horizontal irradiance (GHI), their sum on a horizontal surface. These quantities determine the output of photovoltaic systems (which use GHI) and concentrating solar power (which uses DNI).

Measurement

Ground-based irradiance is measured with pyrheliometers (direct), pyranometers (global and diffuse), and spectroradiometers. Networks such as the Baseline Surface Radiation Network provide reference data. Insolation — the accumulated solar energy over a period, in kWh/m² — is derived by integrating irradiance over time.

Climate relevance

Long-term reconstructions from sunspot records and cosmogenic isotopes suggest modest variations over past centuries, including during the Maunder Minimum of the 17th century. Scientific assessments conclude that changes in solar irradiance since the mid-20th century are far too small to explain observed global warming, which is attributed primarily to increased greenhouse gases.
