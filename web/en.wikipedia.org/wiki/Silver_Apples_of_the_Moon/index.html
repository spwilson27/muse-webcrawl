Silver Apples of the Moon is a 1967 electronic music album by American composer Morton Subotnick. It is widely regarded as the first electronic music album commissioned by a record company — Nonesuch Records ordered the work specifically for release on LP — and it became a landmark in the history of electronic music, remaining one of the best-known compositions for the Buchla modular synthesizer.

Cover of the original 1967 Nonesuch LP release.

Background

Subotnick composed the work in 1966–67 using the Buchla 100 series modular synthesizer, an instrument he had helped inspire through his collaboration with engineer Don Buchla in San Francisco. Unlike the Moog synthesizer, the Buchla instrument was designed without a conventional keyboard and emphasized voltage-controlled sequencing — an approach well suited to Subotnick's interest in what he called "electronic music as chamber music": intimate, studio-composed works for the home listener rather than the concert hall.

The title is taken from the closing lines of W. B. Yeats's poem "The Song of Wandering Aengus": "The silver apples of the moon, / The golden apples of the sun."

Music

The album is divided into two side-long parts. Part I is atmospheric and gestural, built from drifting timbres and melodic fragments; Part II introduces pulsing rhythmic ostinatos generated by the synthesizer's sequencers — among the earliest examples of sequenced rhythmic patterns in electronic music — which build into dense, dance-like grooves. Subotnick described his compositional method as sculpting "sound gestures" directly on tape.

Legacy

Silver Apples of the Moon was a commercial success by the standards of avant-garde music, selling tens of thousands of copies and bringing electronic music to a broad audience. It was selected for the Library of Congress's National Recording Registry in 2009 as a work of cultural and historical significance. The album influenced generations of electronic musicians and is frequently cited alongside works by Wendy Carlos in accounts of the synthesizer's entry into popular culture.
