Emil Artin

Emil Artin (German: [ˈaʁtiːn]; March 3, 1898 – December 20, 1962) was an Austrian mathematician of Armenian descent. He is regarded as one of the founders of modern abstract algebra, and made fundamental contributions to algebraic number theory, especially class field theory and the theory of L-functions.

Life

Artin was born in Vienna and grew up in Reichenberg (now Liberec) in Bohemia. He studied mathematics at the University of Vienna and the University of Leipzig, where he received his doctorate in 1921 under Gustav Herglotz. He then spent a year in Göttingen before joining the University of Hamburg in 1923, where he became a full professor in 1926.

In 1937, because his wife Natascha was of part-Jewish descent, Artin was forced out of his position under Nazi civil service laws and emigrated to the United States. He taught at the University of Notre Dame and Indiana University before moving to Princeton University in 1946. He returned to Hamburg in 1958 and died there in 1962.

Work

Artin's name is attached to many central concepts of modern algebra: Artinian rings and modules, the Artin reciprocity law in class field theory, Artin L-functions, the Artin–Schreier theory, the Artin–Hasse exponential, and Artin's conjecture on primitive roots. His proof of the reciprocity law in 1927 completed a central goal of class field theory. His books, including Galois Theory and the textbook Algebra written with later editions by Michael Artin, his son, shaped the teaching of algebra worldwide.

At Hamburg he supervised or influenced a generation of algebraists, and his lectures were famous for their clarity. His students included Hans Zassenhaus, Max Zorn, and Bartel van der Waerden, whose Moderne Algebra drew heavily on lectures by Artin and Emmy Noether.
