Gauss–Markov theorem

In statistics, the Gauss–Markov theorem states that in a linear regression model in which the errors have expectation zero, are uncorrelated and have equal variances (homoscedasticity), the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) of the coefficients: among all linear unbiased estimators, it has the lowest sampling variance. The result justifies the use of least squares estimation without assuming that the errors are normally distributed.

Assumptions

The classical conditions are: the model is linear in the parameters; observations are randomly sampled; the regressors are not perfectly collinear; the errors have conditional mean zero, E[u|X] = 0 (exogeneity); and the errors have constant conditional variance and are mutually uncorrelated. Under these assumptions OLS is BLUE. Normality of the errors is not required for the theorem, though it is used for exact finite-sample hypothesis tests.

History

The result traces to Carl Friedrich Gauss, who justified least squares in 1823 on the basis of minimum variance among unbiased estimators; the Russian mathematician Andrey Markov restated and extended the argument in 1900, and the combined name honours both. Alexander Aitken later generalised the theorem to correlated or heteroscedastic errors, showing that generalised least squares (GLS) is then the efficient estimator.

Violations and remedies

If errors are heteroscedastic, OLS remains unbiased but is no longer efficient and the usual standard errors are wrong; remedies include heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors or GLS. If errors are autocorrelated (common in time series), similar efficiency losses arise. Omitted-variable bias and measurement error violate exogeneity and destroy unbiasedness itself, which no variance improvement can repair.

Proof sketch

Any linear estimator can be written as a matrix times the response vector. Imposing unbiasedness constrains that matrix; the variance of the estimator then decomposes into the OLS variance plus a positive semi-definite term, showing no other linear unbiased estimator can have smaller variance.
