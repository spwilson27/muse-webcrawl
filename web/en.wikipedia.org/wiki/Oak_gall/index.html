Oak gall

Oak galls are abnormal outgrowths of plant tissue found on oak trees, induced by chemicals secreted by the larvae of certain gall wasps in the family Cynipidae. The gall provides both shelter and a food source for the developing insect, while the tree is usually little harmed.

Formation

A female gall wasp lays her eggs in a bud, leaf, or twig of an oak. The hatching larva releases plant-growth-regulating substances that redirect the tree's own cells into building a structured chamber around it. The gall grows with the larva inside, which feeds on nutritive tissue lining the central cavity. When mature, the adult wasp chews its way out, leaving a small round exit hole.

Many cynipid species alternate between a sexual generation and an all-female (agamic) generation, often producing quite different galls on different parts of the tree in successive seasons.

Common types

  • Oak apples — large, spongy, apple-like galls, up to about 5 cm across, caused by wasps such as Biorhiza pallida.
  • Marble galls — hard, spherical woody galls caused by Andricus kollari; once an important source of tannin.
  • Knopper galls — ridged, distorted acorns produced under the influence of Andricus quercuscalicis.
  • Artichoke galls — bud galls resembling small artichokes or hop cones.
  • Spangle galls — small disc-shaped galls on the undersides of leaves.

Historical uses

Oak galls are exceptionally rich in tannic acid. For centuries they were crushed and combined with iron salts to make iron gall ink, the standard writing ink of Europe from the early medieval period well into the nineteenth century, used for manuscripts, legal documents, and drawings. Aleppo galls from the Near East were especially prized for ink and for tanning and dyeing.
