Catch-22 (logic)

A catch-22 is a paradoxical situation from which an individual cannot escape because of contradictory rules or limitations. The term was coined by Joseph Heller in his 1961 novel Catch-22, and it has entered general usage to describe any no-win bureaucratic dilemma in which the attempt to solve a problem is blocked by the problem itself.

The original Catch-22

In Heller's novel, set among American airmen in Italy during the Second World War, the catch is a fictional military regulation. A pilot who is insane may be grounded and sent home — but he must ask to be grounded, and asking is itself proof of sanity, since only a sane man would want to stop flying dangerous missions. As the character Doc Daneeka explains, anyone who wants to get out of combat duty isn't really crazy, so all "crazy" pilots who ask are judged sane and must keep flying, while a pilot who does not ask can never be evaluated at all. The phrase "Catch-22" thus denotes a self-sealing, circular rule that yields the same outcome whatever one does.

General usage

Everyday examples commonly labelled catch-22s include needing experience to get a job but needing a job to gain experience, or being unable to open a bank account without an address while being unable to rent housing without a bank account. Logicians note that the structure resembles a paradox of self-reference, though in practice the term is used loosely for any dilemma where two requirements each depend on the other being met first.
