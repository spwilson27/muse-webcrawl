Alcohol dehydrogenases (ADH) (EC 1.1.1.1) are a group of dehydrogenase enzymes that occur in many organisms and facilitate the interconversion between alcohols and aldehydes or ketones with the reduction of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) to NADH. In humans and many other animals, they serve to detoxify alcohols that would otherwise be toxic; in yeast and many bacteria, some alcohol dehydrogenases catalyze the opposite reaction as part of fermentation.

Reaction and structure

The enzymes are typically dimeric proteins whose subunits contain two zinc ions, one catalytic and one structural. The reaction proceeds through an ordered mechanism in which NAD+ binds first, the alcohol is deprotonated at the active site, and hydride transfer to NAD+ yields the aldehyde or ketone product.

Human alcohol dehydrogenases

Humans possess seven ADH genes, clustered on chromosome 4, encoding enzymes grouped into five classes. The class I enzymes (ADH1A, ADH1B and ADH1C), abundant in the liver, carry out most ethanol oxidation, converting ethanol to acetaldehyde, which aldehyde dehydrogenase then converts to acetate. Genetic variants in ADH1B and ADH1C affect reaction rates and are associated with differences in alcohol tolerance and risk of alcoholism in populations; a fast-acting ADH1B variant common in East Asian populations, combined with inactive ALDH2, contributes to the alcohol flush reaction.

Role in metabolism and toxicology

ADH also metabolizes methanol and ethylene glycol — converting them to their toxic products formaldehyde/formic acid and glycolaldehyde/glycolic acid — which is why ethanol and fomepizole, which compete for or inhibit the enzyme, are used as antidotes in poisoning by those substances. In yeast, the ADH1 enzyme runs in reverse during fermentation, reducing acetaldehyde to ethanol to regenerate NAD+. Alcohol dehydrogenase in the fruit fly Drosophila is a classic model system in evolutionary and population genetics.
