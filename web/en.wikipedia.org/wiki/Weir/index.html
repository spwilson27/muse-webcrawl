Weir

A weir or low-head dam is a barrier across the width of a river that alters the flow characteristics of water and usually results in a change in the height of the river level. Weirs are used to control the flow of water for rivers, outlets of lakes, ponds, and reservoirs, and to measure discharge. They are smaller and serve different purposes than large dams.

Uses

Historically, weirs powered watermills by creating a mill pond and head of water. Modern uses include diverting water for irrigation, maintaining navigation depths, flow measurement at gauging stations, flood control in small channels, and creating recreational features. Types include broad-crested, sharp-crested (rectangular, triangular V-notch, and trapezoidal notches), compound, and labyrinth weirs.

Flow measurement

Because the discharge over a weir is a predictable function of the upstream water depth, weirs are standard flow-measurement devices in open channels and laboratories. For a rectangular sharp-crested weir, discharge is roughly proportional to the head raised to the 3/2 power; for a V-notch weir, to the 5/2 power.

Safety and ecology

Low-head dams are nicknamed "drowning machines" because the submerged hydraulic jump below them creates a recirculating current that can trap swimmers and boats. Weirs can also impede fish migration; fish passes or removal of obsolete weirs are used to mitigate ecological effects.
