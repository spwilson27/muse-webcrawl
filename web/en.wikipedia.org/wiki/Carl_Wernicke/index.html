Carl Wernicke (15 May 1848 – 15 June 1905) was a German physician, psychiatrist, and neuropathologist. He is best known for his influential research on the neuroanatomical basis of language, in particular the description of the brain region now called Wernicke's area and the form of language disorder known as Wernicke's aphasia (receptive aphasia).

Biography

Wernicke was born in Tarnowitz, Upper Silesia. He studied medicine at the University of Breslau, qualifying in 1870. After serving as an assistant in Breslau and in Berlin under Theodor Meynert in Vienna, he returned to Breslau, where he became professor of psychiatry and neurology in 1885. In 1904 he moved to Halle as director of the psychiatric clinic. He died in 1905 from injuries sustained in a bicycle accident in the Thuringian Forest.

Work

In 1874, at the age of 26, Wernicke published his landmark monograph Der aphasische Symptomencomplex ("The Symptom Complex of Aphasia"). Building on Paul Broca's earlier work localising speech production in the frontal lobe, Wernicke described a distinct syndrome — later called sensory aphasia — caused by lesions of the posterior part of the superior temporal gyrus of the dominant hemisphere. Patients with this lesion speak fluently but with impaired comprehension and produce meaningless or paraphasic speech. He also proposed a connectionist model of language in which Broca's and Wernicke's areas are linked by association fibres (the arcuate fasciculus), and predicted that interruption of this pathway would produce conduction aphasia.

Wernicke also described the condition now known as Wernicke's encephalopathy, an acute neurological disorder caused by thiamine deficiency, characterised by confusion, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia. Together with Sergei Korsakoff's description of the accompanying amnesic syndrome, the combined picture is called Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.

Legacy

Wernicke's model of language processing, extended by later researchers such as Ludwig Lichtheim and Norman Geschwind, remained foundational to aphasiology for over a century. The region of the posterior superior temporal lobe critical for language comprehension still bears his name.
