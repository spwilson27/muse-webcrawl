The Mesha Stele, also known as the Moabite Stone, is a stele dated around 840 BC containing a significant Canaanite inscription in the name of King Mesha of Moab (a kingdom located in modern Jordan). Mesha tells how Chemosh, the god of Moab, had been angry with his people and had allowed them to be subjugated to the Kingdom of Israel, but at length Chemosh returned and assisted Mesha to throw off the yoke of Israel and restore the lands of Moab.

The stele forms part of — and the longest extant inscription from — the corpus of the Iron Age Moabite language, and provides the earliest certain extra-biblical attestation of the name Yahweh. It is written in the Phoenician alphabet.

Discovery

The stone was discovered intact by Frederick Augustus Klein, an Anglican missionary, at the site of ancient Dibon (now Dhiban, Jordan), in August 1868. Local Bedouin later smashed the stele into pieces by heating it and pouring cold water over it, reportedly after disputes over its ownership and resale value. Most of the fragments were recovered, and a paper squeeze (papier-mâché impression) taken before the stone was broken by Charles Simon Clermont-Ganneau's agent allowed much of the text to be reconstructed. The reconstructed stele is now in the Louvre.

Contents

The inscription, of 34 lines, begins with Mesha introducing himself as king of Moab and dedicating a high place to Chemosh. It recounts the oppression of Moab by Omri, king of Israel, and his son, Mesha's victories over Israelite-held towns including Ataroth, Nebo, and Jahaz, the rebuilding of towns such as Qarho and Baal-Meon, the capture of vessels of Yahweh from Nebo, and Mesha's public works including highways, reservoirs, and fortifications.

Significance

The inscription parallels a narrative in the Hebrew Bible (2 Kings 3), in which Mesha rebels against Israel after the death of Ahab, though the biblical account emphasizes an Israelite-Judahite-Edomite campaign against Moab. Together with the Tel Dan stele, the Mesha Stele is among the most important artefacts for the study of the ninth-century BC Levant, the Moabite language, and the history of the alphabet.
