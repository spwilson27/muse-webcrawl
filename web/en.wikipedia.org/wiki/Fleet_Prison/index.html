Fleet Prison

The Fleet Prison was a notorious London prison by the side of the River Fleet. The prison was built in 1197, was rebuilt several times, and was in use until 1844. It was demolished in 1846.

The Fleet Prison, c. 18th century

History

The prison stood on the eastern bank of the Fleet River, in what is now Farringdon Street. It came into particular notoriety as a place of incarceration for debtors and for persons imprisoned for contempt of court by the Court of Chancery. The prison was destroyed during the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 and again during the Great Fire of London in 1666, and was rebuilt on both occasions.

Conditions

Like the Marshalsea, the Fleet was a privately run, profit-making institution. Prisoners had to pay for food, lodging, and even for the privilege of having their irons removed. Those who could afford it could live relatively comfortably, and some were permitted to live outside the walls within the "Rules of the Fleet" — an area of surrounding streets — in exchange for a fee. The poorest prisoners depended on charity begged through a grate facing the street.

Fleet marriages

During the 17th and early 18th centuries, the prison's chapel and its surroundings became notorious for clandestine "Fleet marriages", performed by imprisoned clergymen without banns or licence. The practice was effectively ended by Hardwicke's Marriage Act of 1753.

Closure

Parliamentary inquiries in the 18th and 19th centuries repeatedly exposed abuses by the prison's wardens. The Fleet was closed by an Act of 1842, its remaining prisoners were transferred, and the building was demolished in 1846. The site was later occupied by a railway station.

In literature

The Fleet appears in Charles Dickens's novel The Pickwick Papers, in which Mr Pickwick is imprisoned there for refusing to pay damages in a breach of promise suit.
