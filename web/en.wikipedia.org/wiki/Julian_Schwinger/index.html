Julian Seymour Schwinger (12 February 1918 – 16 July 1994) was an American theoretical physicist and Nobel laureate. He is best known for his work on Quantum electrodynamics (QED), in particular for developing a relativistically invariant perturbation theory and for renormalising QED to one-loop order — work crowned by his 1947–48 calculation of the electron's anomalous magnetic moment, α/2π, the first prediction of its kind.

A prodigy who published his first physics paper at 16 and transferred from City College to Columbia at I. I. Rabi's insistence, Schwinger worked on radar at MIT during the war (developing waveguide theory by night) and became Harvard's youngest full professor at 27. His Green's-function methods were formidably powerful and famously austere — students said attending his lectures was like watching a cathedral being built — and he trained a generation of leading theorists, including several Nobel laureates. In later years he championed source theory and championed cold fusion research, to his peers' dismay.

The g−2 calculation

Prompted by the Columbia measurements of the electron's magnetic moment (Kusch and Foley) and the Lamb shift, Schwinger's June 1948 result — the correction α/2π — matched experiment and proved renormalised QED worked, alongside the parallel achievements of Sin-Itiro Tomonaga in Japan and Richard Feynman's diagrammatic approach, unified by Freeman Dyson. The three principals shared the 1965 Nobel Prize.
