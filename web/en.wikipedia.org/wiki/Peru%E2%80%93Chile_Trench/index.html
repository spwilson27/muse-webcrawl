The Peru–Chile Trench, also known as the Atacama Trench, is an oceanic trench in the eastern Pacific Ocean, about 160 kilometres (100 mi) off the coast of Peru and Chile. It reaches a maximum depth of about 8,065 metres (26,460 ft) below sea level in the Richards Deep and is approximately 5,900 km (3,666 mi) long. The trench marks the boundary where the oceanic Nazca Plate subducts beneath the continental South American Plate.

Geology

Subduction at the Peru–Chile Trench proceeds at a rate of several centimetres per year and is responsible for the uplift of the Andes mountains and for the volcanism of the Andean Volcanic Belt. The trench is largely sediment-starved in its central portion offshore the arid Atacama Desert, so its morphology there is particularly steep and well defined.

Seismicity

The subduction zone is one of the most seismically active on Earth. It produced the largest earthquake ever recorded, the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile (magnitude 9.5), as well as the 2010 Maule earthquake (magnitude 8.8) and the 1868 Arica and 1877 Iquique earthquakes, which generated destructive tsunamis across the Pacific.
