Late Bronze Age collapse

The Late Bronze Age collapse was a period of societal collapse in the Eastern Mediterranean and Near East during the 12th century BC, roughly between c. 1200 and 1150 BC. Palace economies of Mycenaean Greece, the Hittite Empire, and many Levantine cities disintegrated, trade networks broke down, and literacy was lost in some regions, ushering in a "dark age" before the rise of iron-age kingdoms.

ResultFall of Hittite Empire and Mycenaean palaces; decline of Egypt's New Kingdom

Evidence

Destruction layers at cities such as Ugarit, Hattusa, Mycenae, and Tiryns, together with Egyptian records of attacks by the "Sea Peoples" under Ramesses III, attest to widespread violence and abandonment. Some cities, including Cyprus and Phoenician ports, recovered more quickly.

Causes

Proposed causes include invasions and mass migration, earthquakes, drought and famine (supported by pollen and climate data), internal rebellion, disruption of tin supplies vital for bronze, and systems collapse — the cascading failure of highly interdependent palace states. Most scholars favour a combination of factors.

Aftermath

The collapse cleared the way for new peoples and states: the Philistines, Arameans, Phoenician city-states, and eventually Israel, Assyria's resurgence, and Archaic Greece. Ironworking spread widely in the following centuries.
