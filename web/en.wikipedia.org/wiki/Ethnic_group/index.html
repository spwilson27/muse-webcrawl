Ethnic group

An ethnic group, or ethnicity, is a category of people who identify with each other on the basis of perceived shared attributes — commonly a common ancestry, language, history, culture, homeland, religion, or social treatment. Ethnicity is a central concept in anthropology, sociology, and political science, and a basis for both community solidarity and, at times, political mobilization and conflict.

Definitions and approaches

Scholars have debated whether ethnic identities are primordial — rooted in deep, given ties of kinship and culture — or constructed and situational, emphasized or downplayed according to context. Max Weber defined ethnic groups as those that entertain a subjective belief in common descent. Fredrik Barth's influential work (1969) shifted attention from cultural content to the boundaries groups maintain between themselves and others. "Ethnicity" in the modern sense became widely used in social science only after the Second World War.

Characteristics

Common markers include a shared name, myths of common ancestry, shared historical memories, elements of common culture (language, religion, dress, cuisine), association with a homeland, and a sense of solidarity. Ethnic groups differ from races in emphasizing cultural rather than (purported) physical characteristics, and from nations in that they need not claim statehood — though the categories often overlap, as in the "nation-state."

Ethnicity and politics

Ethnic identity structures politics in many states, from census categories and affirmative-action policies to federal arrangements (as in Ethiopia or Belgium) and, in extreme cases, ethnic cleansing and secessionist conflict. Diasporas maintain ethnic identities across generations in new homelands, often blending them with the majority culture.
