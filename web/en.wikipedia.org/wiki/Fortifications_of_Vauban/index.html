The Fortifications of Vauban are a group of twelve sites along the western, northern, and eastern borders of France designed by the military engineer Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban (1633–1707) and built in the late 17th century under Louis XIV. In 2008 they were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as masterpieces of military architecture that exemplified the "second system" of bastioned fortification and influenced defensive design across Europe and the Americas.

Vauban and the pré carré

Vauban, appointed Commissioner General of Fortifications in 1678 and later Marshal of France, built or remodelled more than 300 citadels and fortresses and conducted more than 50 sieges. His strategy of a defensible double line of fortresses on France's northern frontier — the pré carré ("square meadow") — helped fix the borders of modern France.

The World Heritage sites

The twelve inscribed groups include the citadels of Besançon, Arras, Lille, and Briançon; the fortified towns of Neuf-Brisach, Longwy, and Saint-Martin-de-Ré; the mountain fortress of Mont-Dauphin; the sea fort of Camaret-sur-Mer (Tour Vauban); the Fort des Salines (Cova Foradada) groupings; and the locks and canal works associated with his designs. Neuf-Brisach, a purpose-built octagonal fortress town on the Rhine plain, is considered the purest expression of his "third system".

Design principles

Vauban adapted the bastion fort to terrain, using angled bastions, ravelins, glacis slopes, and covered ways to eliminate dead ground and maximise flanking fire, while his siege methods — parallels, saps, and ricochet fire — made fortress warfare systematic.
