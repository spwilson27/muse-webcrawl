Alarm signal

Alarm signal
Field: Ethology, behavioural ecology
Function: Warning conspecifics (and sometimes heterospecifics) of danger
Forms: Vocal calls, chemical (pheromone) releases, visual displays

In animal communication, an alarm signal is an antipredator adaptation in the form of a signal emitted by social animals in response to danger. Many primates and birds produce distinctive alarm calls when they detect predators, and insects and fish release chemicals into the water or air that prompt escape responses in nearby members of the same or even other species.

Types

Vocal alarm calls are best studied in primates such as vervet monkeys, which give acoustically distinct calls for leopards, eagles and snakes, each eliciting an appropriate escape response; similar referential signalling occurs in prairie dogs and meerkats. Chemical alarm signals include the alarm pheromones of ants and bees (which recruit defenders and trigger aggression) and the fright substances (Schreckstoff) released from damaged skin in many fish, causing nearby fish to flee or hide. Some plants likewise release volatile compounds when grazed that warn neighbouring plants or attract predators of the herbivores.

Evolution

Alarm signalling poses an evolutionary puzzle, since calling may draw a predator's attention to the signaller. Explanations include kin selection (warning relatives), pursuit-deterrent signalling ("I have seen you" messages aimed at the predator itself), and manipulation of group mates into fleeing so the signaller can escape. Signals may also be deceptive: some species give false alarms to steal food from group mates.
