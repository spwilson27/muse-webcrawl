Licensing

Licensing is the practice by which the owner of property — the licensor — grants another party — the licensee — permission to use that property under specified conditions, in exchange for payment (royalties or a fee), while retaining ownership. Licences are common for intellectual property such as patents, trademarks, copyrights, trade secrets, brands and designs, as well as for regulated activities and for the use of land or facilities.

Intellectual property licensing

A licence agreement defines the scope of the rights granted: the territory, duration, exclusivity, field of use, and whether the licensee may sublicense. An exclusive licence grants rights to a single licensee; a sole licence reserves the licensor's own right to use; a non-exclusive licence allows multiple licensees. Cross-licensing occurs when two parties license patents to each other, and compulsory licensing allows a government to authorise use of a patent without the owner's consent, for example for public health.

Brand and merchandising licensing

Owners of well-known brands and characters license them for use on products, media and promotions. This is a major revenue stream in entertainment, sport and fashion, and is managed through licensing agents and style guides that control quality and presentation.

Software licensing

Software is typically distributed under licence rather than sold. Proprietary licences restrict copying and modification, while free and open-source licences — such as the GNU General Public License and the MIT License — grant broad rights to use, study, modify and redistribute, sometimes with copyleft conditions.

Occupational and regulatory licensing

Governments license occupations and activities — medicine, law, driving, broadcasting, liquor sales — to protect the public. Occupational licensing typically requires education, examination and ongoing compliance, and its economic effects are widely debated.
