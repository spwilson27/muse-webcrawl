Incidental catch

Incidental catch is a term used in fisheries for the part of a catch of a fishing unit taken incidentally in addition to the target species. It is closely related to the term bycatch, and the two are often used interchangeably; under definitions used by the FAO and other bodies, incidental catch may be retained or discarded, and may include fish, crustaceans, molluscs, marine mammals, sea turtles and other organisms taken unintentionally by fishing gear such as trawls, longlines and gillnets.

Distinction from bycatch

Terminology in fisheries science varies. In some usage, "bycatch" refers to the total catch of non-target species, whether kept or thrown back, while "incidental catch" denotes the non-target portion that is actually landed or taken during an operation, or catch that is a by-product of fishing for a different target. Under United States law, bycatch is defined as fish that are harvested but not sold or kept for personal use, including regulatory and economic discards. Because definitions differ between jurisdictions and organisations, statistics on incidental catch and bycatch are not always directly comparable.

Environmental significance

Incidental catch is a major conservation concern. It can deplete non-target and juvenile fish stocks, and it kills large numbers of threatened species — notably sea turtles in shrimp trawls, seabirds such as albatrosses on longlines, and dolphins and other cetaceans in purse seines and gillnets. FAO estimates have suggested that discards alone amount to millions of tonnes of fish annually worldwide.

Mitigation

Measures to reduce incidental catch include bycatch reduction devices and turtle excluder devices in trawls, circle hooks and bird-scaring lines in longline fisheries, acoustic pingers on gillnets to warn off cetaceans, time-area closures, and gear modifications such as larger mesh sizes. Some fisheries also land and market former incidental catch, converting waste into a retained product.
