Mashing

In brewing and distilling, mashing is the process of combining a mix of ground grains — typically malted barley with supplementary grains such as corn, rye, or wheat — with water and then heating the mixture. Mashing allows the enzymes in the malt to break down the starches in the grain into sugars, most importantly maltose, creating a sugary liquid called wort. The process takes place in a vessel called a mash tun.

Process

Ground malt, known as grist, is mixed with hot water (the "liquor") to form a porridge-like mash. The temperature of the mash is carefully controlled because the key starch-converting enzymes have different optimal ranges: beta-amylase works best around 60–65 °C (140–149 °F) and produces highly fermentable sugars, while alpha-amylase works best around 68–72 °C (154–162 °F) and produces a less fermentable, fuller-bodied wort. Brewers choose mash temperatures to balance fermentability, body, and flavour in the finished beer.

Methods

Infusion mashing, traditional in Britain, holds the mash at a single temperature in an insulated vessel. Step mashing raises the temperature through a series of rests, either by adding hot water or by direct heating. Decoction mashing, historically used in Germany and Central Europe, removes a portion of the mash, boils it, and returns it to the main mash to raise the temperature step by step; it was an important technique before reliable thermometers and remains associated with styles such as German lagers.

When conversion is complete, the liquid wort is separated from the spent grains in a step called lautering, and the grains may be rinsed ("sparged") with hot water to recover remaining sugars. The wort then proceeds to the boil, where hops are added.

Distilling

In whisky production, mashing similarly converts grain starches to fermentable sugars, producing a wort that is fermented without boiling to create wash for distillation. Distillers may use a mixture of malted and unmalted grains, since the enzymes from the malted portion can convert the starches of the whole grist.
