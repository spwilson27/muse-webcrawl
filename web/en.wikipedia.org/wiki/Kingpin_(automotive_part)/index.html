Kingpin (automotive part)

A kingpin forms the main pivot of a beam-axle steering assembly.

The kingpin (also called a king pin or king bolt) is the main pivot in the steering mechanism of a car or other vehicle. Originally used on horse-drawn carriages and early automobiles, the term survives in the steering geometry of heavy trucks and in the coupling of semi-trailers.

Steering kingpin

In a beam-axle front suspension, the stub axle carrying the wheel pivots about a hardened steel pin — the kingpin — mounted at the end of the axle beam. The angle at which the kingpin is inclined, called kingpin inclination, contributes to self-centring of the steering. Modern passenger cars have largely replaced kingpins with ball joints in independent suspensions, but kingpins remain standard on the front axles of heavy commercial vehicles because of their strength and durability.

Fifth-wheel kingpin

On a semi-trailer, the kingpin is a heavy vertical steel pin at the front underside of the trailer that locks into the fifth-wheel coupling of the tractor unit, forming the articulation point between the two. Standard sizes are 2 inches (50.8 mm) and 3.5 inches (88.9 mm) in diameter.
