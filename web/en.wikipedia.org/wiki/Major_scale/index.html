Major scale

The major scale (or Ionian mode) is one of the most commonly used musical scales in Western music. It is a diatonic scale built from a specific pattern of whole steps and half steps: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. The C major scale — C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C — contains no sharps or flats, and its notes form the white keys of the piano keyboard.

Structure

A major scale consists of seven distinct pitch classes plus the octave. In semitones, the intervals from the tonic are 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 12. The half steps fall between the third and fourth degrees and between the seventh and eighth degrees. Degrees of the scale have traditional names: tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant, and leading tone.

Key signatures and the circle of fifths

Each major key has a unique key signature of sharps or flats. Moving up by a perfect fifth adds one sharp (G major has F♯, D major has F♯ and C♯, and so on); moving down by a fifth adds a flat. This progression is charted on the circle of fifths, a fundamental tool for understanding key relationships.

Harmony

Chords built by stacking thirds on each degree of the major scale produce the standard diatonic triads: major on I, IV, and V; minor on ii, iii, and vi; and diminished on vii°. Much of Western tonal harmony — including the ubiquitous I–V–vi–IV progression — is built from these chords.

Character

The major scale is often described as sounding bright, happy, or resolved, in contrast to the darker quality of the natural minor scale, which shares the same notes under rotation and is called the major key's relative minor. See also the diatonic scale and Ionian mode.
