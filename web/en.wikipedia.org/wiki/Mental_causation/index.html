Mental causation

How can a mental event, such as a desire, cause a physical event, such as raising one's arm?

Mental causation is the philosophical problem of how mental states and events — beliefs, desires, intentions, pains — can cause physical events, such as bodily movements, and how physical events can cause mental ones. It is one of the central problems in the philosophy of mind, closely tied to the mind–body problem and the question of free will.

The problem

The difficulty arises from three apparently plausible but jointly inconsistent claims: (1) mental events cause physical events (my desire for water causes me to reach for a glass); (2) the physical domain is causally closed — every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause; and (3) mental events are not identical with, or reducible to, physical events. If all three held, actions would appear systematically overdetermined, or the mental would be epiphenomenal — a causally inert by-product of brain activity.

Kim's exclusion argument

Jaegwon Kim sharpened the problem into the causal exclusion argument: if a mental property is distinct from its physical realizer, and the physical realizer already fully causes the effect, there is no causal work left for the mental property to do — unless one accepts systematic causal overdetermination, which most philosophers reject. The argument presses non-reductive physicalists in particular.

Responses

Proposed solutions include identity theories and other reductionist accounts; epiphenomenalism, which denies that the mental causes the physical; interactionist dualism, which denies causal closure; Donald Davidson's anomalous monism, on which mental events are identical with physical events though mental descriptions are irreducible; interventionist and proportionality accounts, on which causes need only be difference-makers at the appropriate level; and dual-aspect or Russellian monist views.
