The 2008 Chatsworth train collision occurred on September 12, 2008, when a Metrolink commuter train collided head-on with a Union Pacific freight train in the Chatsworth district of Los Angeles, California. Twenty-five people were killed, including the Metrolink engineer, and 135 were injured. It was the deadliest accident in Metrolink's history and one of the worst rail disasters in modern United States history.

Collision

Metrolink train 111, bound for Moorpark on the Ventura County Line with 225 passengers aboard, departed Union Station in downtown Los Angeles during the afternoon rush hour. North of the Chatsworth station, on a single-track section shared with freight traffic, the train passed a red stop signal without stopping and entered the main line in front of an oncoming Union Pacific freight train. The two trains collided at a combined speed of more than 80 mph. The Metrolink locomotive telescoped into the first passenger car, and several cars derailed and caught fire.

Investigation

The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) determined that the Metrolink engineer, who was killed in the crash, had failed to observe the red signal. Investigators found that he had been sending and receiving text messages on his mobile phone while on duty, including one sent 22 seconds before impact. Records also showed he had allowed unauthorised riders into the cab on earlier occasions.

Aftermath

The crash spurred the United States Congress to pass the Rail Safety Improvement Act of 2008, which mandated the implementation of positive train control (PTC) — technology capable of automatically stopping a train before certain accidents — on major rail lines, and restricted the use of electronic devices by train crews. The Federal Railroad Administration issued emergency rules banning texting by operating employees. Metrolink added inward-facing cameras and a second crew member in the cab on some services, and paid out roughly $200 million in claims to victims, reaching the statutory liability cap.
