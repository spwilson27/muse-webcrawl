The 6 February 1934 crisis was an anti-parliamentary street protest and riot in Paris, in which far-right leagues and veterans' associations marched on the Chamber of Deputies. Police and guards fired on the crowds at the Pont de la Concorde; around fifteen people were killed. It remains debated whether the demonstration constituted an attempted fascist coup, but it gravely shook the French Third Republic and led directly to the fall of the government and, in reaction, to the formation of the left-wing Popular Front.

Background

France in early 1934 was gripped by the Stavisky affair, a financial scandal implicating politicians, and by resentment at the dismissal of the popular Paris police prefect Jean Chiappe. The leagues — including the Croix-de-Feu, Action Française's Camelots du Roi, Jeunesses Patriotes and the Solidarité française — called demonstrations for the evening of 6 February, when Édouard Daladier's new government faced the Chamber.

Events and aftermath

Rioters overwhelmed barricades, burned a kiosk and a ministry building, and attempted to storm the Palais Bourbon before mounted guards and police gunfire stopped them. Daladier resigned the next day — the first Third Republic government brought down by street pressure — and Gaston Doumergue formed a government of national union. On 12 February the left answered with a general strike; the alliance that followed became the Popular Front, which won the 1936 elections.
