Collapse of the Pacific sardine industry

Monterey's Cannery Row, centre of the California sardine industry immortalised by John Steinbeck.

The collapse of the Pacific sardine industry refers to the dramatic decline, in the 1940s and 1950s, of the fishery for the Pacific sardine (Sardinops sagax) off the west coast of North America, then the largest fishery in the Western Hemisphere by weight. Centred on Monterey and San Pedro, California, the industry had landed over 700,000 tons in its peak 1936–37 season; within two decades the fish—and the canneries of Cannery Row—had virtually vanished.

Boom

Sardines were canned for food and reduced to fishmeal and oil. Wartime demand drove expansion, and California's fleet of purse seiners fished intensively off California and, increasingly, further afield as local abundance fell.

Collapse

Scientists of the California Cooperative Sardine Research Program debated the causes: heavy overfishing removed older spawners, while a natural shift to cooler ocean conditions reduced recruitment and shifted the population southwards. Whatever the precise mix, spawning biomass crashed in the late 1940s; seasons failed one after another, a moratorium was imposed by California in 1967, and the last Monterey cannery closed in 1973. The episode became a canonical warning in fisheries science, alongside Steinbeck's Cannery Row (1945) as its cultural epitaph.

Recovery and later management

The population slowly rebuilt under protection, and a modest directed fishery reopened in the 1980s–90s under a federal management plan with harvest control rules tied to biomass estimates; the fishery was again closed in 2015 after another steep decline, and sardine abundance continues to fluctuate with ocean conditions.
