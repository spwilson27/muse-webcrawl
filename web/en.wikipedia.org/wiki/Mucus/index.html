Mucus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

Mucus (from Latin mucus, "snot, slime") is a slippery, viscous aqueous secretion produced by, and covering, the mucous membranes of animals. It consists mainly of water, electrolytes, antiseptic enzymes such as lysozyme, immunoglobulins, and long gel-forming glycoproteins called mucins, secreted by goblet cells in epithelial surfaces and by submucosal glands. Mucus protects and lubricates the surfaces it covers.

Respiratory system

In the airways, mucus traps inhaled dust, allergens and microbes. Beating cilia move the mucus sheet steadily upward toward the throat — the "mucociliary escalator" — where it is swallowed or coughed out. An adult produces about a litre of respiratory mucus a day, normally without noticing; infection or irritation increases production and changes its colour and thickness, as in the common cold, bronchitis or sinusitis. In cystic fibrosis, defective chloride channels make mucus abnormally thick and sticky, causing chronic lung infections and pancreatic insufficiency.

Other systems

In the digestive tract, mucus shields the stomach lining from its own acid and lubricates the passage of food. Cervical mucus changes consistency across the menstrual cycle, alternately blocking or admitting sperm. Many animals use mucus externally: fish coats reduce drag and infection, slugs and snails move on mucus trails, and hagfish release copious defensive slime. The word is distinguished from phlegm, which refers specifically to mucus produced by the respiratory system, and from snot, dried or expelled nasal mucus.
