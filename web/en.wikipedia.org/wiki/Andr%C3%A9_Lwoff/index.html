André Lwoff

Born8 May 1902, Ainay-le-Château, France
Died30 September 1994 (aged 92), Paris, France
FieldMicrobiology, virology
AwardsNobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1965)

André Michel Lwoff (1902–1994) was a French microbiologist who shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1965 with François Jacob and Jacques Monod "for their discoveries concerning genetic control of enzyme and virus synthesis". He worked at the Pasteur Institute in Paris for most of his career.

Work

Lwoff began his career studying ciliates and microbial nutrition, work that led him to define growth factors and helped establish the concept of vitamins for microorganisms. His most influential research concerned lysogeny: he showed that bacteriophages can persist in bacteria in a dormant prophage state, integrated with the host, and can be induced — for example by ultraviolet light — to enter the lytic cycle and produce new virus particles. His studies of the poliovirus and of viral classification were also influential; he played a central role in developing a systematic taxonomy of viruses and in defining viruses as entities distinct from cellular organisms.

Honours

Besides the Nobel Prize, Lwoff received numerous honours and was elected to academies in France, Britain and the United States. His book Biological Order (1962) reflects on the philosophy of biology. During the Second World War he participated in the French Resistance.
