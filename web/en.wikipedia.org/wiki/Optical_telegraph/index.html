Optical telegraph

An optical telegraph is a system of relay stations, typically towers, equipped with signaling devices that could be read by telescope from the next station, used to convey textual information over long distances before the invention of the electrical telegraph. The most successful and extensive system was the semaphore network built in France by Claude Chappe in the 1790s.

The Chappe system

Claude Chappe and his brothers developed a semaphore with pivoting arms whose positions encoded symbols from a codebook. In 1794 the first line, between Paris and Lille, famously carried news of a French victory over the Austrians within hours. Under Napoleon the network expanded enormously; by the 1850s France had over 500 stations covering some 5,000 km.

Other systems

Britain built shutter telegraph lines (pioneered by Lord George Murray) linking the Admiralty in London to naval ports such as Portsmouth and Plymouth. Sweden, Prussia, Denmark and other states also constructed optical networks during the Napoleonic era.

Operation and speed

Each station was staffed by operators who watched the neighboring tower through a telescope and reproduced its signals. A message could travel hundreds of kilometers in well under an hour in good weather — far faster than a mounted courier — but fog, rain and darkness could shut the system down entirely.

Decline

Optical telegraphs were rapidly displaced in the 1840s and 1850s by the electrical telegraph, which was faster, weather-independent, and cheaper to operate. The French network, the last major holdout, closed in the 1850s.
