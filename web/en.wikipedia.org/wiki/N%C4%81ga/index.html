Nāga

TraditionsHinduism, Buddhism, Jainism
AbodePātāla (Nāgaloka); waters
Notable nāgasShesha, Vasuki, Takshaka, Mucalinda

Nāga (Sanskrit: नाग, "snake") is a class of serpent-like divine or semi-divine beings in Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism. Nāgas are typically depicted as great cobras, often with multiple heads, or as beings with human torsos and serpent tails. They dwell in the subterranean realm of Pātāla and in lakes, rivers, and seas, where they guard treasures and control rain and waters.

In Hindu tradition

Prominent nāgas include Shesha, the cosmic serpent on whom Vishnu reclines; Vasuki, used as the churning rope in the Samudra Manthana (churning of the ocean of milk); Manasa, goddess of snakes; and Takshaka, who killed King Parikshit. Nāgas can be benevolent protectors or dangerous adversaries, and the festival of Naga Panchami is devoted to their worship.

In Buddhism and beyond

In Buddhist lore, the nāga king Mucalinda sheltered the meditating Buddha from a storm by coiling beneath him and spreading his hoods overhead. Nāgas appear throughout South and Southeast Asian art and folklore — in Cambodia and Thailand as temple guardians, in the Mekong region as river spirits, and their motif spread into Chinese and East Asian dragon traditions.
