Austronesian expansion

The Austronesian expansion was the seaborne spread of Austronesian-speaking peoples from Taiwan, beginning around 3000–1500 BC, which ultimately colonised a vast maritime region: westward to Madagascar, eastward to Rapa Nui (Easter Island), north to Hawai'i, and south to New Zealand — one of the widest dispersals of any language family before the modern era.

Out of Taiwan

Linguistic and archaeological evidence points to Taiwan as the homeland of the Austronesian languages: nine of the family's ten primary branches are found only among Taiwan's Indigenous peoples. Neolithic seafarers crossed to the Batanes and Luzon in the Philippines by about 2200 BC, carrying a package of red-slipped pottery, domesticated pigs and dogs, and crops such as rice, millet, and later taro and yam.

Island Southeast Asia and the Lapita expansion

Austronesian speakers spread through the Philippines and Indonesia, reaching the Bismarck Archipelago by about 1500 BC, where the distinctive dentate-stamped Lapita culture emerged. In one of prehistory's fastest expansions, Lapita voyagers settled Vanuatu, New Caledonia, Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa by about 900 BC, becoming the ancestors of the Polynesians.

Remote Oceania and Madagascar

After a pause of nearly two millennia in western Polynesia, voyagers using double-hulled canoes and sophisticated non-instrument navigation settled the Cook, Society, Marquesas, and Tuamotu islands, then Hawai'i (by c. 1000 AD), Rapa Nui, and finally New Zealand (c. 1250–1300 AD). In the west, Austronesian sailors from Borneo crossed the Indian Ocean to settle Madagascar around the mid-first millennium AD, where their language, Malagasy, retains clear Bornean roots.

Genetics and scholarship

The "Out of Taiwan" model, championed by Peter Bellwood and supported by linguistics (Robert Blust) and increasingly by ancient DNA, is contrasted with slower, more localised "Nusantao" models. Genetic studies show Austronesian voyagers mixed substantially with Papuan populations in Near Oceania.
