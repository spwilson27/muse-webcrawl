Electrode potential

In electrochemistry, electrode potential is the electromotive force of a galvanic cell built from a standard reference electrode and the electrode under study. By convention the reference is the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), assigned a potential of exactly 0 V. The standard electrode potential (E°) of a half-reaction is measured with all species at unit activity at 25 °C.

Origin

Electrode potential arises from the charge separation at the interface between an electrode and its electrolyte: a metal immersed in a solution of its ions tends to dissolve or to deposit ions until the resulting potential difference balances the chemical driving force. The potential depends on the concentrations of the species involved through the Nernst equation, E = E° − (RT/nF)·ln Q.

Use

Tables of standard reduction potentials order half-reactions by oxidising strength: fluorine (+2.87 V) is the strongest common oxidising agent and lithium (−3.04 V) the strongest reducing agent. The difference between two half-cell potentials predicts the voltage of a cell and whether a reaction is spontaneous, and underlies the electrochemical series used to predict corrosion, displacement reactions and battery voltages.

Measurement

Because a single electrode potential cannot be measured in isolation, it is always reported relative to a reference electrode; practical work often uses the silver/silver chloride or saturated calomel electrodes, whose potentials relative to the SHE are known.
