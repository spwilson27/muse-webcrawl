Spontaneous parametric down-conversion

In SPDC a pump photon splits into a signal and an idler photon inside a nonlinear crystal.

Spontaneous parametric down-conversion (also known as SPDC, parametric fluorescence or parametric scattering) is a nonlinear optical process that converts one photon of higher energy (namely, a pump photon), into a pair of photons (namely, a signal photon and an idler photon) of lower energy, in accordance with the law of conservation of energy and law of conservation of momentum. It is an important process in quantum optics, for the generation of entangled photon pairs, and of single photons.

Basic process

A nonlinear crystal is subjected to an intense laser beam. In rare cases a pump photon is spontaneously split by the crystal into two photons of lower energy, conventionally called the signal and the idler. Energy conservation requires ωp = ωs + ωi, and momentum conservation (phase matching) requires the wave vectors to satisfy kp = ks + ki. The efficiency of the process is very low — roughly one conversion per 1012 pump photons in bulk crystals — but the resulting pairs are quantum mechanically entangled in energy, momentum, and often polarization.

Types of phase matching

In type-I down-conversion the signal and idler have the same polarization, perpendicular to the pump; in type-II they have orthogonal polarizations. Type-II sources, particularly with beta-barium borate (BBO) crystals, are the classic way to produce polarization-entangled pairs of the form (|H⟩|V⟩ + |V⟩|H⟩)/√2. Periodically poled crystals such as PPKTP achieve quasi-phase matching and are widely used in waveguide sources.

Applications

SPDC sources are the workhorses of experimental quantum optics. They have been used in tests of Bell's inequality, quantum teleportation, entanglement swapping, quantum cryptography, and ghost imaging. Detecting one photon of a pair "heralds" the presence of the other, giving an approximation to a single-photon source. Squeezed light can also be generated when the down-converted modes are combined with the pump in an optical parametric oscillator.

History

The process was first demonstrated in 1967–1970, shortly after the advent of the laser, and theory was developed by Klyshko and others. Experiments by Burnham and Weinberg (1970) established the paired nature of the emission, and by the 1980s SPDC had become the standard source in foundational experiments on entanglement, including the work of Alain Aspect and later Anton Zeilinger.
