Radioisotope

A radioisotope, also called a radioactive isotope, radionuclide, or radioactive nuclide, is an isotope of a chemical element whose nucleus has an excess of nuclear energy, making it unstable. This excess energy can be emitted as radiation in the form of alpha particles, beta particles, or gamma rays — a process known as radioactive decay.

Production

Some radioisotopes occur naturally, produced by cosmic rays or present since the formation of the Earth, such as uranium-238, potassium-40, and carbon-14. Others are made artificially in nuclear reactors, by neutron irradiation, or in particle accelerators such as cyclotrons.

Applications

Radioisotopes are used as tracers in medicine (e.g. technetium-99m imaging), in radiotherapy (e.g. cobalt-60, iodine-131), in industry for gauging and radiography, in smoke detectors (americium-241), in radioisotope thermoelectric generators for spacecraft, and in radiometric dating, such as radiocarbon dating of archaeological material.

Safety

Because the radiation they emit can damage living tissue, radioisotopes are handled under strict regulatory controls. Their hazard depends on the type of radiation, the half-life, and the chemical behaviour of the element in the body.
