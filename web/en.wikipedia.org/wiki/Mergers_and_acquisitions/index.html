Mergers and acquisitions

Mergers and acquisitions (M&A)
FieldCorporate finance
ParticipantsAcquirer, target company, shareholders, investment banks, regulators
Deal formsMerger, acquisition, tender offer, leveraged buyout, asset purchase
MotivesSynergies, growth, market power, diversification, tax

Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) are business transactions in which the ownership of companies or their operating units is transferred to, or consolidated with, another entity. In a merger, two companies combine into a new or surviving entity, typically by mutual agreement; in an acquisition, one company purchases most or all of another's shares or assets and takes control of it. M&A is a central activity of corporate finance, advised by investment banks and law firms, and scrutinised by competition (antitrust) regulators.

Types

  • Horizontal — competitors in the same industry combine (e.g. two airlines).
  • Vertical — companies at different stages of a supply chain combine (e.g. a manufacturer buys a distributor).
  • Conglomerate — unrelated businesses combine.
  • Market-extension and product-extension mergers — combining to enter new markets or add product lines.
  • Reverse merger — a private company acquires a listed shell to gain a stock-market listing.
  • Leveraged buyout (LBO) — acquisition financed largely with debt, typical of private equity.

Process and valuation

A deal typically proceeds through strategy and target screening, due diligence, valuation (by discounted cash flow, comparable companies and precedent transactions), negotiation of a purchase agreement, financing, regulatory approval and closing, followed by post-merger integration. Payment may be in cash, shares, or both. Friendly deals are agreed with the target's board; hostile takeovers proceed over the board's opposition, classically by tender offer directly to shareholders, and targets may deploy defences such as the poison pill (shareholder rights plan).

Motives and outcomes

Stated motives include revenue and cost synergies, economies of scale, acquiring technology or talent ("acqui-hires"), eliminating competition, and tax advantages. Empirical research, however, finds that many acquisitions fail to create value for the acquirer's shareholders; acquiring-firm returns are on average close to zero, with target shareholders capturing most of the gains through the control premium. Famous large deals include Vodafone's acquisition of Mannesmann (2000, ~$183 billion, the largest ever at the time), AOL–Time Warner (2001, widely judged a failure), Exxon–Mobil (1999) and Disney's acquisition of 21st Century Fox assets (2019, $71 billion).

History

M&A activity proceeds in waves: the horizontal trust-building of the 1890s–1900s (which produced U.S. Steel in 1901), the conglomerate wave of the 1960s, the hostile-takeover and LBO wave of the 1980s (epitomised by KKR's $25 billion buyout of RJR Nabisco in 1988), the stock-fuelled mega-mergers of the 1990s, and the private-equity and cross-border booms of the 2000s and 2020s, when global deal value peaked at over $5 trillion in 2021.
