Iron triangle (US politics)

Iron triangle
The iron triangle: congressional committees, agencies and interest groups exchange favours.
FieldPolitical science; American politics
Three pointsCongressional committees · Federal bureaucracy · Interest groups
EffectMutually beneficial policymaking insulated from outside scrutiny

In United States politics, the iron triangle is a model of policymaking describing a stable, mutually beneficial three-way relationship between congressional committees (or subcommittees), the federal bureaucracy (executive agencies), and organized interest groups. The three parties form a closed, self-reinforcing loop — the "triangle" — that sets policy in a given domain largely insulated from presidential control, judicial review and the general public.

How it works

Each side of the triangle provides something the others need: congressional committees provide agencies with appropriations, authorising legislation and political protection, and provide interest groups with favourable legislation and access; bureaucratic agencies implement programs that benefit the interest groups, testify before Congress in support of committee positions, and supply information and jobs; interest groups provide political support and campaign contributions to committee members, and confer legitimacy and expertise on the agencies' work. Because each side benefits, all three have an interest in preserving the arrangement.

Examples

Classic textbook examples include the defence establishment (the Armed Services committees, the Pentagon, and defence contractors — closely related to Eisenhower's warning about the military–industrial complex), agriculture (the House and Senate Agriculture Committees, the Department of Agriculture, and farm organisations), and transportation. The term is often applied pejoratively to imply that policy is being made in the interest of the triangle's members rather than the public.

Criticism and the "issue network"

Political scientists have noted that the iron triangle model describes policymaking at its most closed and stable. Hugh Heclo argued that in many policy areas the triangle had been displaced by looser "issue networks" — larger, more fluid sets of actors including journalists, think tanks and academics. Others observe that iron triangles persist wherever a narrow, concentrated interest faces little organised opposition — Mancur Olson's logic of collective action in institutional form.
