The Wawel Castle (Polish: Zamek Królewski na Wawelu) is a fortified residency on Wawel Hill in Kraków, Poland, built at the behest of King Casimir III the Great. Together with the adjacent Wawel Cathedral it forms one of Poland's most important historic and cultural complexes: for centuries it was the residence of the kings of Poland and the centre of state power, and the cathedral served as the coronation church and burial place of Polish monarchs.

History

Wawel Hill, a limestone outcrop above the Vistula River, has been inhabited since prehistoric times and is wrapped in the legend of the Wawel Dragon. A royal residence existed by the 11th century. Casimir III built a Gothic castle in the 14th century; after a fire in 1499, Sigismund I the Old rebuilt it in Renaissance style (1504–1536), creating an arcaded courtyard by Italian architects including Bartolomeo Berrecci. The castle declined after the capital moved to Warsaw in the late 16th century and suffered under Swedish invasions and Austrian military occupation in the 19th century, when it served as a barracks and hospital. Returned to Polish hands in the early 20th century, it became a national museum.

Collections

The Wawel Royal Castle – State Art Collection displays the Polish Crown Treasury, famous Renaissance tapestries woven in Brussels for Sigismund II Augustus, Italian paintings, and the Szczerbiec, the coronation sword of Polish kings. The cathedral contains the tombs of kings, national heroes such as Tadeusz Kościuszko, and the poet Adam Mickiewicz, as well as the giant Sigismund Bell of 1520 in its tower.

The Wawel Dragon

Below the hill lies the Dragon's Den (Smocza Jama), a limestone cave linked to the legend of a dragon slain through the cunning of the shoemaker's apprentice Skuba. A fire-breathing statue of the dragon, erected in 1972, stands at the cave's entrance on the Vistula bank.
