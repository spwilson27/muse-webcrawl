Buckling

In solid mechanics, buckling is the sudden change in shape (deformation) of a structural component under load, such as the bowing of a column under compression or the wrinkling of a plate under shear. It occurs when a structure that is stable under small disturbances becomes unstable: once the compressive load exceeds a critical load, deflection grows rapidly even though the material itself may be far below its yield strength. Buckling is thus a failure of stability, not of material strength, and it governs the design of slender columns, thin shells, struts and panels.

Euler's formula

Leonhard Euler derived the critical load for an ideal pin-ended column in 1757: Pcr = π²EI / L², where E is the elastic modulus, I the smallest second moment of area, and L the column length. For other end conditions the effective length factor K adjusts the formula. Euler's formula is valid for long, slender columns (high slenderness ratio); for stocky columns, yielding or crushing governs instead, and design codes blend the regimes with empirical curves such as the Johnson parabola. Real columns buckle below the Euler load because of imperfections, so modern codes apply reduction factors.

Other forms

Local buckling can cripple thin flanges and webs before the whole member fails; thin cylindrical shells under axial load or external pressure buckle at fractions of their classical predicted load; and lateral-torsional buckling affects beams bent about their strong axis. Dynamic and creep buckling also occur under impact loads and at high temperatures.
