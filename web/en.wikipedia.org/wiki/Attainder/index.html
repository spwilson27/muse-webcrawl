Attainder

In English criminal law, attainder or attinctura was the metaphorical "stain" or "corruption of blood" that arose from being condemned for a serious capital crime (felony or treason). It entailed losing not only property and civil rights but typically also the right to pass property or titles to one's heirs; an attained person could neither inherit nor transmit inheritance, because their blood was considered legally corrupted.

Effects

Upon conviction and sentence of death for treason or felony, the offender was "attainted": all goods and chattels were forfeited and lands escheated to the Crown, civil rights were extinguished, and the corruption of blood prevented inheritance by or through the attained person. Attainder followed automatically upon sentence until the Forfeiture Act 1870 abolished forfeiture and corruption of blood in England for most offences.

Bills of attainder

A bill of attainder was a legislative act declaring a person or group guilty of a crime and imposing punishment without a judicial trial. Parliaments used acts of attainder extensively in the Wars of the Roses and Tudor period against political enemies, and later cases—such as those against the Earl of Strafford (1641) and Jacobite rebels—kept the device prominent. Such bills are prohibited by the United States Constitution (Article I, Sections 9 and 10), which forbids both Congress and the states from passing bills of attainder.

Modern position

In the United Kingdom, attainder and its consequences were swept away in the nineteenth century; in the United States, the constitutional prohibition remains an important separation-of-powers safeguard, barring legislatures from singling out individuals for punishment.
