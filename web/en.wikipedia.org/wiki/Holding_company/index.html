Holding company
TypeParent company
PurposeOwning shares of subsidiary companies

A holding company is a company whose primary business is holding a controlling interest in the securities of other companies. A holding company usually does not produce goods or services itself; its purpose is to own shares of other companies (its subsidiaries) in order to form and control a corporate group. In some jurisdictions the term is reserved for such "pure" holding companies, while a company that both owns subsidiaries and runs its own operations is called a parent company more generally.

Advantages

Control of a group can be obtained while owning only a fraction of each subsidiary's shares, especially where holding companies are stacked in a pyramid. The structure limits liability — the failure of one subsidiary need not destroy the group — and can allow losses in one subsidiary to offset profits elsewhere, simplify acquisitions and disposals, and isolate regulated activities in separate entities. Dividends and capital gains moving between group companies may receive favourable tax treatment.

History and regulation

Holding companies flourished in the United States after New Jersey (1888–1896) liberalised its incorporation laws to let corporations own stock in others. The structure underpinned great trusts and utility pyramids of the early twentieth century; abuses of pyramiding in public utilities provoked the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935, which forced the breakup of interstate utility empires. Bank holding companies are regulated under the Bank Holding Company Act of 1956.

Personal holding companies

An individual or family may use a personal holding company to own investments. In the United States such companies are subject to a special tax on undistributed personal holding company income, designed to prevent sheltering investment income at corporate rates; similar incorporated-pocketbook rules exist in Canada and elsewhere.

Examples

Well-known holding companies include Berkshire Hathaway, which wholly owns insurers, railroads and manufacturers while holding large minority stakes in public companies; Alphabet Inc., created in 2015 as the parent of Google and its "Other Bets"; and diversified groups such as Jardine Matheson, Tata Sons, and the Japanese keiretsu successors to pre-war zaibatsu holdings.
