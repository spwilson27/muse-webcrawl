Plurinationalism

Plurinationalism is the political principle that a single state may be constituted by several nations, peoples, or nationalities, each of which retains a distinct collective identity and some measure of self-government, rather than conforming to the classical model of one nation per state. The concept goes beyond multicultural recognition of diversity: in a plurinational state, the constituent peoples are recognised as political subjects and co-founders of the state, often with constitutional status, territorial autonomy, and official languages.

The Wiphala, an emblem of Andean indigenous peoples, flies beside Bolivia's national flag as a symbol of plurinationality.

Development of the concept

The idea developed mainly in Latin America, where indigenous movements challenged the idea that independence-era republics had created culturally homogeneous nations. Bolivian sociologist and politician Álvaro García Linera and others argued that countries such as Bolivia contained several "nations" — Quechua, Aymara, Guaraní, and dozens of smaller peoples — that predated the republic and had never been genuinely incorporated into it. In Ecuador, the indigenous confederation CONAIE made plurinationality a central demand from the 1990s onward.

Constitutional adoption

Bolivia

The Constitution of Bolivia, approved by referendum in 2009 under President Evo Morales, renamed the country the Plurinational State of Bolivia. It recognises 36 indigenous nations and their languages as official alongside Spanish, establishes indigenous autonomies, incorporates indigenous justice systems (subject to constitutional limits), and adopts symbols such as the Wiphala as co-national emblems.

Ecuador

Ecuador's 2008 Constitution defines the country as an intercultural and plurinational state, recognises Kichwa and Shuar as official languages of intercultural relations, and grants collective rights to indigenous peoples, Afro-Ecuadorians, and the Montubio people, including the rights of nature (sumak kawsay, "good living").

Debates elsewhere

Related ideas have been debated in other multinational states. In Spain, some Catalan and Basque nationalists, and Spanish left parties such as Podemos, have described Spain as a "plurinational" country whose constitution recognises "nationalities and regions". In the United Kingdom, the coexistence of England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland is sometimes discussed in similar terms. In 2022, Chile's Constitutional Convention proposed defining Chile as a plurinational state, but the draft constitution was rejected in a national plebiscite that September.

Criticism

Critics argue that plurinational constitutionalism can remain symbolic if power and resources stay centralised, that overlapping jurisdictions (for example in indigenous justice) create legal uncertainty, and that ethnic definitions of nationhood may sit uneasily with liberal equality and individual rights. Supporters respond that plurinationalism is a decolonising correction to a fictitious national homogeneity and a pragmatic recognition of how these societies actually are.
