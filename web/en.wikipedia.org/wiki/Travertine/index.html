Travertine is a form of terrestrial limestone deposited around mineral springs, especially hot springs, and in streams. It is formed by the rapid precipitation of calcium carbonate as mineral-rich water loses carbon dioxide on reaching the surface. Travertine often has a fibrous or concentric appearance and ranges from white through cream to rusty brown, with a characteristic pitted surface of small holes left by escaping gas and decayed vegetation.

Formation

Water percolating through limestone dissolves calcium carbonate under pressure in the presence of carbon dioxide. When the water emerges at a spring or cascades over a fall, the pressure drops, CO₂ escapes, and calcite precipitates rapidly — fast enough to encrust plants, bridges and other objects, and to build terraced pools such as those at Pamukkale in Turkey and the Mammoth Hot Springs at Yellowstone. Related deposits formed in caves are commonly called tufa and flowstone; terminology varies between authors.

Uses

Travertine has been a building material since Roman times: the Colosseum in Rome was built largely of travertine quarried at Tivoli (the source of the name, from lapis tiburtinus, "stone of Tibur"). Because it is easily cut when fresh from the quarry and hardens on exposure, it remains widely used for façades, flooring, paving and bathroom tiles, usually with its pores filled or left open ("unfilled") for a rustic finish. The Getty Center in Los Angeles is clad in roughly a million square feet of Italian travertine.
