The second voyage of HMS Beagle, from 27 December 1831 to 2 October 1836, was the second survey expedition of HMS Beagle, captained by Robert FitzRoy. The ship carried the young naturalist Charles Darwin, whose observations, collections, and geological and biological theorizing during the voyage formed the foundation of his later theory of evolution by natural selection.

Purpose and preparation

The Admiralty commissioned the voyage to complete the hydrographic survey of Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego begun on the first Beagle expedition, to chart the coasts of Chile, Peru, and some Pacific islands, and to carry a chain of chronometric measurements around the world. FitzRoy refitted the ten-gun brig extensively, and, seeking a gentleman companion with scientific interests, recruited Darwin, a 22-year-old Cambridge graduate, as an unpaid naturalist.

The voyage

After delays, Beagle sailed from Plymouth on 27 December 1831, calling at Tenerife, Cape Verde, and Fernando de Noronha before reaching Brazil. The expedition spent more than three years surveying South America: Darwin rode with gauchos in Argentina, witnessed the 1835 Concepción earthquake in Chile, and collected fossils including giant ground sloths and glyptodonts. In September–October 1835 the ship surveyed the Galápagos Islands, where Darwin noted differences among mockingbirds and tortoises between islands. Beagle then crossed the Pacific to Tahiti, New Zealand, and Australia, rounded the Cape of Good Hope, and returned to Falmouth on 2 October 1836.

Results

The surveys produced charts used for over a century. Darwin published his Journal of Researches (1839, later The Voyage of the Beagle), geological volumes on coral reefs, volcanic islands, and South American geology, and the multi-volume Zoology of the Voyage of H.M.S. Beagle. The specimens he brought back — including the Galápagos finches later analysed by John Gould — were central evidence in his development of evolutionary theory, published in On the Origin of Species in 1859.
