The administrative divisions of Japan are structured in two principal tiers below the national government: 47 prefectures and, beneath them, municipalities. Japan is a unitary state, but the 1947 Constitution and the Local Autonomy Law grant prefectures and municipalities elected assemblies and chief executives (governors and mayors) and substantial responsibilities in education, welfare, infrastructure and public safety.

Prefectures

The 47 prefectures (都道府県, todōfuken) comprise one metropolis (to: Tokyo), one circuit or territory (dō: Hokkaidō), two urban prefectures (fu: Osaka and Kyoto) and 43 rural prefectures (ken). They replaced the feudal domains (han) abolished in 1871 (haihan-chiken) and reached their present number in 1888. Each prefecture is headed by a directly elected governor and a unicameral assembly. Prefectures are conventionally grouped into eight regions: Hokkaidō, Tōhoku, Kantō, Chūbu, Kansai (Kinki), Chūgoku, Shikoku and Kyūshū (including Okinawa).

Municipalities

At the local level Japan has cities (shi), towns (machi/chō), and villages (mura/son); great waves of municipal mergers, most recently the "Great Heisei mergers" of the 2000s, reduced their number from over 3,200 to roughly 1,700. Twenty large cities are designated cities (seirei shitei toshi) with ward (ku) subdivisions and powers comparable to prefectures in many fields. Tokyo's 23 special wards (tokubetsu-ku) function as municipalities within the metropolis. Rural districts (gun) survive mainly as addressing units.
