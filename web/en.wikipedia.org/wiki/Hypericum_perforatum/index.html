Hypericum perforatum

Hypericum perforatum, commonly known as St John's wort, is a flowering plant in the family Hypericaceae. It is a perennial herb native to Europe and Asia, now widely naturalised elsewhere, and regarded as an invasive weed in some regions.

Description

The plant grows 30–100 cm tall with opposite, stalkless leaves marked by translucent dots — the "perforations" of its species name — and bright yellow five-petalled flowers that bloom around the summer solstice, traditionally gathered on St John's Day (24 June), which gives the plant its common name. When bruised, the flowers release a red pigment containing hypericin.

Traditional and medical use

St John's wort has a long history in folk medicine for wounds and mood disorders. Clinical studies indicate that standardised extracts may be effective for mild to moderate depression, comparable in some trials to conventional antidepressants, though results are mixed and product quality varies. Important active constituents include hypericin and hyperforin.

Interactions and safety

The herb is a potent inducer of cytochrome P450 enzymes, notably CYP3A4, and can markedly reduce the effectiveness of many prescription drugs, including immunosuppressants, anticoagulants, oral contraceptives and some HIV and cancer medicines. Combined with serotonergic antidepressants it may contribute to serotonin syndrome, and it can cause photosensitivity. As a pasture weed it is poisonous to livestock, causing photosensitisation.
