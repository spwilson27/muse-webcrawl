Aggressive mimicry

Aggressive mimicry is a form of mimicry in which predators, parasites, or parasitoids share signals with a harmless or attractive model, allowing them to approach or lure prey that would otherwise avoid them. Unlike defensive mimicry—such as a harmless species imitating a dangerous one—aggressive mimicry benefits the mimic by increasing its access to food or hosts. The term was developed by the German naturalist Wolfgang Wickler. The phenomenon is sometimes summarised as a "wolf in sheep's clothing" strategy.

Mechanisms

Aggressive mimics may copy the appearance, sounds, pheromones, or behaviour of a model that their prey finds harmless, attractive, or desirable. Strategies include imitating prey of the victim (as when a predator imitates the victim's prey), imitating a mate or social partner, imitating a mutualist such as a cleaner fish, and producing lures that resemble food.

Examples

  • Anglerfish dangle a bioluminescent or fleshy lure (the esca) resembling small prey, drawing other fish within striking range.
  • Alligator snapping turtles wiggle a worm-like projection on the tongue to lure fish into their open jaws.
  • Female Photuris fireflies ("femmes fatales") imitate the mating flash responses of Photinus females to attract and eat the males.
  • Bolas spiders synthesise moth sex pheromones and swing a sticky silk ball to snare male moths drawn to the scent.
  • The false cleanerfish (Aspidontus taeniatus) mimics the cleaner wrasse, gaining access to client fish, which it bites instead of cleaning.
  • Zone-tailed hawks resemble and fly with turkey vultures, which prey animals ignore, allowing the hawk to approach unnoticed.
  • Some orchids and pitcher plants use deceptive signals to attract pollinators or prey insects without reward.

Cleaner-fish mimicry shades into parasitism: brood-parasitic cuckoos and cuckoo catfish similarly exploit the behaviour of hosts through egg mimicry and deceptive signals.

Evolution

Aggressive mimicry evolves when prey cannot easily distinguish the mimic from the model without losing the benefit of responding to the genuine signal—such as mating opportunities or cleaning services. The mimic must usually remain relatively rare compared with the model, or prey would evolve or learn to distrust the signal.
