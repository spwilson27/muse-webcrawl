A war pigeon is a homing pigeon trained to carry messages for military purposes. Because homing pigeons reliably return to their home loft over long distances, armies used them to transmit orders, maps, and reports between headquarters and front-line units, often under conditions where radio, telegraph, or human runners were impractical or compromised.

History

The military use of pigeons dates to antiquity: the Egyptians and Persians used pigeon posts, and Julius Caesar reportedly employed pigeons in the conquest of Gaul. During the Siege of Paris (1870–71), French balloonists carried pigeons out of the encircled city, and the birds returned bearing microfilmed messages — some 150,000 official and a million private dispatches in total.

In the First World War, both sides maintained large pigeon services. The British Army's carrier pigeon service eventually operated tens of thousands of birds, and mobile lofts mounted on buses and trucks followed the front. Aircraft, tanks, and warships also carried pigeons as emergency messengers.

Famous birds

Cher Ami, a hen of the U.S. Army Signal Corps, delivered a message in October 1918 that helped rescue the "Lost Battalion" of the 77th Division in the Argonne Forest, despite being shot and losing a leg; she received the Croix de Guerre. G.I. Joe, a U.S. Army pigeon, flew 20 miles in 20 minutes in October 1943 to deliver a message that stopped the bombing of Calvi Vecchia, Italy, where British troops had already taken the village. In Britain, 32 pigeons received the Dickin Medal, the animals' equivalent of the Victoria Cross.

Later use and decline

Pigeons remained in service through the Second World War and the Korean War, valued because they were silent, hard to intercept, and immune to radio jamming. Switzerland maintained a military pigeon service until 1996. Advances in radio and satellite communication finally ended the role, but war pigeons are commemorated in memorials in London, Lille, and elsewhere.
