Offal

Offal is the collective term for the internal organs and entrails of a butchered animal. Also called variety meats, pluck, or organ meats, offal may be consumed directly as food or used as an ingredient in dishes. Some offal dishes — such as pâté, foie gras, and haggis — are considered delicacies, while in other culinary cultures organ meats are everyday staples. The word derives from Middle English "off-fall", the pieces that fall away during butchering.

Types

Common types of offal include the liver, kidneys, heart, tongue, brain, tripe (stomach lining), sweetbreads (thymus and pancreas), oxtail, trotters, blood, and intestines (used both as food and as sausage casings). Because organs are highly perishable and nutrient-dense, they require careful handling and prompt cooking.

Culinary traditions

Offal features prominently in many cuisines: steak and kidney pie and devilled kidneys in Britain; tripes à la mode de Caen and andouillette in France; lampredotto in Florence; kokoreç in Turkey; menudo in Mexico; dinuguan in the Philippines; and liver, gizzards, and chitterlings in the American South. Scottish haggis, made from sheep's pluck minced with oatmeal and suet and traditionally cooked in the animal's stomach, is Scotland's national dish.

Nutrition and safety

Organ meats are rich in protein, iron, B vitamins (especially B12), and fat-soluble vitamins, but liver is extremely high in vitamin A and many organs are high in cholesterol and purines. Brains carry a risk of prion disease and are restricted in some countries following the BSE crisis.
