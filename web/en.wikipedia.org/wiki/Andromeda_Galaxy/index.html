The Andromeda Galaxy is a barred spiral galaxy and the nearest major galaxy to the Milky Way. It is about 2.5 million light-years from Earth and the largest galaxy in the Local Group, which also contains the Milky Way, the Triangulum Galaxy and about 80 smaller galaxies. It is named after the constellation Andromeda, itself named for the mythological princess.

Visible as a faint, elongated smudge to the naked eye from dark sites, Andromeda was long catalogued as a nebula (Messier 31). In the 1920s Edwin Hubble identified Cepheid variable stars in it, proving it lies far beyond the Milky Way and ending the "Great Debate" over the scale of the universe — observations discussed in every science history since, and refined by telescopes operated by NASA and ESA.

Future collision with the Milky Way

Andromeda is approaching the Milky Way at about 110 km per second (its light is blueshifted). Simulations indicate the two galaxies will collide in roughly 4–5 billion years, their stars mostly passing between each other while gas clouds collide and trigger starbursts, eventually merging into a single elliptical galaxy sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda". The Solar System is expected to survive, likely ending up farther from the new galaxy's centre.

Structure

Andromeda has a double nucleus appearance, a central supermassive black hole of roughly 100 million solar masses, prominent dust lanes, and satellite galaxies M32 and M110 visible in amateur telescopes. Its halo shows streams of stars from digested dwarf galaxies — the same process by which the Milky Way grew.
