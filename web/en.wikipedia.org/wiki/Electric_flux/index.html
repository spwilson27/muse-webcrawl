In electromagnetism, electric flux is the measure of the electric field through a given surface. It is proportional to the number of electric field lines going through the surface: flux is maximal when the surface is perpendicular to the field and zero when parallel. Formally, the flux through a surface S is the surface integral ΦE = ∬S E · dA, where E is the electric field and dA is a vector area element directed along the surface normal.

Gauss's law

For a closed surface, Gauss's law states that the total outward electric flux equals the enclosed electric charge divided by the permittivity of free space: ΦE = Qenc/ε0. This holds regardless of the shape of the surface or the distribution of charge outside it, and is one of Maxwell's equations. It makes flux calculations for symmetric charge distributions — spheres, cylinders, planes — straightforward.

Units and related quantities

The SI unit of electric flux is the volt-metre (V·m), equivalently the newton-metre squared per coulomb (N·m²/C). The flux of the electric displacement field D through a closed surface equals the enclosed free charge, a form of Gauss's law often more convenient in materials. Electric flux should not be confused with flux linkage in inductors, which concerns magnetic flux through a coil.

Intuition

The concept descends from Michael Faraday's picture of lines of force: the density of lines represents field strength, so the number of lines through a surface represents flux. For a point charge, field lines radiate uniformly, so flux through any enclosing surface is the same — the essence of Gauss's law and the inverse-square law.
