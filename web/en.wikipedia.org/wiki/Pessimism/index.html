Philosophical pessimism

Philosophical pessimism
FieldPhilosophy
Key figuresArthur Schopenhauer; Giacomo Leopardi; Emil Cioran
Core claimLife involves unavoidable suffering that outweighs its goods; existence may be worse than non-existence
[Image: A lone figure seated in a bleak landscape under grey clouds]

Philosophical pessimism is the view that life is predominantly bad — that suffering is structural and outweighs happiness, and in strong forms that it would have been better never to exist. Its classical system is Arthur Schopenhauer's: the world as blind striving Will condemns beings to want, suffer, and bore themselves in alternation, with salvation only in denying the will. Leopardi gave pessimism poetic form in Italy.

Modern forms

Contemporary pessimism includes David Benatar's anti-natalism (Better Never to Have Been, 2006), arguing coming into existence is always a harm, and debates with optimists over progress data (Pinker) and over whether pessimism is a judgement or a temperament. Buddhist diagnoses of dukkha are a convergent ancient parallel.
