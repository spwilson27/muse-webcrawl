Ascanius (also called Iulus) is a figure in Roman mythology, the son of the Trojan prince Aeneas and his wife Creusa. After the fall of Troy he fled with his father to Italy, where he became the founder and first king of Alba Longa, the mother-city of Rome. Through the name Iulus, the Julian gens of Rome — including Julius Caesar and the emperor Augustus — claimed descent from Ascanius and hence from Aeneas's mother, the goddess Venus.

In the Aeneid

In Virgil's Aeneid, Ascanius is a child during the fall of Troy; a miraculous flame plays harmlessly around his head, a portent of his future greatness. In Italy, now a youth, he hunts and kills the pet stag of Silvia, an incident that helps ignite the war between the Trojans and the Latins of Turnus. He is kept from the worst of the fighting by Apollo but kills the Rutulian Remulus, taunting him before the shot. At the end of the poem he is on the threshold of manhood, destined to found Alba Longa thirty years after Lavinium.

Alba Longa and the Alban kings

According to Livy and other Roman historians, after Aeneas's death Ascanius succeeded him, left Lavinium to his stepmother Lavinia and half-brother Silvius (in some versions), and founded Alba Longa in the Alban Hills. The line of Alban kings descending from him — the Silvii — filled the gap between Aeneas and Romulus, ending with Numitor and Amulius, the grandfather and great-uncle of Rome's founder. Some traditions instead made Romulus a direct descendant of Ascanius.

Iulus and the Julii

The identification of Ascanius with Iulus appears to have been promoted by the family of Julius Caesar. In the Aeneid, Jupiter's prophecy to Venus already plays on the name: "the boy Ascanius, to whom now the surname Iulus is added." The claim tied the Julii to Venus Genetrix, in whose honour Caesar built a temple in his forum.
