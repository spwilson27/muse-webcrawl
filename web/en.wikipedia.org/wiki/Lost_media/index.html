Lost media

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lost media is any media — including broadcasts, films, recordings, video games, literature and digital content — that is believed to have once existed but is no longer known to survive in any form, or for which no complete copy is publicly available. The term is also used for the online communities dedicated to searching for, documenting and sometimes recovering such material.

Causes of loss

Media becomes lost for many reasons. Early television was often broadcast live and recorded only on reusable videotape, which broadcasters wiped to save costs — a practice that destroyed much of the BBC's 1960s output, including episodes of Doctor Who, some of which were later recovered from overseas film prints. Nitrate film from the silent era decomposed or burned; it is estimated that the majority of American silent feature films are lost. Digital media can vanish through the shutdown of websites and services, format obsolescence, or the failure of storage media.

Famous examples

Well-known lost media includes the 1927 Fritz Lang cut of Metropolis (partially recovered in 2008), Lon Chaney's London After Midnight (1927), the first Super Bowl broadcast tapes, the "lost episodes" sought by fans of various animated series, and unreleased works such as Jerry Lewis's The Day the Clown Cried. Some searches end in success: lost Doctor Who episodes, the 1910 film Frankenstein, and numerous early recordings have been rediscovered in archives, private collections and foreign holdings.

Search communities

Since the 2010s, collaborative internet communities — notably those organised around the Lost Media Wiki, founded in 2012 — have documented thousands of lost works and coordinated searches, sometimes locating material in broadcasters' archives or among collectors. Preservationists caution that copyright law and the reluctance of rights holders can hinder recovery even when copies survive.
