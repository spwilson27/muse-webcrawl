Producer gas

Producer gas (typical)
Composition~25% CO, ~15% H2, ~50–55% N2, small amounts of CO2 and CH4
Energy content~4–6 MJ/m3 (low-Btu gas)
FeedstockCoal, coke, wood, charcoal, biomass
ProcessPartial combustion with air (gasification)

Producer gas is a fuel gas manufactured by blowing air (sometimes with steam) through a bed of incandescent carbonaceous fuel such as coal, coke or wood in a reactor called a gas producer or gasifier. The resulting mixture — mainly carbon monoxide and hydrogen diluted with nitrogen from the air — is a low-calorific-value gas that can be burned in furnaces or used to power internal combustion engines.

Chemistry

In the hot fuel bed, carbon is partially oxidised: C + ½O2 → CO. In the reduction zone, carbon dioxide and steam react with more carbon to form additional CO and hydrogen (the water-gas reaction). Because air rather than pure oxygen is used, roughly half the product is inert nitrogen, limiting the heating value.

History and uses

Producer gas was widely used from the late 19th century to power industrial furnaces, gas engines and early power stations. During the Second World War, petrol shortages led to hundreds of thousands of cars, trucks and buses in Europe and elsewhere being fitted with wood- or charcoal-burning gas producers ("wood gas" vehicles). Interest revived during the 1970s oil crises and continues in small-scale biomass gasification for rural electrification and combined heat and power.

Drawbacks

The gas is toxic because of its carbon monoxide content, carries tars that require cleaning for engine use, and delivers less power than petrol because of its low energy density.
