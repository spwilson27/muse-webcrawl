Land (economics)

In economics, land comprises all naturally occurring resources as well as geographic land itself, whose supply is inherently fixed. Examples of land resources include the ground and soil, forests, fisheries, deposits of minerals and fossil fuels, water, and even airspace and electromagnetic spectrum. Land is one of the classical factors of production, alongside labour and capital. Its owners receive rent as their factor income. Because land is not produced, classical economists — and later Henry George — argued that its rent has a special character and can be taxed with unusually little distortion.

Classical treatment

The Physiocrats of 18th-century France held land to be the sole source of wealth, since agriculture produced a surplus (produit net) over its costs. Adam Smith and, most rigorously, David Ricardo made land one of three great factors. Ricardo defined rent as payment "for the use of the original and indestructible powers of the soil" and developed the theory of differential rent: as population grows, cultivation extends to inferior land, and the more fertile plots earn rent equal to their advantage over the marginal, no-rent land. John Stuart Mill and others noted that much apparent land value is really a return on improvements, which behave like capital.

Georgism and land value tax

Henry George's Progress and Poverty (1879) argued that because land value arises from community growth and nature rather than the owner's effort, rent should be captured for public purposes through a land value tax — the "single tax". Because the supply of land is fixed, taxing its rental value does not discourage its production, an efficiency argument many modern economists endorse. Variants of land value taxation have been applied in places such as Denmark, Estonia, parts of Australia, and Pennsylvania's split-rate cities.

Modern usage

Neoclassical economics folded land into capital as one asset among others, though the distinction is retained in resource and environmental economics, urban economics (where location rent models descend from von Thünen and Alonso), and national accounting, where land is a non-produced asset on balance sheets. In urban economics, the bid-rent theory explains land prices by accessibility and location. Debates over land reform, enclosure, indigenous land rights, and the economics of housing all descend from the classical treatment of land as a fixed, non-reproducible factor.
