Constructivism (mathematics)

In the philosophy of mathematics, constructivism asserts that it is necessary to find (or "construct") a specific example of a mathematical object in order to prove that an example exists. By contrast, classical mathematics asserts the existence of an object merely by refuting its non-existence: under the law of excluded middle, "not (for all x, not P(x))" is equivalent to "there exists x with P(x)". A constructivist finds the former argument unconvincing, since it may give no way to exhibit such an x.

The constructive interpretation of logic

Under the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation, a proof of an existential statement consists of an explicit witness together with a proof that the witness satisfies the stated property; a proof of a disjunction consists of a proof of one of the disjuncts, together with a record of which one; and a proof of an implication is an algorithm converting proofs of the antecedent into proofs of the consequent. Consequently, intuitionistic logic — the logic of constructive reasoning — rejects double-negation elimination and proof by contradiction for existence claims.

Examples

A famous non-constructive proof shows that there exist irrational numbers a and b with ab rational, by considering √2√2 and reasoning by cases without deciding which case holds. A constructivist either supplies explicit numbers or refuses to accept the argument as a proof of existence. Many classical theorems have constructive counterparts, though sometimes with additional hypotheses, as in constructive analysis.

Schools

Important varieties include Brouwer's intuitionism, the recursive constructivism of Markov and the Russian school, and Errett Bishop's programme of constructive analysis, which is compatible with classical mathematics in the sense that removing the computational content of its proofs recovers classical theorems. Because constructive proofs have algorithmic content, constructivism connects closely with type theory, the Curry–Howard correspondence, and proof assistants such as Coq and Agda.

Attitudes

Many mathematicians regard constructive requirements as an unnecessary restriction, and David Hilbert famously protested that forbidding excluded middle was like "forbidding the boxer the use of his fists." Constructivists reply that constructive theorems carry strictly more information, and that every constructive proof is also classically valid.
