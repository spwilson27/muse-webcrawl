In archaeology, seriation is a relative dating method in which artifacts or assemblages from numerous sites in the same culture are placed in chronological order. It rests on the observation that styles of artifacts change over time in patterned ways, and that assemblages with similar styles are likely to be close in age.

History

The method was pioneered by the Egyptologist Flinders Petrie in the 1890s, who sequenced predynastic Egyptian graves at Diospolis Parva by the changing forms of their pottery ("sequence dating"). In the Americas, Alfred Kroeber's analysis of Zuni pottery and the stratigraphic work of Nels Nelson and Alfred Kidder validated the principle. James A. Ford developed frequency seriation for the Lower Mississippi Valley in the mid-twentieth century, and Albert Spaulding promoted statistical approaches to ordering.

Types

Contextual (occurrence) seriation orders assemblages by the presence or absence of artifact types, assuming types have limited duration. Frequency seriation uses the relative proportions of types: a style is assumed to appear, grow in popularity, peak, and decline, producing unimodal "battleship curves" when the assemblages are correctly ordered. Computer-assisted techniques such as correspondence analysis are now commonly used to find orderings that best fit these assumptions.

Assumptions and limits

Seriation assumes that the assemblages come from the same cultural tradition, cover comparable spans of time, and that stylistic change is gradual and unidirectional. It yields relative, not absolute, chronologies, which must be anchored by other dating methods. Despite these limits, seriation remains a core tool for building regional chronologies, especially for ceramic sequences.
