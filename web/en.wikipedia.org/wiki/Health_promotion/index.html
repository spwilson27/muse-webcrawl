Health promotion

Health promotion is, as defined by the World Health Organization in the Ottawa Charter of 1986, "the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health." It moves beyond a focus on individual behaviour toward a wide range of social and environmental interventions, addressing the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age.

The Ottawa Charter

The WHO's First International Conference on Health Promotion, held in Ottawa, Canada, in 1986, produced the Ottawa Charter, the foundational document of the field. It identified five action areas:

  • Build healthy public policy
  • Create supportive environments
  • Strengthen community action
  • Develop personal skills
  • Reorient health services toward prevention

The Charter also named prerequisites for health, including peace, shelter, education, food, income, a stable ecosystem, sustainable resources, social justice and equity.

Approaches

Health promotion strategies include health education campaigns (such as anti-smoking and vaccination promotion), legislation and fiscal measures (tobacco taxes, seat-belt laws, food labelling), community development, and "settings-based" approaches such as health-promoting schools, healthy cities and health-promoting hospitals. The Lalonde Report of 1974 in Canada is often credited as an early landmark, arguing that healthcare services were only one of several determinants of population health, alongside lifestyle, environment and human biology.

Workplace and digital health promotion

Employers run workplace wellness programmes covering physical activity, mental health and screening, though evidence for their return on investment is mixed. Since the 2010s, digital tools — apps, wearables and social media campaigns — have become major channels for health promotion, raising new questions about equity, privacy and misinformation.

Criticism

Critics argue that health promotion can drift into "healthism" — moralising about lifestyle and blaming individuals for socially produced ill health — and that effective promotion requires tackling structural determinants such as poverty and housing rather than exhorting behaviour change.
