Isopsephy

Isopsephy (from Greek isos, "equal", and psephos, "pebble") is the ancient Greek practice of assigning numerical values to the letters of a word and summing them, so that words or phrases with equal totals were considered mystically linked. It is the Greek counterpart of the Hebrew gematria.

System

Isopsephy uses the Milesian (Ionic) numeral system, in which the 24 letters of the Greek alphabet plus three archaic letters represent units (α=1 … θ=9), tens (ι=10 … ϟ=90) and hundreds (ρ=100 … ϡ=900).

Examples and history

Graffiti at Pompeii records a lover's devotion in numerical form ("I love her whose number is 545"). Suetonius reports a lampoon on Nero noting that "Nero" and "killed his own mother" have the same isopsephic value in Greek (1337). Early Christians applied the technique to religious phrases, and the "number of the beast", 666 in the Book of Revelation, is generally interpreted as a Hebrew-alphabet (gematria) calculation for "Nero Caesar". Isopsephy remained popular in Byzantine and early modern numerology and wordplay.
