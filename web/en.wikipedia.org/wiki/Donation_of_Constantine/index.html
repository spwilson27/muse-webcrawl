Donation of Constantine

The Donation of Constantine (Latin: Donatio Constantini) is a forged Roman imperial decree by which the 4th-century emperor Constantine the Great supposedly transferred authority over Rome and the western part of the Roman Empire to Pope Sylvester I and his successors. The document probably dates to the 8th century and was later incorporated into the collection of canon law known as the Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals. It was used by medieval popes to bolster claims to temporal power in Italy and beyond, and underpinned the papal claim to the territories that became the Papal States.

Content

The document purports to record that Constantine, having been miraculously cured of leprosy through baptism by Sylvester, granted the pope primacy over the other patriarchal sees, imperial regalia including the Lateran Palace, and dominion over Rome, Italy, and the western provinces, before withdrawing to the east to found Constantinople so as not to diminish the pope's authority.

Exposure as a forgery

Doubts about its authenticity were raised in the Middle Ages, notably by Nicholas of Cusa and Reginald Pecock. In 1440 the humanist scholar Lorenzo Valla definitively demonstrated, in On the Donation of Constantine, that the document could not have been written in the 4th century: its Latin contains anachronisms, it refers to Constantinople as a patriarchate before the city was founded, mentions satraps as Roman officials, and uses terms and geography impossible for Constantine's era. Valla's philological analysis is regarded as a landmark of Renaissance textual criticism.

Legacy

Although the forgery was exposed, the Papal States endured until 1870. The Donation remains a classic example of a medieval forgery with enormous political consequences, and Valla's treatise an early triumph of historical method.
