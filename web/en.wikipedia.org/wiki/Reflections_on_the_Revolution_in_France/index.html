Reflections on the Revolution in France


Author: Edmund Burke
Published: 1 November 1790

Reflections on the Revolution in France is a political pamphlet written by the Anglo-Irish statesman and philosopher Edmund Burke and published in November 1790. It is one of the best-known intellectual attacks against the French Revolution, and a foundational text of modern conservatism.

Content

The work took the form of a letter to a French correspondent, Charles-Jean-François Depont. Burke defended inherited institutions, gradual reform, prejudice (in the sense of pre-judgement embedded in tradition), and the "entailed inheritance" of rights, against what he saw as the abstract rationalism of the revolutionaries and their attempts to rebuild society from first principles. He argued that society is a partnership "between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born." Famously, Burke predicted early—before the Reign of Terror—that the revolution would descend into violence and end in military dictatorship.

Reception

The pamphlet was an immediate bestseller and ignited the "Revolution controversy" in Britain. Thomas Paine's Rights of Man (1791) and Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Men (1790) were written in direct reply. Burke's defence of Marie Antoinette and his evocation of a lost age of chivalry became some of the most quoted passages in English political writing.
