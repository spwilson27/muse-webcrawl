Pre-established harmony

Pre-established harmony (German: prästabilierte Harmonie) is a philosophical theory of causation propounded by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz according to which every substance in the universe affects only itself, yet all substances appear to interact because God created them in perfect correspondence with one another from the beginning.

The doctrine

In Leibniz's mature metaphysics, the fundamental substances are monads — simple, "windowless" entities that cannot causally influence each other. The apparent interaction between mind and body, and between bodies themselves, is explained by God's pre-arrangement: each monad unfolds its states according to its own internal law, yet in such perfect synchrony with all the others that their histories mutually correspond. Leibniz compared the relation of soul and body to two clocks that keep perfect time: not because one moves the other (interactionism), nor because a watchmaker constantly adjusts them (occasionalism), but because they were constructed with such art that each follows its own mechanism while always agreeing.

Context and rivals

Leibniz set out the theory in his New System of Nature (1695) and popular writings such as the Monadology (1714), partly as an answer to the mind–body problem left by Descartes. It is one of three classical solutions Leibniz distinguished: physical influx (real interaction), occasionalism (divine intervention on each occasion, as in Malebranche), and his own pre-established harmony.

Criticism

Voltaire satirised Leibnizian optimism and harmony in Candide (1759). Philosophers have objected that the doctrine makes genuine causal relations illusory and leans heavily on theological premises; others defend it as a strikingly early expression of ideas about correspondence without interaction. The phrase also acquired wider currency as a metaphor for coordinated action without direct communication.
