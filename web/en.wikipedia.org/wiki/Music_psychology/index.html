Music psychology

Music psychology, or the psychology of music, may be regarded as a branch of both psychology and musicology. It aims to explain and understand musical behaviour and musical experience, including the processes through which music is perceived, created, responded to, and incorporated into everyday life. Modern music psychology is mainly empirical; its knowledge tends to advance on the basis of interpretations of data collected by systematic observation of and interaction with human participants. Music psychology is a field of research with practical relevance for many areas, including music performance, composition, education, criticism, and therapy, as well as investigations of human attitudes, skill, performance, intelligence, creativity, and social behaviour.

History

Before the 19th century, musical thought was centred on the mathematical relationships of tones, from Pythagoras onward. Scientific study of musical experience began in earnest in the 19th century with Hermann von Helmholtz's On the Sensations of Tone (1863) and Carl Stumpf's work on tonal fusion, while Carl Seashore produced the first standardised tests of musical aptitude. The field was consolidated in the later 20th century with Leonard B. Meyer's work on expectation, John Sloboda's The Musical Mind (1985), and the growth of music cognition research drawing on cognitive science and neuroscience.

Research areas

  • Perception and cognition: how listeners perceive pitch, melody, rhythm, harmony and timbre, and how musical expectations are formed.
  • Music and emotion: how music expresses and induces emotion.
  • Performance: the psychology of musical performance, including practice, sight-reading, improvisation and performance anxiety.
  • Development and education: how musical abilities develop across the lifespan and the effects of musical training.
  • Preferences and personality: individual differences in musical taste, and the social functions of music in everyday life.
  • Music therapy and health: applications of music in clinical and wellbeing contexts.
