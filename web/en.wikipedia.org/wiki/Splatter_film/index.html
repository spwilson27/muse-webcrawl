Splatter film

A splatter film is a subgenre of horror film that deliberately focuses on graphic portrayals of gore and violence, achieved through special effects, focusing on the vulnerability of the human body and the theatricality of its mutilation. The term was coined by filmmaker George A. Romero to describe his 1978 film Dawn of the Dead. Splatter films are distinguished from other horror subgenres by their emphasis on spectacle over narrative, often featuring inventive, excessive, and sometimes comic displays of bloodshed.

History

The splatter film emerged from the exploitation film circuit of the 1960s. Herschell Gordon Lewis, seeking a niche unexploited by mainstream cinema, made Blood Feast (1963) and Two Thousand Maniacs! (1964), films remembered as the first "gore films". In the 1970s the genre matured with Romero's Dawn of the Dead (1978), whose effects artist Tom Savini brought realistic battlefield-inspired gore to the screen, and Italian directors such as Lucio Fulci and Umberto Lenzi pushed explicitness further. Peter Jackson's early New Zealand "splatstick" comedies, Bad Taste (1987) and Braindead (1992), fused slapstick with record quantities of stage blood.

Characteristics

Splatter films centre on elaborate practical effects: prosthetic wounds, fake blood in copious quantities, and creatively staged dismemberment. Plot and characterisation are often secondary to set pieces of escalating gore. The subgenre overlaps with the slasher film but differs in intent: where slashers build suspense around the threat of violence, splatter films dwell on the act and its aftermath as spectacle, and frequently leaven it with black humour.

Controversy and influence

The genre has repeatedly drawn censorship battles, notably Britain's "video nasties" panic of the 1980s, in which many splatter titles were prosecuted or banned. Critics have debated whether splatter is misogynistic exploitation or a form of transgressive art that confronts audiences with bodily reality. Its influence persists in modern "extreme cinema", video games, and the practical-effects revival in independent horror.
