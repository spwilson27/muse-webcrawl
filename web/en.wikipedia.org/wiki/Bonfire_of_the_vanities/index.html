Bonfire of the vanities

A bonfire of the vanities (Italian: falò delle vanità) is a burning of objects condemned by religious authorities as occasions of sin. The phrase is most strongly associated with the Dominican friar Girolamo Savonarola and his followers in Renaissance Florence, who burned books, works of art, musical instruments, cosmetics, mirrors, fine clothing and other "vanities" in the city's Piazza della Signoria — most famously on 7 February 1497.

Background

Burnings of gaming tables, carnival masks and other objects had precedents in the campaigns of earlier preachers such as Bernardino of Siena. In Florence in the 1490s, Savonarola's fiery sermons against the corruption of the Church, the Medici regime and Renaissance luxury won a mass following after the expulsion of the Medici in 1494. During Carnival 1497 his supporters, including processions of children, collected "vanities" door to door and built them into a great pyramid in the piazza — some accounts describe mirrors, wigs, perfumes, playing cards, secular books (including works of Boccaccio and Petrarch), paintings and sculptures — which was set alight.

Art losses

The extent of artistic destruction is debated. Vasari reported that the painter Lorenzo di Credi brought his works, and Sandro Botticelli was said (probably with exaggeration) to have thrown some of his own paintings into the flames. Modern historians caution that the bonfire consumed mostly luxury goods and objects of private devotion rather than major public art.

Aftermath and legacy

Within a year Savonarola's movement collapsed: he was excommunicated, arrested, tortured and hanged and burned in the same piazza on 23 May 1498. The phrase "bonfire of the vanities" has since become a general metaphor for ostentatious destruction of frivolous things, and was borrowed as the title of Tom Wolfe's 1987 novel The Bonfire of the Vanities about greed and vanity in 1980s New York.
