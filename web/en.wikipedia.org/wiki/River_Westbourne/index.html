The River Westbourne is a subterranean river in London. It rises in Hampstead and flows south through Kilburn, Westbourne Green, Bayswater, and Hyde Park—where it once fed the Serpentine—and then through Knightsbridge, Sloane Square, and Belgravia before entering the River Thames at Chelsea. Once known in stretches as the Bayswater, the Ranelagh Sewer, and other names, it is now carried in pipes and sewers and is one of the lost rivers of London.

Course

The Westbourne's headwater streams rose at Hampstead and on Whitestone Pond's slopes; united, they ran south-westwards down Kilburn (where the river was crossed by Watling Street at "Kilburn"—the cold burn or Cye's bourne) and through Westbourne Green, which takes its name from the river ("west bourne"). It flowed through Bayswater (named for "Bayard's watering place") and entered Hyde Park, where in 1730 Queen Caroline had it dammed to form the Serpentine. From the park it continued through Knightsbridge—whose name recalls a bridge over this river, scene of a recorded dispute between Londoners and Westminster men in 1141—then beneath Sloane Square station, where it still crosses in an iron pipe above the District line platforms, and on through Belgravia and Chelsea to the Thames near Chelsea Bridge and the Grosvenor Canal basin.

History

The river supplied drinking water to Westminster via conduits from the medieval period, and in the 17th and 18th centuries its cleaner reaches around Paddington were prized. As the districts it flowed through were built over, it degenerated into an open sewer and was progressively covered; by the 1850s it was wholly culverted and incorporated into Bazalgette's sewer system, and the Serpentine was re-fed from artesian and later Thames sources. The pipe over Sloane Square station and storm outfalls at Chelsea are the principal modern reminders of its existence.
