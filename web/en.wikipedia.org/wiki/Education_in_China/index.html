Education in China is primarily managed by the state-run public education system, overseen by the Ministry of Education. China operates the largest education system in the world, with roughly 290 million students. Since the Compulsory Education Law of 1986, all citizens must attend school for a minimum of nine years — six years of primary school and three years of junior secondary school — funded by the government.

Structure

The system comprises preschool kindergartens (ages 3–6), primary schools, junior middle schools, and senior secondary schools, which are divided into academic senior high schools and secondary vocational schools. Entry to senior high school depends on the zhongkao examination. Higher education has expanded massively since the late 1990s; China has more than 3,000 higher-education institutions, including the elite universities of the former Project 985 and Project 211 and the current "Double First-Class" initiative, such as Peking University and Tsinghua University. Gross tertiary enrolment has risen above 60 percent.

The gaokao

The centrepiece of academic selection is the gaokao, the National Higher Education Entrance Examination taken by more than 13 million students annually in recent years — the largest examination cohort in the world. Its results determine university admission and are widely regarded as life-defining, giving rise to intense study culture and a large private tutoring industry. In 2021 the government introduced the "double reduction" policy, banning for-profit tutoring in core school subjects to reduce student burden and family costs.

History and challenges

Modern schooling replaced the Confucian examination tradition after the imperial civil-service examinations were abolished in 1905. After 1949 the People's Republic built a Soviet-influenced system, expanded literacy from a low base to about 97 percent, and saw universities disrupted during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) before reforms under Deng Xiaoping restored examinations and opened the system. Persistent challenges include the urban–rural quality gap, the effects of the hukou household-registration system on migrant children, examination pressure and graduate employment.
