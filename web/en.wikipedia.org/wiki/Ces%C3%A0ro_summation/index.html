Cesàro summation

The partial sums of Grandi's series and their running averages, converging to 1/2.

In mathematical analysis, Cesàro summation assigns values to some infinite sums that are not necessarily convergent in the usual sense. It is named after the Italian mathematician Ernesto Cesàro (1859–1906), who introduced the method in the late 19th century. A series is called Cesàro summable if the arithmetic means of its partial sums converge; the limit of those means is then taken as its Cesàro sum.

Definition

Let sn = a1 + a2 + … + an be the partial sums of a series. The series is Cesàro summable to the limit A if the average of the first n partial sums, (s1 + … + sn) / n, tends to A as n → ∞. Every convergent series is Cesàro summable to the same value, so Cesàro summation is a regular summation method, but the method also assigns sums to some divergent series.

Examples

The classic example is Grandi's series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ⋯, whose partial sums alternate between 1 and 0. Their running averages tend to 1/2, so the series is Cesàro summable to 1/2, even though it does not converge in the ordinary sense. Higher-order versions of the method, called (C, α) summation, apply the averaging process repeatedly or with weights, allowing still more series to be summed; Cesàro means are closely related to Fejér's theorem on the convergence of Fourier series, where the Cesàro means of a Fourier series converge uniformly for continuous functions.
