Coordination complex

A coordination complex is a chemical compound consisting of a central atom or ion — usually a metal — bonded to a surrounding array of molecules or ions called ligands. The bonds between the central atom and the ligands are coordinate bonds, in which both electrons of each shared pair are formally donated by the ligand. Coordination complexes are the subject of coordination chemistry and include many biologically essential substances, such as haemoglobin, chlorophyll and vitamin B12, as well as industrial catalysts and pigments such as Prussian blue.

History

Complexes were known from the eighteenth century but resisted explanation by classical valence theory until Alfred Werner, in 1893, proposed that a metal centre possesses two kinds of valence: a primary valence (its oxidation state) and a secondary valence (its coordination number) directed toward fixed positions in space. Werner's model explained the isomerism of cobalt ammine chlorides such as CoCl3·6NH3 and earned him the 1913 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, founding modern coordination chemistry.

Structure and bonding

The number of ligand donor atoms attached to the central atom is its coordination number, most commonly 4 or 6, giving tetrahedral or square-planar geometries for four-coordinate complexes and octahedral geometry for six-coordinate ones. Ligands are classified by denticity: monodentate ligands such as ammonia, water and chloride bind through one atom; bidentate ligands such as ethylenediamine and oxalate bind through two; polydentate ligands that wrap around a metal are called chelating agents, and their complexes (chelates) are especially stable. Bonding is described successively by crystal field theory and ligand field theory, which explain the colours, magnetism and reactivity of complexes through the splitting of the metal's d-orbitals.

Nomenclature and isomerism

Complexes are named by listing ligands alphabetically before the metal, with prefixes (di-, tri-, tetra-, or bis-, tris- for complex ligand names) indicating ligand counts, and the oxidation state of the metal given as a Roman numeral; anionic complexes take the ending -ate. Coordination complexes display several kinds of isomerism: cis/trans and fac/mer geometrical isomerism, linkage isomerism (as when nitrite binds through N or O), ionisation isomerism, and optical isomerism in chiral octahedral complexes such as [Co(en)3]3+.

Applications

Coordination complexes underpin homogeneous catalysis (for example Wilkinson's catalyst for hydrogenation and the Ziegler–Natta catalysts for polymerisation), analytical chemistry (EDTA titrations and gravimetric reagents), medicine (the anticancer drug cisplatin and gadolinium-based MRI contrast agents), and metallurgy (cyanide leaching of gold). Organometallic compounds, a related class in which ligands bind through carbon, bridge coordination chemistry and organic synthesis.
