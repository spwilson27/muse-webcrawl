South Slavic epic (or South Slavic heroic song) is the oral epic tradition of the South Slavic peoples of the Balkans, performed for centuries by singer-poets — most famously Serbian guslari, who accompanied themselves on the single-stringed gusle. The songs celebrate heroic deeds from the era of the medieval Serbian Empire, the Ottoman conquest, and the long resistance of hajduk outlaws and frontier warriors.

Cycles and themes

The tradition is organised into cycles. The Kosovo cycle surrounds the Battle of Kosovo (1389) and the fall of Prince Lazar, transforming defeat into a covenant of sacrifice. The Marko Kraljević cycle follows the historical King Marko (d. 1395), refashioned in song as a superhuman hero who, though a nominal Ottoman vassal, outwits sultans and protects the poor. Later cycles celebrate hajduks such as Starina Novak and the uskoks of Senj.

Form and performance

The Serbian epic line is a trochaic decasyllable (deseterac) with a caesura after the fourth syllable. Singers compose in performance using a stock of formulas and themes — a technique whose study by Milman Parry and Albert Lord in the 1930s, based on fieldwork with singers such as Avdo Međedović, founded oral-formulaic theory and transformed Homeric scholarship.

Collection and influence

Andrija Kačić Miošić's Razgovor ugodni naroda slovinskog (1756) popularised the songs in print. Vuk Stefanović Karadžić collected and published the canonical corpus in the early 19th century, earning the admiration of Goethe and the Brothers Grimm. Bulgarian and Macedonian traditions, collected by the Miladinov brothers and others, formed a parallel corpus, including songs of Krali Marko shared across the region.
