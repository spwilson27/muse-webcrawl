Rose quartz

Rose quartz is a pink variety of quartz (silicon dioxide, SiO2), coloured by trace amounts of titanium, iron, or manganese. Unlike most quartz varieties, it almost always occurs in massive form without well-developed crystal faces, and its colour ranges from a very pale pink to a deep rose-red. It is widely used as a gemstone and ornamental material.

Properties

Rose quartz has a hardness of 7 on the Mohs scale, a vitreous lustre, and a specific gravity of about 2.65, like other quartz. It is typically translucent rather than transparent. For many years the pink colour was attributed to manganese or titanium impurities in the structure; more recent research attributes the colour of most rose quartz to microscopic fibrous inclusions of a pink borosilicate mineral related to dumortierite. When cut en cabochon, some rose quartz shows asterism — a six-rayed star — owing to oriented needle-like inclusions of rutile.

A distinct, rarer material sometimes called pink quartz forms actual crystals and owes its colour to aluminium and phosphorus substituting in the lattice; its colour is photosensitive and can fade in sunlight, whereas ordinary rose quartz is relatively stable.

Occurrence

Rose quartz forms mainly in pegmatites, where it often makes up the cores of zoned pegmatite bodies in very large masses. Important sources include Brazil (especially Minas Gerais), Madagascar, South Africa, Namibia, India, and the United States, notably South Dakota, Maine, and Colorado. The Black Hills of South Dakota produced large quantities of rose quartz, once used for building decoration and monuments.

Uses and lore

Rose quartz has been carved since antiquity; beads attributed to the region of Mesopotamia date to around 7000 BC, and the Egyptians and Romans used it for seals, jewellery, and face masks. In modern crystal healing and popular folklore it is associated with love and the heart — a belief sometimes traced to Greek myth linking the stone to Aphrodite and Adonis. Today it is cut into cabochons, beads, spheres, figurines, and tumbled stones, and larger pieces serve as decorative stone. Transparent material suitable for faceting is rare and commands higher prices.
