Phèdre is a tragedy in five acts by the French dramatist Jean Racine, first performed on 1 January 1677. Written in rhyming alexandrine couplets and drawn from Euripides' Hippolytus and Seneca's Phaedra, it recounts the forbidden passion of Phaedra, wife of Theseus, for her stepson Hippolytus. It is generally regarded as Racine's masterpiece and the supreme achievement of French classical tragedy.

Plot

Believing her husband Theseus dead, Phaedra, consumed by a passion she regards as a divine curse inherited from her mother Pasiphaë, confesses her love to Hippolytus, who is himself secretly in love with Aricia, a captive princess. Theseus returns alive; to protect her mistress, Phaedra's nurse Œnone accuses Hippolytus of attempting to seduce the queen. Theseus calls on Neptune to destroy his son, and Hippolytus is killed by a sea monster while fleeing into exile — the catastrophe narrated in the play's celebrated set-piece, the récit de Théramène. Œnone drowns herself; Phaedra, having taken poison, dies confessing the truth and clearing Hippolytus's name.

"C'est Vénus tout entière à sa proie attachée." — "It is Venus entire fastened on her prey." (Act I)

Reception and legacy

A cabal of rival patrons promoted Pradon's competing Phèdre et Hippolyte at the same moment, and the quarrel contributed to Racine's retirement from the secular stage for twelve years. Posterity reversed the verdict: Voltaire called the play "the masterpiece of the human mind", and the role of Phèdre has been a pinnacle for French tragediennes, above all Sarah Bernhardt and Rachel. The play has inspired operas by Rameau (Hippolyte et Aricie) and others, Benjamin Britten's cantata Phaedra (1975, written for Janet Baker), and numerous translations, including Robert Lowell's English version.
