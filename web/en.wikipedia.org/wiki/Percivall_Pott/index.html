Percivall Pott, leading surgeon of 18th-century London

Percivall Pott (6 January 1714 – 22 December 1788) was an English surgeon at St Bartholomew's Hospital in London, regarded as one of the founders of orthopaedics and a pioneer of occupational medicine. Several conditions bear his name.

Contributions

In 1756 Pott himself sustained a compound fracture of the leg after a fall from his horse; refusing amputation, he wrote extensively during his convalescence. The injury pattern he described — a fracture of the distal fibula with dislocation of the ankle — is still known as Pott's fracture. In 1779 he described the tuberculous destruction of the vertebrae causing spinal deformity and paralysis, known as Pott's disease, and showed that the paralysis could be relieved by draining the associated abscess.

Pott's most historically significant work came in 1775, when he identified the high incidence of scrotal cancer among London chimney sweeps and attributed it to their exposure to soot — the first clear description of an occupational cancer and of an environmental carcinogen, anticipating by more than a century the experimental demonstration that coal tar causes cancer. His observations eventually contributed to protective legislation for apprentice sweeps. Pott also wrote influentially on head injuries, hydrocele, hernia, and anal fistula, insisting on observation and clinical evidence over inherited doctrine. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society and became a dominant figure in London surgery, teaching the celebrated surgeon John Hunter among many others.
