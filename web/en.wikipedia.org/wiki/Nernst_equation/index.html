Nernst equation

In electrochemistry, the Nernst equation relates the reduction potential of an electrochemical reaction (half-cell or full cell) to the standard electrode potential, temperature, and the activities (often approximated by concentrations) of the chemical species undergoing reduction and oxidation. It is named after the German physical chemist Walther Nernst, who formulated it in 1889. The equation is a cornerstone of electrochemistry, underpinning pH measurement, battery potentials, and the membrane potentials of neurons.

Equation

For the reaction Ox + ne− → Red, the electrode potential is:

E = E° − (RT / nF) · ln(aRed / aOx)

where E is the reduction potential, E° the standard reduction potential, R the gas constant, T the absolute temperature, n the number of electrons transferred, and F Faraday's constant. At 25 °C the factor RT/F equals about 25.7 mV, or 59.16 mV per decade when the natural logarithm is converted to base 10.

Applications

The Nernst equation predicts the voltage of galvanic cells under non-standard conditions and concentration cells, determines equilibrium constants from standard potentials, and explains the operation of the glass pH electrode. In physiology, its ionic analogue underlies the resting membrane potential and the reversal potentials of ion channels, extended by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation for membranes permeable to several ions.

Derivation and limits

The equation follows from the relation between electrical work and Gibbs free energy change, ΔG = −nFE, combined with the reaction-quotient expression for ΔG. Its accuracy is limited by the use of concentrations in place of activities and by junction potentials and non-ideal behaviour in real solutions.
