Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns

The Académie française, where the quarrel began in 1687.

The Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns (French: Querelle des Anciens et des Modernes) was a literary and artistic debate that erupted in the French Academy in the late 17th century and continued into the early 18th. The "Ancients" argued that the writers and artists of Greek and Roman antiquity remained unsurpassable models and that modern authors should imitate them; the "Moderns" countered that their own age — the age of Louis XIV — equalled or surpassed antiquity and that literature should progress and adapt like the sciences.

Course of the quarrel

The quarrel opened on 27 January 1687, when Charles Perrault read his poem Le Siècle de Louis le Grand to the Académie française, praising modern writers above the ancients and provoking Nicolas Boileau to protest. Perrault developed the case in his Parallèle des Anciens et des Modernes (1688–1697), and Fontenelle's Digression sur les Anciens et les Modernes (1688) argued that human nature is constant but knowledge accumulates. Boileau, Racine, and La Bruyère led the Ancients; the debate reignited in 1713–1716 over Homer, when Anne Dacier defended faithful prose translation against Antoine Houdar de la Motte's modernised verse version.

Significance

The quarrel spread to England, where it became the "Battle of the Books" satirised by Jonathan Swift, and it crystallised early Enlightenment ideas: the possibility of progress, the critique of authority, and the legitimacy of vernacular modern literature. In the long run the Moderns prevailed in practice, even though classical models retained their prestige in education.
