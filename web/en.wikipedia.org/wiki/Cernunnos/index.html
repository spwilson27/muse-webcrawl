Cernunnos

[Image: Antlered figure identified as Cernunnos on the Gundestrup cauldron]

Cernunnos is the conventional name given in Celtic studies to the horned or antlered god depicted in Gallo-Roman and Celtic iconography. The name is attested only once, on the Pillar of the Boatmen (1st century AD, now in the Musée de Cluny, Paris), erected by Parisian boatmen under Tiberius, where an antlered figure is labelled "[C]ernunnos". The name is commonly interpreted as "the horned one", from the Celtic root korno-, "horn".

Iconography

The god is typically shown seated cross-legged, with the antlers of a stag, wearing a torc around his neck and holding another torc and sometimes a purse or cornucopia. He is frequently accompanied by animals, especially a stag and a distinctive ram-horned serpent. The most famous image is on the Gundestrup cauldron (found in Denmark, 1st century BC), where the antlered figure sits among beasts holding a torc and the ram-horned snake.

Interpretation

Because no myths about Cernunnos survive, his functions are debated: he is variously interpreted as a god of nature, animals, fertility, wealth, travel, and the underworld. In modern paganism and Wicca, Cernunnos is revered as the Horned God, consort of the Goddess, associated with the wild, masculinity and the cycle of life and death — an image that draws on Cernunnos together with figures such as Herne the Hunter and Pan.
