Agreement theorem

Aumann's agreement theorem, formulated by Nobel laureate Robert Aumann in his 1976 paper "Agreeing to Disagree," states that two rational agents with common prior beliefs cannot have common knowledge of differing posterior beliefs: if it is common knowledge that one assigns a certain probability to an event and the other assigns a different probability, then the two probabilities must in fact be equal. In short, rational agents cannot "agree to disagree" about their posteriors when those posteriors are common knowledge.

Proposed byRobert Aumann
Paper"Agreeing to Disagree," Annals of Statistics (1976)
FieldsGame theory, epistemic logic, Bayesian statistics
RequiresCommon prior; common knowledge of posteriors

Statement

Formally, consider two agents with a common prior probability distribution over a set of possible worlds, each with their own information partition. For an event E, each agent computes a posterior probability given their information. Aumann showed that if at some world the two posteriors are common knowledge—each knows them, each knows the other knows them, and so on—then the posteriors must be identical. The proof uses the meet of the agents' information partitions: common knowledge aggregates their information, and averaging over the common-knowledge component forces the posteriors to coincide.

Interpretation and discussion

The theorem is a foundational result of epistemic game theory and the theory of common knowledge. It seems to imply that honest, rational people who fully share their opinions should converge—motivating empirical questions about why people persistently disagree. Explanations include that real agents lack common priors, that posteriors are rarely truly common knowledge, and that bounded rationality lets people process information differently. Economists have connected the theorem to the "no-trade" results in financial economics: with a common prior, rational agents should not speculate against each other purely on differing beliefs.

Scott Aaronson and others have explored how quickly agents converge when they repeatedly announce their posteriors; experiments and simulations suggest convergence can be slow but is robust. Philosophers debate the realism of the common-prior assumption (the Harsanyi doctrine).
