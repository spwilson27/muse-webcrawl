Hurricane Michael was a very powerful and destructive tropical cyclone that became the first Category 5 hurricane to strike the contiguous United States since Hurricane Andrew in 1992. Michael made landfall near Mexico Beach, Florida, on 10 October 2018, with maximum sustained winds of 160 mph (260 km/h) and a central pressure of 919 mbar, making it the strongest hurricane on record to hit the Florida Panhandle and the third most intense Atlantic hurricane to make landfall in the United States by pressure.

Meteorological history

Michael originated from a broad low-pressure area in the southwestern Caribbean Sea in early October 2018. It organized into a tropical storm on 7 October near the Yucatán Peninsula and moved northward into the Gulf of Mexico, where it underwent rapid intensification over warm water with low wind shear. Contrary to some forecasts that it might level off or weaken before landfall, Michael continued strengthening until it struck the coast near Tyndall Air Force Base and Mexico Beach on 10 October.

Impact

Storm surge and extreme winds devastated Mexico Beach, where the great majority of buildings were damaged or destroyed, and caused severe destruction in Panama City and surrounding Bay County. Tyndall Air Force Base sustained catastrophic damage to nearly every building and to aircraft that could not be evacuated. Michael remained a hurricane into Georgia, damaging agriculture — including the state's pecan and cotton crops — and brought flooding and tornadoes across the Carolinas and Virginia, where flash flooding caused several deaths. The storm killed 74 people in total and caused an estimated $25.1 billion in damage.

Aftermath

Post-storm analysis by the National Hurricane Center upgraded Michael to Category 5 at landfall, making it one of only four Category 5 landfalls on record in the continental United States. The name Michael was retired by the World Meteorological Organization and replaced with Milton. Rebuilding in the Panama City area proceeded slowly, and the disaster spurred discussion of building codes, which in the Panhandle had historically been weaker than in South Florida.
