Pets.com

TypeDot-com company
FoundedAugust 1998
DefunctNovember 2000
HeadquartersSan Francisco, California, US
IndustryOnline pet supplies retail
Key peopleGreg McLemore (founder); Julie Wainwright (CEO)

Pets.com was a dot-com company based in San Francisco, California, that sold pet supplies online. Founded in August 1998 and launched to the public in early 1999, it became one of the most widely recognised symbols of the dot-com bubble. Despite a highly successful advertising campaign built around its sock puppet mascot, the company never came close to profitability and liquidated in November 2000, only about nine months after its initial public offering.

History

Pets.com was founded by Greg McLemore. The business model centred on selling pet food and supplies over the Internet, shipping heavy goods such as bags of dog food at low margins. The company raised substantial venture capital, including an investment from the retailer Petstore.com's backers and a stake sold to the online retailer Amazon.com. In November 1999 the company appointed Julie Wainwright as chief executive.

The company went public on the NASDAQ in February 2000, raising roughly $82.5 million. However, Pets.com was losing money on nearly every sale: it sold products for roughly one-third of their cost once shipping was included, and spent heavily on advertising and infrastructure. When capital markets tightened in 2000, the company was unable to raise further funds or find a buyer. On 6 November 2000 it announced liquidation, and its assets, including the sock puppet trademark, were sold off.

The sock puppet

The Pets.com advertising campaign, created by the San Francisco office of TBWA\Chiat\Day, featured a wisecracking sock puppet dog voiced by comedian Michael Ian Black. The character appeared in television commercials, a Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade balloon, a Super Bowl advertisement in January 2000, and on the cover of magazines. The campaign won advertising awards and gave the company enormous brand recognition, but the cost of the marketing far outpaced the revenue the business generated. The puppet was later licensed by the consumer finance company BarNone, which used it in its own advertising.

Legacy

Pets.com is frequently cited as a canonical example of dot-com era excess — a company whose brand became famous while its underlying business lost money from the start. The domain name was later acquired by PetSmart. The company's rise and collapse featured prominently in post-mortems of the bubble, including the observation that it spent more on advertising in its final year than its total lifetime revenue.
