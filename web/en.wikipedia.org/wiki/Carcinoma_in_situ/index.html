Carcinoma in situ

Carcinoma in situ (CIS) is a group of abnormal cells that have the microscopic features of cancer but remain confined to the place where they formed and have not invaded through the basement membrane into surrounding tissue. Because the cells have not invaded, carcinoma in situ cannot metastasise, and complete removal is usually curative. It is classified as a pre-invasive lesion — often reported as stage 0 cancer — although the natural history varies: some lesions progress to invasive cancer, others remain stable or regress.

Ductal carcinoma in situ: malignant cells confined within the duct by an intact basement membrane.

Terminology

"In situ" is Latin for "in its original place." For many sites the preferred modern terms avoid the word carcinoma: cervical lesions are called high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL, formerly CIN 3), and breast lesions are termed ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) or lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS). The distinction matters clinically because behaviour differs — flat urothelial CIS of the bladder, for instance, is an aggressive lesion with a high risk of progression, whereas LCIS is mainly a risk marker.

Common sites

  • Cervix: CIN 3 / HSIL, detected by Pap smear or HPV testing; treated by excision (e.g., LEEP) or ablation.
  • Breast: DCIS, usually found as microcalcifications on screening mammography; treated with surgery, often plus radiotherapy and endocrine therapy.
  • Skin: Bowen's disease (squamous cell carcinoma in situ) and melanoma in situ, including lentigo maligna.
  • Bladder: flat urothelial CIS, often treated with intravesical BCG.
  • Other: in-situ lesions of the lung, oral cavity (erythroplakia), colon and prostate (high-grade PIN).

Diagnosis and management

Because CIS causes no symptoms, it is overwhelmingly detected through screening programmes or incidental biopsy. Diagnosis is histological: malignant cytology across the full thickness of the epithelium with an intact basement membrane. Management ranges from active surveillance for low-risk lesions to complete local excision. The risk of "overtreatment" of indolent lesions — especially screen-detected DCIS — is a major research and policy question.
