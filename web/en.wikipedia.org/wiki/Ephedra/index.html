Ephedra is a genus of gymnosperm shrubs, the only genus in the family Ephedraceae and order Ephedrales. The approximately 50 species are distributed through arid regions worldwide — southwestern North America, southern Europe, northern Africa, and southwest and central Asia. Plants of this genus have a long history of medicinal use and are the natural source of the alkaloid ephedrine.

Jointed, nearly leafless stems of Ephedra, adapted to dry habitats.

Description

Ephedra species are much-branched shrubs, occasionally small trees or climbers. Their green stems are slender, jointed, and grooved, and perform most of the plant's photosynthesis; the leaves are reduced to small scales at the nodes, an adaptation that limits water loss in deserts and dry mountain habitats. The plants are usually dioecious, bearing small cones — pollen cones in males, seed cones in females — though Ephedra is an unusual gymnosperm in undergoing a form of double fertilization, a feature it shares with the other gnetophytes, Gnetum and Welwitschia. In several species the seed cones become fleshy and red at maturity and are eaten by birds, which disperse the seeds.

Traditional use and ephedrine

The Chinese species Ephedra sinica, known as ma huang, has been used in traditional Chinese medicine for more than 5,000 years to treat asthma, hay fever, and the common cold. Central Asian species were similarly valued, and some scholars have associated Ephedra with the ritual drink soma mentioned in ancient Indo-Iranian texts. In 1885 the Japanese chemist Nagayoshi Nagai isolated ephedrine from the plant; the alkaloid and the related pseudoephedrine became widely used as decongestants, bronchodilators, and stimulants, and as precursors in pharmaceutical manufacture.

Regulation

Because ephedrine-containing dietary supplements were linked to cardiovascular adverse events — including heart attacks and strokes — the US Food and Drug Administration banned the sale of ephedra dietary supplements in 2004, a decision upheld by federal courts. Ephedrine and pseudoephedrine remain regulated worldwide because they can be used to manufacture methamphetamine. Several Ephedra species are also of conservation concern in parts of their range owing to over-collection for the herbal trade.
