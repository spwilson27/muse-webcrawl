Compact disc

A standard 120 mm compact disc stores up to about 700 MB of data or 80 minutes of audio.

The compact disc (CD) is a digital optical disc data storage format co-developed by Philips and Sony and released in 1982. Originally designed to store and play digital audio recordings, the Compact Disc Digital Audio (CD-DA, described in the "Red Book" standard) was the first commercial CD format; the technology was later adapted for data storage (CD-ROM), recordable (CD-R) and rewritable (CD-RW) discs, and derivatives including Video CD and Super Audio CD.

Technical description

A standard CD is 120 mm in diameter and 1.2 mm thick, made of polycarbonate plastic with a thin reflective aluminium layer protected by lacquer. Data are stored as a spiral track of microscopic pits and lands, read by a 780 nm infrared laser diode. Audio is encoded as 16-bit stereo PCM sampled at 44.1 kHz, giving a playing time originally of 74 minutes — reputedly chosen so that Beethoven's Ninth Symphony would fit on a single disc — later extended to about 80 minutes. Error correction (CIRC) makes the format robust against scratches and dust.

History and impact

The CD launched in Japan in October 1982, with players such as the Sony CDP-101 and titles including Billy Joel's 52nd Street. Through the late 1980s and 1990s the CD displaced the vinyl LP and the cassette to become the dominant music format, and CD-ROM transformed software distribution and multimedia computing. Global CD sales peaked around the turn of the millennium before declining under competition from MP3 downloads, file sharing, and later streaming services; by the 2010s the CD had become a niche format, though it retains a devoted market, notably in Japan, and has seen modest revivals as a collectible.
