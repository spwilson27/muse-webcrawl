Unscented transform

Sigma points capture the mean and covariance of a distribution

The unscented transform (UT) is a mathematical function used to estimate the result of applying a given nonlinear transformation to a probability distribution that is characterized only in terms of a finite set of statistics, most commonly its mean and covariance. "Unscented" refers, by the inventors' account, to the joking notion that the transform did not "stink" — unlike crude linearization — and the name stuck.

Motivation

Propagating a random variable through a nonlinear function is generally difficult: the exact transformed mean and covariance usually require intractable integrals. The classical remedy, used in the extended Kalman filter (EKF), is to linearize the function with a first-order Taylor series about the current estimate, which can introduce large errors when the nonlinearity is strong. The unscented transform instead takes a deterministic sampling approach summarized by its developers' slogan: "it is easier to approximate a probability distribution than it is to approximate an arbitrary nonlinear function or transformation."

Sigma points

Given an n-dimensional random variable with mean x̄ and covariance P, the UT deterministically selects a set of 2n + 1 sigma points:

χ0 = x̄;   χi = x̄ + (√((n+λ)P))i for i = 1..n;   χi = x̄ − (√((n+λ)P))i−n for i = n+1..2n

where λ is a scaling parameter determined by tuning constants α, β and κ, and the weights Wi are chosen so the sample mean and covariance of the sigma points match those of the original distribution. Each sigma point is propagated through the nonlinear function, and the transformed mean and covariance are recovered as weighted averages of the propagated points. The resulting estimates are accurate to at least the second order of the Taylor series expansion for any nonlinearity, and to third order for Gaussian inputs, without computing any Jacobians.

Applications

The unscented transform is the core of the unscented Kalman filter (UKF), developed by Simon Julier and Jeffrey Uhlmann in the 1990s, and is widely used in navigation, target tracking, robotics, and control. Variants include the scaled unscented transform, square-root formulations, and spherical simplex sigma-point sets that use fewer points.
