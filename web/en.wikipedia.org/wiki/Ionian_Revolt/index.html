The Ionian Revolt (499–493 BC) was a series of uprisings by the Greek cities of Ionia and neighbouring regions of Asia Minor against Persian rule. Together with the mainland Greek responses it provoked, it formed the opening phase of the Greco-Persian Wars.

Origins

The revolt was instigated by Aristagoras, acting tyrant of Miletus, after a failed joint expedition with the Persian satrap Artaphernes against Naxos. Fearing punishment, Aristagoras proclaimed a revolt and renounced his tyranny, establishing democracies in Miletus and encouraging the other Ionian cities to expel their Persian-backed tyrants. Seeking allies, he travelled to mainland Greece; Sparta refused, but Athens sent twenty ships and Eretria five — the ships Herodotus called "the beginning of evils for Greeks and barbarians alike."

Course

The rebels initially achieved a striking success, marching inland and burning Sardis, the satrapal capital, though they were defeated on the retreat at Ephesus. The revolt spread to Cyprus and Caria, and the Persians gradually regained ground. The decisive engagement was the naval Battle of Lade (494 BC), fought off Miletus, where the Ionian fleet — undermined by desertion of the Samian contingent — was destroyed. Miletus was stormed and its population deported. By 493 BC the remaining rebel strongholds, including Byzantium and the Hellespontine cities, had been reduced.

Aftermath

Darius I's determination to punish Athens and Eretria for aiding the rebels led directly to the first Persian invasion of Greece and the Battle of Marathon in 490 BC. The aftermath saw the Persian commander Mardonius depose the Ionian tyrants and install democracies, a conciliatory settlement reported by Herodotus.
