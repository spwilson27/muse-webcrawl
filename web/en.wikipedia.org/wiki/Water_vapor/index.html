Water vapor

Water vapor
State:Gaseous phase of water
Share of atmosphere:~0–4% by volume (variable)
Role:Greenhouse gas; driver of weather and the water cycle
Key measures:Relative humidity, dew point, specific humidity

Water vapor is the gaseous phase of water. It is one state of water within the hydrosphere. Water vapor can be produced from the evaporation or boiling of liquid water or from the sublimation of ice. Unlike other forms of water, water vapor is invisible; the white "steam" seen above boiling water consists of tiny condensed droplets.

Properties

Under typical atmospheric conditions, water vapor is continuously generated by evaporation and removed by condensation. The maximum amount of vapor air can hold increases strongly with temperature, roughly following the Clausius–Clapeyron relation. When air is saturated, excess vapor condenses into dew, fog or clouds.

Atmospheric water vapor

Water vapor is Earth's most abundant greenhouse gas by volume fraction, though its concentration varies from trace amounts in cold, dry air to about 4% in hot, humid tropics. It is central to the water cycle: evaporated from oceans, lakes and land, it is transported by winds and returns as precipitation.

Role in climate

Because warmer air holds more vapor, water vapor creates a positive feedback that amplifies warming initiated by other greenhouse gases. Its atmospheric lifetime is short — about nine days on average — so its concentration responds to temperature rather than driving long-term change directly. Latent heat carried by water vapor powers thunderstorms, hurricanes and atmospheric circulation.

Measurement

Humidity is measured with hygrometers and radiosondes, and globally by satellites using microwave and infrared sounding. Common quantities include relative humidity, dew point, mixing ratio and precipitable water.
