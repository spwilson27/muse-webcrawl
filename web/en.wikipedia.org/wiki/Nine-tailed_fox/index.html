Nine-tailed fox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A nine-tailed fox in classical East Asian art

The nine-tailed fox is a mythological fox entity originating from Chinese folklore that has since spread throughout East Asia. Known as the jiǔwěihú in Chinese, the kitsune in Japanese, and the kumiho in Korean, the creature typically appears as a fox that has lived for a thousand years and gained magical powers, growing up to nine tails.

Chinese tradition

The nine-tailed fox first appears in the Shanhaijing (Classic of Mountains and Seas), where it is said to dwell in the land of Qingqiu. Early depictions were auspicious: a white nine-tailed fox was regarded as an omen of peace and prosperity, and one was said to have appeared to Yu the Great. In later literature, particularly the Ming-dynasty novel Fengshen Yanyi (Investiture of the Gods), the creature took on a more sinister cast: the fox spirit Daji possessed the consort of King Zhou of Shang and helped bring about the dynasty's fall.

Japanese tradition

In Japan, the nine-tailed fox (kyūbi no kitsune) is among the most powerful of fox spirits, possessing great wisdom and magical ability, including shapeshifting into human form — often that of a beautiful woman. The famous tale of Tamamo-no-Mae, a courtesan whose true form was exposed at the court of Emperor Konoe, ends with the fox slain and its spirit sealed in the Sessho-seki, the "Killing Stone" of Nasu.

Korean tradition

The Korean kumiho is generally portrayed as malevolent, a creature that transforms into a beautiful woman to seduce men and eat their hearts or livers. Folktales hold that if a kumiho abstains from killing for a thousand days, or fulfils certain conditions, it may become fully human.

In popular culture

The nine-tailed fox remains a staple of East Asian fantasy media, appearing in anime, video games, television dramas and novels, from the Pokémon Ninetales to the Korean drama My Girlfriend Is a Gumiho and the Chinese fantasy series Eternal Love.
