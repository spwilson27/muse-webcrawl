The Zuni (Zuni: A:shiwi; formerly spelled Zuñi) are Native American Pueblo peoples native to the Zuni River valley in western New Mexico, United States. The majority live in the Pueblo of Zuni on the Zuni Reservation, with a smaller community at Zuni Pueblo and in surrounding areas. The Zuni are one of the 19 Pueblo tribes of New Mexico and are known for their distinctive language, which is a language isolate unrelated to the languages of any other Native American group.

The Zuni language, Shiwi'ma, is spoken by roughly 9,500 people, mostly in the vicinity of Zuni Pueblo. Unlike most indigenous languages in the United States, the Zuni language is still spoken by a large proportion of children and remains the everyday language of the community, though it is classified as threatened because some children do not learn it as a first language.

History

Archaeological evidence suggests that the Zuni have occupied the Zuni River valley for roughly 3,000 to 4,000 years. Their ancestors built large multi-storey settlements of adobe and stone; during the Coronado expedition of 1540, the Spanish encountered the Zuni towns, which they called the Seven Cities of Cibola. The first contact was hostile: the expedition, led by Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, attacked the Zuni village of Hawikuh after the Zuni killed the scout Estevanico.

Following the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, the Zuni consolidated their settlements onto the defensible mesa of Dowa Yalanne, returning to a single main pueblo after the Spanish reconquest. The principal modern community, Halona Idiwan'a ("the Middle Place"), stands on the site of one of the original six Zuni villages.

Culture

Zuni society is organized into matrilineal clans, and religious life centres on the kachina cult, masked dances, and a complex ceremonial calendar. The Zuni are renowned for their jewellery — particularly needlepoint and petit point turquoise inlay set in silver — as well as pottery and carved stone fetishes representing animals and spirits.

The Zuni religious system includes the Kachina society, medicine societies, and the priesthood. The annual Shalako festival, held in late November or early December, is the most significant public ceremony, featuring towering masked figures representing messengers of the gods.

Economy

Traditionally, the Zuni farmed the desert valley using waffle gardens and dry farming techniques, growing corn, beans, and squash. Today, jewellery-making and arts and crafts form a major part of the tribal economy; it has been estimated that some 80 percent of Zuni households are involved in art production in some form.
