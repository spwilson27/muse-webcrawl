Octave

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In music, an octave (Latin: octavus: eighth) is the interval between one musical pitch and another with double its frequency. The octave relationship is a natural phenomenon that has been referred to as the "basic miracle of music": pitches an octave apart are perceived as fundamentally similar, and in most musical cultures notes an octave apart share the same pitch-class name (for example, all Cs).

Acoustics and notation

The frequency ratio of an octave is 2:1. A tone at 440 Hz (the A above middle C) has its octave at 880 Hz above and 220 Hz below. The octave is divided in equal temperament into twelve semitones. In staff notation, notes an octave apart occupy the same line/space position shifted by one full register; scientific pitch notation distinguishes them by number (e.g. C4, "middle C", and C5).

Perceptual and cultural role

Octave equivalence — treating pitches an octave apart as in some sense "the same" — is found in virtually all musical cultures and appears to have a basis in the harmonic series: the second harmonic of any periodic tone is exactly an octave above the fundamental. Singers of different sexes and ages naturally sing melodies an octave apart when singing "in unison". The perfect octave, along with the perfect fifth, is among the most consonant intervals and forms the frame of most scale systems, including the Western diatonic scale, whose eighth degree completes the octave — hence the name.

Other uses

In electronics and acoustics, "octave" also denotes a frequency band whose upper limit is twice its lower limit (hence "octave bands" in sound measurement), and in poetry an octave is an eight-line stanza or the first eight lines of a sonnet.
