Foster's rule

Foster's rule, also known as the island rule, is an ecogeographical rule in evolutionary biology stating that members of a species tend to become smaller or larger on islands depending on the resources available and predation pressure. The rule was first articulated by mammalogist J. Bristol Foster in a 1964 paper in Nature.

Statement

Foster observed that small mainland mammals tend to evolve larger body size on islands (island gigantism), while large mainland mammals tend to evolve smaller size (insular dwarfism). Later, Leigh Van Valen generalized it as the "island rule" in 1973, framing size change as a graded trend: species converge toward an intermediate optimal size where predators and competitors are few.

Examples

Dwarf elephants and hippopotamuses evolved on Mediterranean islands; the giant rat of Flores and the Komodo dragon illustrate the opposite trend among small ancestors. The rule's generality is debated: studies differ on whether it applies across all taxa or is confounded by phylogeny and island area.
