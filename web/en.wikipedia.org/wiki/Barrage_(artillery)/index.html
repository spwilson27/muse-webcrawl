Barrage (artillery)


A creeping barrage in the First World War

In military usage, a barrage is massed sustained artillery fire aimed at a series of points along a line. The term, from the French barrage ("barrier"), came into wide use during the First World War, when artillery was concentrated to suppress or destroy enemy defences immediately in front of — or moving ahead of — an infantry assault.

Types

A creeping barrage (or rolling barrage) moves forward in timed "lifts" of typically 50–100 yards, with the infantry advancing close behind the curtain of shellfire so as to reach the enemy trenches before the defenders can emerge from shelter. A standing barrage remains fixed on one line, for example to block a counter-attack. A box barrage forms three sides of a box around a target area to isolate it, while an emergency barrage is pre-registered defensive fire called down on a unit's own front in case of attack.

History

Precursors appeared in the Boer War, notably at the relief of Ladysmith, and in the Russo-Japanese War. The technique matured on the Western Front: creeping barrages were used at the Somme in 1916 with mixed success — infantry often could not keep up with the lifts — and were refined by 1917 at Vimy Ridge and Messines, where precisely surveyed guns and detailed timetables let the infantry "lean on the barrage".

In the Second World War, barrages remained central to set-piece attacks such as El Alamein and the Soviet deep-battle offensives, where massed artillery of entire artillery divisions fired planned concentrations. Although modern precision munitions have reduced reliance on sheer volume of fire, planned suppressive barrages remain part of artillery doctrine.
