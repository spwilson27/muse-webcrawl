Induction coil

An induction coil is an electrical device used to produce high-voltage pulses from a low-voltage direct current (DC) supply. It consists of two coils of insulated wire wound around a common iron core: a primary coil of few turns of thick wire and a secondary coil of many thousands of turns of fine wire, together with an interrupter that repeatedly makes and breaks the primary circuit.

Operation

When the primary current is suddenly interrupted, the collapsing magnetic field induces a very high voltage in the secondary winding, proportional to the turns ratio, producing sparks across a discharge gap. Induction coils were developed in the 1830s–1850s by Nicholas Callan, Heinrich Ruhmkorff, and others.

Uses

Ruhmkorff coils powered early experiments in electricity, spectroscopy, and X-ray tubes, and were used in wireless telegraphy spark-gap transmitters. Their descendant, the ignition coil, remains standard in spark-ignition petrol engines, and the principle underlies modern flyback transformers and Tesla coils.
