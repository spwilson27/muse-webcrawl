Gayageum

ClassificationString instrument (plucked zither)
OriginGaya confederacy, Korea, 6th century
Strings12 (traditional); up to 25 (modernised)
Related instrumentsGuzheng, koto, geomungo

The gayageum (Korean: 가야금; also spelled kayagum) is a traditional Korean plucked zither with 12 strings, though modern versions may have 18, 21, or 25 strings. It is one of the best-known Korean musical instruments. The gayageum is closely related to the Chinese guzheng, the Japanese koto, and the Vietnamese đàn tranh.

History

According to tradition, the instrument was devised in the 6th century by King Gasil of the Gaya confederacy, after he saw a Chinese zheng; the musician Ureuk was charged with composing music for it. When Gaya fell to Silla, Ureuk took the instrument to the Silla court, where it entered the mainstream of Korean court and folk music. The body is traditionally carved from paulownia wood, with silk strings passing over movable bridges called anjok ("goose feet").

Playing and repertoire

The gayageum is played seated on the floor, the instrument resting across the player's lap. The right hand plucks and strikes the strings while the left hand presses them to produce vibrato and pitch bends characteristic of Korean music. It is the principal melodic instrument in sanjo, a virtuoso solo genre, and is also used in court music, folk ensembles, and the sung narrative art of pansori accompaniment. Hwang Byungki (1936–2018) was among its most celebrated modern exponents and composers.
