Point at infinity

In geometry, a point at infinity is an idealised point adjoined to an affine space so that parallel lines can be treated as meeting there. Adding one point at infinity for each direction of parallel lines converts a Euclidean plane into a projective plane, in which any two distinct lines intersect in exactly one point. The collection of all points at infinity of the plane forms the line at infinity.

In perspective drawing, parallel lines appear to converge at a vanishing point — the visual counterpart of a point at infinity.

Projective geometry

In homogeneous coordinates a point at infinity in the projective plane has its last coordinate equal to zero. Projective transformations map points at infinity to ordinary points and vice versa, so the distinction between them is not intrinsic to the projective plane: what counts as the line at infinity depends on the choice of coordinate system, or equivalently on which line is removed to recover an affine plane.

Other contexts

  • Complex analysis: the Riemann sphere adjoins a single point at infinity to the complex plane, making functions such as 1/z well-defined everywhere on the sphere.
  • Hyperbolic geometry: points at infinity form the boundary circle of the Poincaré disk model and are used to define ideal triangles and horocycles.
  • Affine and algebraic geometry: projective completions of curves are obtained by adding points at infinity, enabling a uniform statement of Bézout's theorem.

History

The idea grew out of Renaissance perspective construction and was formalised in the seventeenth century by Girard Desargues and later by Jean-Victor Poncelet, who placed points at infinity on the same footing as ordinary points in his Traité des propriétés projectives des figures (1822).
