False consciousness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

False consciousness is a concept in Marxist theory denoting a way of thinking that prevents a person or class from perceiving the true nature of their social and economic situation. The term is associated with Friedrich Engels, who used it in an 1893 letter to Franz Mehring to describe ideology as a process in which the thinker is unaware of the real motives driving their thought.

In the classic account, the bourgeois class that controls the means of production also dominates the production of ideas — through religion, education, media and law — so that subordinate classes come to accept arrangements contrary to their interests as natural, just or inevitable. The concept is linked to Marx's analysis of commodity fetishism in Das Kapital, where social relations between people appear as relations between things, disguising exploitation.

Development and criticism

Later theorists extended the idea: Georg Lukács treated class consciousness as the awareness a class would have if it grasped its situation rationally; Antonio Gramsci's theory of cultural hegemony explained consent without crude deception. Critics object that false consciousness is condescending and unfalsifiable — any disagreement with the theory can be re-described as its symptom — and that it licenses vanguard parties to override workers' expressed preferences in the name of their “real” interests. Contemporary uses are usually looser, describing systematic misperception of one's social position whatever its cause.
