Jolly Roger is the traditional English name for the flags flown to identify a pirate ship about to attack, during the early 18th century. The best-known design is the skull and crossbones — a human skull above two crossed long bones — on a black field, which has become the generic popular symbol of piracy.

Use

Pirate flags were a tool of psychological warfare: flown as a ship closed on its target, the flag signalled that the attackers offered quarter if the victim surrendered without a fight, and death if it resisted. Some pirates flew a plain red flag — from the French joli rouge, one proposed origin of the name — signifying that no quarter would be given. Individual captains used personalised designs: Calico Jack Rackham's flag crossed cutlasses beneath a skull, Blackbeard's showed a horned skeleton spearing a heart, and Bartholomew Roberts used several designs including one showing him standing on the skulls of two governors.

Etymology

The origin of the name is uncertain. It may derive from the French joli rouge ("pretty red"), from "Roger" as a contemporary nickname for the Devil, or from earlier terms for wandering rogues. The earliest recorded use of "Jolly Roger" for a pirate flag dates to the 1720s–1730s.

In later culture

The skull-and-crossbones design passed into general use as a warning of danger — on poison labels and high-voltage signs — and remains the emblem of piracy in popular culture, from Treasure Island onward. Royal Navy submarines adopted the practice of flying a Jolly Roger on returning from successful patrols during the First World War, a tradition maintained to this day.
