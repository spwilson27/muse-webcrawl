The levator ani is a broad, thin muscle group situated on either side of the pelvis. It forms the greater part of the pelvic floor (pelvic diaphragm) and is essential for supporting the pelvic viscera, resisting increases in intra-abdominal pressure, and maintaining continence. It is conventionally described as having three parts: the puborectalis, the pubococcygeus, and the iliococcygeus.

Structure

The puborectalis arises from the back of the pubic bones and forms a U-shaped sling around the anorectal junction, pulling it forward to create the anorectal angle that helps keep the anal canal closed at rest. The pubococcygeus runs from the pubis to the coccyx and the midline raphe, with fibres attaching around the urethra, vagina or prostate, and rectum. The iliococcygeus arises from the tendinous arch of the levator ani (a thickening of the obturator fascia) and inserts into the coccyx and anococcygeal raphe, forming a shelf across the back of the pelvic outlet.

Function and clinical significance

Together with the coccygeus muscles, the levator ani closes the pelvic outlet while allowing passage of the urethra, vagina (in females), and anal canal through openings in the sling. Its resting tone supports the bladder, uterus, and rectum against gravity and abdominal pressure, and it relaxes in a coordinated fashion during urination and defecation.

Injury or weakness of the levator ani — particularly avulsion of the pubococcygeus from the pubic bone during vaginal childbirth — is a major risk factor for pelvic organ prolapse. Chronic straining and spasm of the muscle are implicated in pelvic floor dysfunction and levator ani syndrome, a condition of recurrent dull rectal pain attributed to spasm of the levator, treated with biofeedback, massage, and muscle relaxation techniques. Strengthening exercises of the pelvic floor (Kegel exercises) primarily target this muscle group.
