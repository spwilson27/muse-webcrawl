Scanning probe microscopy


Type: Microscopy technique
Invented: 1981 (scanning tunneling microscope)
Resolution: up to atomic scale

Scanning probe microscopy (SPM) is a branch of microscopy that forms images of surfaces using a physical probe that scans the specimen. SPM was founded in 1981 with the invention of the scanning tunneling microscope by Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM Zürich, an achievement that earned them the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986. Unlike optical and electron microscopes, SPM instruments interact with the surface through a sharp tip, achieving resolutions down to the atomic scale.

Types

Many scanning probe techniques have been developed, including:

  • Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) — measures tunneling current between tip and conductive surface;
  • Atomic force microscopy (AFM) — measures forces between the tip and the surface, usable on insulators, in air or liquid;
  • Scanning near-field optical microscopy (SNOM/NSOM) — collects light beyond the diffraction limit;
  • Magnetic force microscopy (MFM), Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM), and others sensitive to magnetic, electrostatic, thermal, or chemical properties.

Applications

SPM is used across physics, chemistry, materials science, and biology to image nanostructures, manipulate individual atoms and molecules, study friction and adhesion, and characterize semiconductors and biomolecules. Its ability to operate in ambient air and liquids distinguishes it from vacuum-based techniques.
