New Cross house fire

Date18 January 1981
Location439 New Cross Road, London
Deaths13 (a 14th victim died in 1983)
CauseUndetermined; arson suspected by victims' families

The New Cross house fire was a fire that occurred during a birthday party at a house in New Cross, south-east London, in the early hours of Sunday, 18 January 1981. The blaze killed 13 young black people aged between 14 and 22; one survivor died two years later. The party, at 439 New Cross Road, was a joint birthday celebration attended by young people from the local black community.

Investigation

The fire began in the front room of the house. Many in the black community believed from the outset that the fire had been started deliberately, in a racist arson attack — the far-right National Front was active in the area and there had been other attacks — while police investigations focused on the possibility that it began accidentally or from a dispute among guests. Two inquests, in 1981 and 2004, both returned open verdicts. A 2004 police forensic review concluded that the fire most likely started inside the front room, possibly by accident, but no cause was ever proven and nobody was charged.

Aftermath

The official response was widely perceived in the black community as indifferent — the Queen was criticised for not sending condolences, in contrast to her response to other tragedies — and the fire became a defining grievance. On 2 February 1981 some 20,000 people took part in the Black People's Day of Action, marching from New Cross to Hyde Park, one of the largest mobilisations of black people in British history up to that time. The event is regarded as a turning point that helped set the stage for the uprisings in Brixton and elsewhere in 1981, prompted the Scarman Inquiry into policing, and energised black political organisation in Britain. A memorial stands near the site, and the victims' names are read at annual commemorations.
