Flysch
A sequence of sedimentary rocks deposited in a deep marine facies in a foreland basin, typically in the early stage of an orogeny.

Flysch (pronounced "fleesh") is a sequence of sedimentary rocks that is deposited in a deep marine facies in the foreland basin of a developing orogen. Flysch is typically formed during the early stages of continental collisions, when the basin begins to be deformed by the advancing tectonic load.

Flysch consists typically of repeated rhythmically alternating layers of coarse and fine sediments — typically shales and sandstones, often with conglomerates and marls — deposited by turbidity currents on the deep ocean floor in front of a rising mountain chain. The individual layers (beds) can be tens of centimetres to several metres thick, and the whole sequence may be thousands of metres thick.

Etymology and history

The term was introduced in geologic literature by the Swiss geologist Bernhard Studer in 1827, from the German verb fliessen, "to flow," because he thought flysch was deposited by rivers. At that time it was thought that flysch had been deposited in shallow water. Later, when flysch sequences were found all around the Alps, Carpathians, and other young mountain belts, their deep-water origin was recognized with the identification of turbidites and their graded bedding, sole marks, and typical Bouma sequences.

Deposition

Flysch is deposited in a rapidly subsiding deep-water basin (a foredeep) ahead of an advancing mountain belt. Sediment shed from the rising orogen is carried by turbidity currents down the submarine slope and spread across the basin floor as sheet-like turbidite beds; between events, fine mud settles out to form the shale intervals. As the orogen advances, the flysch basin migrates and is eventually itself folded and thrust into the mountain belt — the deposits are thus often spectacularly deformed. With continued collision, the deep-marine flysch typically gives way upward to shallow-marine and continental deposits known as molasse.

Occurrences

  • The Alps — e.g., the flysch zones of Austria and Switzerland (Prättigau, Glarus)
  • The Carpathians, where vast flysch nappes form much of the mountain chain in Poland, Slovakia, and Romania
  • The Pyrenees and the Basque coast of Spain, where tilted flysch strata form striking coastal cliffs (e.g., at Zumaia)
  • The Apennines of Italy
  • The Hellenides of Greece and the Taurides of Turkey
  • Deep-water sequences in the Appalachians (e.g., the Martinsburg Formation) formed in an analogous ancient setting

Scientific importance

Flysch sequences were central to the development of ideas about turbidity currents, submarine fans, and the Wilson cycle of basin evolution. The Basque flysch at Zumaia preserves a continuous record through the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary and serves as a global reference section for the Danian and Selandian stages. In petroleum geology, fractured flysch sandstones can act as hydrocarbon reservoirs, and flysch belts are important hosts of landslide hazards because their interbedded weak shales fail readily on slopes.
