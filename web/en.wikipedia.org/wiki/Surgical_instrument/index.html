Surgical instrument

A surgical instrument is a tool or device designed to perform specific actions during an operation, such as cutting, dissecting, grasping, holding, retracting, clamping, cauterising or suturing tissue. Modern instruments are usually manufactured from high-grade stainless steel, sometimes with tungsten carbide inserts, and are built to withstand repeated sterilisation.

Classification

  • Cutting and dissecting — scalpels, surgical scissors (e.g. Mayo and Metzenbaum), surgical blades, osteotomes and dermatomes.
  • Grasping and holding — forceps (thumb forceps and ring forceps), tissue forceps such as Allis and Babcock, and needle holders.
  • Clamping and occluding — haemostats and clamps (e.g. Kelly, Crile and mosquito forceps) used to control bleeding.
  • Retracting and exposing — hand-held and self-retaining retractors that hold incisions open or move organs aside.
  • Suturing and stapling — needle holders, suture needles and mechanical staplers.
  • Suctioning and aspirating — suction tips such as the Yankauer.
  • Energy devices — electrosurgical (diathermy) instruments, ultrasonic scalpels and lasers.

History

Surgical tools date to antiquity: bronze and iron scalpels, probes and bone levers survive from Greek and Roman medicine, and Sushruta described numerous instruments in ancient India. The 19th-century introduction of anaesthesia and antisepsis, and later stainless steel in the early 20th century, standardised instrument manufacture; many instruments still bear the names of their surgeon-designers, such as Kocher, Halsted and DeBakey.

Care

Instruments must be cleaned, inspected and sterilised — typically by steam autoclave — between cases, and their condition is checked as part of surgical counting and safety protocols to prevent retained foreign objects.
