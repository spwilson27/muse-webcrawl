The Shikoku Pilgrimage (四国遍路, Shikoku Henro) or Shikoku Junrei is a multi-site pilgrimage of 88 temples associated with the Buddhist monk Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi) on the island of Shikoku, Japan. A popular and historically significant feature of the island's culture, the full circuit covers some 1,200 kilometres and traditionally takes between 30 and 60 days on foot. Pilgrims, called henro or o-henro-san, undertake the journey for religious devotion, in memory of the dead, for healing, or for personal reflection.

The route

The 88 temples are distributed among Shikoku's four prefectures, which tradition interprets as four stages of spiritual development: Tokushima (temples 1–23, the place of spiritual awakening, hosshin), Kōchi (24–39, ascetic training, shugyō), Ehime (40–65, enlightenment, bodai) and Kagawa (66–88, nirvana, nehan). The route begins at Ryōzen-ji in Naruto, Tokushima, and ends at Ōkubo-ji in Sanuki, Kagawa; many pilgrims then visit Mount Kōya, Kūkai's mausoleum, as a thanksgiving. The first written guidebook, Shikoku Henro Michishirube (1687), attributed to the monk Jakuhon, fixed the numbering of the temples.

Pilgrim practice

Walking pilgrims (aruki-henro) traditionally dress in white — symbolising purity and readiness for death — wearing a sedge hat (sugegasa), a white vest (hakui) printed with the phrase dōgyō ninin ("two travelling together", meaning the pilgrim walks with Kōbō Daishi), and carrying a wooden staff (kongōzue), a stole, rosary and a stamp book (nōkyōchō) in which calligraphy seals are collected at each temple. At each temple pilgrims recite sutras, notably the Heart Sutra, at the main hall and the Daishi hall. Local custom of osettai — free gifts of food, drink or rides to pilgrims, offered as devotion to Kōbō Daishi — remains a distinctive feature of Shikoku hospitality. Today most pilgrims complete the circuit by car, bus tours or bicycle, in sections if not all at once.

History

Ascetic practices around sites connected with the Shikoku-born Kūkai date to the Heian period, and the circuit crystallised in the Edo period as pilgrimage became possible for commoners. The pilgrimage has been the subject of renewed national and international interest, including efforts toward World Heritage recognition.
