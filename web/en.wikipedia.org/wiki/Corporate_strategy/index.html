Corporate strategy

Corporate strategy is the overall plan and scope of a firm — in particular a diversified firm — that determines which industries and markets it will compete in, how businesses within the portfolio relate to one another, and how the corporate center adds value to them. It is distinguished from business strategy, which concerns how a single business competes within its market.

Key questions

Corporate strategy addresses three fundamental questions: the overall scope of the firm (what businesses to be in), vertical scope (which stages of the value chain to own), and geographic scope (which countries and regions to operate in). A sound corporate strategy is often said to rest on a compelling vision, a portfolio of businesses that fit together, and a corporate structure and management systems that create advantage.

Portfolio management

In the 1970s, tools such as the Boston Consulting Group's growth-share matrix classified business units as stars, cash cows, question marks, and dogs according to market growth and relative market share, guiding investment and divestment. Portfolio planning fell from favor as diversification often destroyed value — the so-called “conglomerate discount” — but portfolio thinking remains part of corporate development through mergers, acquisitions, alliances, and divestitures.

Corporate advantage and parenting

The test of corporate strategy is whether businesses are worth more together than apart. Sources of corporate advantage include sharing activities and resources across units (economies of scope), transferring skills, vertical integration, market power, and internal capital markets. Goold, Campbell, and Alexander's parenting advantage framework asks whether the corporate parent is the best owner of each business and whether it adds more value than rival parents would.

Diversification

Firms diversify for growth, risk spreading, and the exploitation of underused resources, but empirical research finds an inverted-U relationship between diversification and performance: related diversification tends to outperform unrelated diversification, and excessive diversification is associated with lower returns.
