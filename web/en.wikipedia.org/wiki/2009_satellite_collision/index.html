Date10 February 2009, 16:56 UTC
Location776 km above the Taymyr Peninsula, Siberia
SpacecraftIridium 33 (active, United States) and Kosmos-2251 (derelict, Russia)

OutcomeBoth satellites destroyed; more than 2,300 pieces of trackable debris produced

The 2009 satellite collision occurred on 10 February 2009, when the active American commercial satellite Iridium 33 and the defunct Russian military satellite Kosmos-2251 collided at a speed of roughly 11.7 kilometres per second (42,000 km/h) at an altitude of 776 kilometres above Siberia. It was the first known accidental hypervelocity collision between two intact artificial satellites.

The satellites

Iridium 33 was a 560-kilogram communications satellite of the Iridium constellation, launched in 1997 and in active service. Kosmos-2251 was a 950-kilogram Russian Strela-2M communications satellite launched in 1993; it had been out of service since about 1995 and was uncontrolled space debris.

Collision and debris

The two satellites passed through nearly the same point at right angles and struck each other, destroying both. The impact generated two clouds of debris — more than 2,300 fragments large enough to be tracked, many of which remained in orbit for years. Debris from the collision forced precautionary manoeuvres by other spacecraft and raised concerns about collision risk to the International Space Station and the Hubble Space Telescope, though no further major impacts resulted.

Significance

The event dramatically illustrated the growing problem of space debris and the risk of cascading collisions known as the Kessler syndrome. It led to improved conjunction warnings for satellite operators and strengthened calls for active debris removal and better space traffic management.
