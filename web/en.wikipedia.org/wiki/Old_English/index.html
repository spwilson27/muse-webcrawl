Old English

Old English (Ænglisc)
RegionEngland and southern Scotland
Erac. 450 – c. 1150; developed into Middle English
FamilyIndo-European → Germanic → West Germanic
Writing systemRunes (futhorc); Latin alphabet with insular letters (æ, ð, þ, ƿ)

Old English (Ænglisc, pronounced [ˈæŋɡliʃ]), or Anglo-Saxon, is the earliest recorded form of the English language, spoken in England and southern and eastern Scotland in the Early Middle Ages. It developed from the dialects spoken by the Germanic peoples — Angles, Saxons, and Jutes — who settled Britain in the fifth and sixth centuries, and it was spoken and written until roughly the mid-twelfth century, when it gave way to Middle English after the Norman Conquest.

Characteristics

Old English differs strikingly from the modern language. It was a fully inflected language with five grammatical cases, three genders, and strong and weak verb systems; its word order was freer than today's, and its vocabulary was overwhelmingly Germanic, with relatively modest borrowings from Latin (many via Christianity) and Old Norse. Four major dialects are distinguished: Northumbrian, Mercian, Kentish, and West Saxon, the last becoming the standard literary language under King Alfred and his successors.

Literature

Old English literature is among the richest vernacular literatures of early medieval Europe. Its most famous work is the epic poem Beowulf; other poems include The Wanderer, The Seafarer, and The Dream of the Rood. Prose flourished under Alfred the Great, who sponsored translations of Latin works including Boethius and Bede, and in the sermons of Ælfric and Wulfstan. The opening of the Lord's Prayer gives a taste of the language:

Fæder ūre þū þe eart on heofonum, sīe þīn nama ġehālgod — "Our father thou who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name."

Transition

The Viking settlements of the ninth to eleventh centuries simplified Old English grammar in the north and injected thousands of Norse words; the Norman Conquest of 1066 then displaced English from court and administration for two centuries. When written English re-emerged, inflections had eroded and French vocabulary had flooded in: the language of Chaucer, Middle English, was recognizably ancestral to our own in a way Old English is not.
