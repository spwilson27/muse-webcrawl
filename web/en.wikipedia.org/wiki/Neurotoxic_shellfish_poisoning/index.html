Neurotoxic shellfish poisoning

Neurotoxic shellfish poisoning (NSP) is caused by the consumption of shellfish (mollusks) contaminated with brevetoxins, a group of toxins produced by the dinoflagellate Karenia brevis (formerly called Gymnodinium breve or Ptychodiscus brevis). These toxins are most commonly associated with "red tide" blooms along the Gulf of Mexico coast of Florida and Texas.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin 30 minutes to a few hours after eating contaminated shellfish and resemble a milder form of ciguatera poisoning. They include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal cramps, together with neurological symptoms such as tingling and numbness of the lips, tongue, and extremities, slurred speech, dizziness, and a characteristic reversal of hot and cold temperature sensation. Symptoms generally resolve within a few days, and no human fatalities from NSP have been recorded.

Cause and prevention

Brevetoxins accumulate in filter-feeding shellfish such as oysters, clams, and mussels during algal blooms. The toxins are heat stable, so cooking does not destroy them. Prevention relies on monitoring of shellfish beds and closure of harvesting areas during red tide events. Inhalation of aerosolized brevetoxins in sea spray can also cause respiratory irritation in people along affected beaches.
