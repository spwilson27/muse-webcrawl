Lithuanian mythology

Lithuanian mythology is the body of myths, legends and religious beliefs of the ancient Lithuanians, part of the wider Baltic mythology. Lithuania was the last country in Europe to be Christianised (the baptism of 1387, with Samogitia following in 1413), so elements of the pre-Christian religion survived unusually long in folklore, songs (dainos) and customs. Sources are fragmentary and often hostile, coming largely from medieval chroniclers such as Peter of Dusburg and Jan Długosz, supplemented by archaeology and comparative Indo-European studies.

Principal deities

  • Dievas — the supreme sky god, whose name is cognate with Zeus and Dyaus.
  • Perkūnas — god of thunder, oak trees and storms; one of the most important and widely attested Baltic deities.
  • Žemyna — goddess of the earth and fertility.
  • Saulė — the sun goddess, a central figure in folk songs.
  • Mėnulis — the moon god.
  • Teliavelis (Kalvelis) — a smith god associated with the sun's creation.
  • Velnias — later demonised as the Devil, originally a god of the dead and cattle, related to Veles.
  • Laima — goddess of fate and childbirth.
  • Bangpūtys — god of the sea and storms.
  • Medeina — goddess of forests and hunting.
  • Gabija — goddess/spirit of the hearth fire.
  • Aušrinė — the morning star (Venus), a goddess figure in songs.
  • Žvorūna / Kaukas / Aitvaras — various household and nature spirits.

Cosmology and practice

The world was imagined with a world tree, and the dead were believed to travel to a realm ruled over by Velnias or to be reincarnated. Sacred groves (alkos) and oak trees, hilltop shrines, and perpetual fires tended by priests (kriviai) and priestesses (vaidilutės) were central to cult practice; the high priest was called the krivė krivaitis. Offerings included animals and, according to chroniclers, occasionally captives. Serpents (the harmless grass snake, žaltys) were kept in homes as sacred beings connected with fertility and fortune.

Festivals

Seasonal festivals marked the agricultural year: Užgavėnės (end of winter), Jore (spring), Rasos or Joninės (midsummer, associated with dew and herb-gathering), Žolinė (harvest of herbs), and Vėlinės (feast of the dead in autumn), several of which were later overlaid with Christian feasts.

Modern revival

The modern neo-pagan movement Romuva, named after the Baltic Prussian sanctuary described by chroniclers, revives Lithuanian pre-Christian religion and folklore and was formally organised in the late 1960s by Jonas Trinkūnas.
