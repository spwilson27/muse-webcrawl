Proper (liturgy)

The proper (Latin: proprium) is that part of the Christian liturgy which varies according to the date — either the season of the liturgical year or the feast of a saint being celebrated — as distinct from the ordinary, which remains constant from day to day. The term is used chiefly of the Roman Catholic Mass and the Divine Office, and by extension of related Western rites such as those of Lutheran and Anglican worship.

A missal open at the proper of a feast

Parts of the proper at Mass

In the traditional Roman Rite, the sung and spoken proper of the Mass includes:

  • The Introit (entrance antiphon) with its psalm verse;
  • The Collect (opening prayer);
  • The Epistle and Gospel readings of the day;
  • The Gradual and Alleluia (or Tract in penitential seasons);
  • The Sequence, on a small number of great feasts;
  • The Offertory antiphon and the Secret prayer;
  • The Preface, when a proper preface is appointed;
  • The Communion antiphon and the Postcommunion prayer.

The five sung texts of the proper — Introit, Gradual, Alleluia/Tract, Offertory, and Communion — form a cycle of chant preserved in the Graduale Romanum and were historically the default music of the Mass, before polyphonic settings of the ordinary (Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei) came to dominate the composed repertoire.

Divisions of the proper

The proper is traditionally organised into the Proprium de Tempore (proper of the season), following the temporal cycle from Advent through the Sundays after Pentecost, and the Proprium Sanctorum (proper of the saints), following the calendar of feast days. Rules of precedence determine which proper is used when a feast falls on a Sunday or another major day.
