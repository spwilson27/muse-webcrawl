Jane Doe is a placeholder name used in law, medicine and popular culture for a woman whose true identity is unknown or legally protected. It is the feminine counterpart of John Doe. Variant forms include Jane Roe (used in some court cases) and Janie Doe (used in American law enforcement for unidentified girls).

Uses

Legal proceedings

Courts permit plaintiffs to proceed under a pseudonym such as Jane Doe where disclosing a real name would expose the person to harm, humiliation or retaliation — for example in cases involving sexual assault, minors, or sensitive medical matters. The most famous example is Roe v. Wade (1973), in which "Jane Roe" was later identified as Norma McCorvey. Unnamed defendants may likewise be sued as John Doe or Jane Doe until their identities are discovered.

Unidentified bodies and missing persons

Police, coroners and hospitals use "Jane Doe" for an unidentified female patient, decedent or missing person. Cases are often logged in databases such as the US National Missing and Unidentified Persons System (NamUs). Some Jane Doe cases have become famous in their own right and have later been resolved by forensic genealogy, such as the "Buckskin Girl" (Marcia King) and "Princess Doe".

Other uses

The name is used on specimen forms, insurance examples and software test data. In fiction and film, "Jane Doe" frequently signals amnesia or an erased identity, as in the television series Blindspot, whose protagonist is initially known only as Jane Doe.

History

"John Doe" and its companion "Richard Roe" arose in English legal fictions connected with actions of ejectment, attested from the medieval period and common by the 18th century. "Jane Doe" appears in American usage by the 19th century and became standard in 20th-century policing and hospital administration. Equivalent placeholder names in other countries include "Erika Mustermann" in Germany and "N.N." in many civil-law traditions.
