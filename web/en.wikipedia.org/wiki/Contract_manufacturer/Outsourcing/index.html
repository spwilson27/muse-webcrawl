Outsourcing

Outsourcing includes the contracting out of a business process (e.g., payroll processing) and operational, and/or non-core functions (e.g., manufacturing, facility management, call center support) to another party (see also business process outsourcing). The practice is a business strategy that involves contracting out major functions to specialized and efficient service providers, who ultimately become business partners, rather than simply performing the functions in-house.

Overview

Outsourcing includes both foreign and domestic contracting, and sometimes includes offshoring (relocating a business function to a distant country) or nearshoring (transferring a business process to a nearby country). The two terms differ primarily in the distance and cultural gap between the parties involved. Financial savings from lower international labour rates can provide a major motivation for outsourcing or offshoring.

Commonly outsourced functions include information technology services, customer support and call centres, human resources, accounting, and manufacturing. Companies outsource to reduce costs, gain access to specialized expertise, increase flexibility, and focus management attention on core activities. Critics point to job losses in the home country, quality control problems, security risks and hidden coordination costs.

History

Although contracting out has existed for centuries, modern outsourcing grew rapidly from the 1980s and 1990s, when large corporations such as Kodak outsourced their information systems to IBM, and when improvements in telecommunications made it practical to deliver services from low-cost locations such as India and the Philippines. Business process outsourcing (BPO) and knowledge process outsourcing (KPO) extended the model from manufacturing and IT into professional services.
