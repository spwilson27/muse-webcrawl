Variety store


Inside a variety store

A variety store is a retail store that sells a wide range of inexpensive household goods. Variety stores often have very low-priced items, with a specific price range. They have a full range of merchandise in a variety of categories, unlike department stores or big-box stores, which specialize in specific departments. In the United States, the classic variety store was the "five and dime" (or five-and-ten-cent store), where everything cost five or ten cents.

History

The concept was pioneered by Frank Winfield Woolworth, who opened the first successful five-and-dime in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, in 1879. The F. W. Woolworth Company grew into a vast chain across the United States and internationally, with competitors including S. S. Kresge (later Kmart), J. J. Newberry, W. T. Grant and Ben Franklin stores. Their lunch counters became fixtures of American main streets—and, in Greensboro, North Carolina, in 1960, a Woolworth counter was the site of the sit-ins that became a landmark of the civil rights movement. As suburban shopping malls, discount chains and rising prices eroded the model, the last American Woolworth stores closed in 1997.

Modern equivalents

Contemporary forms include dollar stores in North America (Dollar General, Dollar Tree, Family Dollar), pound shops in the United Kingdom (Poundland), 100-yen shops in Japan (notably Daiso), and variety chains such as Flying Tiger and Miniso. These stores sell a broad, rotating assortment of low-cost goods—houseware, stationery, snacks, seasonal items—typically sourced from inexpensive overseas manufacturing.
