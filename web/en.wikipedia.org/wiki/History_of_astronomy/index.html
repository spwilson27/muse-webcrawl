The history of astronomy is among the oldest of the sciences. From prehistoric sky-watching to modern space telescopes, astronomy developed through Babylonian, Greek, Islamic, and European traditions into a central pillar of modern physics.

Ancient astronomy

Babylonian astronomers recorded planetary motions and predicted eclipses, while Egyptian astronomy informed calendars and temple alignments. Greek astronomy culminated in Ptolemy's geocentric model in the Almagest (2nd century CE), which dominated for over a millennium. Chinese astronomers kept meticulous records of comets, novae, and sunspots; Indian and later Islamic scholars made major advances in trigonometry and observational instruments.

The Copernican Revolution

In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus published De revolutionibus, proposing a heliocentric cosmos. Tycho Brahe's precise observations enabled Johannes Kepler to formulate his laws of planetary motion (1609–1619). Galileo Galilei's telescopic discoveries of 1609–10—Jupiter's moons, the phases of Venus—undermined the old cosmology, and Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation (1687) provided its physical foundation.

Modern astronomy

The 19th century brought spectroscopy and photography, revealing the composition and motion of stars. In the 1920s Edwin Hubble showed that galaxies lie far beyond the Milky Way and that the universe is expanding. Radio astronomy, space telescopes such as Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope, and the detection of exoplanets and gravitational waves have continued to transform the field.
