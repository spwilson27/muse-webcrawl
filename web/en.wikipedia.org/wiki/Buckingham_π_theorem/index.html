Buckingham π theorem

Buckingham π theorem
FieldDimensional analysis, applied mathematics
Key resultA relation among n variables in k independent dimensions can be rewritten using n − k dimensionless groups
Named forEdgar Buckingham (1914)

In engineering, applied mathematics, and physics, the Buckingham π theorem is a key theorem in dimensional analysis. It states that if there is a physically meaningful equation involving n physical variables, and these variables are expressed in terms of k independent fundamental physical quantities, then the original equation can be rewritten in terms of a set of p = n − k dimensionless parameters π1, π2, …, πp constructed from the original variables. The theorem, published by the American physicist Edgar Buckingham in 1914 (building on work by Aimé Vaschy and others), provides a method for computing sets of dimensionless parameters from given variables, even if the form of the equation is unknown.

Application

The theorem underlies the systematic use of dimensionless numbers in fluid mechanics and heat transfer — such as the Reynolds number and Nusselt number — and the design of scale-model experiments (similitude): a model and prototype behave identically when their governing π groups are equal. A classic worked example analyses the period of a simple pendulum in terms of its length, bob mass and gravitational acceleration, showing that the mass cannot appear in the final law and that the period must be proportional to √(l/g).

Formal statement

Informally: if a physical law can be written as f(q1, …, qn) = 0, where the qi involve k independent dimensions, then there exists a function F of the p = n − k dimensionless products πi such that F(π1, …, πp) = 0, and every dimensionless product of the variables is a product of powers of the πi.
