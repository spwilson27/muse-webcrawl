Overview

The Uyghurs are a Turkic ethnic group native to the Xinjiang region of northwestern China, centred on the oasis cities of the Tarim Basin (Kashgar, Hotan, Aksu, Turpan) and the regional capital Ürümqi. They speak Uyghur, a Karluk Turkic language written in a modified Arabic script, and are predominantly Sunni Muslims. Uyghur identity crystallised from the Uyghur Khaganate (744–840) and the later Qocho and Karakhanid states that Islamised the region between the 10th and 16th centuries.

Modern situation

Xinjiang was incorporated into the Qing empire in the 1750s, experienced two short-lived East Turkestan republics (1933–34, 1944–49), and became the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region of the PRC in 1955. Since 2016–17, China has conducted a mass detention campaign — internment camps that Beijing called vocational training centres — alongside pervasive surveillance, forced-labour allegations and restrictions on religion and language; the UN Human Rights Office's 2022 assessment found the abuses may constitute crimes against humanity, several Western parliaments and the US government have used the term genocide, and China rejects the characterisations as lies, attributing its policies to counter-terrorism after attacks such as Ürümqi 2009 and Kunming 2014. Diaspora organisations campaign for Uyghur rights from Munich, Washington and Istanbul.
