Pancho Villa Expedition

The Pancho Villa Expedition — now known officially in the United States as the Mexican Expedition — was a military operation conducted by the United States Army against the paramilitary forces of the Mexican revolutionary Francisco "Pancho" Villa from 14 March 1916 to 7 February 1917, during the Mexican Revolution.

Background

On 9 March 1916 Villa's men raided the border town of Columbus, New Mexico, killing American soldiers and civilians and looting the town. President Woodrow Wilson ordered a punitive expedition under Brigadier General John J. Pershing to capture Villa and disperse his forces.

The campaign

Some 10,000 American troops penetrated several hundred kilometres into Chihuahua. The expedition pioneered the US Army's use of motor trucks and aircraft — the Curtiss JN-3s of the 1st Aero Squadron — in field operations. American forces won skirmishes at Guerrero and elsewhere but never caught Villa, and in June 1916 they clashed with Mexican federal troops at Carrizal, nearly bringing the two countries to war. With the United States entering the First World War, the expedition was withdrawn in February 1917.

Legacy

Though it failed to capture Villa, the expedition served as a mobilisation and training exercise that prepared officers — including the young George S. Patton, who led an early motorised raid — and equipment for the American Expeditionary Forces in Europe.
