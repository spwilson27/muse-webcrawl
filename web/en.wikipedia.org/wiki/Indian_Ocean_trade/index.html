Indian Ocean trade

Indian Ocean trade refers to the maritime trade networks that linked East Africa, the Middle East, South Asia and Southeast Asia (and, by extension, China) across the Indian Ocean over many centuries. Before the rise of the Atlantic economy, it was among the largest and richest trading systems in the world, carried on monsoon winds in vessels such as the dhow and the Chinese junk.

The monsoon system

Trade depended on the seasonal reversal of the monsoon winds: the southwest monsoon carried ships from Arabia and East Africa toward India in the summer, while the northeast monsoon carried them back in winter. Greek and Roman sailors learned to exploit this pattern — the 1st-century CE Periplus of the Erythraean Sea describes ports and goods from the Red Sea to India — and Roman demand for spices, silk and ivory drew Indian Ocean goods into the Mediterranean world.

Medieval networks

From the 7th century onwards, Muslim merchants from Arabia and Persia dominated much of the trade, spreading Islam along the Swahili Coast of East Africa and into Southeast Asia. Swahili city-states such as Kilwa grew wealthy exporting gold, ivory and enslaved people and importing Chinese porcelain and Indian textiles. Indian merchants — Gujarati, Tamil and others — traded cotton cloth for spices from the Indonesian archipelago, while Chinese fleets, culminating in the voyages of Zheng He (1405–1433), projected Ming power into the ocean.

European intervention

The arrival of Vasco da Gama at Calicut in 1498 opened a direct sea route from Europe and began a period of Portuguese, then Dutch, French and British competition, conducted largely through chartered companies such as the Dutch East India Company (VOC) and the English East India Company. Europeans inserted themselves into — and gradually militarized and monopolized — the older, largely free trading system, which nonetheless persisted in the hands of Asian and African merchants well into the colonial period.
