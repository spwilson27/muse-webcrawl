The Euro Interbank Offered Rate (EURIBOR) is a daily reference rate, published by the European Money Markets Institute, based on the averaged interest rates at which Eurozone banks offer to lend unsecured funds to other banks in the euro wholesale money market. It is a benchmark for an enormous volume of mortgages, loans, and interest-rate derivatives denominated in euros.

Calculation

EURIBOR is published for five maturities (1 week and 1, 3, 6 and 12 months) on each TARGET business day. Following reforms after the benchmark-manipulation scandals of the 2010s, the rate is computed using a "hybrid methodology" anchored in actual transactions by a panel of banks, with waterfall fallbacks to related transactions and expert judgment. The highest and lowest 15% of submissions have historically been trimmed from the average.

History and use

EURIBOR was first published on 30 December 1998, just before the launch of the euro on 1 January 1999. It is distinct from €STR, the overnight risk-free rate published by the European Central Bank. Variable-rate mortgages in countries such as Spain, Italy and Finland commonly track 12-month EURIBOR, so movements in the rate directly affect household payments. EURIBOR differs from the defunct LIBOR in being based on euro interbank lending and in having survived post-scandal reform as a regulated EU benchmark.
