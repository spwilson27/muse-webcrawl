The Nazarene movement was an early 19th-century German Romantic school of painting, founded in 1809 in Vienna by a group of young artists who sought to revive honesty and spirituality in art. Reacting against the perceived artificiality and virtuoso emptiness of late Neoclassicism taught in the academies, the Nazarenes looked back to the art of the late Middle Ages and the early Renaissance — to painters such as Fra Angelico, Perugino and the young Raphael — as models of sincere, devotional picture-making.

History

In 1809, six students at the Vienna Academy, dissatisfied with their training, formed the Lukasbund (Brotherhood of St. Luke), taking the medieval painters' guild patron as their emblem. The leading spirits were Friedrich Overbeck and Franz Pforr. In 1810 four of the brothers moved to Rome, where they took up residence in the abandoned monastery of Sant'Isidoro on the Pincian Hill, living a quasi-monastic communal life. Their long hair and archaic dress prompted the Romans to nickname them I Nazareni — the Nazarenes — after the hairstyle associated with depictions of Christ and his followers.

In Rome the circle expanded to include Peter von Cornelius, Julius Schnorr von Carolsfeld, Wilhelm von Schadow and others. Two major fresco commissions established their reputation: the decoration of the Casa Bartholdy (1816–17), home of the Prussian consul Jakob Salomon Bartholdy, with scenes from the life of Joseph; and the Casino Massimo frescoes (1819–30), illustrating Dante, Tasso and Ariosto. These works made the Nazarenes the most influential school of German painting of their generation.

Style and ideals

Nazarene painting is characterised by clear outlines, deliberate composition, subdued and often deliberately "primitive" colour, and an avoidance of dramatic chiaroscuro in favour of a calm, legible surface. The Nazarenes held that art should serve religion and moral edification; many of the artists converted to Catholicism, as Overbeck did in 1813. Their programme joined a revival of fresco painting with a revival of national German subject matter and folk piety.

Legacy

Although the brotherhood dissolved as its members took up professorships in Düsseldorf, Munich and elsewhere, Nazarene ideals profoundly influenced 19th-century religious art throughout Europe and fed into later movements, including the English Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, whose members admired Overbeck's work. The Nazarenes' emphasis on sincerity, craftsmanship and medieval models anticipated the Arts and Crafts movement.
