Satellite laser ranging

Satellite laser ranging (SLR) is a technique for measuring the distance between a ground station and a satellite with millimetre-level precision by timing the round trip of short laser pulses reflected from the spacecraft. Satellites used for the purpose carry arrays of corner-cube retroreflectors; dedicated geodetic satellites such as LAGEOS-1 and -2 (launched 1976 and 1992) are dense spheres covered with retroreflectors and serve almost purely as ranging targets.

Principle

A ground station fires picosecond laser pulses at a tracked satellite and times the returning photons with an event timer referenced to an atomic clock. Half the round-trip time multiplied by the speed of light gives the range, corrected for atmospheric delay. Modern systems achieve normal-point precisions of a few millimetres.

Applications

SLR is a cornerstone of geodesy. Together with VLBI, GNSS and DORIS it defines the International Terrestrial Reference Frame (ITRF), measuring station positions and velocities and thereby tectonic plate motion. Ranging to LAGEOS determines Earth's centre of mass (the geocentre), the planet's oblateness and its slow changes, and provides tests of general relativity — including measurement of the Lense–Thirring (frame-dragging) effect. Lunar laser ranging to retroreflectors left by Apollo astronauts is a closely related technique.

Network

About forty stations worldwide contribute data coordinated by the International Laser Ranging Service (ILRS), founded in 1998. Notable stations include Herstmonceux (England), Wettzell (Germany), Greenbelt (USA) and Yarragadee (Australia), the latter among the most productive.
