Economic policy refers to the actions governments and other authorities take to influence the economy. It encompasses the setting of taxation and public spending, the supply of money and interest rates, trade rules, labour-market regulation, competition policy and the provision of public goods. Its usual aims include stable growth, low inflation, high employment, a sustainable balance of payments and an acceptable distribution of income.

Types of policy

Fiscal policy uses government revenue and expenditure: expansionary budgets (tax cuts or spending increases) are used to stimulate demand in recessions, while contractionary budgets aim to cool inflation or reduce public debt. Monetary policy, usually conducted by an independent central bank, steers interest rates and the money supply to meet targets such as an inflation rate of around 2 per cent. Supply-side policies — education, infrastructure, deregulation and competition reform — seek to raise the economy's productive capacity. Other fields include trade policy (tariffs and agreements), exchange-rate policy, industrial policy and macroprudential regulation of the financial system.

Debates

The proper scale and tools of economic policy are perennially contested. Keynesian economics favours active demand management, while monetarists and later new classical economists emphasised rules, expectations and the limits of discretion. Policy must also contend with implementation lags, political pressures and the possibility that measures such as deficit spending or money creation will raise inflation if overused.
