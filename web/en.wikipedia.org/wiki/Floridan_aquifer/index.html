Floridan aquifer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Floridan aquifer system is one of the world's most productive aquifers and a principal source of water supply in the southeastern United States. It underlies the entire state of Florida, as well as parts of southern Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and South Carolina, covering about 100,000 square miles (260,000 km²). The aquifer consists of thick sequences of Cenozoic carbonate rocks — mainly limestone and dolomite — and supplies drinking water to roughly ten million people as well as water for agriculture and industry.

Hydrogeology

The system is divided into the Upper Floridan aquifer, a middle confining unit, and the Lower Floridan aquifer. In northern and central Florida the Upper Floridan is unconfined or thinly confined, highly karstified, and recharged directly by rainfall; its abundant dissolution openings feed Florida's famous artesian springs, including Silver Springs and Wakulla Springs. Toward the coasts and southward the aquifer becomes more deeply buried and confined, and water quality declines, becoming brackish in the Lower Floridan. In South Florida the shallow Biscayne aquifer, a separate system, supplies the Miami metropolitan area.

Use and concerns

Pumping for growing cities and irrigation has lowered water levels in some areas, reduced spring flows, and caused localised saltwater intrusion near the coasts. Florida's water management districts regulate withdrawals and promote conservation, aquifer recharge and alternative supplies to protect springs and wetlands dependent on aquifer discharge, including those of the Everglades region.
