Libretto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A libretto (Italian for "little book"; plural libretti) is the text of an opera, operetta, masque, oratorio, cantata or musical — comprising all the words of the sung and spoken parts, as distinct from the music itself. The author of a libretto is a librettist. The term is also used for the printed booklet containing the text, historically sold to audiences and now often replaced by projected surtitles.

Librettists and composers

Some librettists achieved lasting fame through their partnerships with composers: Pietro Metastasio, whose opera seria texts were set dozens of times in the eighteenth century; Lorenzo Da Ponte, who wrote The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni and Così fan tutte for Mozart; Arrigo Boito for Verdi; and W. S. Gilbert for Arthur Sullivan. Some composers wrote their own libretti, most famously Richard Wagner, while in musical theatre the libretto ("book") is distinguished from lyrics and score. Sources for libretti range from classical myth and history to plays and novels, usually heavily adapted to musical and dramatic requirements.

Characteristics

A libretto is structured in acts and scenes and mixes recitative or dialogue with closed musical numbers — arias, duets, ensembles and choruses. Because sung text is less intelligible and slower than speech, libretti generally use simpler, more repetitive language than spoken drama, a constraint that has often made them targets of literary criticism even when the resulting works are masterpieces.
