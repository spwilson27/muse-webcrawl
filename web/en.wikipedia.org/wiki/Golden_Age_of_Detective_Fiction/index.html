Golden Age of Detective Fiction

The Golden Age of Detective Fiction was an era of classic murder-mystery writing, principally between the First and Second World Wars — roughly the 1920s and 1930s. Dominated by British writers, its hallmark form was the "whodunit": a puzzle-mystery in which the reader is given the clues and invited to match wits with the detective. Its four most celebrated practitioners are remembered as the "Queens of Crime": Agatha Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, Margery Allingham, and Ngaio Marsh.

Golden Age mysteries emphasized fair-play puzzle plotting and fair presentation of clues.

Conventions

Golden Age fiction codified rules of "fair play": Ronald Knox's tongue-in-cheek "Ten Commandments" (1929) and S. S. Van Dine's "Twenty Rules" insisted the reader must have equal opportunity with the detective, and banned devices such as secret passages used unfairly or supernatural solutions. Typical settings were closed circles of suspects — country houses, trains, vicarages, and colleges — and typical plots hinged on timetables, alibis, wills, and poisons. London's Detection Club, whose members included Christie, Sayers, and G. K. Chesterton, embodied the era's game-like ethos.

Major works

Landmarks of the period include Christie's The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926), famous for its audacious narrator, Murder on the Orient Express (1934), and And Then There Were None (1939) — the best-selling mystery novel of all time. Sayers brought literary ambition to the form in Gaudy Night (1935), while John Dickson Carr perfected the locked-room mystery and Michael Innes and Josephine Tey stretched the genre's boundaries.

Decline and legacy

The harder-edged American hardboiled school of Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler offered a rival model, and after 1939 tastes shifted toward psychological suspense and the police procedural. The Golden Age puzzle nevertheless endures in continuing popularity, countless pastiches, and modern revivals such as the British Library Crime Classics reissue series.
