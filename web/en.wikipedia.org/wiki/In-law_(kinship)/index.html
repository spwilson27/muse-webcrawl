In-law (kinship)

An in-law is a person related by marriage rather than by blood — a relationship anthropologists call affinity, as opposed to consanguinity. The most common in-law relationships are with the parents and siblings of one's spouse: a mother-in-law, father-in-law, brother-in-law, and sister-in-law. English extends the suffix "-in-law" to these relations; the term comes from canon law, which treated the spouse's relatives as one's own for purposes such as prohibited degrees of marriage — the relationship existed "in law" if not in blood.

Marriage joins two families and creates a network of in-law relationships.

Types of in-law relationships

A parent-in-law is the parent of one's spouse; a child-in-law (son-in-law or daughter-in-law) is the spouse of one's child. A sibling-in-law may be the sibling of one's spouse or the spouse of one's sibling. Some languages and cultures make finer distinctions: many distinguish, for example, between the husband's parents and the wife's parents, or have specific terms for the relationship between the two sets of parents of a married couple, such as Spanish consuegros or the Yiddish machatonim.

Social and legal aspects

In-law relationships carry obligations that vary widely by culture — from expected deference and co-residence with parents-in-law in parts of Asia, to largely voluntary ties in most Western societies. The mother-in-law relationship is a stock subject of humour in many cultures. Legally, affinity historically barred marriage between certain in-laws even after a spouse's death, and in-law relations can still matter for inheritance, immigration sponsorship, and next-of-kin status, though rules differ by jurisdiction.
