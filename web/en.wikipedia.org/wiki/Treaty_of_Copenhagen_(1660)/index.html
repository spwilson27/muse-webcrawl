Treaty of Copenhagen (1660)

Signed6 June 1660
LocationCopenhagen, Denmark
PartiesDenmark–Norway; Swedish Empire
ContextSecond Northern War

The Treaty of Copenhagen was a peace treaty signed on 6 June 1660 between the Swedish Empire and Denmark–Norway, concluding the Danish theatre of the Second Northern War. It largely confirmed the settlement of the Treaty of Roskilde (1658), with the significant exception that Denmark recovered the island of Bornholm and the province of Trøndelag in Norway.

Scandinavia after the Treaty of Copenhagen

Background

Under the Treaty of Roskilde in 1658, Denmark–Norway had been forced to cede Scania, Halland, Blekinge, Bohuslän, Bornholm, and Trøndelag to Sweden after Charles X Gustav's march across the frozen Danish straits. Dissatisfied, Charles X Gustav renewed the war in 1658, besieging Copenhagen, but the city held out with Dutch naval support, and Sweden's position weakened. The king's sudden death in February 1660 opened the way to peace, negotiated under the regency for the young Charles XI, with France, England, and the Dutch Republic pressing for a settlement that preserved a balance of power in the Baltic.

Terms

Sweden kept the former Danish provinces east of the Sound — Scania, Halland, and Blekinge — as well as Bohuslän, permanently redrawing the map of Scandinavia; the Scanian lands remain Swedish today. Denmark recovered Bornholm, whose inhabitants had already risen against their Swedish garrison in 1658, and Trøndelag in central Norway. The related Treaty of Oliva, concluded the same spring, settled Sweden's war with Poland-Lithuania and Brandenburg.

Consequences

The peace confirmed Sweden as the dominant Baltic power and Denmark's permanent loss of its eastern provinces. In Denmark, the war's fiscal crisis enabled Frederick III to carry out a coup in 1660 that established hereditary absolute monarchy. The settlement's Baltic balance endured until the Great Northern War two generations later.
