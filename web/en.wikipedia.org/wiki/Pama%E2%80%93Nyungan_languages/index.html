Pama–Nyungan languages

Geographic distributionMost of mainland Australia
Languages~300 historically
Proto-languageProto-Pama–Nyungan
OriginPossibly Gulf Plains, Queensland, ~4,000–6,000 years ago

The Pama–Nyungan languages form the largest family of Australian Aboriginal languages, historically spoken across roughly seven-eighths of the Australian mainland — everywhere except the far north-west (the Kimberley and Top End), where non-Pama–Nyungan families predominate.

Classification and spread

Proposed by Kenneth Hale in the 1960s and named for the words for "man" in its north-eastern (Pama) and south-western (Nyungan) extremes, the family comprises some 300 languages in numerous subgroups, including Yolngu, Western Desert, Arrernte, and the languages of the southeast. Its speakers likely spread across the continent within the last several thousand years, possibly aided by new technologies and social changes.

Features and status

Pama–Nyungan languages commonly feature ergative case marking, avoidance registers such as mother-in-law speech, and elaborate kinship semantics. European colonization devastated the family: most of its languages are now endangered or extinct, though revitalization efforts are under way for languages such as Kaurna and Wiradjuri. The best-known member internationally is Pitjantjatjara of the Western Desert group.
