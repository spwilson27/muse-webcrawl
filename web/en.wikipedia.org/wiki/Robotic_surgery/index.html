Robotic surgery

Other namesRobot-assisted surgery
SpecialtySurgery
Major systemda Vinci Surgical System

Robotic surgery, or robot-assisted surgery, allows doctors to perform many types of complex procedures with more precision, flexibility and control than is possible with conventional techniques. The surgeon operates from a console, viewing a magnified three-dimensional image of the surgical field and moving master controls whose motions are scaled and translated to robotic arms and wristed instruments inside the patient, typically through minimally invasive incisions.

History

Early surgical robots included the PUMA-based systems of the 1980s and PROBOT and ROBODOC in the early 1990s. The field was transformed by the da Vinci system, developed by Intuitive Surgical from research at SRI International and cleared by the U.S. FDA in 2000, which came to dominate soft-tissue robotic surgery in urology, gynaecology, general and cardiothoracic surgery.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages include tremor filtration, wristed dexterity exceeding the human hand, ergonomic surgeon positioning and smaller incisions with less blood loss and faster recovery for many procedures. Limitations include high capital and per-case costs, longer setup times in some settings, loss of direct haptic (force) feedback, and evidence questions about whether outcomes justify cost for many common operations.

Telesurgery

Because the console and patient cart are separable, robotic surgery permits remote operation; in the 2001 "Lindbergh operation" a gallbladder was removed from a patient in Strasbourg by surgeons in New York over a dedicated fibre link.
