Ice giant

An ice giant is a giant planet composed mainly of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium, such as oxygen, carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur. There are two ice giants in the Solar System: Uranus and Neptune. The term, in use since the 1990s, reflects their difference from the gas giants Jupiter and Saturn, which are more than 90% hydrogen and helium by mass.

Uranus (left) and Neptune, the Solar System's two ice giants, seen by Voyager 2.

Composition

Ice giants are roughly 20% hydrogen and helium by mass; most of their bulk consists of "ices" — in planetary-science usage, volatile compounds such as water, methane, and ammonia — which in their interiors exist not as solid ice but as a hot, dense supercritical fluid forming a slushy mantle around a rocky core. This ionic ocean is thought to generate their unusually offset, multipolar magnetic fields. Their blue-green colours come from atmospheric methane absorbing red light.

Exoplanets

Planets of ice-giant mass (roughly 10–20 Earth masses) are among the most common exoplanets found by surveys such as Kepler — the "mini-Neptune" or sub-Neptune class — even though the Solar System's two examples lie far from the Sun. Their prevalence makes understanding Uranus and Neptune important for comparative planetology, and a dedicated orbiter mission to Uranus was made the highest-priority flagship in the 2022 U.S. planetary science decadal survey.
