Wisdom literature is a genre of literature common in the ancient Near East. It consists of statements by sages and wise men that offer teachings about divinity and virtue. Although this genre uses writing techniques of traditional storytelling, disseminating wisdom in a didactic fashion, it was drawn from oral tradition, sayings and folk literature.

In the Hebrew Bible

The canonical examples in the Hebrew Bible are the Book of Proverbs, the Book of Job, and Ecclesiastes; the shorter Song of Songs, some Psalms, and Lamentations have also been classed as wisdom literature. Wisdom books focus less on Israel's covenant history than on universal questions: how to live well, why the righteous suffer, and the limits of human understanding. The deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach extend the genre into the Hellenistic period.

Comparative background

Egyptian "instructions" (sebayt), such as the Instruction of Ptahhotep, and Mesopotamian texts like the Babylonian Theodicy show close parallels; scholars debate the extent of direct influence on Israelite wisdom writing, though the international character of the genre is widely accepted.
