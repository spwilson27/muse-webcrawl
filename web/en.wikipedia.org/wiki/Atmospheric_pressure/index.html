Atmospheric pressure, also known as air pressure or barometric pressure, is the pressure within the atmosphere of Earth. It is caused by the gravitational attraction of the planet on the atmospheric gases above a surface, and is a function of the mass of the gas, the altitude, and local factors such as temperature and weather systems. The standard atmosphere (symbol: atm) is a unit of pressure defined as 101,325 Pa (1,013.25 hPa), which is equivalent to 1,013.25 millibars, 760 mm Hg, or 29.9212 inches Hg.

Atmospheric pressure is closely approximated by the hydrostatic pressure caused by the weight of air above the measurement point. As elevation increases, there is less overlying atmospheric mass, so pressure decreases; roughly, it halves for every 5.5 km (18,000 ft) of ascent in the lower atmosphere.

History

In 1643, Evangelista Torricelli invented the mercury barometer and demonstrated that air has weight, overturning the Aristotelian doctrine that nature abhors a vacuum. Blaise Pascal's brother-in-law carried a barometer up the Puy de Dôme in 1648, confirming that pressure falls with altitude. Otto von Guericke's Magdeburg hemispheres (1654) dramatically demonstrated the force of air pressure.

Variation and weather

Surface pressure varies with weather systems: high-pressure areas (anticyclones) generally bring settled weather, while low-pressure areas (cyclones) bring cloud and precipitation. The highest recorded sea-level pressure was 1,083.8 hPa in Mongolia in 2004; the lowest in a non-tornadic system was 870 hPa in Typhoon Tip in 1979. On weather maps, lines of equal pressure are called isobars.

Effects

Pressure determines the boiling point of liquids — water boils at about 68 °C at the summit of Mount Everest — and affects human physiology at altitude, where reduced partial pressure of oxygen causes altitude sickness. Aircraft altimeters and weather forecasting both rely on precise pressure measurement.
