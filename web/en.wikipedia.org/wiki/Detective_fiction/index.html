Detective fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A detective's desk in lamplight: magnifying glass, notebook, a single gloved handprint in talc on a photograph]

Detective fiction is a subgenre of crime and mystery fiction in which an investigator — professional or amateur — unravels a crime, usually a murder, from clues the narrative fairly (or nearly) conceals and reveals. It is conventionally dated from Edgar Allan Poe's 'The Murders in the Rue Morgue' (1841), whose C. Auguste Dupin founded the type: the brilliant reasoner and his admiring narrator, inherited by Arthur Conan Doyle for Sherlock Holmes (from 1887).

The genre's Golden Age (roughly the 1920s–30s) — Agatha Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, Margery Allingham, Ngaio Marsh — codified the puzzle plot of closed circles, red herrings and final-chapter assembly; the American hardboiled school (Hammett, Chandler) simultaneously took detection to the mean streets.

The rules of the game

Golden Age detection was codified as fair play — Knox's 'Ten Commandments' (1929), Van Dine's twenty rules — the reader given every clue the detective sees. Christie, the best-selling novelist of all time, perfected and then delighted in subverting the forms (the narrator-as-murderer of The Murder of Roger Ackroyd, 1926, remains the genre's great scandal); And Then There Were None (1939) is the best-selling mystery ever published, by the usual accounting.

After the Golden Age

The form ramified: police procedurals (from Ed McBain's 87th Precinct to the Scandinavian wave — Sjöwall and Wahlöö, then Stieg Larsson and Jo Nesbø), the psychological suspense of Patricia Highsmith and Ruth Rendell, historical detection, and the contemporary 'Nordic noir' and domestic-thriller booms. Through all mutations the contract holds: disorder in chapter one, order — of a moral or merely intellectual kind — by the last page.
