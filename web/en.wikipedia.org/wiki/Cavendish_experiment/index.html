Cavendish experiment

Performed byHenry Cavendish
Date1797–1798
Published1798, Philosophical Transactions
MeasuredMean density (mass) of the Earth
ResultEarth density ≈ 5.448 × water

The Cavendish experiment, performed in 1797–98 by English scientist Henry Cavendish, was the first experiment to measure the force of gravity between masses in the laboratory and the first to yield accurate values for the density of the Earth. Because of the unit conventions then in use, the gravitational constant G does not appear explicitly in Cavendish's work; his stated goal was to determine the Earth's density, from which its mass could be calculated — the experiment is often described as "weighing the Earth".

Apparatus

The apparatus, originally designed by the Reverend John Michell, consisted of a torsion balance: a horizontal wooden rod about 1.8 m long with a small lead sphere (about 5 cm diameter, 0.73 kg) at each end, suspended from a fine silvered-copper wire. Two large lead spheres (about 30 cm diameter, 158 kg each) were placed near the small spheres, on opposite sides, so that their gravitational attraction twisted the rod through a small angle. The whole apparatus was enclosed in a mahogany case to exclude air currents, and Cavendish observed the deflection remotely with a telescope from another room to avoid disturbing it.

Method and results

From the measured deflection and the torsion constant of the wire (found from the period of oscillation of the balance), Cavendish computed the tiny gravitational force between the spheres — roughly 1/50,000,000 of their weight. Comparing this force with the weight of the small spheres gave the Earth's mean density. Cavendish reported a value of 5.448 times the density of water, remarkably close to the modern accepted value of about 5.51. The experiment also allowed later scientists to derive the gravitational constant G; the first explicit value appeared in the work of Cornu and Baille in 1873.

Legacy

The Cavendish experiment established that Newton's law of universal gravitation applies to ordinary laboratory masses, and versions of the torsion-balance method dominated measurements of G for two centuries. The experiment is commemorated in the name of the Cavendish Laboratory at the University of Cambridge.
