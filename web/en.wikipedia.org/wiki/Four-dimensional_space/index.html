Four-dimensional space

Four-dimensional space (4D) is the mathematical extension of the concept of three-dimensional space. Whereas points in three dimensions are located by three coordinates, a four-dimensional Euclidean space requires four coordinates, conventionally (x, y, z, w). The idea generalises the familiar pattern: a line has one dimension, a plane two, and ordinary space three.

Geometry

Four-dimensional geometry studies objects such as the tesseract (the 4D analogue of the cube), the hypersphere, and the six convex regular 4-polytopes, which go beyond the five Platonic solids of three dimensions. Rotations in four dimensions are richer than in three: a rotation can occur in a plane, and two independent rotations can happen simultaneously (a double rotation), so a general rotation is specified by two angles rather than one axis and one angle.

History

Mathematicians began treating the fourth dimension seriously in the nineteenth century. Charles Howard Hinton popularised the terms tesseract and coined "ana" and "kata" for directions into and out of the fourth dimension. Edwin Abbott's novella Flatland (1884) used analogy with lower dimensions to make the concept vivid. In physics, Hermann Minkowski showed in 1908 that Einstein's special relativity could be elegantly formulated in a four-dimensional spacetime, where the fourth coordinate is time rather than a fourth spatial direction.

Perception

Humans cannot directly perceive a fourth spatial dimension, but four-dimensional objects can be studied through projections and cross-sections, much as a three-dimensional object casts a two-dimensional shadow. Researchers have found that with practice people can develop intuitions for navigating four-dimensional virtual environments.
