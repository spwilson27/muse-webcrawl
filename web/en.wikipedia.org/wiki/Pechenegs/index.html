Pechenegs

The Pechenegs dominated the Pontic steppe between the Danube and the Don.

The Pechenegs were a semi-nomadic Turkic people of the Central Asian steppe who, from around the 9th to the 11th century, controlled the grasslands north of the Black Sea, between the Danube and the Don. They spoke Pecheneg, an Oghuz Turkic language now extinct.

History

Driven westward by the Oghuz and other peoples, the Pechenegs crossed the Volga around the 890s and displaced the Magyars, pushing them into the Carpathian Basin. For two centuries they were a major power in the politics of Kievan Rus', the Byzantine Empire, and Hungary: Byzantine diplomacy, described in Constantine VII's De administrando imperio, treated them as a key counterweight to the Rus' and Bulgarians, while the Rus' princes alternately fought them (Sviatoslav I was killed by Pechenegs in 972) and hired them as mercenaries.

Pecheneg power was broken by converging pressures. In 1091 the Byzantines under Alexios I Komnenos, allied with the Cumans, annihilated a Pecheneg army at the Battle of Levounion — a defeat chroniclers described as the destruction of a whole people. The survivors were settled as frontier soldiers in the Balkans or absorbed by neighbouring peoples, and the Cumans took their place on the steppe.
