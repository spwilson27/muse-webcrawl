Francisco "Pancho" Villa (born José Doroteo Arango Arámbula; 5 June 1878 – 20 July 1923) was a Mexican revolutionary leader and general in the Mexican Revolution. As commander of the División del Norte (Division of the North) in the Constitutional Army, he was a charismatic and highly effective cavalry commander and a provisional governor of Chihuahua in 1913–14.

Revolutionary career

Arango, a peon-turned-bandit who adopted the name Francisco "Pancho" Villa, joined Francisco Madero's uprising against the dictator Porfirio Díaz in 1910. In the second phase of the revolution he built the División del Norte into the largest revolutionary army in Mexico, winning major victories at Ciudad Juárez, Torreón, Zacatecas and elsewhere with daring cavalry operations and effective use of railways and artillery.

Defeat and the Columbus raid

After Venustiano Carranza's forces, led by Álvaro Obregón, crushed Villa's army at the Battle of Celaya in 1915, Villa's strength waned. On 9 March 1916 his men raided Columbus, New Mexico, killing Americans and prompting the United States to launch the Punitive Expedition under General John J. Pershing, which failed to capture him. Villa retired in 1920 under an agreement with the government, settling at the hacienda of Canutillo in Durango.

Death and legacy

Villa was assassinated in 1923 while driving in Parral, Chihuahua. He remains one of the most famous figures of the Mexican Revolution — celebrated in corridos and film as a champion of the poor (he even signed a contract with a Hollywood studio in 1914 to film his battles) and condemned by critics for banditry and brutality. He was reinterred in the Monument to the Revolution in Mexico City in 1976.
