Venetian Cyprus was the period of Venetian rule over the island of Cyprus, from 1489 until the Ottoman conquest of 1570–1571. Cyprus was Venice's last major overseas possession in the eastern Mediterranean, valued for its sugar, cotton, wine and salt production and for its strategic position on trade routes to the Levant.

Acquisition

Venice's influence grew through the 15th century as the Lusignan kingdom declined. In 1468 James II became king and married the Venetian noblewoman Caterina Cornaro. After James's death and that of their infant son, Caterina reigned as queen, effectively under Venetian control. In 1489 she was persuaded — under considerable pressure — to abdicate and cede Cyprus to the Republic of Venice, receiving the town of Asolo in compensation. The banner of Saint Mark was raised in Famagusta, and Cyprus formally became a Venetian colony.

Venetian rule

The island was administered by Venetian officials answerable to the Senate, with a lieutenant (luogotenente) resident in Nicosia. Venice exploited Cyprus's agricultural wealth, exporting sugar from plantations at Kolossi and Episkopi, Commandaria wine, cotton and salt from the Larnaca salt lake. The Greek Orthodox population was subordinated to a Latin ecclesiastical hierarchy, and heavy taxation and feudal obligations provoked periodic peasant unrest.

Anticipating Ottoman attack, Venice invested heavily in fortifications: the great walls of Nicosia were rebuilt from 1567 in a compact star-fort design by Giulio Savorgnan, demolishing much of the medieval city, while Famagusta's defences were strengthened.

Ottoman conquest

In 1570 an Ottoman expedition under Lala Mustafa Pasha invaded. Nicosia fell in September 1570 with a massacre of its inhabitants; Famagusta held out under Marcantonio Bragadin until August 1571, capitulating on terms that were then violated — Bragadin was flayed alive. The loss of Cyprus, despite the Christian victory at Lepanto two months later, ended nearly a century of Venetian rule.
