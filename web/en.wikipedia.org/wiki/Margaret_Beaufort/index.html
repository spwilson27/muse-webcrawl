Lady Margaret Beaufort (31 May 1443 – 29 June 1509) was a major figure in the Wars of the Roses and the mother of King Henry VII of England, the founder of the Tudor dynasty. A wealthy heiress and a descendant of John of Gaunt, she was a key political player who worked tirelessly to place her only son on the English throne.

Early life and marriages

Margaret was the daughter and heiress of John Beaufort, 1st Duke of Somerset, a great-grandson of Edward III. Her father died when she was an infant, leaving her one of the richest heiresses in England. She was first married as a small child to John de la Pole, a marriage later annulled. In 1455 she married Edmund Tudor, 1st Earl of Richmond, half-brother of Henry VI. Edmund died of plague in captivity in November 1456, leaving the thirteen-year-old Margaret pregnant. On 28 January 1457 she gave birth to her only child, Henry Tudor, at Pembroke Castle in Wales — a delivery so early in her life that it may have left her unable to bear further children.

Later marriages

Margaret subsequently married Sir Henry Stafford (died 1471) and then, in 1472, Thomas Stanley, later 1st Earl of Derby. Through careful political manoeuvring she survived the turbulent reigns of Edward IV and Richard III. Although she outwardly served at court, she conspired with Elizabeth Woodville to marry her son Henry to Elizabeth of York, uniting the rival claims of Lancaster and York.

The accession of Henry VII

After Henry's victory at the Battle of Bosworth Field in 1485, Margaret was styled "My Lady the King's Mother" and wielded considerable influence at court, though she never took the title of queen. She took a vow of chastity in 1499 while remaining married to Stanley, an unusual step requiring special permission. Known for her piety and learning, she was a noted patron of education.

Patronage and legacy

Margaret founded Christ's College, Cambridge (1505), and endowed St John's College, Cambridge, which was established after her death using her estate. She also founded a chair of divinity at Cambridge (the Lady Margaret's Professorship of Divinity) and patronised the printers William Caxton and Wynkyn de Worde. She died on 29 June 1509, just two months after her son Henry VII, and was buried in the Henry VII Chapel in Westminster Abbey. See also Henry VII of England and Wars of the Roses.
