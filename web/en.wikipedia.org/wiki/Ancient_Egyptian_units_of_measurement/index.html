Ancient Egyptian units of measurement

The ancient Egyptian units of measurement are those used by the dynasties of ancient Egypt prior to its incorporation into the Roman Empire and general adoption of Roman, Greek, and Byzantine units. The units of length seem to have originally been anthropic, based on the human forearm, hand, and fingers, and were standardised using rods of wood or stone — most famously the royal cubit.

The royal cubit, used in monumental architecture including the Great Pyramid of Giza, was subdivided into seven palms of four digits each. Surviving cubit rods in museums, such as the wooden rod of the architect Kha, confirm a length of about 52.3–52.9 cm.

Area, volume, and weight

The standard unit of area was the setat, a square of one khet (100 cubits) on a side, about 2,756 m², subdivided into cubit strips. Land measurement was a central function of the state, fields being re-surveyed after each Nile inundation. For volume, grain and liquids were measured in the hekat (about 4.8 litres), divided into fractions expressed through the famous "Horus eye" series of binary fractions. Weights were expressed in the deben (about 91 g in the New Kingdom) and its tenth, the qedet or kite.

Time

The Egyptians divided the year into 12 months of 30 days plus five epagomenal days, grouped into three seasons of four months — Akhet (inundation), Peret (growing), and Shemu (harvest). The day was divided into 12 hours of daylight and 12 of night, whose lengths varied seasonally, tracked with shadow clocks and water clocks.
