Jarrow

Location: South Tyneside, Tyne and Wear, England
Population: approx. 43,000

Jarrow is a town in South Tyneside, in the county of Tyne and Wear, north-east England, on the south bank of the River Tyne near its mouth. It has a history stretching from the Anglo-Saxon period to a major shipbuilding era, and is best known internationally for the Jarrow March of 1936.

History

Jarrow was the site of the monastery of St Paul, founded in 681–682 as the twin house of Monkwearmouth-Jarrow Abbey. The Venerable Bede spent his life there and wrote his Ecclesiastical History of the English People. The surviving church of St Paul's contains Anglo-Saxon fabric and some of the oldest stained glass in the world. In the nineteenth century Jarrow grew rapidly as a coal-shipping and shipbuilding town, dominated by Palmer's shipyard, founded in 1852, which built hundreds of ships.

The Jarrow March

The closure of Palmer's yard in the Great Depression devastated the town. In October 1936, about 200 unemployed men marched from Jarrow to London — the "Jarrow Crusade" — to present a petition to Parliament asking for work. Though the petition was received without immediate policy change, the march became an enduring symbol of the human cost of interwar unemployment and of peaceful protest. MP Ellen Wilkinson, who accompanied the marchers, wrote The Town That Was Murdered about Jarrow's plight.

Jarrow today

Modern Jarrow is a residential and commercial centre within the Tyneside conurbation, served by the Tyne and Wear Metro. The Bede's World museum (now part of Jarrow Hall) interprets the Anglo-Saxon heritage of the site, which together with St Paul's Church is on the UK's tentative list for World Heritage status.
