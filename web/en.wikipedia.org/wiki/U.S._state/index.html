In the United States, a state is a constituent political entity, of which there are currently 50. Bound together in a political union, each state holds governmental jurisdiction over a separate and defined geographic territory where it shares its sovereignty with the federal government. Due to this shared sovereignty, Americans are citizens both of the federal republic and of the state in which they reside.

Government

State governments are allocated power by the people of each state through their individual state constitutions. All are grounded in republican principles, and each provides for a government consisting of three branches — executive, legislative and judicial — mirroring the federal structure. The head of government of each state is an elected governor. State legislatures are bicameral in 49 states; Nebraska's is unicameral.

Admission to the Union

Article IV, Section 3, Clause 1 of the United States Constitution empowers Congress to admit new states into the Union. The original thirteen states were the successors of the Thirteen Colonies that rebelled against British rule. Most later states were created from federal territories; a few — Vermont, Texas and Hawaii among them — were sovereign states in their own right before joining.

The most recent states to be admitted are Alaska and Hawaii, both in 1959. Congress has set as a general principle that new states enter on an "equal footing" with the original thirteen, as articulated in cases such as Coyle v. Smith (1911).

Relationship with the federal government

Under the Tenth Amendment, powers not granted to the federal government are reserved to the states or the people. States regulate areas including education, police, intrastate commerce and elections. The Supremacy Clause provides that federal law prevails where the two conflict. States are represented in Congress by two senators each, and in the House of Representatives in proportion to population.
