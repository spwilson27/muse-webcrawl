Bretton Woods system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Bretton Woods system was the monetary order established by the agreements signed at the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in July 1944, and operating from 1945 until the early 1970s. It created fixed but adjustable exchange rates pegged to the US dollar, itself convertible into gold for foreign governments at $35 an ounce, and founded the International Monetary Fund (to lend to countries in balance-of-payments difficulty) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (the original World Bank institution, for reconstruction and development lending).

The system, designed chiefly by John Maynard Keynes (for Britain) and Harry Dexter White (for the US, whose plan prevailed), sought to avoid the competitive devaluations and protectionism of the 1930s while preserving national policy autonomy — “embedded liberalism”. Capital controls were permitted; trade liberalisation proceeded under the GATT. The system strained under the weight of US deficits and the Triffin dilemma (the world's need for dollars versus confidence in their gold backing), and collapsed when President Nixon suspended gold convertibility in August 1971; generalised floating followed by 1973. The IMF and World Bank survived as the central institutions of the successor order.
