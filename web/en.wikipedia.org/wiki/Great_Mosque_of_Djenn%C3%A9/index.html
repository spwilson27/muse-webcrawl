Great Mosque of Djenné
LocationDjenné, Mali
Completed1907 (current building)
StyleSudanese-Sahelian (adobe)
DesignationUNESCO World Heritage Site (1988)

The Great Mosque of Djenné is a large banco (mud-brick) building in the town of Djenné, Mali, and is considered by many architects to be one of the greatest achievements of the Sudano-Sahelian architectural style. It is the largest mud-brick building in the world. The first mosque on the site was built around the 13th century; the current structure dates from 1907.

Architecture

The mosque is built of sun-baked mud bricks coated in mud plaster, with wooden palm beams (toron) protruding from the walls that serve both as structural supports and as scaffolding for annual replastering. Three large minarets on the eastern facade are topped with ostrich eggs, symbols of fertility and purity.

The replastering festival

Each year the community holds the Crépissage de la Grande Mosquée, a festival in which the whole town participates in replastering the mosque with fresh mud before the rainy season. The work is accompanied by music, food and races among the teams carrying mud.

History

The original mosque was built under Koi Kunboro, the first Muslim ruler of Djenné. The 19th-century ruler Seku Amadu ordered it demolished and a plainer mosque built nearby; in 1907 the French colonial administration rebuilt the mosque on the original site in its present form.
