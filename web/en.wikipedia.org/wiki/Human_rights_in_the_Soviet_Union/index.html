Human rights in the Soviet Union

Human rights in the Soviet Union were severely limited throughout the state's existence (1922–1991). Official Soviet doctrine held that rights were granted by the state and subordinate to the goals of building socialism; in practice the Communist Party monopolised political life, and freedoms of speech, press, assembly, religion and movement were restricted or denied.

Legal framework and practice

Soviet constitutions proclaimed extensive rights — the 1977 constitution guaranteed speech, press and assembly "in accordance with the interests of the people" — but these were conditioned on conformity with state aims. Independent political parties, unions and media were banned; censorship was administered by Glavlit; foreign travel and internal movement were controlled through passports and the propiska residence system.

Repression

Under Joseph Stalin, terror reached its height: show trials, the Great Purge of 1936–38, mass deportations of nationalities, and the Gulag camp system, which held millions. After Stalin's death in 1953 the scale of repression fell, but political prisoners, punitive psychiatry against dissidents, and trials of human rights activists continued under his successors. Campaigns against religion restricted worship, and Jewish citizens who sought to emigrate ("refuseniks") were persecuted.

Dissidents and international pressure

From the 1960s a small dissident movement publicised abuses through samizdat publications such as the Chronicle of Current Events. Figures including Andrei Sakharov, Alexander Solzhenitsyn and members of the Moscow Helsinki Group — founded after the USSR signed the 1975 Helsinki Accords — drew Western attention. Under Mikhail Gorbachev's glasnost after 1986, political prisoners were released, censorship relaxed and emigration opened, reforms that preceded the Soviet Union's dissolution in 1991.
