House Targaryen is a fictional noble family in George R. R. Martin's fantasy series A Song of Ice and Fire and its television adaptations Game of Thrones and House of the Dragon. The Targaryens are the descendants of dragonlords of the Valyrian Freehold who survived its destruction (the Doom of Valyria) and, under Aegon the Conqueror, conquered and unified six of the Seven Kingdoms of Westeros, ruling as kings for nearly 300 years. They are distinguished by their silver-gold hair, violet eyes, resistance to heat, prophetic dreams, close affinity with dragons, and their dynastic practice of sibling marriage to keep the bloodline "pure" — as well as a hereditary streak of madness summed up in the saying that whenever a Targaryen is born, "the gods toss a coin."

Background in the fictional world

The Targaryens were a relatively minor family of Valyrian dragonlords who settled on Dragonstone, an island in the Narrow Sea, twelve years before the Doom of Valyria destroyed the Freehold in a cataclysm — Aenar Targaryen having moved his family, dragons, and household east on the strength of his daughter Daenys's prophetic dream. A century later, Aegon I Targaryen, riding the dragon Balerion the Black Dread alongside his sister-wives Visenya and Rhaenys and their dragons Vhagar and Meraxes, launched the Conquest of Westeros (2 BC–1 AC in the fictional dating). Only Dorne resisted successfully. Aegon founded King's Landing and the Iron Throne, forged — in legend — from the swords of his enemies.

Dynastic history

The dynasty's high points and catastrophes dominate Westerosi history as elaborated in Martin's Fire & Blood (2018), the source of House of the Dragon. Jaehaerus I, the Conciliator, reigned 55 years in peace and codified law and doctrine to accommodate the Faith. The succession crisis after his death led to the Dance of the Dragons (129–131 AC), a civil war between Rhaenyra Targaryen and her half-brother Aegon II in which most of the family's dragons died fighting each other; dragons dwindled thereafter and the last, stunted specimen died under Aegon III ("the Dragonbane") in 153 AC. The dynasty survived through diplomacy (Dorne joined by marriage under Daeron II), endured the Blackfyre rebellions led by legitimised royal bastards, and produced admired and reviled kings alike — Baelor the Blessed, the saintly ascetic; Aegon IV "the Unworthy"; and Aegon V "Egg," the unlikely hero of the Tales of Dunk and Egg novellas, who died with his son Duncan in the fire at Summerhall (259 AC) in an apparent attempt to hatch dragon eggs.

The last Targaryen king, Aerys II, the Mad King, descended into paranoia and cruelty; his son Rhaegar's abduction (or elopement) with Lyanna Stark triggered Robert's Rebellion (282–283 AC), in which Aerys was killed by his own Kingsguard, Jaime Lannister, and the royal family was nearly exterminated. The infant Daenerys and her brother Viserys were smuggled to the Free Cities — setting in motion the events of the novels.

In the novels and adaptations

In A Game of Thrones (1996), Daenerys Targaryen is sold in marriage to the Dothraki khal Drogo and receives three petrified dragon eggs as a wedding gift; at the novel's climax she hatches them in a funeral pyre — the first dragons in over a century — earning the name "Mother of Dragons." Her quest to reclaim the Iron Throne is one of the series' central arcs. In Game of Thrones (HBO, 2011–2019), Emilia Clarke's Daenerys became one of television's most recognisable characters; the show's final season, in which Daenerys burns King's Landing and is killed by Jon Snow — revealed as Aegon Targaryen, Rhaegar's secret son — proved fiercely controversial. The prequel House of the Dragon (2022– ), centred on Rhaenyra (Emma D'Arcy), Alicent Hightower (Olivia Cooke), and Daemon Targaryen (Matt Smith), dramatises the Dance of the Dragons.

Themes

Martin uses the Targaryens to explore absolute power, hereditary rule, and the romance and horror of dynastic exceptionalism: the same "blood of the dragon" that gives them dragons and dreams also brings incest, madness, and civil war. Their Valyrian traits set them apart from the peoples they rule — foreign conquerors whose legitimacy rests on a monopoly of living weapons, and whose glamour fades once the dragons do.
