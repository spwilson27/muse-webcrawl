Economy of the Soviet Union

The economy of the Soviet Union was organised as a centrally planned system in which the state owned the means of production and allocated resources through administrative commands rather than markets. From the late 1920s, economic activity was directed by five-year plans drawn up by Gosplan, the State Planning Committee. At its height the Soviet economy was the second largest in the world.

History

After the Russian Revolution, the Bolsheviks imposed War Communism, then retreated to the mixed New Economic Policy (NEP) in 1921. Joseph Stalin ended the NEP, launched the first Five-Year Plan in 1928 and collectivised agriculture, forcing peasants into collective farms (kolkhozes) and state farms (sovkhozes) with devastating famine in Ukraine, Kazakhstan and Russia. Rapid industrialisation built huge steel, machinery and armaments industries and helped the USSR defeat Nazi Germany, at enormous human cost.

Post-war growth and stagnation

Growth was rapid in the 1950s, driven by investment, urbanisation and technology transfer, and the USSR achieved milestones such as the first satellite and human spaceflight. From the 1970s, however, productivity growth faltered: soft budget constraints, distorted prices, weak innovation incentives and chronic shortages of consumer goods produced the "Era of Stagnation". Oil exports masked the decline. Mikhail Gorbachev's perestroika reforms after 1985 partially liberalised the economy but undermined the planning system before market institutions existed, contributing to economic collapse and the dissolution of the USSR in 1991.

Characteristics

Prices, wages and output targets were set by planners; money played a largely passive role for enterprises. The system achieved near-full employment, basic welfare provision and heavy-industry strength, but consumer sectors were neglected, quality was poor, and an extensive informal "second economy" filled the gaps left by the plan.
