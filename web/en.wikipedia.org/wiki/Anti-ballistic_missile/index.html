Anti-ballistic missile

Anti-ballistic missile (ABM)
TypeSurface-to-air missile system
PurposeInterception of ballistic missiles
Interception phasesBoost, midcourse, terminal
Kill mechanismsHit-to-kill kinetic impact; blast fragmentation; (historically) nuclear warheads
ExamplesGMD, Aegis BMD/SM-3, THAAD, Patriot PAC-3, S-400, Arrow 3

An anti-ballistic missile (ABM) is a surface-to-air missile designed to counter ballistic missiles — that is, to intercept and destroy them before they reach their target. Ballistic missiles follow a high arc through or above the atmosphere, reaching speeds of many kilometres per second, which makes them the most demanding targets in air defence.

ABM systems combine early-warning and tracking radars, battle management computers and interceptor missiles. They are classified by the phase of the target's flight in which interception occurs: boost phase (during ascent, while the rocket motor burns), midcourse phase (in space, the longest portion of an intercontinental trajectory) and terminal phase (during re-entry into the atmosphere).

History

The United States and Soviet Union began ABM research in the 1950s. Early interceptors such as Nike Zeus, and later Spartan and Sprint of the US Safeguard system, carried nuclear warheads to destroy incoming re-entry vehicles by blast and radiation. The Soviet A-35 system defended Moscow. The 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty limited each superpower's deployments, reflecting the doctrine that vulnerability to retaliation preserved strategic stability; the United States withdrew from the treaty in 2002.

Modern systems

Contemporary systems generally use hit-to-kill kinetic interceptors that destroy the target by direct collision. They include the US Ground-based Midcourse Defense (GMD) interceptors in Alaska and California, the sea-based Aegis BMD system with the SM-3 interceptor, the terminal-area THAAD system, the Patriot PAC-3, Israel's Arrow 2 and Arrow 3 (the latter intercepting in space), India's Prithvi Defence Vehicle, and Russia's S-400/S-500 and A-135/A-235 systems around Moscow.

Effectiveness and criticism

ABM effectiveness is contested. Critics argue that midcourse interception can be defeated by decoys and countermeasures that are cheap relative to the defence, and that test programmes occur under scripted conditions. Supporters contend that even imperfect defences complicate an attacker's planning and can protect against limited strikes. The problem of distinguishing warheads from decoys in space remains the central technical challenge of midcourse defence.
