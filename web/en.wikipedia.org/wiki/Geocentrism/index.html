Geocentrism is the belief that Earth is the centre of the universe or of the celestial sphere. As an astronomical theory it was embodied in the geocentric model developed by Greek astronomers and perfected by Ptolemy, which held sway for over a millennium before being displaced by heliocentrism during the Scientific Revolution.

History

Geocentrism arose naturally from naked-eye observation: the Sun, Moon, and stars appear to circle a stationary Earth, and no stellar parallax was detectable with ancient instruments. It was systematised by Aristotle and Ptolemy, absorbed into medieval Christian, Jewish, and Islamic scholarship, and defended on both observational and scriptural grounds. The Copernican revolution, the work of Galileo and Kepler, and Newton's mechanics undermined it between the 16th and 18th centuries; the Catholic Church's prohibition of heliocentric teaching was relaxed in the 18th and 19th centuries as evidence accumulated.

Modern geocentrism

A small number of religious fundamentalists continue to advocate geocentrism on literal readings of scripture, and opinion polls have at times found a surprising minority of the general public endorsing the statement that the Sun revolves around the Earth. In physics, the choice of coordinate origin is arbitrary, and Earth-centred frames are legitimately used for navigation and satellite work — but this mathematical convenience does not imply a physically central or stationary Earth.
