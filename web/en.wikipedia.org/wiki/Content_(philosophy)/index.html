Content (philosophy)

In philosophy, content most often refers to what a representation is about — the information or meaning carried by a mental state, a sentence, or another representational vehicle. The notion is central to the philosophy of mind, the philosophy of language, and epistemology, where it connects with the problems of intentionality and meaning.

Mental content

Beliefs, desires, perceptions, and other intentional mental states are said to have representational content: the belief that it is raining represents the world as being a certain way and is true or false depending on whether the world matches that content. Philosophers distinguish contents by their objects and conditions of satisfaction, and debate how content is determined. Internalists hold that content depends only on what is inside the subject's head, while externalists — following Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment and Tyler Burge's social externalism — argue that the environment and linguistic community partly fix what our thoughts are about: "meanings just ain't in the head." Closely related is the contrast between narrow and wide content.

Semantic content

In the philosophy of language, the content of a sentence is often identified with the proposition it expresses — what is said — as opposed to what is merely implicated or presupposed. Semantics aims to assign contents compositionally, so that the content of a whole sentence is determined by the contents of its parts and their mode of combination. Debates concern how content relates to reference, truth conditions, and cognitive significance (Frege's distinction between sense and reference).

Perceptual and phenomenal content

Perceptual experiences also have content — they present the world as being some way — and philosophers dispute whether this content is conceptual or non-conceptual, and whether phenomenal character (what an experience is like) is exhausted by its representational content, a view called representationalism.
