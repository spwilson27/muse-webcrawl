Ichthyosauria

Ichthyosauria (from Greek ichthys, fish, and sauros, lizard) is an order of extinct marine reptiles that thrived during much of the Mesozoic Era, from the Early Triassic, about 250 million years ago, until their extinction in the early Late Cretaceous, about 90 million years ago. Ichthyosaurs evolved from land reptiles that returned to the sea, and over their long history converged strikingly on the body form of dolphins and fast-swimming fish such as tuna: streamlined bodies, crescent-shaped tail flukes, dorsal fins, and limbs transformed into flippers.

Evolution and history

Ichthyosaurs appear in the fossil record already fully marine in the Early Triassic, so their land-dwelling ancestors remain incompletely known; forms such as Cartorhynchus from China, a small, amphibious early ichthyosauriform described in 2014, bridge part of the gap. Triassic ichthyosaurs included the shastasaurids, some exceeding 15–20 metres and among the largest marine reptiles ever. After surviving the end-Triassic extinction, Jurassic forms such as Ichthyosaurus and Stenopterygius adopted the classic dolphin-like form. Diversity declined through the Jurassic and Early Cretaceous, and the last ichthyosaurs, platypterygiines, disappeared around the Cenomanian–Turonian boundary, some 30 million years before the end-Cretaceous mass extinction.

Anatomy

Ichthyosaurs had large eyes — Ophthalmosaurus had among the largest eyes relative to body size of any vertebrate — protected by bony sclerotic rings, suggesting deep-diving or nocturnal habits. Later forms had a strongly downward-bent tail supporting the lower lobe of a vertical tail fluke, soft-tissue preservation showing a dorsal fin without skeletal support, and smooth, scaleless skin; exceptional fossils from the Holzmaden deposits preserve body outlines and even blubber-like tissue and dark pigmentation. Their jaws were lined with conical teeth for grasping fish and cephalopods, though some giants were toothless suction or bulk feeders.

Biology

Ichthyosaurs were air-breathing and fully aquatic; unable to haul out on land, they gave birth to live young tail-first, as shown by abundant fossils of pregnant females and embryos, especially of Stenopterygius. Stomach contents and coprolites indicate diets of belemnites, ammonites, and fish. Studies of bone histology and oxygen isotopes suggest ichthyosaurs maintained elevated body temperatures and were likely endothermic to a degree.

Extinction

The cause of the ichthyosaurs' final decline is debated; proposed factors include climate instability and rapid environmental change during the Cenomanian, falling diversity of their belemnite prey, and competition with evolving mosasaurs, plesiosaurs, and early sharks. Their extinction left the large marine-predator niche to the mosasaurs in the later Late Cretaceous.

Discovery

The first complete ichthyosaur skeleton was found around 1811–1812 at Lyme Regis, England, by Mary Anning and her brother Joseph, and described by Home, Conybeare, and others. Ichthyosaurus was named in 1818–1821, and the order was established by Henri Marie Ducrotay de Blainville in 1835. Anning's finds made ichthyosaurs central to early palaeontology and to public fascination with "sea dragons".
