Non-stop flight

A non-stop flight is a flight by an aircraft with no intermediate stops. This contrasts with a direct flight, which in airline terminology may include one or more stops for refuelling or passenger exchange while keeping the same flight number, and with connecting itineraries involving a change of aircraft.

Ultra-long-haul flights

Advances in aircraft range have enabled non-stop flights of 18 hours or more. Among the longest scheduled passenger routes are Singapore Airlines' Singapore–New York services (about 15,300 km, flown with the Airbus A350-900ULR) and Qantas's Perth–London route. Qantas's Project Sunrise aims at non-stop Sydney–London and Sydney–New York flights of up to about 20 hours.

Considerations

Ultra-long non-stop flights require special attention to crew rest (usually two full flight crews), passenger health risks such as deep-vein thrombosis, and fuel-versus-payload trade-offs: carrying enough fuel for the whole route can require restricting cargo or seats. Airlines market non-stop service at a premium because it saves total travel time and avoids misconnection risk.

History

The first non-stop transatlantic flight was made by Alcock and Brown in June 1919, flying a modified Vickers Vimy from Newfoundland to Ireland. Charles Lindbergh's solo non-stop New York–Paris flight followed in 1927.
