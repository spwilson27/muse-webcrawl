Howard Saul Becker (April 18, 1928 – August 16, 2023) was an American sociologist who taught at Northwestern University and the University of Washington. A central figure of the "second Chicago school," Becker is best known for his contributions to the sociology of deviance—particularly labeling theory—as well as the sociology of art, music, and qualitative research methods.

Early life and career

Born in Chicago, Becker began playing piano professionally in bars and strip clubs as a teenager, an experience that later informed his ethnographic studies of dance musicians. He studied sociology at the University of Chicago, receiving his PhD in 1951; his dissertation was based on fieldwork in Kansas City public schools. He came under the influence of Everett Hughes, whose emphasis on occupations and fieldwork shaped Becker's approach.

Work

Deviance and labeling

In Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance (1963), Becker argued that deviance is not a quality of an act itself but a consequence of the application of rules and sanctions: "deviant behavior is behavior that people so label." The book's studies of marijuana users and dance musicians became classics of symbolic interactionism and helped establish the labeling perspective in criminology.

Art worlds

Art Worlds (1982) treated art as collective action produced by networks of people—artists, fabricators, dealers, critics, and audiences—working according to shared conventions. The book became foundational for the sociology of art and production-of-culture scholarship.

Methods and writing

Becker also wrote influential methodological works, including Writing for Social Scientists (1986), Tricks of the Trade (1998), and Telling About Society (2007), defending ethnography and modest, empirically grounded theorizing against what he saw as overly abstract sociology.
