The Apalachin meeting was a summit of the American Mafia held on 14 November 1957 at the home of mobster Joseph Barbara in Apalachin, New York. Around one hundred Mafia bosses, underbosses and associates from the United States, Cuba and Italy were believed to have gathered to discuss matters including narcotics trafficking, gambling, the garment industry rackets, and the leadership questions left by the recent shooting of Frank Costello and the murder of Albert Anastasia.

The raid

Local state trooper Sergeant Edgar Croswell had long kept the Barbara estate under observation and, alerted by the motel bookings and the arrival of many cars with out-of-state plates, set up a roadblock with reinforcements. As word spread, attendees fled across the fields and woods; about sixty were detained and questioned, while others escaped. No delegate was charged with a crime arising from the meeting itself — most were released — and conspiracy convictions obtained against twenty attendees in 1959 were overturned on appeal in 1960.

Consequences

The meeting's exposure was a public-relations catastrophe for the Mafia. It forced FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover — who had long downplayed the existence of a national crime syndicate — to acknowledge organised crime's national scope and to expand the FBI's Top Hoodlum Program. The publicity also damaged Vito Genovese, widely blamed for convening the summit to ratify his position after Anastasia's murder. The Apalachin fiasco became a landmark in the history of American law enforcement and of the Commission system it had been meant to showcase.
