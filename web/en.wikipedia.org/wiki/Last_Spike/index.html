Last spike

Driving the last spike completes a transcontinental railway.
TypeRailway completion ceremony

The last spike is the final spike driven into the track to complete a railway line, traditionally marked by a ceremony. Ceremonial spikes were often made of precious metal — most famously the Golden Spike driven by Leland Stanford at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869, completing the First Transcontinental Railroad in the United States.

Canada

In Canada, the last spike of the Canadian Pacific Railway was driven by Donald Smith, Lord Strathcona, at Craigellachie, British Columbia, on 7 November 1885. The plain iron spike — not gold — became an enduring national symbol; the famous photograph of Smith driving it is among the best-known images in Canadian history. The ceremony marked the fulfilment of the promise of a transcontinental railway that had brought British Columbia into Confederation in 1871.

Other examples

Similar ceremonies accompanied the completion of railways elsewhere, including lines in Australia and New Zealand, and the term has since entered general use for the completion of any major construction project.
