Sex segregation

Sex segregation, or sex separation, is the physical, legal or cultural separation of people according to their sex. It may be mandated by law, enforced by institutions, or arise through custom and self-segregation. Sex segregation can occur in education, employment, public facilities, religious life, sport and housing, and is distinct from — though historically connected with — sex-based discrimination.

Types

  • Mandatory segregation required by law, such as separate prisons for men and women.
  • Administrative segregation in public and private institutions, such as single-sex schools.
  • Permissive segregation, allowed but not required, such as sex-separated sports competitions.
  • Voluntary segregation, arising from cultural norms or individual choice in the absence of legal rules.

Areas

Education

Single-sex schooling was historically the norm in many countries and remains common in some. Research findings on its academic effects are mixed, and its legality differs by jurisdiction; in the United States it is constrained by equal-protection and Title IX principles.

Public facilities

Sex-separated toilets and changing rooms are widespread and usually justified on privacy and safety grounds; their design and the treatment of transgender and non-binary people have been subjects of public debate.

Employment

Occupational sex segregation — the unequal distribution of men and women across jobs — is a persistent feature of labour markets and a contributor to the gender pay gap. Some jobs remain formally restricted by sex in certain countries, for example certain religious offices.

Religion

Many religious traditions practise forms of sex separation in worship, such as separate seating in some mosques, synagogues and churches, and sex-segregated religious orders.
