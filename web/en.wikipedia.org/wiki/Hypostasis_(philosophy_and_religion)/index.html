Hypostasis (philosophy and religion)

Hypostasis (Greek: ὑπόστασις, hypóstasis) is the underlying state or underlying substance — literally, that which "stands beneath" — and is the fundamental reality that supports all else. The term has been used in Greek philosophy, in Neoplatonism, and centrally in Christian theology, where it came to denote an individual person or subsistence, especially each of the three persons of the Trinity.

Neoplatonism

In the system of Plotinus, reality proceeds from the One by emanation through a hierarchy of hypostases: the One (or the Good), the Nous or intellect, and the Psyche or soul. Later Neoplatonists such as Proclus elaborated the scheme considerably. Here a hypostasis is a real, self-subsistent level of being, not merely an attribute or appearance.

Christian theology

In Trinitarian theology the Cappadocian Fathers settled the formula "one ousia (essence) in three hypostases (persons)" to describe the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. In Christology, the doctrine of the hypostatic union, affirmed at the Council of Chalcedon in 451, holds that in the single person (hypostasis) of Christ, a complete divine nature and a complete human nature are united "without confusion, change, division, or separation". Disputes over the term — in Latin rendered variously as substantia and persona — were a major source of East–West theological friction.

Other uses

In medicine, "hypostasis" historically denoted a sediment or deposit, and survives in terms for the livid discoloration of dependent parts after death. In linguistics and literary theory, the verb to hypostatise (reify) means to treat an abstraction as if it were a concrete entity.
