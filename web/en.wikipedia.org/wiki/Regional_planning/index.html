Regional planning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
A regional plan coordinating land use and transport across municipal boundaries

Regional planning deals with the efficient placement of land-use activities, infrastructure, and settlement growth across a significantly larger area of land than an individual city or town. A region can be defined by administrative boundaries, functional relationships such as commuting and labour markets, or physical features such as a river basin. Regional planning is a branch of urban and land-use planning that addresses problems that transcend local jurisdictions, including transportation networks, water supply, environmental protection, economic development and the distribution of population and employment.

History

The discipline emerged in the early 20th century with figures such as Patrick Geddes, who advocated planning "by region" based on survey of place, work and folk, and Lewis Mumford and the Regional Planning Association of America (founded 1923). Large public programmes such as the Tennessee Valley Authority in the United States combined regional economic development with resource management. In Britain, the Barlow Report (1940) and post-war regional policy distributed industry to depressed areas, while regional spatial strategies later coordinated housing and development across counties.

Scope and methods

Regional plans typically set a settlement hierarchy, designate growth areas and protected landscapes or green belts, reserve corridors for regional transport and utilities, and allocate housing and employment targets among constituent local authorities. Methods include demographic and economic forecasting, land-use/transport interaction modelling, strategic environmental assessment and public consultation. Implementation is usually indirect, through the conformity of local plans and infrastructure investment decisions, since regional bodies often lack direct development-control powers.

Examples

Examples of regional planning bodies and frameworks include metropolitan planning organizations in the United States (required for federal transport funding since the 1960s), the regional councils of France and New Zealand, German Regionalplanung under federal spatial planning law, and river-basin authorities worldwide. Cross-border regions, such as the Øresund Region between Denmark and Sweden, add an international dimension.
