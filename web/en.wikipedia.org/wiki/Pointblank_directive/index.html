Pointblank directive

The Pointblank directive was issued by the Allied Combined Chiefs of Staff on 14 June 1943 during the Second World War, authorising the initiation of Operation Pointblank, the portion of the Combined Bomber Offensive intended to cripple the German war economy — above all the Luftwaffe's fighter force — before the planned invasion of France, Operation Overlord.

Background

The Casablanca directive of February 1943 had set out general objectives for the strategic bombing of Germany. The Pointblank directive refined these instructions, modifying the list of target priorities and directing the British and American bomber commands to give first priority to destroying German fighter strength: aircraft factories, assembly plants, and repair depots, together with the ball-bearing and other supporting industries. Intermediate objectives included submarine yards and bases, the remainder of the aircraft industry, and oil and rubber production.

Implementation

The directive authorised the US Eighth Air Force to mount daylight precision attacks on fighter production while RAF Bomber Command continued area bombing at night. In practice, unescorted American bombers suffered severe losses during deep-penetration raids such as the Schweinfurt–Regensburg mission of August 1943 and the second Schweinfurt raid in October. The introduction of long-range escort fighters, especially the P-51 Mustang, in early 1944 allowed the Allies to attrit the Luftwaffe's pilot force — the central aim of Pointblank — during missions such as "Big Week" (Operation Argument) in February 1944.

Outcome

By the time of the Normandy landings in June 1944, Allied air superiority over France had been decisively achieved: the Luftwaffe was unable to mount effective opposition to the invasion. Historians continue to debate the relative contribution of Pointblank's factory attacks versus the attrition of German pilots in air combat.
