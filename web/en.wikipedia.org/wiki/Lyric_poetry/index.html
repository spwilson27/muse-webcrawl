Lyric poetry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Sappho playing the lyre, as imagined in a classical-style painting]
Sappho playing the lyre, as imagined in a classical-style painting

Lyric poetry is a form of Poetry expressing personal emotions or feelings, typically spoken in the first person. The term derives from the ancient Greek lyre, to whose accompaniment such poems were sung. It is one of the three broad categories of poetry in the Western tradition, alongside epic (narrative) and dramatic poetry, and by far the dominant mode of modern poetry.

History

Greek lyric flourished in the seventh to fifth centuries BC: Sappho and Alcaeus of Lesbos (monody), Pindar's choral victory odes, Anacreon's wine-and-love songs. Rome adapted the forms — Catullus's passionate brevity, Horace's odes — and the Troubadours and Petrarch's sonnets made lyric the vehicle of courtly love in medieval and Renaissance Europe. The Sonnet, Ode and Elegy became its set forms.

Romantic and modern lyric

The Romantics — William Wordsworth, Percy Bysshe Shelley, John Keats, Lord Byron — redefined the lyric as the spontaneous overflow of the self, the poet's consciousness itself the subject. The nineteenth century added the dramatic monologue (Robert Browning); the twentieth shattered and remade the 'I' through modernism (T. S. Eliot, W. H. Auden) and confessional poetry (Sylvia Plath, Robert Lowell).

Characteristics

Lyric is marked by brevity, musicality, present-tense immediacy and a speaker distinct (though never simply identical) from the poet. Though no longer sung, its heritage in song remains literal: popular music's words are still called 'lyrics'.
