The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, sometimes shortened to Decline and Fall, is a six-volume work by the English historian Edward Gibbon. It traces the Roman Empire from the height of its power in the second century AD to the fall of Constantinople in 1453. Volume I was published in 1776 and went through six printings. Volumes II and III were published in 1781; volumes IV, V, and VI in 1788–89.

Contents and scope

The work covers the period from AD 98 to 1590, encompassing the history of the Roman Empire, the early church, the emergence of the papacy, the rise of Islam, the Mongol invasions, and the Byzantine Empire down to its final conquest by the Ottoman Turks under Mehmed II. Gibbon's narrative is famous for its irony, its elegant prose style, and its controversial treatment of Christianity, which he blamed — alongside "barbarism and religion" — for hastening the empire's decay.

Thesis

Gibbon argued that the empire succumbed to internal decay: the loss of civic virtue among citizens, the professionalization of the army, the growth of an idle capital city, and the diversion of energy and wealth into theological disputes. In the famous formulation of his General Observations on the Fall of the Roman Empire in the West, he wrote that "the decline of Rome was the natural and inevitable effect of immoderate greatness."

Reception

The first volume was an immediate success, praised by figures such as David Hume, though chapters 15 and 16, which treated the rise of Christianity with sceptical detachment, provoked a storm of pamphlets from Anglican divines. The work remains a landmark of English historiography and literature, and its opening sentence — "In the second century of the Christian era, the Empire of Rome comprehended the fairest part of the earth, and the most civilized portion of mankind" — is among the most quoted in historical writing.

Editions

The standard modern edition is the three-volume set edited by David Womersley (1994), based on the text of the last lifetime edition. John Bagnell Bury's seven-volume edition (1896–1900) long remained the scholarly reference for its extensive notes.
