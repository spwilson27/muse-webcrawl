Linguistic typology is a field of linguistics that studies and classifies languages according to their structural features, in order to find patterns and limits in the variation among the world's languages. Unlike historical linguistics, typology classifies languages independently of their genealogical relationships; similarities between unrelated languages in distant areas may reveal universal tendencies of language.

Morphological typology

The oldest classification, developed by the Schlegel brothers, Humboldt and Edward Sapir, groups languages by how they build words. Isolating (analytic) languages such as Vietnamese use mostly single-morpheme words. Agglutinative languages such as Turkish and Swahili string together clearly separable affixes. Fusional (inflectional) languages such as Latin and Russian pack several meanings into single endings. Polysynthetic languages, including many Indigenous languages of the Americas, combine many morphemes into very long words that can correspond to whole sentences. Most languages mix these strategies, so modern typologists treat the categories as scales rather than boxes.

Word order and universals

Joseph Greenberg's 1963 study of 30 languages founded modern typology by proposing implicational universals — statements of the form "if a language has feature X, it tends to have feature Y". Basic word order typology (SOV, SVO, VSO and rarer orders) correlates systematically with other features, such as whether adpositions precede or follow nouns. Subsequent large databases, above all the World Atlas of Language Structures, allow statistical testing of such correlations across hundreds of languages. Typologists debate whether observed universals stem from cognitive constraints, language processing, or historical accident, and modern work combines typological sampling with phylogenetic methods.

Other domains

Typology covers every level of structure: sound inventories and tone, alignment of grammatical relations (nominative–accusative versus ergative–absolutive systems), evidentiality, numeral systems, colour terms, and kinship vocabularies, among many others.
