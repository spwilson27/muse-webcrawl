The Dutch Revolt (Dutch: De Opstand) or Eighty Years' War (1568–1648) was the revolt of the Seventeen Provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands against Philip II of Spain, the sovereign of the Spanish Netherlands. Driven by resistance to Spanish centralisation, heavy taxation and the persecution of Protestants, the revolt began with the iconoclastic fury of 1566 and decades of war ended only in 1648, when the Peace of Münster recognised the independence of the northern Dutch Republic — by then a great commercial and naval power — while the southern provinces (roughly modern Belgium) remained Spanish.

Causes

Philip II, who inherited the Netherlands in 1555, governed from Spain through regents and angered the native nobility by excluding them from power, quartering Spanish troops, and enforcing the heresy laws against a growing Calvinist population. High taxes — notably the proposed "Tenth Penny" sales tax of the Duke of Alba — and the Inquisition's executions fed resistance among nobles led by William of Orange ("the Silent"), and the Counts of Egmont and Hoorn.

Outbreak and Alba's terror

In August 1566 Calvinist crowds swept the churches of Flanders in the Beeldenstorm (iconoclasm), smashing images and altars. Philip sent Fernando Álvarez de Toledo, Duke of Alba, with a Spanish army in 1567. Alba's Council of Troubles — the "Council of Blood" — condemned thousands and executed Egmont and Hoorn in Brussels in 1568. William of Orange's invasions of 1568 failed, and the conventional start of the war is dated from his campaign and the rebel victory at Heiligerlee that year.

Sea Beggars and the northern revolt

The capture of Brielle by the Sea Beggars (privateers licensed by William) on 1 April 1572 sparked revolts across Holland and Zeeland. Spanish attempts to crush them failed heroically at Alkmaar (1573) and Leiden (1574), where the Dutch cut the dikes to flood the Spanish siege lines. The Spanish Fury at Antwerp (1576), in which mutinous unpaid Spanish troops sacked the richest city of northern Europe, united the provinces briefly in the Pacification of Ghent.

Union and division

The provinces soon split: the Catholic south formed the Union of Arras (1579) and reconciled with Spain, while the north concluded the Union of Utrecht (1579), the effective founding charter of the Dutch Republic. In 1581 the States General issued the Act of Abjuration, deposing Philip — Europe's first formal declaration of independence by a rebellious province. William of Orange was assassinated by Balthasar Gérard in Delft in 1584, and English intervention under the Earl of Leicester (1585–87) proved disappointing, but Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, could not reconquer the waterlogged north.

Maurice, the Twelve Years' Truce and conclusion

Under William's son Maurice of Nassau, with the political leadership of Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, the Republic professionalised its army, retook the east and north in the 1590s, and projected power globally through the Dutch East India Company (founded 1602). The Twelve Years' Truce (1609–1621) was a de facto recognition of independence; war resumed with the Thirty Years' War and ended with the Peace of Münster (30 January 1648, within the Peace of Westphalia), in which Spain formally recognised Dutch independence.
