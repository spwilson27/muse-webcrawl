Beta oxidation

In biochemistry and metabolism, beta oxidation is the catabolic process by which fatty acid molecules are broken down in the mitochondria (in eukaryotes) to generate acetyl-CoA, which enters the citric acid cycle, and NADH and FADH2, which are co-enzymes used in the electron transport chain.

Overview

Free fatty acids are first activated in the cytosol by attachment to coenzyme A, forming fatty acyl-CoA. Long-chain acyl-CoA is transported across the mitochondrial membrane by the carnitine shuttle. Inside the mitochondrion, each cycle of beta oxidation removes two carbon atoms as one acetyl-CoA through four reactions:

  1. Oxidation — acyl-CoA dehydrogenase introduces a double bond between the alpha and beta carbons, producing FADH2.
  2. Hydration — enoyl-CoA hydratase adds water across the double bond.
  3. Oxidation — hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase oxidises the hydroxyl group, producing NADH.
  4. Thiolysis — thiolase cleaves the chain, releasing acetyl-CoA and an acyl-CoA shortened by two carbons.

The cycle repeats until the chain is fully degraded (or a three-carbon propionyl-CoA remains, for odd-chain fatty acids). Beta oxidation of one palmitate (C16) yields 8 acetyl-CoA, 7 NADH and 7 FADH2, making fatty acids a highly energy-rich fuel. Defects in the pathway, such as medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (MCAD) deficiency, cause serious metabolic disease.
