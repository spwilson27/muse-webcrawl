The Lausanne School of economics was a tendency within neoclassical economics centred on the University of Lausanne in Switzerland, founded by Léon Walras and developed by his successor Vilfredo Pareto. It is distinguished by its mathematical treatment of general equilibrium — the simultaneous determination of prices and quantities in all markets of an economy — and by its emphasis on the interdependence of markets rather than partial analysis of one market at a time.

Walras and general equilibrium

Walras, appointed to the chair of political economy at Lausanne in 1870, presented in his Éléments d'économie politique pure (1874–77) the first comprehensive system of equations describing an exchange economy in equilibrium. His concept of tâtonnement ("groping") described how prices might adjust toward equilibrium through a hypothetical auction process. Walras is also regarded as a founder of marginal utility theory, alongside Jevons and Menger, though he worked largely independently of them.

Pareto and ordinalism

Vilfredo Pareto, who succeeded Walras in 1893, rebuilt the theory of consumer choice on ordinal rather than cardinal utility, showing that equilibrium required only rankings of preferences, represented by indifference curves — an approach he set out in his Manuale di economia politica (1906). From this work came the concept of Pareto efficiency, or Pareto optimality: a state in which no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. Pareto also contributed the empirical observation of the 80/20 distribution of income and wealth, later called the Pareto principle.

Influence

The Lausanne tradition — mathematical, general-equilibrium and welfare-oriented — flowed into 20th-century economics through Hicks, Samuelson and the Arrow–Debreu model, and its two fundamental theorems of welfare economics restate in modern form insights first systematised at Lausanne.
