Treaty of Ghent

The Treaty of Ghent was the peace treaty that ended the War of 1812 between the United States and the United Kingdom. It was signed in the city of Ghent (then part of the United Netherlands, now in Belgium) on 24 December 1814. The treaty restored relations between the two nations to status quo ante bellum — the state in existence before the war — took effect when ratified by both governments (the United States Senate ratified unanimously on 16 February 1815), and was proclaimed on 17 February 1815.

Neither side gained or lost territory, and the treaty said nothing about the issues over which the United States had gone to war — impressment and neutral trading rights — which the end of the Napoleonic Wars had in any case made moot. The war's largest battles in the south, including the Battle of New Orleans (8 January 1815), were fought after the treaty was signed but before the news crossed the Atlantic.

Negotiations

Peace talks opened at Ghent in August 1814. The British, then on the offensive, began with demands including a Native American buffer state in the Northwest, American demilitarisation of the Great Lakes, and territorial adjustments at Maine and elsewhere, and at first refused even to discuss impressment or maritime rights. The American commissioners — an unusually able group led by John Quincy Adams and including Henry Clay and Albert Gallatin — rejected the buffer state as an infringement of sovereignty. British enthusiasm for hard terms cooled after the American victory at Plattsburgh in September and the repulse at Baltimore, and the Duke of Wellington advised the government that, having failed to win territory in war, it had no right to demand any at the table. Both exhausted governments settled for the pre-war status quo.

Terms

The treaty provided for the cessation of hostilities, the release of prisoners, and the mutual restoration of all territory, places and possessions taken during the war — including, in practice, the restoration of lands to Native peoples as they had stood in 1811, a provision the United States subsequently did not honour. Boundary questions between the United States and British North America (the Maine frontier, the Great Lakes, and the line westward) were referred to joint commissions, whose work under the treaty led ultimately to the Rush–Bagot Agreement (1817), the Convention of 1818 and the Webster–Ashburton Treaty (1842). In its tenth article both parties, remarking that the traffic in slaves was "irreconcilable with the principles of humanity and justice", engaged to use their best endeavours to abolish it.

Reception and legacy

In Britain the treaty was received without much comment, as a sideshow to the greater struggle with Napoleon. In the United States its arrival, nearly simultaneous with the news of New Orleans, was celebrated as a triumph: the "second war of independence" had ended, no territory had been lost, and the Federalists who had opposed the war were discredited. The treaty began a century of Anglo-American rapprochement along the Canadian border, which remains the world's longest undefended frontier.
