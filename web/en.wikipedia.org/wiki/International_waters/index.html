International waters

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Maritime zones under the law of the sea.

International waters, or the high seas, are areas of the sea or ocean that lie beyond the jurisdiction of any state. Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982), the high seas begin beyond the 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone of coastal states. The term "international waters" is informal and not defined in UNCLOS itself, which speaks of the high seas and of maritime zones — internal waters, territorial sea, contiguous zone, exclusive economic zone, and continental shelf.

Freedoms of the high seas

The high seas are open to all states, and no state may validly subject any part of them to its sovereignty. Freedoms of the high seas include freedom of navigation, freedom of overflight, freedom to lay submarine cables and pipelines, freedom to construct artificial islands and installations permitted under international law, freedom of fishing (subject to conservation duties and treaties), and freedom of scientific research. Ships on the high seas are generally subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of their flag state.

Exceptions and enforcement

Flag-state exclusivity has exceptions: warships and authorised vessels may board a foreign ship on reasonable suspicion of piracy, slave trading, unauthorised broadcasting, or statelessness (the right of visit). Piracy is subject to universal jurisdiction — any state may seize pirate ships and try the offenders. Treaties on drug trafficking, migrant smuggling, fisheries conservation, and safety (SOLAS) and pollution (MARPOL) create further boarding and enforcement regimes. Port states exercise extensive control when foreign ships voluntarily enter their ports.

Beyond national jurisdiction

The seabed beyond national jurisdiction — "the Area" — and its mineral resources are declared the common heritage of mankind, administered by the International Seabed Authority. In 2023 states adopted the High Seas Treaty (the BBNJ Agreement) to protect marine biodiversity in areas beyond national jurisdiction, including through marine protected areas and environmental impact assessments.

Disputes

Because EEZs are not sovereign territory, militaries contest the extent of coastal-state control over foreign military activities in EEZs, and overlapping claims in seas such as the South China Sea have produced persistent disputes and arbitral proceedings (notably the 2016 Philippines v. China award, which Beijing rejected).
