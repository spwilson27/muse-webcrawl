Indium

Indium is a chemical element with the symbol In and atomic number 49. It is a silvery-white, soft post-transition metal, chemically similar to gallium and thallium. Indium is the softest metal that is not an alkali metal; it can be cut with a knife and emits a high-pitched "cry" when bent, similar to tin. It is rare in the Earth's crust and is obtained mainly as a by-product of zinc refining.

Discovery and properties

Indium was discovered in 1863 by Ferdinand Reich and Hieronymus Theodor Richter at Freiberg, who identified it spectroscopically by its bright indigo-blue emission line — the source of its name. The metal melts at 156.6 °C and boils at 2,072 °C. Its most important compound is indium tin oxide (ITO), a transparent conductor used in touchscreens and LCDs. Indium has two primordial isotopes, 113In and the slightly radioactive 115In, which makes up about 95.7% of natural indium.

Applications

The dominant use of indium (historically 60–70% of supply) is ITO thin films for flat-panel displays, touchscreens, solar cells and smart windows. Indium also forms low-melting alloys used as solders, vacuum seals and fusible safety plugs; indium phosphide (InP) and related III–V semiconductors are used in high-speed electronics and photonics. Indium's price and supply volatility, driven by its by-product status and concentration of refining in China, have made it a critical raw material for the European Union and the United States.

Biological role and safety

Indium has no known biological role. Soluble indium compounds are toxic, and inhalation of indium tin oxide dust has been linked to "indium lung", a serious occupational pulmonary disease identified among Japanese ITO workers.
