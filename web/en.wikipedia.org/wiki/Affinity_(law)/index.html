Affinity (law)

In law and in kinship terminology, affinity is the relationship that each spouse acquires, through marriage, with the blood relatives (consanguinity) of the other spouse. Relations linked by affinity are called in-laws: a wife's father, for example, is her husband's father-in-law. Affinity must be distinguished from consanguinity, the relationship between persons descended from a common ancestor.

Legal consequences

Affinity has historically been significant chiefly in the law of marriage. Canon law and many civil-law systems treated affinity, like consanguinity, as an impediment to marriage within prohibited degrees — a man might not marry his deceased wife's sister, for example, a restriction long debated in England and abolished by the Deceased Wife's Sister's Marriage Act 1907. In canon law, affinity arises not only from valid marriage but, in some doctrines, from intercourse (affinitas ex copula illicita). Affinity may also matter in rules disqualifying judges or officials from acting in matters involving their spouse's relatives.

Degrees of affinity

The degree of affinity between a spouse and the other spouse's relative corresponds to the degree of consanguinity between that spouse and the relative: one's spouse's parent is a relation by affinity in the first degree, a spouse's sibling in the second degree, and so on. In most systems affinity does not create affinity (affinitas non parit affinitatem): the relatives of one spouse are not, merely by that fact, related by affinity to the relatives of the other.
