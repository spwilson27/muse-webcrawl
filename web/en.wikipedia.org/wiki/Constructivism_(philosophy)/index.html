Constructivism (philosophy)

Constructivism is a family of views in philosophy, mathematics, epistemology and the social sciences holding that knowledge, truth, or certain objects and categories are not simply discovered in a mind-independent world but are in some way constructed — by the mind, by social practices, or by the procedures used to investigate them.

Epistemological constructivism

In epistemology, constructivism holds that knowledge is actively built by the knower rather than passively received. Giambattista Vico's principle verum ipsum factum ("the true is precisely what is made") is an early statement. Kant's "Copernican revolution" — the idea that objects of experience conform to the structures of cognition — is another forerunner. In the twentieth century, Jean Piaget's genetic epistemology described knowledge as constructed through stages of cognitive development, and Ernst von Glasersfeld developed "radical constructivism", according to which knowledge is judged by its viability rather than by correspondence to an external reality.

Mathematical constructivism

In the philosophy of mathematics, constructivism (closely related to intuitionism, founded by L. E. J. Brouwer) requires that a mathematical object be shown to exist by actually constructing it, and that a proof of a disjunction must provide a proof of one of the disjuncts. Constructivists therefore reject unrestricted use of the law of excluded middle and of proof by contradiction for existence claims. Constructive systems, such as Errett Bishop's constructive analysis, recover much of ordinary mathematics while tracking computational content.

Social constructivism

Social constructivism and the related sociology of knowledge, classically stated in Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann's The Social Construction of Reality (1966), examine how phenomena such as money, marriage, or gender categories are created and maintained through social processes. In political philosophy, John Rawls described his theory of justice as "political constructivism": principles are the outcome of a specified procedure of construction rather than reflections of an independent moral order. Christine Korsgaard developed a Kantian version in ethics.

Criticism

Critics argue that strong constructivism slides into relativism or idealism, struggles to explain the success of science, and may confuse the process by which beliefs arise with their justification. Defenders reply that constructivism is compatible with realism about the world that constrains our constructions.
