Mesolimbic pathway

The mesolimbic pathway, sometimes referred to as the reward pathway, is a dopaminergic pathway in the brain. It connects the ventral tegmental area (VTA) in the midbrain to the ventral striatum of the basal ganglia in the forebrain, which includes the nucleus accumbens and the olfactory tubercle. It is one of the four major dopaminergic pathways, alongside the mesocortical, nigrostriatal, and tuberoinfundibular pathways.

Dopaminergic projections of the mesolimbic pathway

Anatomy and function

Dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area release dopamine in the nucleus accumbens, amygdala, hippocampus, and related limbic structures. The pathway is central to the brain's reward system: it mediates the incentive salience of rewarding stimuli — food, sex, social interaction, and addictive drugs — and underlies reinforcement learning and motivation. Phasic firing of VTA neurons encodes reward prediction errors, signalling outcomes that are better or worse than expected.

Clinical significance

Virtually all addictive drugs, from cocaine and amphetamine to nicotine, alcohol, and opioids, ultimately increase dopamine transmission in the nucleus accumbens, and chronic use produces long-lasting adaptations in the pathway that drive craving and relapse. Dysregulation of mesolimbic dopamine has also been implicated in schizophrenia, where excessive dopamine signalling is associated with positive symptoms such as delusions and hallucinations; most antipsychotic drugs act by blocking dopamine D2 receptors. Conversely, blunted reward signalling in this pathway is associated with anhedonia in depression.
