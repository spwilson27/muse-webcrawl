Secondary hypertension

Secondary hypertension is high blood pressure caused by an identifiable underlying condition, in contrast to primary (essential) hypertension, which has no single discoverable cause. Secondary causes account for roughly 5–10% of hypertension in adults, and a much larger share in children and in patients with early-onset, severe, or treatment-resistant hypertension. Identifying the cause matters because many secondary forms can be cured or specifically treated — for example by surgery or by targeted medication — rather than requiring lifelong nonspecific therapy.

Causes

  • Kidney disease — chronic kidney disease and renal artery stenosis (from atherosclerosis or fibromuscular dysplasia) are the commonest secondary causes overall.
  • Primary aldosteronism (Conn syndrome) — autonomous aldosterone production by an adrenal adenoma or bilateral hyperplasia; now recognised as a leading and frequently missed cause, suggested by low potassium and resistant hypertension.
  • Obstructive sleep apnea — strongly associated with resistant and nocturnal hypertension.
  • Thyroid and parathyroid disorders — hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, and hyperparathyroidism.
  • Cushing syndrome — excess cortisol from pituitary, adrenal, or ectopic sources.
  • Pheochromocytoma — a rare catecholamine-secreting tumour causing episodic palpitations, headache, sweating, and labile blood pressure.
  • Coarctation of the aorta — a congenital narrowing, classically causing higher pressures in the arms than the legs in young patients.
  • Drugs and substances — oral contraceptives, NSAIDs, decongestants, stimulants, calcineurin inhibitors, liquorice, alcohol, and others.

Diagnosis and treatment

Clues prompting a secondary workup include onset before age 30 or after 55, abrupt onset or sudden worsening, blood pressure resistant to three drugs, an abdominal bruit, unprovoked hypokalemia, or a suggestive pattern of symptoms. Investigation is directed by the suspected cause: plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio for primary aldosteronism, renal imaging and Doppler studies for renal artery stenosis, metanephrines for pheochromocytoma, and sleep studies for apnea. Treatment addresses the cause — adrenalectomy or mineralocorticoid-receptor blockers for aldosteronism, stenting or surgery in selected cases of renal artery stenosis or coarctation, tumour removal for pheochromocytoma — together with standard antihypertensive therapy and cardiovascular risk management.
