Battle of Geok Tepe

The Battle of Geok Tepe (also spelled Gökdepe) in 1881 was the decisive engagement of the Russian conquest of Central Asia, in which Imperial Russian forces under General Mikhail Skobelev stormed the fortress of Geok Tepe, held by the Teke Turkmens of the Akhal oasis. The storming was followed by a massacre of thousands of fleeing defenders and non-combatants, an event that remains central to Turkmen national memory.

Background

Russia's first expedition against the Teke Turkmens in 1879 ended in humiliating repulse before Geok Tepe. Determined to avenge the defeat and secure the Transcaspian region against the flank of British India, the Tsarist government mounted a methodical second campaign under the flamboyant General Mikhail Skobelev, who built a supply railway inland and assembled overwhelming artillery. The Teke concentrated their people, flocks and stores inside the great mud-walled fortress of Geok Tepe (Denghil Tepe), a perimeter of some 3.5 miles.

The siege and storming

Skobelev invested the fortress in late December 1880 and sapped forward under fire despite fierce Turkmen sorties, one of which nearly captured the Russian camp on the night of 23/24 December; the Teke commander Berdi Murad Khan was killed by shellfire early in the siege. On 24 January 1881 a mine detonated beneath the wall blew a great breach, and Russian columns stormed in behind a heavy bombardment. The defenders broke and fled across the desert toward the north, pursued by Cossack cavalry and dragoons who cut down fugitives for miles. Estimates of Turkmen dead range from Skobelev's own figure of about 6,500 killed inside the fortress and 8,000 in the pursuit, to higher local traditions.

Aftermath

The fall of Geok Tepe broke Turkmen resistance; Ashgabat was occupied days later, and the Akhal oasis was annexed, paving the way for the submission of Merv in 1884 and the completion of the Russian conquest of what became Russian Turkestan. Skobelev's maxim — "the harder you hit them, the longer they remain quiet" — summed up the deliberate terror of the operation, which was reported in the European press and criticised even in Russia. In independent Turkmenistan the battle is commemorated as a national tragedy; a great mosque and memorial complex now stands at Geok Tepe, and 12 January is a day of remembrance.
