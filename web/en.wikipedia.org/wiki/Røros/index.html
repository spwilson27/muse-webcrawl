Røros (Southern Sámi: Plassje) is a municipality and mining town in Trøndelag county, Norway. The town was founded in 1644 after the discovery of copper ore, and the Røros Copper Works operated for 333 years, until 1977. It is one of Europe's oldest towns of wooden buildings and was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1980 as the "Røros Mining Town and the Circumference".

History

Copper was discovered in the mountains near Røros in 1644, and within two years a smelter was built on the Hitterelva river. The town grew up around the works with a grid of streets, miners' cottages, and farms, many of which survive. The town was burned by Swedish forces in 1678 and 1679 during the Scanian War and rebuilt.

Røros Church, a large octagonal stone church consecrated in 1784 and seating about 1,600, is one of Norway's most prominent wooden-era churches and a symbol of the mining company's power. The Slag heaps (Slegghaugan) and the preserved "Circumference" — the mining company's privileged area of 45 km radius — form part of the World Heritage listing.

Geography and climate

Røros lies at about 630 m above sea level near the Glåma river and the Swedish border. It has a subarctic climate and is famous for extreme winter cold; temperatures below −40 °C have been recorded, and −50.4 °C was measured in 1914, among the lowest ever in Norway.

Culture

The town is the setting for Johan Falkberget's novels of mining life. Røros hosts an annual winter market (Rørosmartnan, since 1854) and the region is known for traditional foods such as pjalt and sour-cream products. The author Dag Solstad and the painter Harald Sohlberg, who painted the iconic "Winter Night in Rondane" and scenes of Røros's streets, are associated with the town.
