The Ramsey problem, formulated by the economist and mathematician Frank P. Ramsey in his 1928 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Saving," asks how much of its income a nation (or a representative household) should save at each moment in order to maximize well-being over time. It is one of the foundational problems of intertemporal choice in economics and the basis of the Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model of economic growth.

Formulation

Ramsey modelled an infinitely lived household that chooses a path of consumption c(t) to maximize the discounted integral of utility over time, subject to the accumulation of capital: saving equals output minus consumption and depreciation. Unusually for his time, Ramsey refused to discount future utility on purely ethical grounds, writing that discounting "is ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination"; instead the integral is kept finite by comparing consumption paths to a bliss point of maximum attainable utility.

Solution and legacy

Applying the calculus of variations, Ramsey derived the Keynes–Ramsey rule, which relates the optimal growth rate of consumption to the interest rate, the discount rate, and the elasticity of marginal utility. David Cass and Tjalling Koopmans independently revived and reformulated the model in the 1960s, embedding it in the neoclassical growth framework. The Ramsey framework remains the workhorse of modern dynamic macroeconomics, optimal taxation, and climate-economics models of optimal saving and investment.
