Halva

Halva (also halvah, halwa and similar spellings) is a group of dense, sweet confections found across the Middle East, Central and South Asia, the Balkans and North Africa. The word derives from the Arabic ḥalwā (حلوى), meaning "sweet". Recipes date back at least to the early medieval Islamic world, and from there halva spread along trade routes from Spain to India.

Types

Two broad families are usually distinguished. Sesame-based halva, the best-known type in the Levant, Turkey, Greece and the Jewish diaspora, is made by mixing tahini (sesame paste) with hot sugar syrup — often with soapwort or other emulsifiers — producing a crumbly, crystalline block that may be plain or flavoured with cocoa, coffee, vanilla or pistachio. Flour- or semolina-based halva is made by toasting flour or semolina in oil or butter and combining it with syrup or sugar; variants are common in Iran, Turkey (irmik helvası), India (suji ka halwa) and Pakistan. Other regional types use sunflower seeds (in Russia and Ukraine, where sunflower halva is a mass-market sweet), corn starch, rice flour, chickpeas or nut butters.

Cultural role

Halva is associated with both celebration and mourning. In Turkey a semolina helva is traditionally served after funerals and on religious nights; in Greece it is a standard Lenten sweet; in Jewish tradition sesame halva, sold in delicatessens worldwide, is an everyday treat; and in India halwa is prepared for festivals and religious offerings. Because it is energy-dense, keeps well without refrigeration and in its sesame form contains no animal products, it has also been used as a ration food and features among the foods carried on expeditions and spaceflights.
