Fitts's law

Formulated1954
Formulated byPaul Fitts
FieldHuman–computer interaction, psychology

Movement time increases with distance to a target and decreases with target width

Fitts's law (often quoted as Fitts' law) is a predictive model of human movement primarily used in human–computer interaction and ergonomics. Proposed by Paul Fitts in 1954, it states that the time required to move rapidly to a target area is a function of the ratio between the distance to the target and the width of the target.

Formulation

In its common modern (Shannon) form, movement time MT = a + b · log₂(D/W + 1), where D is the distance to the target centre, W is the width of the target along the axis of motion, and the logarithmic term is called the index of difficulty, measured in bits. Larger, nearer targets are quicker to acquire; small, distant ones take longer and are more error-prone.

Applications

Fitts's law guides user-interface design: important buttons are made large, frequently used controls are placed near the pointer's typical position, and screen edges and corners are prized locations because the pointer stops there, effectively making targets infinitely wide. The law applies to pointing with mice, touchpads, touchscreens, and other devices, and to movements by both healthy users and people with motor impairments.
