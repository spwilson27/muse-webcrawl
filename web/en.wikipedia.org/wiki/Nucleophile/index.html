Nucleophile

A nucleophile (Nu−) attacks an electrophilic carbon, displacing a leaving group.

A nucleophile is a chemical species that forms a bond by donating a pair of electrons to an electrophile. The term, meaning "nucleus-loving", was introduced by Christopher Ingold in the 1930s. Because they donate electrons, all nucleophiles are Lewis bases, though nucleophilicity describes reaction rates toward carbon and other electrophilic centres rather than thermodynamic basicity alone.

Examples

Common nucleophiles include anions such as hydroxide (OH−), cyanide (CN−), halides and hydride, and neutral molecules with lone pairs such as water, ammonia and amines, alcohols, and the π bonds of alkenes and aromatic rings. In organometallic chemistry, reagents such as Grignard reagents act as powerful carbon nucleophiles.

Nucleophilicity

Nucleophilic strength depends on charge (anions are usually stronger than neutral analogues), polarisability, solvation and steric hindrance. In polar protic solvents, larger, more polarisable halides such as iodide are the strongest nucleophiles, whereas in polar aprotic solvents the order reverses and fluoride becomes highly reactive. Nucleophiles are central to the substitution mechanisms of organic chemistry — the backside attack of the SN2 reaction and the capture of carbocations in SN1 reactions — as well as to addition reactions at carbonyl groups. Quantitative scales, such as the Swain–Scott and Mayr nucleophilicity scales, rank nucleophiles empirically.
