Thematic Apperception Test

Thematic Apperception Test

Abbreviation: TAT

Developed by: Christiana D. Morgan and Henry A. Murray

Institution: Harvard Psychological Clinic

First described: 1935

Type: Projective psychological test

The Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) is a projective psychological test widely used to assess personality, motives, and patterns of thought. Examinees are shown a series of ambiguous picture cards — there are 31 cards in the standard set, depicting people in everyday but equivocal situations — and asked to invent a dramatic story for each: what is happening, what led up to it, what the characters are thinking and feeling, and how it will end.

History

The test was developed in the 1930s at the Harvard Psychological Clinic by Christiana D. Morgan and Henry A. Murray, and described in Murray's Explorations in Personality (1938). It rests on the assumption that people project their own needs, conflicts and attitudes onto ambiguous stimuli — "apperception" referring to the interpretation of new experience in terms of past experience. Murray's framework analysed stories in terms of the protagonist's "needs" and the environmental "press" acting on them.

Use and criticism

The TAT became one of the most commonly administered projective tests in clinical psychology and was adapted for research on achievement motivation by David McClelland and colleagues, who developed more standardised scoring systems. Critics note that scoring is often impressionistic, with variable reliability and validity, and that the cards reflect the culture of their time. A children's version, the Children's Apperception Test (CAT), was developed by Leopold Bellak. Despite the criticisms, the TAT remains in clinical and research use.
