Romantic poetry

Romantic poetry is the poetry of the Romantic era, an artistic, literary, musical and intellectual movement that originated in Europe towards the end of the 18th century. It involved a reaction against prevailing Enlightenment ideas, emphasising emotion, imagination, individualism and the sublime in nature. In most areas the movement flourished from about 1800 to 1850.

English Romantic poetry

The first generation of English Romantics included William Blake, William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge; their jointly published Lyrical Ballads (1798), with Wordsworth's preface (1800), is regarded as the movement's manifesto, arguing for "the real language of men". The second generation — Lord Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley and John Keats — produced famous lyrical and narrative works such as Don Juan, Prometheus Unbound and Ode to a Nightingale, before all three died young abroad.

Characteristics

Romantic poets prized spontaneity ("the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings", in Wordsworth's phrase), celebrated common life and childhood, and treated nature as a living presence and moral teacher. They revived medieval and folk forms, experimented with metre and diction, and frequently wrote about political liberty, inspired and later disillusioned by the French Revolution.

Other traditions

In Germany, poets such as Goethe, Schiller, Novalis and Hölderlin developed Romantic themes; in France, Lamartine, Hugo and Musset led the movement; in Poland, Adam Mickiewicz became the national bard; and in Russia, Alexander Pushkin fused Romantic themes with classical form. American poets including William Cullen Bryant and later Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson absorbed Romantic influence.

Legacy

Romantic poetry reshaped expectations of the lyric, the poet's persona and the place of feeling in art, influencing the Victorians, the Symbolists and modern poetry.
