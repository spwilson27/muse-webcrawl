Anti-lock braking system

An anti-lock braking system (ABS) is a safety anti-skid braking system used on aircraft and land vehicles such as cars, motorcycles, trucks and buses. ABS operates by preventing the wheels from locking up (ceasing rotation) during braking, thereby maintaining tractive contact with the road surface and allowing the driver to maintain steering control.

Operation

Wheel-speed sensors monitor each wheel's rotation. If the electronic control unit detects a wheel decelerating much faster than the others — indicating imminent lock-up — it commands hydraulic valves to reduce brake pressure at that wheel, then reapplies it, cycling many times per second. The driver may feel pulsation through the brake pedal. Modern systems form the basis of electronic stability control and traction control.

History

Early anti-skid concepts were developed for aircraft (Dunlop's Maxaret, 1950s) and the 1966 Jensen FF's mechanical system. Bosch and Mercedes-Benz introduced electronic ABS on the S-Class in 1978, and it became widespread in the 1980s–90s. ABS has been mandatory on new cars in the European Union since 2004 and in the United States since 2013 (as part of ESC requirements).

Effectiveness

ABS generally reduces stopping distances on dry and wet pavement and improves steerability during emergency braking, though on loose gravel or snow a locked wheel can sometimes stop a vehicle sooner.
