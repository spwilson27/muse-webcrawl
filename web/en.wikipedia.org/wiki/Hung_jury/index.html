Hung jury

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hung jury, also called a deadlocked jury, is a jury that cannot agree upon a verdict after extended deliberation, where a specified majority (unanimity in US criminal trials after Ramos v. Louisiana, 2020; majorities of 10–2 or more in England and Wales after a set period) is required. When the judge accepts the deadlock is genuine, the result is a mistrial; the defendant is neither convicted nor acquitted, and double-jeopardy law generally permits retrial (United States v. Perez, 1824, on manifest necessity).

Dynamics

Before declaring deadlock, judges may give a supplemental instruction urging minority jurors to reconsider in light of the majority's views — the controversial Allen charge (1896), criticised as coercive and softened or abandoned in many states ("dynamite charge" is its critics' name). Hung juries are relatively uncommon (empirical estimates in US trials vary widely, often in the low single digits to around one in ten cases depending on jurisdiction and case type) but salient: they can signal evidentiary weakness, jury nullification, or genuine community division, and prosecutors weigh retrial against plea offers and dropped charges in their shadow. The publicity asymmetry — a lone holdout lionised or vilified — is a staple of trial commentary and fiction.
