Hot Jupiter

Hot Jupiters are a class of gas giant exoplanets that orbit very close to their stars — typically within 0.1 astronomical units, with periods of a few days — and are therefore heated to temperatures of 1,000 K or more. Their discovery in the 1990s overturned the expectation, drawn from the Solar System, that giant planets form only far from their stars.

Discovery

The first exoplanet found around a sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b (announced 1995 by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, who won the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics), was a hot Jupiter: roughly half Jupiter's mass orbiting in 4.2 days. The pulsar planets found in 1992 had been stranger still, but hot Jupiters posed the direct theoretical puzzle. They were initially easy to find because their large masses and short periods produce strong radial-velocity signals, and many early exoplanets were of this type — a selection effect rather than cosmic typicality: surveys suggest only about 1% of sun-like stars host one.

Formation theories

Because gas giants need the cold conditions beyond the snow line to accrete, hot Jupiters are thought to form farther out and migrate inward, either by interacting with the protoplanetary disc (migration) or by being flung onto eccentric orbits through planet–planet scattering or Kozai–Lidov cycles and then tidally circularised. Many are tidally locked, some orbit retrograde, and their atmospheres — studied by transit spectroscopy — show inflated radii, escaping hydrogen, and winds redistributing heat between day and night sides. The most extreme, such as KELT-9b, exceed 4,000 K on the dayside.
