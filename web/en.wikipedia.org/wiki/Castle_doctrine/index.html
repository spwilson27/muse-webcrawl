Castle doctrine

Castle doctrine
Type: Legal doctrine (common law)
Related: Self-defense, stand-your-ground law, duty to retreat

The castle doctrine, also known as the castle law or defence of habitation law, is a legal doctrine that designates a person's abode — or in some jurisdictions any legally occupied place such as a vehicle or workplace — as a place in which that person has protections and immunities permitting them, in certain circumstances, to use force (up to and including deadly force) to defend themselves against an intruder, free from legal prosecution for the consequences.

Origins

The principle derives from English common law, summarised in the maxim that "an Englishman's home is his castle," associated with Sir Edward Coke's Institutes and the Semayne's Case (1604). Unlike jurisdictions imposing a duty to retreat, castle doctrine jurisdictions generally impose no such duty inside the home.

United States

Most U.S. states have some form of castle doctrine, often codified or reinforced by statute. Stronger "stand-your-ground" laws, such as Florida's 2005 statute, extend the no-retreat principle beyond the home. Conditions typically include that the occupant reasonably believes the intruder intends violence and that the occupant is not the initial aggressor.

Other jurisdictions

Many countries permit force in home defence under general self-defence law but impose proportionality requirements stricter than U.S. castle statutes; the doctrine as such is chiefly a common-law and American concept.
