Robert Houghwout Jackson (February 13, 1892 – October 9, 1954) was an American lawyer, jurist and statesman who served as an associate justice of the Supreme Court of the United States from 1941 until his death. He had previously served as United States Solicitor General and Attorney General under President Franklin D. Roosevelt. Jackson is remembered above all as the chief prosecutor for the United States at the Nuremberg trials of Nazi war criminals after the Second World War, and as one of the Court's most eloquent stylists.

Early career

Raised in Frewsburg, New York, Jackson never earned a law degree — he read law and spent a year at Albany Law School — yet built a successful practice in Jamestown before joining the Roosevelt administration. As head of the Justice Department's Tax and Antitrust divisions, Solicitor General (1938), and Attorney General (1940), he became Roosevelt's leading advocate for the New Deal and for aid to Britain before America's entry into the war.

Supreme Court

Roosevelt appointed Jackson to the Supreme Court in 1941. His opinions are prized for their prose and their defence of civil liberties: he wrote the majority opinion in West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette (1943), holding that schoolchildren could not be compelled to salute the flag — "If there is any fixed star in our constitutional constellation, it is that no official … can prescribe what shall be orthodox in politics, nationalism, religion, or other matters of opinion." He dissented in Korematsu v. United States (1944) against the upheld internment of Japanese Americans. His concurrence in the Steel Seizure Case, Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952), setting out a three-part framework for presidential power, became one of the most cited analyses in American constitutional law.

Nuremberg

On leave from the Court in 1945–1946, Jackson served as chief counsel for the United States before the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg. He negotiated the London Charter establishing the tribunal and delivered the celebrated opening statement: "That four great nations, flushed with victory and stung with injury, stay the hand of vengeance and voluntarily submit their captive enemies to the judgment of the law is one of the most significant tributes that Power has ever paid to Reason." The trial's legacy shaped international criminal law and the later tribunals for Yugoslavia, Rwanda and the International Criminal Court. Jackson's absence and his disappointment at not becoming Chief Justice fuelled a bitter public feud with Justice Hugo Black. He died of a heart attack in 1954, months after joining the unanimous opinion in Brown v. Board of Education.
