Occupy Wall Street (OWS) was a protest movement that began on 17 September 2011 with the occupation of Zuccotti Park in New York City's financial district, protesting social and economic inequality, corporate greed and the influence of money in politics in the wake of the 2008 financial crisis. Its slogan, "We are the 99%", contrasted the wealthiest 1% with the rest of the population and entered global political vocabulary.

Origins

The protest was prompted by a call from the Canadian anti-consumerist magazine Adbusters in July 2011, drawing inspiration from the Arab Spring and Spain's anti-austerity 15-M movement. Activists adopted leaderless, consensus-based general assemblies and the "human microphone" — crowd repetition of a speaker's words — after amplification was banned.

Spread and eviction

Within weeks, "Occupy" encampments appeared in hundreds of cities across the United States and around the world; a coordinated day of action took place on 15 October 2011. Mass arrests (some 700 on the Brooklyn Bridge on 1 October) and incidents such as the pepper-spraying of UC Davis students drew wide coverage. On 15 November 2011 New York police cleared the Zuccotti camp in an overnight raid; most other encampments were evicted in the following weeks, though activism continued through offshoots such as Occupy Sandy (hurricane relief, 2012), Strike Debt and campaigns against foreclosures.

Impact

Although OWS declined to issue formal demands and achieved few immediate policy changes, it is widely credited with shifting public debate toward inequality — a theme later central to politicians such as Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren — popularising the "1% versus 99%" framing, and influencing subsequent movements and labour and debt activism. Critics faulted its horizontal structure and refusal of leadership for limiting its effectiveness.
