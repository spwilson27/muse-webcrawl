Managed lane

A managed lane is a type of highway lane that is operated according to a management scheme — such as tolling, vehicle occupancy requirements or vehicle-type restrictions — in order to optimise traffic flow, reliability and throughput. Managed lanes are used on freeways in many metropolitan areas, especially in the United States.

Types

Common forms of managed lanes include:

  • High-occupancy vehicle (HOV) lanes, restricted to vehicles carrying a minimum number of occupants, typically two or three.
  • High-occupancy toll (HOT) lanes, in which single-occupant vehicles may use HOV lanes by paying a toll, often varied dynamically with congestion.
  • Express toll lanes, open to all paying users, with prices adjusted to maintain a target speed.
  • Bus-only and transit lanes, and lanes restricted to trucks or giving priority to certain vehicle types.

Purpose and operation

Rather than simply adding general-purpose capacity, transportation agencies use managed lanes to provide a reliable travel-time option, encourage carpooling and transit use, and generate revenue. Tolls may be fixed by schedule, vary by time of day, or be set dynamically in response to measured traffic conditions. Many facilities are reversible or separated from general traffic by barriers or painted buffers.

Criticism

Managed lanes have drawn criticism on equity grounds — toll lanes are sometimes derided as "Lexus lanes" — while supporters argue they provide a dependable alternative for all users and a source of funding for corridor improvements, including transit.
