Yayue

Yayue (Chinese: 雅樂; pinyin: yǎyuè, "elegant music") was the formal ritual and court music of ancient and imperial China, performed at sacrifices to Heaven, Earth, and imperial ancestors, at state banquets, and at other ceremonies of the Confucian state cult. The term is credited to the Duke of Zhou in the early Zhou dynasty, and Confucius famously praised properly ordered yayue while condemning the "decadent" popular styles (suyue, "vulgar music") that he thought corrupted it.

Instruments and performance

A yayue ensemble is built around melodic percussion: sets of tuned bronze bells (bianzhong) and stone chimes (bianqing), accompanied by zithers (se, qin), flutes (xiao, chi), mouth organs (sheng), ocarinas (xun), and drums. Performances included stately dances — civil dances with feather and flute, and martial dances with shield and axe — arranged in ranks of dancers whose number signalled the rank of the patron. The music is deliberately slow, spare, and solemn, in keeping with the Confucian view that music should harmonize society and express cosmic order rather than excite the senses.

History and legacy

Chinese dynasties repeatedly "restored" yayue, each reform debating correct pitch standards and instrumentation; the Song-dynasty emperor Huizong's Dasheng bureau was a famous attempt. Because court repertories were repeatedly lost to war, the living traditions were transmitted most fully abroad: Korean aak (also read "yayue") and Japanese gagaku preserve music and instruments descended from Tang and Song court styles, and Vietnamese nhã nhạc derives from Ming forms. Within China, Confucian ritual music survives in ceremonies such as those at the Temple of Confucius in Qufu, and in the reconstructed ritual music maintained in Taiwan. Korean aak and Vietnamese court music have both been proclaimed UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage.
