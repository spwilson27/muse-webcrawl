The Gregorian mission (also called the Augustinian mission) was the mission sent by Pope Gregory I ("Gregory the Great") in 596 to convert the pagan Anglo-Saxons of England to Christianity. Led by Augustine, prior of St Andrew's monastery in Rome, the party of about forty monks landed in the Kingdom of Kent in 597 and was received by King Æthelberht, whose Frankish wife Bertha was already Christian.

The mission

Æthelberht gave the missionaries a base in Canterbury, permission to preach, and eventually his own baptism —traditionally at Pentecost 597 — followed by the mass baptism of thousands of his subjects. Augustine was consecrated bishop and then archbishop, founding the see of Canterbury and churches dedicated to Christ and to Saints Peter and Paul (later St Augustine's Abbey). Gregory's surviving letters to the mission include the famous Libellus Responsionum, answering Augustine's practical questions about the young church, and advice — relayed in Bede's history — to adapt rather than destroy pagan temples and festivals. A second wave of missionaries arrived in 601, including Mellitus and Paulinus.

Legacy

From its Kentish bridgehead, Christianity spread — unevenly and with setbacks, as kingdoms relapsed and were re-converted — across the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms over the following century, while Canterbury retained the primacy Gregory had intended (his original plan had envisaged metropolitan sees at London and York). The mission is the conventional starting point of the history of the English Church.
