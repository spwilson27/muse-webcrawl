Forty-Eighters

OriginGerman states and Central Europe
PeakedAfter the Revolutions of 1848
Known forLiberal reform, abolitionism, Union service

The Forty-Eighters were Europeans who participated in or supported the Revolutions of 1848 that swept Europe in the spring of that year. Many emigrated to the United States, Australia, and elsewhere after the revolutions were suppressed, bringing with them strong commitments to democracy, liberal reform, and in the United States, abolitionism.

Emigration to the United States

German Forty-Eighters settled heavily in the Midwest — Cincinnati, St. Louis, Milwaukee — and in Texas. They founded German-language newspapers and schools, supported the new Republican Party, and were a significant anti-slavery constituency. Prominent examples include Carl Schurz, who became a Union general, U.S. Senator, and Secretary of the Interior, and Franz Sigel, a Union general.

Role in the Civil War

Thousands of Forty-Eighters served in the Union Army during the American Civil War, and their political organizing helped keep immigrant communities aligned with the Union cause. Others, such as the Texas Hill Country Germans, resisted the Confederacy at considerable personal cost.

Legacy

The Forty-Eighters shaped American education (including the spread of kindergartens), journalism, brewing, and civic life in the cities where they settled, and they remain a celebrated chapter in German-American history.
