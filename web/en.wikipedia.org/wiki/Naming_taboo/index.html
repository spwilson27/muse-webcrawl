Naming taboo

A naming taboo is a cultural taboo against speaking or writing the given name of an exalted person, such as an emperor, one's ancestors, or a religious figure. Naming taboos were especially systematic in imperial China, where they were known as bìhuì (避諱), and also occurred in Korea, Vietnam, and Japan, as well as in many other cultures worldwide.

China

In imperial China it was forbidden to use the personal name of the reigning emperor, of one's own ancestors, and of Confucius. When a character contained in such a name had to be written, scribes substituted a synonym, used a variant or incomplete form of the character (often omitting a stroke), or left a blank. Historians exploit these substitutions to date documents and detect later editing of texts.

Famous examples include:

  • The name of the First Emperor, Ying Zheng (政), caused the first month of the year, zhēngyuè (政月), to be respelled and re-pronounced as zhèngyuè (正月) — a pronunciation retained today.
  • Under the Tang dynasty, the characters in Emperor Taizong's name Li Shimin (世民) led to "世" being replaced by "代" and "民" by "人" in many contexts; the bodhisattva Guanshiyin (觀世音) was shortened to Guanyin (觀音).
  • The Kangxi Emperor's name, Xuanye (玄燁), led to the renaming of the Xuanwu Gate in the Forbidden City as Shenwu Gate.

Other cultures

Analogous customs include the avoidance of the names of the dead in many Aboriginal Australian and other indigenous cultures; the avoidance of a mother-in-law's name in some African societies; the Jewish practice of not pronouncing the Tetragrammaton; and the use of courtesy names, posthumous names, and temple names in East Asia to avoid personal names of the deceased.
