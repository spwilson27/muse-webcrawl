Municipal solid waste

Municipal solid waste (MSW), commonly known as trash or garbage in the United States and rubbish in Britain, is a waste type consisting of everyday items discarded by the public. It typically includes food scraps, paper, cardboard, plastics, textiles, glass, metals, yard waste and bulky items such as furniture and appliances, collected from households, offices, schools and commercial establishments. It excludes industrial, hazardous, construction and agricultural wastes, which are usually managed separately.

The World Bank estimated that the world generated about 2.01 billion tonnes of municipal solid waste in 2016, roughly 0.74 kilograms per person per day, and projected this to rise to about 3.40 billion tonnes by 2050. High-income countries generate a disproportionate share relative to their population.

Composition

Composition varies widely by income level and region. Globally, food and green waste form the largest fraction (around 44%), followed by paper and cardboard (about 17%) and plastic (about 12%), with smaller shares of glass, metal, rubber, wood and other materials.

Management

MSW management typically follows a hierarchy: prevention and reduction at source, reuse, recycling and composting, energy recovery (such as waste-to-energy incineration), and finally disposal in landfills. Collection is usually organised by local government or contracted private firms. Poorly managed waste contributes to air and water pollution, greenhouse gas emissions (particularly methane from landfills) and the spread of disease; open dumping and open burning remain common in many low-income countries.
