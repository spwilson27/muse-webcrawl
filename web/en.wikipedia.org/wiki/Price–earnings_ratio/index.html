Price–earnings ratio

The price–earnings ratio, also known as P/E ratio, PER, or price multiple, is the ratio of a company's share price to its earnings per share. It is widely used by investors and analysts to judge the relative value of a company's shares in an apples-to-apples comparison to others in its own industry, or to its own history.

Versions

Several variants exist:

  • Trailing P/E uses net income from the most recent 12-month period divided by the weighted average shares outstanding.
  • Forward P/E uses estimated future earnings, usually analysts' forecasts for the next fiscal year.
  • CAPE ratio (cyclically adjusted P/E, or Shiller P/E) uses average inflation-adjusted earnings over the previous ten years to smooth business-cycle effects.

Interpretation

A high P/E may indicate that investors expect strong future growth, or that a stock is overvalued; a low P/E may signal an undervalued company or one facing difficulties. Typical values for broad stock indices have historically ranged from about 15 to 25, rising during speculative bubbles — the US market P/E spiked far above historical norms in 2000 and again briefly during the earnings collapse of 2009. The inverse, earnings yield, is sometimes compared with bond yields.

Limitations

Earnings can be distorted by accounting choices, one-off items, and leverage; the ratio is meaningless for companies with negative earnings, and it ignores growth rates and risk, motivating alternatives such as the PEG ratio and EV/EBITDA.
