Taxonomy (biology)

[Image: Linnaeus's Systema Naturae founded modern taxonomy]

Taxonomy is the science of naming, defining (circumscribing) and classifying groups of biological organisms on the basis of shared characteristics. Organisms are grouped into taxa, and these groups are given a taxonomic rank; groups of a given rank can be aggregated to form a higher rank, creating a taxonomic hierarchy. The discipline encompasses nomenclature, governed by international codes, and systematics, the study of evolutionary relationships.

History

Folk and scholarly classification dates to antiquity — Aristotle grouped animals by mode of reproduction — but modern taxonomy begins with Carl Linnaeus, whose Systema Naturae (1735) established binomial nomenclature and a hierarchy of class, order, genus and species. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by common descent reframed classification as a representation of genealogy, a programme carried forward by Willi Hennig's cladistics in the 20th century and transformed by molecular phylogenetics and DNA barcoding thereafter.

Practice

Taxonomists describe new taxa from specimens, depositing a holotype in a museum or herbarium and publishing a formal diagnosis. Names are regulated by codes for zoology, botany, bacteria and viruses, which enforce priority and typification. The total number of described species is roughly 1.6–2 million, while estimates of true global diversity range from several million to tens of millions; a "taxonomic impediment" — a shortage of specialists and funding — slows the work of description even as extinction proceeds.
