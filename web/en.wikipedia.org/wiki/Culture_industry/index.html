Culture industry

The culture industry (German: Kulturindustrie) is a term coined by the Frankfurt School theorists Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer in their work Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947). It describes the industrial production and mass distribution of cultural goods — films, radio programmes, magazines, and popular music — under capitalism.

Theory

Adorno and Horkheimer argued that mass culture is produced like any other factory output: standardised, formulaic, and designed to pacify audiences rather than provoke thought. The apparent variety of cultural products is, in their phrase, "pseudo-individuality" — superficial differences masking identical underlying structures. Rather than offering genuine escape from work, leisure under the culture industry merely reproduces the conditions of labour, reconciling consumers to the existing social order.

The culture industry thesis holds that:

  • Cultural goods are commodified and produced for profit.
  • Standardisation makes products predictable and interchangeable.
  • Amusement discourages critical thinking and political resistance.
  • Audiences are treated as passive consumers rather than active creators.

Criticism and legacy

Later cultural studies scholars, such as Stuart Hall and John Fiske, criticised the thesis as elitist and overly pessimistic, emphasising that audiences actively reinterpret and resist media messages. Nevertheless, the concept remains influential in media studies, and contemporary debates about algorithmic recommendation, franchise filmmaking, and streaming platforms frequently revive Adorno and Horkheimer's arguments. The related term "creative industries" is used, usually without the critical intent, in cultural policy.
