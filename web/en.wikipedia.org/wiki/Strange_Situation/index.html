Strange Situation

The Strange Situation is a laboratory procedure devised by developmental psychologist Mary Ainsworth in the 1970s to study attachment in children, usually between 12 and 18 months old. The child is observed playing for about twenty minutes while caregivers and strangers enter and leave the room, creating a series of mildly stressful separations and reunions. The child's behaviour on reunion with the caregiver is used to classify the attachment relationship. (See also the companion overview at Strange situation.)

Attachment classifications

From the original Baltimore study Ainsworth identified three patterns. Secure attachment (B): the infant is distressed by separation, seeks contact at reunion, is comforted and returns to play — about 60–65% of middle-class American samples. Insecure-avoidant (A): the infant shows little distress and avoids or ignores the caregiver on reunion. Insecure-resistant/ambivalent (C): the infant is highly distressed and seeks contact at reunion but is not easily soothed, mixing clinging with anger. Mary Main later added a fourth pattern, disorganized/disoriented (D), marked by contradictory or fearful behaviour, associated with maltreatment and later psychopathology.

Criticism and legacy

The procedure has been criticised for being stressful, for classifying a relationship rather than a trait of the child, and for cultural bias — studies in Japan and Germany, for example, produced very different distributions reflecting different child-rearing norms. Despite this, the Strange Situation founded the empirical study of attachment patterns and remains a standard research tool.
