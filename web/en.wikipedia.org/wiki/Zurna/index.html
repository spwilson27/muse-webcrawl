Zurna

The zurna (also surnay, zurla, and related names) is a wind instrument played in central Eurasia, western Asia and parts of North Africa and the Balkans. It is a double-reed, woodwind instrument with a conical bore and a flared bell, giving it a loud, piercing sound. It is the ancestor of the European shawm and a relative of the Chinese suona and the Indian shehnai. The name derives from Persian sor-nāy, "feast flute/pipe".

Description

The zurna is usually turned from a single piece of hardwood, often apricot, plum or boxwood. It has seven finger holes on the front and one thumb hole. A large double reed, held between disks (a lip rest called a kalem assembly in Turkish practice), sits at the narrow top; a removable wooden bell flares from the bottom. Lengths range from about 30 to 60 cm. Because of its volume — a zurna can easily be heard over crowds outdoors — it is traditionally played outside, at weddings, dances, wrestling matches, festivals and processions.

Playing tradition

Across Turkey, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Iran, Iraq, the Balkans (where it is called zurla), and Central Asia, the zurna is classically paired with a large cylindrical drum — the Turkish davul, Armenian dhol, or Balkan tapan — in the zurna–davul duo that provides music for folk dances such as the halay and zeybek. Players employ circular breathing to sustain an unbroken tone. The instrument was also central to the Ottoman mehter (Janissary) military bands, from which some of its repertoire and prestige derives.

Related instruments

Regional variants include the smaller Turkish cura zurna, the Chinese suona (introduced via Central Asia along the Silk Road), the Korean taepyeongso, the Indian shehnai and nadaswaram, and the medieval European shawm, which developed into the oboe.
