Project-based learning (PBL) is a student-centered pedagogy in which students learn about a subject through the experience of solving an open-ended problem found in trigger material, or by working for an extended period to investigate and respond to a complex question, problem, or challenge, producing a public product or presentation. Skills developed include collaboration, communication, critical thinking, and creativity.

History

The approach descends from John Dewey's advocacy of learning by doing and William Heard Kilpatrick's "project method" (1918). In medical education, problem-based and project-based methods were pioneered at McMaster University and Aalborg University in the late 1960s and 1970s. The Buck Institute for Education (now PBLWorks) popularised design standards for rigorous school projects in the 2000s.

Structure

Well-designed projects are organised around a driving question, require sustained inquiry, give students voice and choice, include critique and revision, and end with a public product. Teachers act as facilitators and coaches rather than lecturers. Research reviews find PBL can improve retention and application of knowledge, though outcomes depend heavily on implementation quality and it is sometimes criticised for covering less content, being difficult to assess, and placing heavy planning demands on teachers. PBL is closely related to, but distinct from, problem-based learning, inquiry-based learning, and design-based learning. See also Problem-based learning and Progressive education.
