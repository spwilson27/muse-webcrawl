Successor state

A successor state is a sovereign state that has succeeded a predecessor state as its continuation in international law, or that has emerged from the territory of a former state following its dissolution, secession, or decolonization. The concept is central to the law of state succession, which governs the transfer of rights and obligations — including treaties, debts, and membership in international organizations — from a predecessor to a successor state.

Types of succession

Succession may occur through several mechanisms: the dissolution of a state into multiple new states (as with the Soviet Union in 1991 and Czechoslovakia in 1993), the secession of part of a state's territory (as with South Sudan from Sudan in 2011), decolonization (as with the many states that emerged from European empires after the Second World War), annexation or merger, and the transfer of territory between states.

Continuity versus new statehood

A key distinction is drawn between a successor state that is legally identical to the predecessor — a "continuator" — and an entirely new state. Russia, for example, was accepted as the continuator of the Soviet Union, inheriting its permanent seat on the United Nations Security Council. By contrast, the republics of the former Yugoslavia were generally treated as new states, and Serbia's claim to continue the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia was rejected by the UN Security Council in 1992.

Legal framework

The 1978 Vienna Convention on Succession of States in Respect of Treaties and the 1983 Vienna Convention on Succession of States in Respect of State Property, Archives and Debts attempt to codify the rules, though neither has been widely ratified, and much of the law remains customary.
