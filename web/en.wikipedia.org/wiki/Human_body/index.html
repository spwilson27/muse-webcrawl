Human body

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The human body is the entire structure of a human being, composed of many types of cells organised into tissues, organs and organ systems. An adult body contains on the order of 37 trillion cells and is studied structurally by Anatomy and functionally by Physiology.

The body is conventionally described by its systems: integumentary (skin), skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular, lymphatic and immune, respiratory, digestive, urinary and reproductive. Their coordinated activity maintains homeostasis — the stable internal environment described by Claude Bernard and Walter Cannon.

Composition

By mass the body is mostly oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen; by count, the most numerous cells are red blood cells. Roughly 60% of an adult's mass is water. The gut hosts a microbiome whose bacterial cells are comparable in number to the body's own cells and influence digestion, immunity and even mood.

Development and variation

The body develops from a single fertilised egg through embryonic stages governed by genes and signalling gradients. Adult bodies vary by sex, age, ancestry and environment; anthropometry (height, mass, proportions) has shifted markedly with nutrition over the last two centuries.
