Emery–Dreifuss muscular dystrophy

Emery–Dreifuss muscular dystrophy (EDMD) is a type of muscular dystrophy, a group of heritable diseases that cause progressive impairment of the muscles. EDMD is defined by a collection of three "cardinal signs": early contractures of the muscles (such as in the elbows, Achilles tendons, and neck extensors), slowly progressive muscle weakness and atrophy, and cardiac involvement, particularly conduction defects and arrhythmias that can lead to sudden death. The disease is named after Alan Emery and Fritz Dreifuss, who first described it in a Virginia family in the 1960s.

Genetics

EDMD is genetically heterogeneous, but all known forms are caused by mutations in genes encoding proteins of the nuclear envelope. The classic X-linked form is caused by mutations in the EMD gene, which encodes the inner nuclear membrane protein emerin. Autosomal dominant and recessive forms are most often caused by mutations in LMNA, encoding lamins A and C, intermediate-filament proteins of the nuclear lamina. Rarer forms involve genes such as FHL1, SYNE1, SYNE2, and TMEM43. Because of the involvement of nuclear-envelope proteins, EDMD is classified among the nuclear envelopathies and laminopathies.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin in early childhood with contractures — notably limited elbow extension, Achilles tendon tightness causing toe-walking, and rigidity of the neck and spine. Muscle weakness develops slowly, in a distinctive humeroperoneal distribution affecting the upper arms and lower legs. Cardiac disease usually appears after the muscular symptoms, with sinus node dysfunction, atrioventricular block, atrial paralysis, and risk of sudden cardiac death; female carriers of the X-linked form can also develop serious cardiac disease.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is based on the clinical triad, family history, muscle biopsy and immunostaining (for example, absent emerin staining), and confirmatory genetic testing. There is no cure; management centres on cardiac surveillance, pacemaker or implantable defibrillator placement where indicated, physiotherapy to maintain mobility, and orthopaedic treatment of contractures. Regular cardiology follow-up is considered essential even when muscle symptoms are mild.
