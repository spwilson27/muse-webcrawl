Johann Heinrich Lambert (26 August 1728 – 25 September 1777) was a Swiss polymath who made important contributions to mathematics, physics, astronomy and philosophy. Largely self-taught, he became a member of the Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin, where he worked alongside Euler and corresponded with Kant.

Mathematics

Lambert is best remembered for his 1761 proof that π is irrational — the first rigorous proof of irrationality for a major constant — using continued fractions for tan(x); he also conjectured that e and π are transcendental. In his Theorie der Parallellinien (written 1766, published posthumously) he explored the consequences of denying Euclid's parallel postulate, anticipating non-Euclidean geometry, and derived results now associated with hyperbolic triangles. He introduced the hyperbolic functions into trigonometry and developed the Lambert W function arises from his work on transcendental equations (named for him by later mathematicians).

Physics and other work

Lambert's Photometria (1760) founded the science of photometry: he formulated the cosine law of illumination and the law relating absorption of light to the thickness and concentration of a medium, known as the Lambert–Beer law. The unit of luminance, the lambert, is named for him. In astronomy he speculated about the structure of the Milky Way and multitudes of solar systems. His philosophical Neues Organon (1764) examined appearance and error, and introduced the term "phenomenology" in something close to its modern sense. In cartography he published several map projections in 1772, including the Lambert conformal conic and Lambert azimuthal equal-area projections, still in use today.
