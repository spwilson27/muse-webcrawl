The Yi people (Nuosu: ꆈꌠ, transliterated as Nuosu; Chinese: 彝族; pinyin: Yízú) are an ethnic group in southwestern China, recognised as one of the country's 56 officially recognised ethnic groups. Numbering around nine million, they are the seventh-largest recognised minority in China. The largest concentration lives in the Liangshan Yi Autonomous Prefecture of Sichuan Province, with substantial populations in Yunnan, Guizhou and Guangxi. Related groups are also found in northern Vietnam and Thailand.

Language and script

The Yi languages belong to the Loloish branch of the Tibeto-Burman language family and comprise several mutually unintelligible varieties, including Northern Yi (Nuosu), Eastern Yi and Central Yi. The traditional Yi script is a logo-syllabic writing system with a long history; a standardised syllabary based on the Liangshan variety was adopted in the 1970s and is taught in schools in the autonomous prefecture.

Society and culture

Traditional Nuosu society in Liangshan was organised into a caste-like hierarchy of clans, with hereditary priests known as bimo preserving religious texts and oral literature. The traditional religion, sometimes called Bimoism, centres on ancestor veneration and nature spirits. Yi festivals include the Torch Festival (Dutzie), held in the sixth lunar month, which features wrestling, horse racing, singing and dancing around bonfires. The Yi calendar traditionally divided the year into ten months.

History

Yi ancestors are associated with ancient peoples of the southwest, and Yi groups established local kingdoms and chieftaincies in the mountainous regions between Sichuan, Yunnan and Guizhou. Under the Ming and Qing dynasties, native chieftains (tusi) governed Yi areas under imperial suzerainty. The remote highlands of Greater Liangshan remained largely outside direct administration until the mid-twentieth century, after which social and economic reforms were introduced and Liangshan became an autonomous prefecture in 1952.
