Bootlegging

Bootlegging is the illegal production, transportation, or sale of goods, most famously alcoholic beverages. The term is most strongly associated with the United States during Prohibition (1920–1933), when the Eighteenth Amendment banned the manufacture and sale of intoxicating liquor nationwide. The word is said to derive from the practice of concealing a bottle in the leg of a boot.

Prohibition era

During Prohibition, bootleggers supplied speakeasies and private customers with smuggled or domestically distilled liquor ("moonshine"), while rum-running brought in alcohol by sea from Canada, the Caribbean, and Europe. Organized crime figures such as Al Capone built empires on the trade, and enforcement by the Bureau of Prohibition was hampered by corruption and the scale of public demand. The repeal of Prohibition by the Twenty-first Amendment in 1933 ended the era, though bootlegging persists where alcohol or other goods are banned, heavily taxed, or restricted—for example in dry counties and in countries with alcohol prohibitions.

Other uses

The term is also applied to unauthorized recordings ("bootleg recordings") and other unlicensed copies of copyrighted works, and to smuggling in general.
