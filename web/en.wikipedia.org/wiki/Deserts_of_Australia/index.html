Deserts of Australia

The deserts of Australia are a series of low-rainfall regions that together cover 2,700,000 km2 (1,000,000 sq mi), or 35% of the Australian mainland. The deserts are located mainly in the west and the interior of the country. The largest are the Great Victoria Desert and the Great Sandy Desert.

Major deserts

  • Great Victoria Desert — 348,750 km2, spanning Western Australia and South Australia.
  • Great Sandy Desert — 267,250 km2 in north-western Australia.
  • Tanami Desert — 184,500 km2 in the Northern Territory and Western Australia.
  • Simpson Desert — 176,500 km2, famous for its long parallel sand dunes.
  • Gibson Desert — 156,000 km2 in Western Australia.
  • Little Sandy Desert, Great Stony Desert, Strzelecki Desert, Sturt Stony Desert and the Tirari–Sturt stony desert complete the ten largest.

Climate

Australia is the driest inhabited continent: average rainfall over the desert regions is below 250 mm per year, with high evaporation rates. Temperatures regularly exceed 40 °C in summer. Rain is highly variable and is influenced by the El Niño–Southern Oscillation and the Australian monsoon in the north.

Flora and fauna

Despite the harsh conditions, Australian deserts support spinifex grasslands, mulga and mallee woodlands, and animals such as the red kangaroo, bilby, thorny devil, perentie and dingo, many of which are nocturnal or burrowing to escape heat. The region also contains iconic landforms such as Uluru in the south-west of the Northern Territory.

Human presence

Aboriginal Australians have inhabited the deserts for tens of thousands of years. Today the deserts support cattle stations, mining operations, remote Indigenous communities and major transport routes such as the Stuart Highway and the Gunbarrel Highway.
