Shinbutsu-shūgō

Shinbutsu-shūgō is the syncretism of Shinto and Buddhism that characterised Japanese religion for roughly a thousand years, from the introduction of Buddhism in the 6th century until the separation edicts of the Meiji Restoration in 1868.

Development

As Buddhism spread in Japan, kami (Shinto deities) came to be interpreted as manifestations or protectors of buddhas and bodhisattvas, a doctrine systematised as honji suijaku — kami as "trace manifestations" of universal Buddhist divinities. Shrines were built within temple precincts and temples within shrine grounds; shrine-temple complexes (jingū-ji) were administered by Buddhist monks, and Shinto rites took on Buddhist forms.

Separation

The Meiji government's 1868 policy of shinbutsu bunri (separation of kami and buddhas) forcibly dismantled the syncretic system; in the accompanying haibutsu kishaku movement many Buddhist images and institutions at shrines were destroyed or removed. Despite this, syncretic habits persist in everyday Japanese religious life, where many people participate in both Shinto and Buddhist practices.
