The Mull of Kintyre (from Scottish Gaelic Maol Chinn Tìre, "bare headland of Kintyre") is the southwesternmost tip of the Kintyre Peninsula in southwest Scotland. From here, on a clear day, the coast of Northern Ireland — only about 12 miles (19 km) away across the North Channel — is plainly visible, making the Mull the closest point of Great Britain to Ireland.

Geography and lighthouse

The headland is rugged and exposed to the Atlantic, with steep cliffs falling to a rocky shore. A lighthouse designed by Thomas Smith was first lit in 1788, among the earliest built by the Northern Lighthouse Board; the present tower dates from the 1820s and was automated in 1996. The single-track road down the Mull, descending from the peninsula's ridge, is famously steep and winding.

History

The Mull has long been a strategic landfall. It is associated with the medieval Lordship of the Isles and with Dunaverty Castle at nearby Southend, site of a massacre in 1647 during the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. In June 1994 a Royal Air Force Chinook helicopter, ZD576, crashed on the Mull in fog, killing all 29 people aboard, including 25 senior intelligence experts from Northern Ireland — one of the RAF's worst peacetime losses.

In popular culture

The Mull is internationally known through Paul McCartney's band Wings, whose 1977 single "Mull of Kintyre", co-written with Denny Laine, celebrated McCartney's farm near Campbeltown. Featuring the Campbeltown Pipe Band, it became the UK Christmas number one of 1977 and the first single to sell over two million copies in the United Kingdom.
