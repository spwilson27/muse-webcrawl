Anthroposophy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anthroposophy is a spiritual worldview founded in the early twentieth century by the Austrian esotericist Rudolf Steiner (1861–1925). It postulates the existence of an objective, intellectually comprehensible spiritual world accessible through disciplined inner development and meditation, and it aims to apply the clarity of scientific thinking to spiritual questions.

History

Steiner was originally a leading figure in the German section of the Theosophical Society, but broke with it in 1912–13 — chiefly over its promotion of Jiddu Krishnamurti as a world teacher and over Steiner's emphasis on a Western, Christ-centred path — and founded the Anthroposophical Society in 1913. Its headquarters, the Goetheanum, a distinctive sculptural concrete building in Dornach, Switzerland, opened in 1928.

Applications

Anthroposophy has inspired a wide range of practical initiatives: Waldorf (Steiner) education, founded in 1919 and now one of the largest independent school movements in the world; biodynamic agriculture; anthroposophical medicine; eurythmy, a form of movement art; and Camphill communities for people with developmental disabilities.

Reception

Anthroposophy is generally classified by scholars as a new religious or esoteric movement. Its empirical claims — concerning reincarnation, karma, cosmic evolution and clairvoyant perception — are regarded as pseudoscientific by critics, while its educational and agricultural methods have many practitioners worldwide.
