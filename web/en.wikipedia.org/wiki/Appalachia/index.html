Appalachia is a geographic region located in the central and southern sections of the Appalachian Mountains in the east of North America. In the United States, it is the region defined by the Appalachian Regional Commission (ARC), stretching from the western Catskill Mountains of New York southwestward into Pennsylvania, Ohio, West Virginia, Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, and northern Mississippi — 423 counties across 13 states. The region is home to roughly 26 million people.

Geography

The region encompasses the Allegheny and Cumberland plateaus, the Ridge-and-Valley province, and the Blue Ridge and Great Smoky Mountains, including Mount Mitchell in North Carolina, the highest peak east of the Mississippi River at 6,684 feet (2,037 m). The terrain — steep ridges, narrow valleys ("hollows"), and abundant streams — shaped scattered settlement patterns and historically limited transportation and agriculture, while rich bituminous coal seams underlay much of central Appalachia.

History

Native American peoples, including the Cherokee, Shawnee, and Iroquois nations, inhabited the region long before European settlement. In the eighteenth century, Scots-Irish, German, and English settlers moved into the backcountry, developing a distinctive culture blending British and Irish folk traditions with frontier conditions. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries brought industrial timbering and, above all, coal mining, which drew immigrant and African American workers and produced company towns, labour conflicts such as the Battle of Blair Mountain (1921), and boom-and-bust economics. After the Second World War, mechanisation cut mining employment, and widespread poverty drew national attention in the 1960s; President Lyndon B. Johnson launched his "War on Poverty" partly from Appalachian Kentucky, and Congress created the ARC in 1965 to coordinate development, highway building (the Appalachian Development Highway System), health, and education programmes.

Economy

Coal production has declined sharply since the 1990s due to mechanisation, cheaper natural gas, and environmental regulation, contributing to economic distress in many counties. The region has diversified unevenly into healthcare, education, tourism (notably around the Great Smoky Mountains National Park), manufacturing, and outdoor recreation. Persistent challenges include lower incomes and educational attainment than national averages, infrastructure gaps, and the opioid epidemic, which has hit Appalachian communities especially hard.

Culture

Appalachian culture is known for bluegrass and old-time music (with deep roots in ballad traditions of the British Isles), storytelling, quilting and other crafts, distinctive dialects of American English, and strong traditions of church and family. Literature from the region includes works by James Still, Wendell Berry, and Lee Smith, while J. D. Vance's 2016 memoir Hillbilly Elegy brought renewed national attention to the region's social conditions. Stereotypes of Appalachia in popular media have been criticised by scholars and residents alike as reductive.
