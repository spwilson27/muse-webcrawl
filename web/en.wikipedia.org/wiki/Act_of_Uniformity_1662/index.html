Act of Uniformity 1662

Citation14 Cha. 2. c. 4
Enacted byParliament of England (Cavalier Parliament)
Royal assent19 May 1662
Commencement24 August 1662 (St Bartholomew's Day)
StatusRepealed in most respects

The Act of Uniformity 1662 (14 Cha. 2. c. 4) is an Act of the Parliament of England that prescribed the form of public worship in the Church of England after the Restoration of Charles II. It required all clergy and schoolteachers to use the revised Book of Common Prayer, to declare their "unfeigned assent and consent" to everything in it, and to be ordained by a bishop if they had not already been so ordained.

Provisions

The Act formed part of the Clarendon Code, a series of statutes enacted by the Cavalier Parliament to consolidate the restored Anglican establishment and exclude Protestant Nonconformists from public worship and office. Ministers were required to subscribe to the Act's terms by St Bartholomew's Day, 24 August 1662, and those who refused lost their livings. The Act also imposed requirements for the public reading of morning and evening prayer and for the use of the liturgy in cathedrals, colleges, and schools.

The Great Ejection

Roughly 2,000 Puritan and Presbyterian ministers who could not in conscience conform were ejected from their parishes in what became known as the Great Ejection of 1662. Many of these ejected ministers became founders of English Dissent, conducting worship outside the established church despite subsequent penal legislation such as the Conventicle Act 1664 and the Five Mile Act 1665.

Repeal

The Act's restrictions were gradually dismantled over the following centuries as religious toleration expanded, beginning with the Toleration Act 1689 for Protestant dissenters. Most of the 1662 Act has since been repealed, though its requirement that Church of England worship follow the authorised liturgy shaped Anglican practice for generations.
