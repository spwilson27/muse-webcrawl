Politics of Denmark

Christiansborg Palace, seat of the Folketing

The politics of Denmark take place within the framework of a parliamentary, representative democratic constitutional monarchy, in which the monarch is head of state and the prime minister is head of government. Executive power is exercised by the government, legislative power is vested in the Folketing (parliament), and the judiciary is independent. Denmark has a multi-party system in which no single party usually wins an outright majority, so governments are typically coalitions or minority governments supported by allied parties.

Constitution

The current constitution dates from 5 June 1953, succeeding earlier constitutions of 1849 and 1915. It establishes the Folketing as a unicameral legislature of 179 members, including two each from Greenland and the Faroe Islands, the autonomous territories within the Kingdom of Denmark. Elections use proportional representation with a 2 per cent threshold for levelling seats.

Monarchy

The monarch formally appoints the prime minister and other ministers and represents Denmark abroad, but by convention acts on ministerial advice. Frederik X became king in January 2024 upon the abdication of his mother, Margrethe II.

Political parties

Danish politics has traditionally been organised around a "red bloc" of left-of-centre parties, led by the Social Democrats, and a "blue bloc" of right-of-centre parties, including Venstre and the Conservatives. Other significant parties include the Socialist People's Party, the Danish People's Party, the Social Liberal Party, the Liberal Alliance, the Denmark Democrats, the Moderates, and the Red–Green Alliance. Mette Frederiksen of the Social Democrats has been prime minister since 2019, leading from 2022 an unusual cross-bloc coalition with Venstre and the Moderates.

Welfare state

Denmark is known for its extensive welfare state, often described as part of the Nordic model, with universal healthcare, generous social benefits, and the "flexicurity" labour-market system combining flexible hiring rules with strong unemployment insurance and active labour-market policies.
