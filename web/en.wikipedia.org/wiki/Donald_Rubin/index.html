Donald Rubin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Donald Bruce Rubin (born December 22, 1943) is an American statistician, emeritus professor at Harvard University, known for the Rubin causal model, a framework for causal inference based on potential outcomes, and for foundational work on missing data, multiple imputation, and the propensity score.

Career

Rubin studied psychology at Princeton and completed his PhD in statistics at Harvard in 1970 under William Cochran. After positions at the Educational Testing Service and the University of Wisconsin–Madison, he joined Harvard's statistics department, which he later chaired. He has advised on matters ranging from the 1980 United States census litigation to estimating casualties and treatment effects in medicine.

Contributions

In papers beginning in 1974, Rubin formalised causal inference in terms of the outcomes each unit would show under each treatment — the potential outcomes or Rubin causal model — clarifying the role of random assignment and of the propensity score, developed with Paul Rosenbaum. His 1976 classification of missing-data mechanisms (missing completely at random, at random, and not at random) and his book on multiple imputation (1987) transformed the statistical handling of incomplete data. He also co-developed the EM algorithm's applications and Bayesian methods for survey sampling and matching in observational studies.
