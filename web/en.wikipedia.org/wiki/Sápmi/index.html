Sápmi

Region: Northern Europe
Countries: Norway, Sweden, Finland, Russia
Population (Sámi): est. 80,000–100,000
Languages: Sámi languages, Norwegian, Swedish, Finnish, Russian

Sápmi is the cultural region traditionally inhabited by the Sámi people. Sápmi is in Northern Europe and includes the northern parts of Fennoscandia — the northern reaches of Norway, Sweden, and Finland — together with Russia's Kola Peninsula in the east. The region stretches from the Norwegian Sea coast across the Scandinavian Mountains and the boreal forests to the Barents and White Seas.

Historically the area was called "Lapland" in English and the Sámi "Lapps", terms now widely regarded as dated or pejorative; the Sámi flag, adopted in 1986, flies across the region, and Sámi National Day is celebrated on 6 February, commemorating the first Sámi congress at Trondheim in 1917.

Geography and livelihoods

Sápmi covers roughly 390,000 km² of tundra, mountains, and taiga. Traditional livelihoods centred on semi-nomadic reindeer herding — in which herders follow migratory herds between seasonal pastures — alongside coastal and river fishing, hunting, and gathering. Only a minority of Sámi herd reindeer today; reindeer-herding rights in Norway, Sweden, and Finland are nevertheless legally reserved to Sámi and organized through herding districts (the Swedish sameby).

Political organization

Sámi parliaments — representative bodies elected by Sámi voters — were established in Finland (1973), Norway (1989), and Sweden (1993); the Sámi in Russia lack such a body. The Sámi Parliamentary Council coordinates across borders. Key modern issues include land and water rights (affirmed in the Norwegian Finnmark Act of 2005), the protection of Sámi languages — several of which are endangered — resource extraction and wind-power development on grazing lands, and the legacy of 19th–20th-century assimilation policies ("Norwegianization" and boarding schools), for which official truth and reconciliation processes have been undertaken.
