The Winged Victory of Samothrace, also called the Nike of Samothrace, is a marble Hellenistic sculpture of Nike, the Greek goddess of victory. Created around 200–190 BC, it is regarded as one of the supreme masterpieces of Hellenistic art and one of the most celebrated sculptures in the world. It has been displayed at the Louvre Museum in Paris since 1884, standing at the top of the Daru staircase.

Description

The statue depicts a winged female figure alighting on the prow of a warship, her wind-blown drapery clinging to her body and swirling behind her. The figure itself, carved from white Parian marble, stands 2.44 metres tall; mounted on its grey marble ship's-prow base, the monument reaches 3.28 metres. The head and arms have never been recovered, yet the sense of triumphant movement remains overwhelming.

History

The sculpture was likely commissioned to commemorate a naval victory — possibly by the people of Rhodes — and stood in the Sanctuary of the Great Gods on the island of Samothrace in the northern Aegean. It was discovered in pieces in 1863 by the French consul and amateur archaeologist Charles Champoiseau and shipped to Paris, where it was painstakingly reassembled. The right wing is largely a plaster reconstruction, mirror-copied from the surviving left wing.

Legacy

The Winged Victory has influenced generations of artists and designers; its silhouette inspired, among other things, the hood ornament of early Rolls-Royce cars ("Spirit of Ecstasy") and countless evocations of triumph in flight. Together with the Venus de Milo and the Mona Lisa, it forms the trio of the Louvre's most famous works.
