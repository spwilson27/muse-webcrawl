Circumlocution

Circumlocution (also called circumduction, circumvocalution, or amphigory) is the use of an unnecessarily large number of words to express an idea. It is sometimes used in communication as a tactic to avoid saying something directly, to soften an uncomfortable statement, or to pad out speech; in rhetoric and literature it can also be a deliberate stylistic device.

The word derives from the Latin circum ("around") and locutio ("speech"), so it literally means "speaking around" a topic. A classic comic example is referring to a ladder as "a portable, step-based vertical transportation device".

Usage

Circumlocution is common in bureaucratic and legal language, in diplomacy, and in everyday euphemism, where a speaker wishes to avoid giving offence or to evade a direct answer. In literature, verbose characters who speak in circumlocutions are a staple of comedy; Charles Dickens's Mr. Micawber and the officials of the fictional Circumlocution Office in Little Dorrit are famous examples.

Related concepts

Circumlocution is related to periphrasis, euphemism, pleonasm, and verbosity in general. It is the opposite of concision: where a circumlocution expands, laconic speech compresses. In language learning, circumlocution is also a useful strategy: learners who lack a word can describe the concept instead, for example saying "the thing you open bottles with" for "corkscrew".
