Khazar Khaganate

The Khazar Khaganate was a major commercial empire in southeastern Europe and Central Asia, established by the semi-nomadic Turkic Khazars in the late 7th century. At its height it controlled much of what is today southern Russia, Ukraine, Crimea, western Kazakhstan and the Caucasus, dominating the trade routes between the Black Sea, the Caspian Sea and the Baltic.

History

The Khazars emerged from the collapse of the Western Turkic Khaganate and established their state around the lower Volga, with the capital at Atil near the Volga delta. As allies of the Byzantine Empire they fought a long series of wars against the Arab Caliphates between the 640s and 737, halting Arab expansion into Eastern Europe; the second war ended with a Khazar defeat at the Battle of Marj Ardabil but the Arabs withdrew north of the Caucasus. Khazar armies also reached deep into Transcaucasia.

From the ninth century the Khaganate was increasingly run by a dual kingship: the sacred khagan, a largely ceremonial figure, and the khagan bek or ishad, who commanded the army and administered the state. The empire declined in the tenth century under pressure from the Rus' and the Pechenegs, and Sviatoslav I of Kiev destroyed Atil and the Khazar fortress of Sarkel around 965–969, effectively ending the Khaganate.

Religion

The Khazar ruling class famously converted to Judaism—probably in stages during the eighth and ninth centuries—while the population practised Judaism, Christianity, Islam and Tengrism side by side. The conversion is described in the Khazar Correspondence and in Arabic and Hebrew sources. The extent to which Judaism spread beyond the court remains debated among historians.

Economy

Khazaria grew rich on transit trade: furs, slaves, wax and honey from the forest zone moved south in exchange for silver dirhams, silks and spices. Tolls levied at Atil and on the Volga route were a mainstay of state revenue, and Khazar coins were imitated across the region.
