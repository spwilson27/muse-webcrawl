Testimony of peace

The testimony of peace (or peace testimony) is the best-known of the testimonies of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers). It is a belief that flows from the view of all human beings as partakers of the divine Light, and it has led Quakers for three and a half centuries to refuse to participate in war, to seek nonviolent solutions to conflict, and to work to remove the causes of war.

Origins

The testimony is most famously expressed in the declaration made to Charles II in 1660–1661 by George Fox and other Friends: "We utterly deny all outward wars and strife and fightings with outward weapons, for any end or under any pretence whatsoever. And this is our testimony to the whole world." Early Friends cited the teachings of Jesus — loving one's enemies and turning the other cheek — as the basis of the testimony.

Practice

In practice, the peace testimony has led Friends to refuse military service, with many becoming conscientious objectors in both world wars and serving instead in units such as the Friends' Ambulance Unit. It also motivates Quaker work in peacebuilding, mediation, reconciliation and disarmament campaigning. In 1947 the Nobel Peace Prize was awarded to Quakers worldwide, accepted on their behalf by the Friends Service Council and the American Friends Service Committee. The testimony is understood not as mere abstention from violence but as an active witness: many Friends phrase it as a commitment to "live in the virtue of that life and power that takes away the occasion of all wars".

Not all Friends interpret the testimony identically, and some Quakers have served in the armed forces; nevertheless, the peace testimony remains a defining feature of Quaker identity and is affirmed in the books of discipline of yearly meetings around the world.
