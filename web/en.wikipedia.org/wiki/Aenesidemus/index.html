Aenesidemus (Greek: Αἰνησίδημος) was a Greek philosopher of the 1st century BC who revived Pyrrhonism, the sceptical philosophy of Pyrrho of Elis, after its eclipse by the sceptical Academy. He is credited with the Ten Modes (or tropes), the classic set of arguments for suspending judgement (epochē) about how things are in themselves.

Life and works

Little is known of his life. He was probably born at Knossos in Crete, taught at Alexandria, and had earlier been associated with the Platonic Academy in Athens, whose dogmatic turns he rejected. His main work, the Pyrrhonian Discourses (Pyrrhoneioi logoi), is lost, but its outline is preserved in the Myriobiblion of the 9th-century scholar Photius, and his arguments deeply shaped the surviving works of Sextus Empiricus.

The Ten Modes

The Ten Modes systematically exploit relativity: (1) different animals perceive differently; (2) different humans differ; (3) the senses disagree among themselves; (4) perception depends on circumstances and states (health, sleep, emotion); (5) on positions, distances and places; (6) on admixtures with media; (7) on quantities and compositions; (8) on relations generally; (9) on frequency or rarity; (10) on customs, laws and conventions. Faced with such conflicts and no non-circular criterion to resolve them, the Pyrrhonist suspends judgement and thereby attains tranquillity (ataraxia).

Influence

Aenesidemus' revival provided the framework that Sextus Empiricus codified two centuries later; Pyrrhonian arguments transmitted through Sextus became a central challenge for early modern philosophy, influencing Montaigne and Descartes' sceptical method. The 19th-century philosopher Gottlob Ernst Schulze adopted the name "Aenesidemus" as a pseudonym for his influential critique of Kant.
