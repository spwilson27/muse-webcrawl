Respiratory distress

Respiratory distress is a state in which breathing is laboured and the work of breathing is increased, without necessarily yet involving respiratory failure (inadequate gas exchange). It is a clinical presentation, not a diagnosis, and can herald life-threatening conditions requiring urgent treatment.

Signs

Characteristic signs include tachypnoea (rapid breathing), use of accessory muscles of respiration, intercostal and subcostal recession (retractions), nasal flaring and grunting in infants, stridor or wheeze, cyanosis, and altered consciousness in severe cases. The patient may be unable to speak in full sentences and prefers to sit upright or in the "tripod" position.

Causes

Causes span the airway (foreign body, croup, anaphylaxis), the lungs (pneumonia, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pulmonary oedema, pneumothorax), the circulation (heart failure, pulmonary embolism, severe anaemia or sepsis), and the nervous system or chest wall (drug overdose, trauma, neuromuscular disease). In newborns, infant respiratory distress syndrome from surfactant deficiency is a leading cause in premature babies, distinct from acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in older patients.

Assessment and management

Assessment follows an airway–breathing–circulation approach with pulse oximetry, blood gas analysis and chest imaging. Initial management is supportive: positioning, supplemental oxygen, and treatment of the underlying cause — bronchodilators for asthma, diuretics for pulmonary oedema, antibiotics for pneumonia, adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Escalation may involve high-flow oxygen, non-invasive ventilation, or intubation and mechanical ventilation in intensive care.
