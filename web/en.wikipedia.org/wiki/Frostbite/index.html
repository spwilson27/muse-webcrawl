Frostbite

Frostbitten fingers appear white or waxy and feel numb.

Frostbite is the medical condition of skin and underlying tissue freezing due to exposure to cold, most commonly affecting the fingers, toes, nose, ears, cheeks and chin. In severe cases the frozen tissue dies and may require amputation. Superficial frostbite, sometimes called frostnip when mildest, damages skin without permanent injury; deep frostbite extends into muscle and bone.

Mechanism and symptoms

In response to cold, blood vessels in the extremities constrict to preserve core temperature. Prolonged constriction starves tissue of oxygen, and ice crystals form within and between cells, damaging cell membranes and blood vessel linings. Early signs are cold, numb, white or pale waxy skin; as tissue thaws, it becomes red, painful and swollen, with blisters in more serious cases. Risk factors include very low temperatures, wind chill, wet clothing, alcohol use, poor circulation and high altitude — frostbite is a well-known hazard to mountaineers and soldiers.

Treatment and prevention

First aid consists of moving the person to warmth and rewarming the affected part in warm (not hot) water at about 37–39 °C, provided there is no risk of refreezing, which greatly worsens damage. Rubbing the tissue or using dry heat is harmful. Hospital treatment may include thrombolytic drugs to restore circulation, pain relief and wound care; severely damaged tissue may need surgical removal. Prevention is by layered, dry clothing, covering exposed skin, and limiting exposure in extreme cold.
