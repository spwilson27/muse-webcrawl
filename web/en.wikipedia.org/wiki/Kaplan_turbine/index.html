Kaplan turbine

The Kaplan turbine is a propeller-type water turbine with adjustable blades, developed in 1913 by the Austrian professor Viktor Kaplan. It is an axial-flow reaction turbine used for low-head, high-flow hydroelectric generation, typically where the water head is between roughly 2 and 40 metres. Because both its runner blades and its guide vanes (wicket gates) can be adjusted, a double-regulated Kaplan turbine maintains high efficiency across a wide range of flows and loads.

Development

Viktor Kaplan, working at the German Technical University in Brno, sought a turbine suited to the low heads and large discharges of big rivers. His key insight was to shape the runner like a ship's propeller and to make the blade pitch adjustable so the blades could meet the water at the optimal angle under varying conditions. After early cavitation problems were overcome, the first large Kaplan turbine was installed in 1919 at a textile mill in Velm, Austria, and large units followed in Sweden and elsewhere during the 1920s.

Design

Water enters through a spiral casing and adjustable guide vanes, which impart a swirl, then flows axially through the propeller-like runner. In a double-regulated machine, a servomotor inside the runner hub changes blade pitch in coordination with the guide vane opening (a relationship known as the "on-cam" combination), keeping efficiency high — often above 90% — from full load down to roughly a third of capacity. Single-regulated variants adjust only the guide vanes. A related design, the bulb turbine, packages the generator in a watertight bulb in the water passage and is common in tidal and very-low-head plants.

Applications

Kaplan and Kaplan-derived turbines power many of the world's great low-head river stations, including plants on the Danube, the Rhine, the Columbia and Snake rivers in the United States, and numerous run-of-river schemes worldwide. Individual units can exceed 200 MW.
