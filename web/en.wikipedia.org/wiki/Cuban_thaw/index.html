Cuban thaw

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The U.S. embassy in Havana reopened in August 2015 as part of the thaw.

The Cuban thaw (Spanish: deshielo cubano) was the normalization of relations between the United States and Cuba that began in December 2014 and reversed more than five decades of hostility dating to the Cold War. On December 17, 2014, U.S. President Barack Obama and Cuban President Raúl Castro announced in simultaneous televised addresses that the two countries would restore diplomatic relations, following more than a year of secret negotiations hosted by Canada and encouraged by Pope Francis.

Background

The United States severed diplomatic relations with Cuba in 1961, after Fidel Castro's revolution and the nationalization of American-owned property, and imposed a comprehensive trade embargo that remained in force for decades. Relations were limited to interests sections in each other's capitals from 1977 onward. Obama had eased restrictions on travel and remittances after taking office in 2009, and secret talks in 2013–14, along with a prisoner exchange involving U.S. contractor Alan Gross and Cuban intelligence agents, cleared the way for the announcement.

Normalization

During 2015 the United States removed Cuba from its list of state sponsors of terrorism, both countries reopened embassies in Washington and Havana, and the Obama administration used executive authority to loosen rules on travel, remittances, trade, and telecommunications. Direct mail service resumed, U.S. airlines began scheduled flights to Cuba in 2016, and American cruise ships called at Havana. In March 2016 Obama visited Havana — the first visit by a sitting U.S. president since 1928 — meeting Raúl Castro and attending an exhibition baseball game. The United States also ended the "wet foot, dry foot" migration policy in January 2017.

Reversal

The thaw was substantially rolled back under President Donald Trump, who in 2017 restricted individual travel and transactions with Cuban military-controlled businesses, and later allowed U.S. lawsuits over expropriated property and re-designated Cuba a state sponsor of terrorism in January 2021. The Biden administration eased some measures but left the broader framework largely intact, so the full normalization envisioned in 2014 remained incomplete. The U.S. embargo itself, codified by statute including the Helms–Burton Act, could only be lifted by Congress and remained in place throughout.
