Diaeresis (diacritic)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diaeresis
◌̈
UnicodeU+0308 combining diaeresis; precomposed forms such as ä, ë, ï, ö, ü, ÿ
NamesDiaeresis, trema; (in Germanic contexts) umlaut
FunctionMarks separate vowel sounds, or a modified vowel quality, depending on language

The diaeresis (also spelled dieresis; from Greek diaíresis, "division"), known in French as tréma, is a diacritical mark consisting of two dots placed over a letter, most commonly a vowel: ä, ë, ï, ö, ü, ÿ. It has two historically distinct functions. In its original and still common use, it indicates that the marked vowel is pronounced in a separate syllable from an adjacent vowel — that a vowel sequence forms a hiatus rather than a diphthong, as in naïve, Zoë, French Noël, and Spanish pingüino (where the mark over the u shows it is sounded). In German and several other languages, the same mark instead denotes umlaut, a change in vowel quality — a function with a different history, the dots having developed from a small superscript e.

Uses by language

In French, the tréma separates vowels (maïs, "maize", vs mais, "but") and is central to spelling reforms' treatment of words like aiguë. In Dutch, the trema breaks up vowel combinations (België, "Belgium"; ruïne) and is dropped when a hyphen or capital changes the reading. In German, ä, ö and ü are umlauted vowels treated as separate letters for some purposes; names can also be transliterated with ae, oe, ue. In Spanish, the diaeresis (diéresis) appears only over u in the sequences güe and güi to show the u is pronounced. In Turkish and in transcription systems, ö and ü represent front rounded vowels. English usage is now largely confined to names and a stylistic survival in a few publications, notably The New Yorker, which retains spellings such as coöperate and reëlect.

Computing

In Unicode, the diaeresis exists both as precomposed characters and as the combining character U+0308. The visually identical "umlaut" is encoded the same way; Unicode unified the two marks because they are indistinguishable in form. Legacy encodings such as ISO 8859-1 include the common precomposed forms.
