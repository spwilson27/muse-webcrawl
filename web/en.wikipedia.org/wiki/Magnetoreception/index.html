Magnetoreception
Many migratory birds sense Earth's magnetic field.
Proposed mechanismsRadical pair (cryptochrome); magnetite-based receptors; electromagnetic induction
Observed inBirds, sea turtles, fish, insects, bacteria, and others
UsesCompass orientation and geographic "map" sense

Magnetoreception is a sense which allows an organism to detect the Earth's magnetic field. Animals with this sense include some arthropods, molluscs, and vertebrates — among them birds, sea turtles, sharks, and certain mammals. The sense is used primarily for orientation and navigation, providing both a compass (direction) and, in some species, a map-like sense of position derived from variations in field intensity and inclination across the globe.

Evidence

The first evidence that animals use magnetic information came from experiments on European robins by Wolfgang Wiltschko in the 1960s, which showed that caged migratory birds orient using the inclination of the field lines rather than polarity. Since then magnetic orientation has been demonstrated in sea turtles (which appear to imprint on the magnetic signature of their natal beach), salmon, rainbow trout, spiny lobsters, mole rats, fruit flies, and magnetotactic bacteria, which contain chains of magnetite crystals (magnetosomes) that passively align them with the field. Captive experiments indicate that birds' compass is light-dependent and disrupted by weak radio-frequency fields, evidence pointing to a photochemical mechanism.

Proposed mechanisms

Three main mechanisms have been proposed. (1) The radical pair mechanism: light striking the photopigment cryptochrome in the retina creates pairs of radicals whose reaction yields are influenced by the direction of the magnetic field, potentially giving the animal a visual modulation pattern aligned with the field. (2) Magnetite-based reception: crystals of the iron mineral magnetite, found in structures such as the beaks and inner ears of birds and the olfactory region of trout, could act as tiny compass needles transducing field information mechanically. (3) Electromagnetic induction: in elasmobranch fish, movement through the field may generate detectable voltages in the highly sensitive ampullae of Lorenzini. The relative contribution of these mechanisms remains an active research question; evidence suggests birds may use a radical-pair compass in the eye and a magnetite-based intensity sensor in the beak.

Humans

Humans possess magnetite in some tissues and the cryptochrome protein Cry2, and some experiments have reported subconscious brain responses to rotating magnetic fields, but a functional human magnetic sense is not established and claims remain controversial.
