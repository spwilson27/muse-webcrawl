Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (VCDR) is an international treaty that defines a framework for diplomatic relations between independent countries, specifying the privileges of a diplomatic mission that enable diplomats to perform their function without fear of coercion or harassment by the host country. Adopted in 1961 by the United Nations Conference on Diplomatic Intercourse and Immunities in Vienna, it entered into force in 1964 and has been ratified by nearly every state (around 190 parties), making it one of the most successful codifications in international law.

Key provisions

The Convention establishes the functions of missions (Article 3), the agrément procedure for ambassadors and the host's right to declare any member persona non grata without explanation (Article 9), the inviolability of mission premises (Article 22) and archives, freedom of communication including the diplomatic bag (Article 27), personal inviolability of diplomatic agents (Article 29) and their immunity from criminal — and broadly civil — jurisdiction (Article 31), with waiver possible only by the sending state (Article 32). Disputes have reached the International Court of Justice, most consequentially in the Tehran Hostages case (1980), where Iran's seizure of the US embassy was held a flagrant violation — the judgment reaffirming the Convention as the cornerstone of the diplomatic system even amid its breach.
