Living root bridge

A living root bridge is a type of simple suspension bridge formed of living plant roots by tree shaping. They are common in the southern part of the Indian state of Meghalaya, particularly around Cherrapunji and Mawsynram in the Khasi and Jaińtia hills — among the wettest places on Earth. The bridges are made by the Khasi and Jaińtia peoples, who train the aerial roots of rubber fig trees (Ficus elastica) across streams and gorges.

Construction

To create a bridge, young, flexible aerial roots of a fig tree growing on a riverbank are threaded through hollowed-out betel nut tree trunks, which protect the roots and guide them straight across the span. The roots are guided over many years — a bridge may take 15 to 30 years to become strong enough to cross — until they reach the far bank and take root in the soil. Once established, the roots thicken and strengthen over time, and the bridge can last for hundreds of years, growing stronger as the tree grows, in contrast to wooden bridges that rot quickly in the humid climate.

Notable examples

The best-known example is the double-decker root bridge at Nongriat village near Cherrapunji, where two bridges are stacked one above the other across the Umshiang river; it is often called the Umshiang Double-Decker Root Bridge. Some bridges are over 50 metres long and can support the weight of dozens of people at once. The longest known examples exceed 50 metres, and one bridge is estimated to be around 200 years old.

Decline and tourism

As concrete and steel bridges have spread, fewer new root bridges are being trained, though the practice continues in some villages. The surviving bridges have become a significant attraction for ecotourism in Meghalaya, and efforts have been made to document and preserve them, including recognition on UNESCO's tentative list of World Heritage Sites as part of the cultural landscape of the region.
