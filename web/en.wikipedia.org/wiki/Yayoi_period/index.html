The Yayoi period (弥生時代, Yayoi jidai) is an Iron Age era in Japanese history, traditionally dated from about 300 BCE to 300 CE, though radiocarbon evidence has led many scholars to push its beginning back to around 1000 BCE. It is named after the Yayoi district of Tokyo, where distinctive reddish pottery of the period was first identified in 1884. The period follows the Jōmon period and precedes the Kofun period.

Characteristics

The Yayoi period saw transformative changes driven by migrants from the Asian mainland, principally via the Korean Peninsula: the introduction of wet-rice agriculture in paddy fields, bronze and iron metallurgy, new styles of pottery made on the wheel, weaving, and glass manufacture. Settled farming villages grew, some — such as Yoshinogari in Kyushu — becoming large, moated settlements with watchtowers and raised-floor storehouses.

Society

Agricultural surplus supported increasing social stratification, hereditary elites, and warfare between communities, attested by weapons, fortified villages, and burials with bronze mirrors, swords, and bells (dōtaku). Chinese histories such as the Wei Zhi describe the land of Wa divided into many small polities, and record the shaman-queen Himiko of Yamataikoku, who sent embassies to the Wei court in the 3rd century CE.

Transition

By around 300 CE the Yayoi gave way to the Kofun period, marked by great keyhole-shaped tomb mounds and the emergence of a more unified Yamato state.
