Eugène Viollet-le-Duc

Eugène Emmanuel Viollet-le-Duc (27 January 1814 – 17 September 1879) was a French architect and author who restored many prominent medieval buildings in France, including Notre-Dame de Paris, the Basilica of Saint-Denis, the fortified city of Carcassonne, and the Château de Pierrefonds. A central figure of the Gothic Revival, he was also the most influential architectural theorist of the 19th century, arguing that restoration should return a building to a complete state "that may never have existed at any given time".

Career

Rejecting the academic route of the École des Beaux-Arts, Viollet-le-Duc trained through travel and practical study of medieval buildings. In 1840 he began restoring the abbey church of Vézelay; with Prosper Mérimée's Commission of Historic Monuments he went on to work on Sainte-Chapelle, Amiens Cathedral, and, from 1844, Notre-Dame de Paris (with Jean-Baptiste Lassus), where he designed the famous spire — destroyed by fire in 2019 and rebuilt in his form — and many of the cathedral's chimeras and liturgical furnishings.

Writings and influence

His ten-volume Dictionnaire raisonné de l'architecture française (1854–1868) set out a rationalist reading of Gothic structure that deeply influenced modern architecture, and his Entretiens sur l'architecture argued for designs honestly expressing materials such as iron. His restorations were controversial even in his lifetime — John Ruskin called restoration "a lie from beginning to end" — and modern conservation, guided by principles such as those of the Venice Charter, generally rejects his creative approach; yet many of France's iconic medieval monuments owe their present form, and in some cases their survival, to him.
