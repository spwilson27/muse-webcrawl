The golden wattle (Acacia pycnantha) is a tree of the family Fabaceae native to southeastern Australia. It grows to a height of about 8 metres (26 ft) and has smooth, dark brown to grey bark and flattened leaf-like phyllodes in place of true leaves. In late winter and spring it produces masses of fragrant, bright golden-yellow spherical flower heads.

Description and distribution

The species occurs naturally in the understorey of dry sclerophyll forests and woodland from Victoria through parts of South Australia and New South Wales. Like many acacias, it fixes nitrogen and is a pioneer species after fire, its hard-coated seeds germinating readily after heat breaks their dormancy.

National emblem

The golden wattle is Australia's national floral emblem. Wattle had long been a popular national symbol — it appears on the Australian coat of arms and Wattle Day is celebrated on 1 September — and Acacia pycnantha was officially proclaimed the floral emblem on 1 September 1988, during the bicentennial year. The national colours of green and gold are traditionally taken to derive from the foliage and blossom of the wattle.

Cultivation and uses

Golden wattle is widely planted as an ornamental and as a source of tannin from its bark, once an important local industry. It has naturalised in parts of South Africa, California, and southern Europe, where it can become an environmental weed.
