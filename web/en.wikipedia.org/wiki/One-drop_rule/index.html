One-drop rule

The one-drop rule was a legal principle of racial classification that was prominent in the 20th-century United States. It asserted that any person with even one ancestor of African descent ("one drop" of "Black blood") is considered Black (Negro or colored in historical terminology). The concept evolved over the course of the 19th century and became codified in law in the early 20th century.

History

Under slavery, the doctrine of partus sequitur ventrem made a child's enslaved status follow that of the mother, which encouraged classification of mixed-ancestry children as Black. After emancipation, states experimented with fractional definitions — requiring one-quarter or one-eighth African ancestry. Virginia's Racial Integrity Act of 1924 defined a "white person" as one with "no trace whatsoever" of non-Caucasian blood (with a limited exception for Native American ancestry), effectively enshrining the one-drop standard.

The rule reflected and reinforced the social system of hypodescent, in which mixed-race children are assigned to the subordinate group. It contrasted with classification systems in Latin America and elsewhere that recognized intermediate categories.

Legacy

Although the statutes enforcing the one-drop rule were dismantled in the civil rights era, scholars note its lasting influence on American racial identity and the binary framing of race in the United States. Since 2000, the US census has allowed respondents to select more than one race.
