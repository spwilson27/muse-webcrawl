Harris Tweed (Scottish Gaelic: Clò Hearadh) is a tweed cloth handwoven by islanders at their homes in the Outer Hebrides of Scotland, finished in the islands, and made from pure virgin wool dyed and spun in the islands. It is one of very few textiles in the world protected by its own Act of Parliament — the Harris Tweed Act 1993 — and every length is inspected and stamped with the Orb certification trademark by the Harris Tweed Authority.

History

Cloth weaving was a domestic craft in the Hebrides for centuries, producing sturdy cloth (in Gaelic clò mòr, "the big cloth") for local use. In the 1840s Lady Dunmore, widow of the Earl of Dunmore, promoted the cloth among her aristocratic circle, launching it as a fashionable fabric for sporting estates. The industry industrialised partly in the early twentieth century: yarn production and finishing moved to mills in Stornoway on Lewis, while weaving remained a crofter craft performed in islanders' sheds, characteristically on single-width Hattersley looms. The famous trademark dispute of 1909 settled the Orb mark, registered by the Harris Tweed Association.

Definition and production

Under the 1993 Act, genuine Harris Tweed must be handwoven (in practice, woven on treadle-powered looms) by islanders at their homes in Lewis, Harris, the Uists or Barra, from wool dyed and spun in the Outer Hebrides. The wool is dyed before spinning, so colours — traditionally drawn from the landscape of peat, heather, moss and sea — can be blended in the yarn. The woven cloth is returned to the mill for washing, finishing and Orb stamping. Production of around a million metres a year at its 1960s peak fell sharply by the 2000s, then revived with fashion demand from brands such as Nike (whose 2004 Harris Tweed trainers caused controversy over a design patent claim) and a renewed emphasis on heritage tailoring.
