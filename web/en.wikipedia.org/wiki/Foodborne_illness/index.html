Foodborne illness

Foodborne illness (also foodborne disease and food poisoning) is any illness resulting from the contamination of food by pathogenic bacteria, viruses, or parasites, as well as by prions and toxins such as aflatoxins in peanut products. Foodborne illness usually arises from improper handling, preparation, or food storage.

The World Health Organization estimates that hundreds of millions of people fall ill from contaminated food each year worldwide, with young children bearing a disproportionate share of deaths.

Causes

Common bacterial causes include Salmonella, Campylobacter, Escherichia coli (notably Shiga toxin–producing strains), Listeria monocytogenes and Clostridium botulinum, which causes botulism. The most common viral cause is norovirus. Contamination may occur at any stage of production, processing, distribution or preparation; cross-contamination and inadequate cooking or refrigeration are frequent factors.

Symptoms and incubation

Symptoms typically include nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps and diarrhoea, sometimes with fever. Onset may be within hours for preformed toxins, such as those of Staphylococcus aureus, or days to weeks for organisms such as Listeria. Most cases are self-limiting, but severe illness can cause dehydration, haemolytic uraemic syndrome, reactive arthritis, or death, especially in the very young, elderly, pregnant, or immunocompromised.

Prevention

Prevention rests on hand and surface hygiene, separating raw and cooked foods, cooking to safe internal temperatures, prompt refrigeration, and safe water and raw materials — the principles summarised in the WHO "Five Keys to Safer Food". Food safety systems such as HACCP manage hazards along the supply chain.
