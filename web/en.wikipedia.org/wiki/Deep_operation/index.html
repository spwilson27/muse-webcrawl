Deep operation

Deep operation (Russian: глубокая операция, glubokaya operatsiya), also known as Soviet deep battle, was a military theory developed by the Soviet Union for its armed forces during the 1920s and 1930s. It held that military operations should strike simultaneously throughout the depth of the enemy's formations — not only breaking through the front line, but destroying reserves, headquarters, logistics, and artillery in the rear — so that the enemy's entire defensive system collapsed at once rather than being pushed back.

Development

The theory emerged from study of the First World War's stalemate and the Russian Civil War's mobile campaigns. Its principal architects were Mikhail Tukhachevsky, Vladimir Triandafillov (whose The Nature of the Operations of Modern Armies, 1929, laid its foundations), Mikhail Frunze, and Georgii Isserson. It was codified in the Red Army's 1936 Field Regulations (PU-36). The Great Purge of 1937–38 destroyed much of its leadership — Tukhachevsky was executed — and set back its institutional development.

Concepts

Deep battle distinguished between the tactical breakthrough of the enemy's forward defenses and the operational exploitation into the depth: formations were organized in echelons, with shock armies rupturing the front, mobile groups (tank and mechanized corps) driving deep into the rear, airborne forces seizing objectives ahead of the advance, and massed artillery and aviation striking targets throughout the operational depth. The related concept of successive operations held that modern war could not be won in a single battle; instead, a series of coordinated operations across a whole front, each building on the last, would progressively destroy the enemy — an idea Isserson called the "strategy of successive operations".

Application

After painful early-war lessons, the Red Army applied deep operations from 1943 onward, most famously in Operation Bagration (1944), which destroyed German Army Group Centre, and in the Vistula–Oder Offensive and invasion of Manchuria (1945). Deep operations influenced later Soviet and Russian doctrine, and parallels are drawn with the Western concepts of blitzkrieg (which it differed from in emphasizing scale and successive operations) and the US AirLand Battle doctrine.
