Samaritanism is the Abrahamic religion of the Samaritan people, an ethnoreligious group native to the Levant who regard themselves as the true Israelites (Bene Israel) and guardians (shamerim) of the original Torah. Samaritanism shares ancient roots with Judaism but differs fundamentally in recognising Mount Gerizim, rather than Jerusalem, as the chosen place of worship, and in accepting only the Samaritan Pentateuch as scripture.

Beliefs

Samaritan faith rests on five principles: the unity and uniqueness of the God of Israel; the Torah given to Moses as the sole scripture; Moses as the supreme prophet (with expectation of a future restorer figure, the Taheb, in the last days); Mount Gerizim as the one chosen sanctuary; and a future day of judgment and resurrection. Samaritans reject the Oral Torah, the Prophets, and the Writings of the Jewish Bible, and maintain their own liturgy in Samaritan Hebrew and Samaritan Aramaic, a calendar computed independently, and priestly leadership descending from Aaron through the high priest at Gerizim.

History

Samaritan tradition traces the schism with Jerusalem to the time of Eli in the 11th century BC; Jewish sources associate Samaritan origins with foreign settlers brought to Samaria after the Assyrian conquest of 722 BC. A Samaritan temple stood on Mount Gerizim from the Persian period until its destruction by the Hasmonean king John Hyrcanus around 110 BC. The community, once numbering perhaps a million across the Roman Near East, was devastated by the suppression of its revolts under Byzantine rule and dwindled over the centuries under Islamic, Crusader, and Ottoman rule, reaching a nadir of about 146 souls in 1917.

Modern community

Today the community has recovered to roughly 900 people, divided between Kiryat Luza on Mount Gerizim near Nablus and the city of Holon in Israel, holding both Israeli and Palestinian civic ties. Its most distinctive observance is the Passover sacrifice (Korban Pesach), still offered with whole roasted lambs on Mount Gerizim each spring — the only surviving animal sacrifice of its kind in an Israelite tradition.
