The Anxiety of Influence


Harold Bloom's The Anxiety of Influence (1973)

The Anxiety of Influence: A Theory of Poetry is a 1973 book of literary criticism by Harold Bloom. It is considered one of the most influential works of literary theory of the twentieth century. In it, Bloom argues that poets are hindered in their creative process by the ambiguous relationship they have with precursor poets: the later poet experiences an "anxiety of influence" and must engage in creative misreading — a "misprision" — of the precursor's work in order to clear imaginative space for his or her own.

The six revisionary ratios

Bloom describes the struggle between poet and precursor through six "revisionary ratios": clinamen (a swerve away from the precursor), tessera (completion and antithesis), kenosis (a breaking device similar to the defence mechanisms of the psyche), daemonization (a movement toward a personalized counter-sublime), askesis (self-purgation), and apophrades (the return of the dead, in which the later poet's work seems to have been written by the precursor).

Reception

The book drew on Freud's notion of the Oedipus complex and the family romance, recasting literary history as a series of agonistic struggles. It provoked sharp debate: admirers praised its originality and sweep, while critics objected to its masculinist framing — feminist critics Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar responded with the notion of an "anxiety of authorship" in The Madwoman in the Attic (1979). Bloom's model strongly influenced the Yale School of deconstruction and literary studies generally.
