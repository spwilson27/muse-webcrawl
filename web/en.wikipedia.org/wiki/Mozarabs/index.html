Mozarabs

The Mozarabs (Spanish: mozárabes; from Arabic mustaʿrib, "Arabised") were the Christians who lived under Muslim rule in al-Andalus, the Islamic-ruled parts of the Iberian Peninsula, after the Muslim conquest of the 8th century. Retaining their Christian faith and their distinctive Hispanic (Mozarabic) liturgy, they gradually adopted Arabic language and many customs of the surrounding society while remaining a subordinate religious minority (dhimmi) under Islamic law.

History

Following the conquest of 711, most Iberian Christians remained in place under Muslim government, keeping their bishops, churches and local law in return for payment of the jizya tax. Over the following centuries Mozarabic communities — concentrated in cities such as Córdoba, Toledo, Seville and Mérida — became increasingly Arabised in dress, names and language, and many spoke both Romance and Arabic. In the 850s, a group of Córdoba Christians who publicly denounced Islam were executed, becoming known as the Martyrs of Córdoba. Pressure increased under the Almoravid and Almohad dynasties, which deported many Mozarabs to North Africa in the 12th century; others emigrated north to the expanding Christian kingdoms. Mozarab communities from the south played a major role in repopulating Toledo after its capture by Alfonso VI in 1085.

Culture and legacy

Mozarabic art and architecture — visible in churches such as San Miguel de Escalada and San Baudelio de Berlanga — combined Visigothic, Islamic and northern European elements, notably the horseshoe arch. The community's spoken Romance language, written in Arabic script (aljamiado), is preserved in the kharjas, short lyric refrains in Arabic and Hebrew poems, which are among the earliest recorded examples of Iberian Romance lyric poetry. The Mozarabic Rite — the old Hispanic liturgy distinct from the Roman Rite — survives in a small number of parishes in Toledo, where Cardinal Cisneros had its service books printed around 1500 to preserve it.
