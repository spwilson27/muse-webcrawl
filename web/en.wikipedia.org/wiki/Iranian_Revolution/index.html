The Iranian Revolution (Persian: انقلاب ایران), also known as the Islamic Revolution, was a series of events that culminated in the overthrow of the Pahlavi dynasty in 1979 and the establishment of the Islamic Republic of Iran under Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini. It replaced the pro-Western secular monarchy of Mohammad Reza Shah with an Islamic theocratic republic based on Khomeini's doctrine of velayat-e faqih (guardianship of the Islamic jurist).

Background

Discontent with the Shah drew on resentment of his authoritarian rule and the SAVAK secret police, the perception of subservience to the United States (cemented by the 1953 coup against Mohammad Mosaddegh), rapid and uneven modernisation under the White Revolution, corruption, and clerical opposition to secularisation. A broad coalition — Islamists, liberals, nationalists, Marxists and bazaar merchants — mobilised through mosque networks and cassette-tape sermons from the exiled Khomeini.

Course of the revolution

Protests escalated through 1978 in a cycle of demonstrations and mourning rituals; the army's killing of demonstrators on "Black Friday" (8 September 1978) and a general strike, including in the oil industry, paralysed the regime. The Shah left Iran on 16 January 1979; Khomeini returned on 1 February, and the monarchy collapsed on 11 February after the military declared neutrality. A referendum approved an Islamic Republic, and a new constitution institutionalised clerical supremacy. The seizure of the U.S. embassy in November 1979 and the ensuing hostage crisis, followed by Iraq's invasion in 1980, consolidated the revolutionary regime, which suppressed its erstwhile liberal and leftist allies.

Legacy

The revolution transformed Iran's society and foreign policy, reshaped Middle Eastern politics, energised Islamist movements worldwide and set Iran and the United States on a course of enduring hostility.
