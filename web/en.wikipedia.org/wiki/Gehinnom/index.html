Gehinnom

Hebrewגֵּיא בֶן־הִנֹּם / גֵּיהִנּוֹם
LocationValley south and west of Jerusalem
Greek formGehenna
ConceptPlace of judgment/purification in the afterlife

Gehinnom (Hebrew: גֵּיהִנּוֹם, Gēhinnom) is the Jewish term, derived from the Valley of Hinnom (גֵּיא בֶן־הִנֹּם, "Valley of the Son of Hinnom") outside Jerusalem, for a realm of punishment and purification of souls in the afterlife. In Greek transcription it became Gehenna, a term used repeatedly in the New Testament and a major source of the later Christian concept of Hell.

The valley and the Hebrew Bible

The Valley of Hinnom runs south and west of the Old City of Jerusalem. In the Hebrew Bible it marks a boundary between the territories of Judah and Benjamin, and it is condemned as the site of the Tophet, where, according to Jeremiah and 2 Kings, children were sacrificed to the god Moloch. Kings Ahaz and Manasseh are accused of the practice, and Josiah defiled the site during his reforms.

Development as an afterlife concept

In Second Temple and rabbinic Judaism, Gehinnom developed into a place where the wicked are judged and purified after death. Rabbinic tradition generally limited the punishment of most souls to twelve months, after which the purified soul ascends to Gan Eden (paradise), while the utterly wicked are annihilated or remain. This contrasts with the eternal damnation of many Christian interpretations of Gehenna. Gehinnom features in Jewish eschatology alongside Sheol, Gan Eden and the resurrection of the dead, and Kabbalah elaborated its geography in detail.

Today the valley itself is a park and archaeological area, containing ancient tombs, the Monastery of St Onuphrius, and the site of Akeldama.
