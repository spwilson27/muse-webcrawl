Overconsumption

Overconsumption describes the use of goods and resources at a rate that exceeds the capacity of ecosystems to regenerate them or absorb the resulting waste — consumption beyond sustainable levels and, in many formulations, beyond what is needed for a decent standard of living. It is usually discussed alongside overpopulation as a driver of environmental degradation, captured in the I = PAT identity (impact = population × affluence × technology).

Patterns

Consumption is distributed very unevenly: the wealthiest fraction of the world's population accounts for a disproportionate share of energy use, material throughput, air travel and carbon emissions, while billions consume too little to meet basic needs. Indicators used to measure the phenomenon include the ecological footprint — humanity's demand is often estimated at roughly 1.7 Earths' worth of biocapacity, marked each year by Earth Overshoot Day — and raw material consumption per capita.

Causes and effects

Drivers identified by researchers include rising incomes, advertising and consumer culture, planned obsolescence, cheap credit, and infrastructures that lock in high consumption of energy, meat and transport. Consequences attributed to overconsumption include climate change, biodiversity loss, deforestation, water stress and accumulating waste streams such as plastics and e-waste. Proposed responses range from efficiency improvements and circular-economy design to "sufficiency" policies, degrowth arguments and voluntary simplicity movements.
