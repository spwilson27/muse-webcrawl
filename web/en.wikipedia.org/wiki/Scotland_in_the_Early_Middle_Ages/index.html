Scotland in the Early Middle Ages


Period: c. 400 – c. 1058
Preceded by: Roman Britain / Caledonia
Succeeded by: Scotland in the High Middle Ages

Scotland in the Early Middle Ages, between the end of Roman authority in southern and central Britain around 400 AD and the beginnings of the so-called Davidian Revolution in the eleventh century, covers the period in which the diverse peoples of northern Britain — Picts, Gaels (Scots), Britons, and Angles — gradually coalesced into the Kingdom of Alba, the predecessor of the Kingdom of Scotland.

Peoples and kingdoms

By the sixth century, four major cultural and political groups occupied northern Britain: the Picts in the northeast, the Gaels of Dál Riata in the west, the Britons of Strathclyde and other kingdoms in the southwest, and the Angles of Bernicia in the southeast. Saint Columba's mission from Ireland to Iona in 563 was pivotal in spreading Christianity and Gaelic influence among the Picts. Viking invasions from the late eighth century destabilised all these kingdoms, weakening Pictland and Dál Riata alike.

Kingdom of Alba

Tradition credits Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpín), who became king of the Picts around 843, with uniting the Gaels and Picts, though modern historians see a gradual Gaelicisation culminating under his successors. By 900 the kingdom was known in Gaelic as Alba. Kenneth's descendants, alternating between branches of the royal house, expanded southward at the expense of Strathclyde and Lothian, laying the foundations of the medieval Scottish kingdom.
