Amoeba proteus is a large species of free-living amoeba in the genus Amoeba, found in freshwater streams, ponds and other moist environments. Among the best-known of the giant amoebae, it typically measures around 500 micrometres in length (though individuals may reach 750 µm) and is just visible to the naked eye. It is widely used as a model organism in cell biology and teaching because of its size and ease of culture.

Structure and locomotion

The cell is constantly changing shape, extending blunt, lobe-like projections called pseudopodia ("false feet") used both for movement and for engulfing food — a process known as phagocytosis. Its name refers to Proteus, the shape-shifting Greek sea god. The cytoplasm is divided into a clear outer ectoplasm and a granular inner endoplasm, and contains a single prominent nucleus, a contractile vacuole that expels excess water, and numerous food vacuoles.

Feeding and reproduction

A. proteus feeds on bacteria, algae, small protozoans and organic debris, surrounding prey with pseudopodia and digesting it within food vacuoles. Reproduction is asexual, by binary fission: the nucleus divides by mitosis and the cell splits into two daughter cells. Under unfavourable conditions the amoeba can round up and form a protective cyst.

History

The organism was first described in the 18th century by August Johann Rösel von Rosenhof, whose 1755 illustration of a "little Proteus" is among the earliest depictions of an amoeba. Amoeba proteus became a classic laboratory organism for studying cytoplasmic streaming, amoeboid motion and the role of the nucleus, including famous nuclear-transplant experiments of the 20th century.
