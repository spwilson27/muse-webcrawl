Structural realism (philosophy of science)

Structural realism is a position in the philosophy of science that holds that scientific theories describe the structure of the world — the relations between entities — rather than (or in addition to) the intrinsic nature of the entities themselves. It is commonly presented as a compromise between scientific realism and antirealism, designed to answer both the "no miracles" argument for realism and the pessimistic meta-induction against it.

History and variants

The view was revived in modern form by John Worrall in 1989, drawing on earlier ideas of Henri Poincaré and Bertrand Russell. Worrall's example was the transition from Fresnel's wave theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetism: although Fresnel's ontology (an elastic ether) was abandoned, his equations were retained within Maxwell's theory, suggesting that what successive theories preserve is mathematical structure.

Epistemic structural realism (Worrall) holds that structure is all we can know. Ontic structural realism, developed by James Ladyman, Steven French and others, goes further: structure is all there is, and objects are secondary to, or constituted by, the relations in which they stand. This ontic variant has been influential in discussions of quantum mechanics and spacetime physics, where the identity of fundamental "objects" seems elusive.

Criticisms

Critics raise the "Newman problem", based on an objection by M. H. A. Newman to Russell: purely structural claims, expressed only as the existence of relations with a certain cardinality, risk triviality unless supplemented with some non-structural content. Others question whether structure can be separated from the nature of its relata, and whether theory change really preserves structure as cleanly as the Fresnel–Maxwell case suggests.
