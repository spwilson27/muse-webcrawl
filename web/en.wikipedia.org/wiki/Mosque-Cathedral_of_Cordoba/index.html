The Mosque–Cathedral of Córdoba (Spanish: Mezquita-Catedral de Córdoba), officially known by its ecclesiastical name, the Cathedral of Our Lady of the Assumption, is the Catholic cathedral of the Diocese of Córdoba in Andalusia, Spain. It is one of the most celebrated monuments of Islamic architecture, built as the Great Mosque of Córdoba under the Umayyad emirs and caliphs, and converted into a cathedral after the Christian reconquest of the city in 1236.

History

Construction of the Great Mosque began in 785 under Abd al-Rahman I, founder of the Umayyad emirate of al-Andalus, on the site of a Visigothic church. The mosque was expanded repeatedly over two centuries — under Abd al-Rahman II, al-Hakam II and the chamberlain al-Mansur — eventually covering some 23,400 square metres and ranking among the largest mosques in the Islamic world.

After Ferdinand III of Castile captured Córdoba in 1236, the mosque was consecrated as a cathedral. Chapels were inserted around its edges, and in the 16th century a full Renaissance cruciform cathedral nave was built in its centre, a decision said to have drawn the displeasure of Charles V, who remarked that the builders had destroyed something unique to construct something commonplace.

Architecture

The building is famed for its vast hypostyle prayer hall, supported by more than 850 columns of jasper, onyx, marble and granite, many of them reused Roman and Visigothic spolia. The columns carry the building's signature double arches — a lower horseshoe arch and an upper semicircular arch — built in alternating red brick and pale stone voussoirs. The richly decorated mihrab, a small octagonal room rather than a simple niche, and its carved Byzantine-influenced mosaics date from the expansion of al-Hakam II. Other features include the ribbed domes, the Patio de los Naranjos (Courtyard of the Orange Trees) and a minaret now encased within a Baroque bell tower.

Heritage status

The mosque-cathedral, together with the historic centre of Córdoba, was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984. It remains a working Catholic cathedral and one of Spain's most visited monuments. Public Islamic worship is not permitted inside the building, a policy that has periodically been the subject of public debate.
