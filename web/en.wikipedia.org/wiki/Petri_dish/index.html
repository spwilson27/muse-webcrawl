A Petri dish (alternatively known as a Petri plate or cell-culture dish) is a shallow transparent lidded dish that biologists use to hold growth medium in which cells can be cultured, originally cells of bacteria, fungi and small mosses. The container is named after its inventor, German bacteriologist Julius Richard Petri, who developed it in 1887 while working as an assistant to Robert Koch. It is among the most common items of laboratory equipment in microbiology.

Design

The dish is a shallow cylinder of glass or clear plastic with a loose-fitting lid of slightly larger diameter. The lid limits airborne contamination while still allowing gas exchange. Glass dishes can be sterilised and reused; plastic dishes are supplied sterile, are disposable, and are often moulded with vents and stacking rings. Standard microbiological dishes are about 90–100 mm in diameter.

Use

Petri dishes are most commonly filled with agar-based media: molten agar is poured in, solidifies into a gel, and can incorporate nutrients and selective agents. Microbes are applied by streaking, spreading or pouring, and the plates are incubated — often inverted, so condensation does not drip onto the culture — until visible colonies form, each generally derived from a single cell. Dishes are also used for cell culture, culturing small plants and mosses, observing insects and seeds, and evaporating solvents. Agar plates famously featured in Alexander Fleming's 1928 discovery of penicillin, when a mould contaminating a Petri dish killed the surrounding staphylococci; they were also the setting for the Luria–Delbrück fluctuation test and much of classical genetics.
