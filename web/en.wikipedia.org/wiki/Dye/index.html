Dye

A dye is a coloured substance that chemically bonds to the substrate to which it is applied, imparting colour to materials such as textiles, hair, paper and plastics. This distinguishes dyes from pigments, which do not chemically attach and instead are held by a binder. Dyes are generally soluble, or made soluble, in the application medium, usually water.

History

Natural dyes from plants, insects and molluscs have been used since prehistory; famous examples include indigo (blue), madder (red), weld (yellow), and the costly Tyrian purple from sea snails, reserved in antiquity for elites. In 1856 the English chemist William Henry Perkin accidentally discovered mauveine, the first synthetic dye, while trying to synthesise quinine. His discovery launched the modern synthetic dye industry, which rapidly produced a full spectrum of bright, cheap colours from coal-tar chemicals.

Classes and use

Dyes are classed by chemistry and by application method: direct, reactive, vat, disperse, acid and basic dyes are chosen according to fibre type (cotton, wool, silk, polyester, etc.). Reactive dyes form covalent bonds with cellulose and dominate cotton dyeing. Dyeing is a major part of the textile industry and a significant source of water pollution when effluent is untreated.

Other applications

Dyes are also used in hair colouring, food colouring, biological staining (such as hematoxylin and eosin in histology), laser dyes and dye-sensitised solar cells.
