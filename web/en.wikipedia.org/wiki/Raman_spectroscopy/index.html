Raman spectroscopy is a spectroscopic technique typically used to determine vibrational modes of molecules, although rotational and other low-frequency modes of systems may also be observed. It relies upon inelastic scattering of photons, known as Raman scattering: monochromatic light, usually from a laser, interacts with molecular vibrations, and the energy of the scattered photons is shifted up or down by amounts corresponding to the vibrational energies of the sample.

Principles

Most photons scatter elastically (Rayleigh scattering) with unchanged energy. A small fraction — roughly one in ten million — scatter inelastically, exchanging energy with molecular vibrations. Scattered light with lower energy (having excited a vibration) forms Stokes lines; the rarer higher-energy anti-Stokes lines arise from molecules already in excited vibrational states. Plotting the intensity against the Raman shift, measured in wavenumbers (cm⁻¹), yields a spectrum that acts as a chemical fingerprint, complementary to infrared absorption spectroscopy; selection rules differ, since Raman activity requires a change in polarizability rather than dipole moment.

History

The effect was predicted theoretically by Adolf Smekal in 1923 and observed experimentally in 1928 by the Indian physicist C. V. Raman and his colleague K. S. Krishnan, for which Raman received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930. Practical Raman spectroscopy expanded greatly with the laser in the 1960s and with modern filters, detectors, and FT-Raman instruments.

Applications

Raman spectroscopy is used in chemistry for identification of compounds and study of bonding; in pharmaceuticals for quality control; in geology and gemology; in art conservation to identify pigments non-destructively; in forensics; and in medicine for tissue characterization. Enhanced variants include surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), which amplifies signals by many orders of magnitude near nanostructured metals, and coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) microscopy.
