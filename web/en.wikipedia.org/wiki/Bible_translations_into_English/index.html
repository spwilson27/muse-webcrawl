The Bible has been translated into English many times, in whole and in part, from the early Middle Ages to the present day. The history of English Bible translation reflects changes in the English language, in scholarship about the biblical languages (Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek), and in the religious politics of the English-speaking world.

Early translations

Partial renderings into Old English date from the seventh century: the poet Cædmon paraphrased biblical stories, the Venerable Bede is said to have translated the Gospel of John, and interlinear glosses (such as those in the Lindisfarne Gospels) aided readers of the Latin text. In the late fourteenth century, followers of John Wycliffe produced the first complete English Bibles, translated from the Latin Vulgate; these Wycliffite Bibles circulated in manuscript and were later condemned by church authorities.

The printed era and the King James Version

William Tyndale translated the New Testament from Greek and printed it in 1526; his work, smuggled into England, laid the foundation for nearly all later versions, and Tyndale was executed in 1536. The first complete printed English Bible was Miles Coverdale's of 1535, followed by the Great Bible (1539), the Geneva Bible (1560) — popular with Puritans and Shakespeare — and the Bishops' Bible (1568). In 1611 the King James Version (Authorized Version), commissioned by James I and produced by teams of scholars, was published; its majestic prose profoundly shaped English literature and idiom for centuries.

Modern translations

The discovery of older manuscripts and advances in textual criticism led to the Revised Version (1881–85) and the American Standard Version (1901). The twentieth century produced many new translations spanning a spectrum from formal equivalence ("word-for-word") to dynamic equivalence ("thought-for-thought"): among them the Revised Standard Version (1952), the New International Version (1978), the New Revised Standard Version (1989), the English Standard Version (2001), and paraphrases such as The Message (2002). Translation choice remains a matter of lively debate among churches and scholars.
