Focalization

Focalization is a term coined by the narratologist Gérard Genette in Narrative Discourse (1972) for the perspective through which a narrative is presented — the relation between the knowledge of the narrator and that of the characters. Genette introduced the term to replace the ambiguous "point of view", separating the question of who speaks (narration, voice) from the question of who sees or perceives (mood, focalization).

Focalization distinguishes who speaks from who perceives in a narrative.

Types

Genette distinguished three main types:

  • Zero focalization (non-focalized narrative): the narrator knows more than any character — the traditional "omniscient" narration
  • Internal focalization: the narrative is restricted to what a particular character knows, feels and perceives; it may be fixed (one character throughout), variable (different characters in different sections), or multiple (the same event recounted through several characters)
  • External focalization: the narrator says less than the characters know, describing only outward behaviour, as in behaviourist or "camera-eye" fiction such as Hemingway's short story "The Killers"

Development and criticism

The concept was refined by Mieke Bal, who emphasised the relation between a focalizing subject (the focalizer) and a focalized object, and by Shlomith Rimmon-Kenan, who distinguished perceptual, psychological and ideological facets of focalization. Critics have debated whether focalization can be purely visual-perceptual, and how it interacts with unreliable narration and free indirect discourse, in which a narrator's voice merges with a character's consciousness.
