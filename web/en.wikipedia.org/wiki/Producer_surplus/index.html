Producer surplus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FieldMicroeconomics, welfare economics
Related conceptsConsumer surplus, economic surplus, marginal cost

Producer surplus is an economic measure of the difference between the amount a producer of a good receives (the market price) and the minimum amount the producer would be willing to accept for the good. The difference, or surplus amount, is the benefit the producer receives for selling the good in the market.

On a supply-and-demand diagram, producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the equilibrium price, bounded by the quantity sold. Because the supply curve represents marginal cost, producer surplus for a firm equals total revenue minus total variable cost; in the long run it relates to economic profit and to payments to fixed factors of production.

Calculation

For a market with supply curve S(q) and equilibrium price P*, producer surplus is the integral of (P* − S(q)) over quantities from 0 to the equilibrium quantity Q*. For an individual firm selling quantity q at price p, producer surplus equals revenue minus variable cost: PS = p·q − VC(q).

Relationship to consumer surplus

Producer surplus plus consumer surplus equals total economic surplus (social surplus), a measure of the total welfare generated by trade in a market. Changes in price, taxes, or subsidies redistribute surplus between producers, consumers, and government, and may create deadweight loss.
