Paul Cézanne

Born19 January 1839, Aix-en-Provence, France
Died22 October 1906 (aged 67), Aix-en-Provence
NationalityFrench
MovementPost-Impressionism
Known forPainting: The Card Players, The Bathers, Mont Sainte-Victoire series

Paul Cézanne (19 January 1839 – 22 October 1906) was a French Post-Impressionist painter whose work formed the bridge between late 19th-century Impressionism and the radical art of the 20th century. Matisse and Picasso both called him "the father of us all." His distinctive method — building form with modulated patches of color and small, repetitive brushstrokes, often in multiple viewpoints — laid the groundwork for Cubism and modern abstraction.

Life

Cézanne was born in Aix-en-Provence in the south of France, the son of a prosperous banker. At school he formed a close friendship with the writer Émile Zola, who later used him as a model for the failed artist Claude Lantier in the novel L'Œuvre, a portrayal that ended their friendship. Against his father's wishes, Cézanne pursued painting in Paris in the 1860s, enduring repeated rejections from the Salon and exhibiting with the Impressionists in their first (1874) and third (1877) exhibitions. He worked with Camille Pissarro at Pontoise and Auvers in the 1870s, lightening his palette and adopting the broken brushwork of Impressionism.

Mature work

In the 1880s Cézanne retreated increasingly to Provence, where his family estate, the Jas de Bouffan, and the limestone mountain Mont Sainte-Victoire — which he painted more than 80 times — became central motifs. Pursuing his stated aim "to make of Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of the museums," he analyzed nature in terms of geometric essentials: "treat nature by the cylinder, the sphere, the cone." His major figure compositions include the five large versions of The Card Players (1890s) and the monumental late series of The Bathers.

Recognition and legacy

Recognition came late: the dealer Ambroise Vollard gave him his first one-man show in 1895, and younger artists made pilgrimages to Aix in his final years. After his death in 1906 — he collapsed while painting outdoors in a storm and died days later — a retrospective at the 1907 Salon d'Automne profoundly influenced the emerging Cubists. His still lifes, with their tilted tabletops and shifting perspectives, and his watercolors and constructive brushwork, are now regarded as foundational to modern art.
