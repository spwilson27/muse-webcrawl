Cygnus (constellation)

Cygnus is a northern constellation on the plane of the Milky Way, deriving its name from the Latinized Greek word for swan. Cygnus was among the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy, and it remains one of the 88 modern constellations. Its brightest star Deneb forms one vertex of the Summer Triangle asterism, and the constellation's cross-like shape gives it the alternative name of the Northern Cross.

Notable features

Deneb, a blue-white supergiant, marks the swan's tail and is one of the most luminous stars known. Albireo (β Cygni), at the head, is a celebrated double star with striking gold and blue-green components. Sadr (γ Cygni) sits at the centre of the cross, surrounded by the diffuse emission nebulosity of the Gamma Cygni region. Cygnus contains rich Milky Way star fields including the North America Nebula (NGC 7000), the Veil Nebula supernova remnant, and the Cygnus X star-forming complex, one of the largest known.

Science

Cygnus X-1, a strong X-ray source in the constellation, was the first source widely accepted to be a black hole, identified in the early 1970s through the work of, among others, Charles Thomas Bolton, Louise Webster and Paul Murdin. The Kepler space telescope's original field of view lay in Cygnus and neighbouring Lyra and Draco, yielding thousands of exoplanet candidates, and the 1964 Cygnus A galaxy outside the constellation's pattern is one of the brightest radio sources in the sky.

Mythology

In Greek mythology the swan is most often identified with Zeus, who transformed himself into a swan to seduce Leda; other traditions associate it with Orpheus, who was placed in the sky near his lyre, or with Cycnus, a son of Poseidon slain by Achilles in the Trojan War.
