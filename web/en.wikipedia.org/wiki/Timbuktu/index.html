Timbuktu is a city in Mali, situated about 20 km north of the Niger River, at the southern edge of the Sahara. Founded around 1100, it grew into one of the world's great centres of trade and learning by the 15th century, when caravans brought salt slabs from the desert mines of Taghaza and Taoudenni to exchange for gold from the south. Its name became proverbial in Europe for remoteness and fabled wealth.

Scholarly golden age

Under the Mali and Songhai empires, Timbuktu's Sankore Madrasah and other schools formed a university drawing thousands of students, teaching law, astronomy, mathematics, medicine and rhetoric. Families accumulated private libraries of manuscripts — hundreds of thousands are said to survive in the region, copied and traded across the Islamic world. Explorers reached the city only with difficulty: the Scotsman Alexander Gordon Laing in 1826 (murdered on departure) and the Frenchman René Caillié in 1828, who found a faded town. French colonial rule followed in 1893.

Modern times

Timbuktu is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 1988 for its mosques (Djinguereber, Sankore, Sidi Yahya) and mausolea. In 2012, jihadist groups occupying northern Mali destroyed shrines and threatened the manuscripts; librarians and families smuggled most manuscripts to Bamako in a celebrated rescue. Desertification, poverty and insecurity continue to threaten the city, which lies on the great river system that also feeds the floodplains near the Zambezi far to the south — a continent-spanning pattern of river cities trading across the Silk Road-like desert routes of Africa.
