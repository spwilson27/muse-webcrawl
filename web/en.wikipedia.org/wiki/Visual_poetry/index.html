Visual poetry

Visual poetry is a form of poetry in which the visual arrangement of text, images, and symbols on the page (or screen) is a fundamental part of the work's meaning — the poem is meant to be seen as much as read. It overlaps with concrete poetry, calligrams, pattern poetry, and artists' books, and spans a history from ancient Greek shaped verses to modernist typographic experiment and contemporary digital work.

History

Pattern poems date to the Hellenistic technopaegnia — verses by Simmias of Rhodes and others shaped as wings, an axe, or an egg. The tradition continued through medieval and Renaissance carmina figurata, George Herbert's "Easter Wings" and "The Altar" (1633), and Lewis Carroll's mouse's-tail-shaped verse in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland. In the early twentieth century, Guillaume Apollinaire's Calligrammes (1918) arranged type into pictures of rain, fountains, and objects, while Futurist and Dada poets such as Marinetti and Hugo Ball exploded conventional typography.

Concrete poetry and after

In the 1950s, the term "concrete poetry" was promoted independently by the Brazilian Noigandres group (Augusto and Haroldo de Campos, Décio Pignatari) and by Eugen Gomringer in Switzerland, treating words as visual objects in space. From the 1960s onward, poets including Ian Hamilton Finlay, Bob Cobbing, and Mary Ellen Solt expanded the field into typewriter art, Letraset, collage, and later computer and video poetry. Visual poetry today encompasses kinetic and digital poems in which text moves and changes on screen.
