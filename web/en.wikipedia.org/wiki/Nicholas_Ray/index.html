Nicholas Ray (born Raymond Nicholas Kienzle Jr.; August 7, 1911 – June 16, 1979) was an American film director, screenwriter, and actor. He is best known for the films They Live by Night (1948), In a Lonely Place (1950), Johnny Guitar (1954), and Rebel Without a Cause (1955).

Nicholas Ray

Nicholas Ray (August 7, 1911 – June 16, 1979) was an American film director whose work in the 1940s and 1950s made him one of the most admired auteurs of classical Hollywood. A restless, emotionally intense storyteller, Ray specialised in films about outsiders, doomed lovers and troubled young people, shot with vivid colour and bold compositions that later won him a devoted following among French New Wave critics and directors such as Jean-Luc Godard, who declared that "the cinema is Nicholas Ray".

Early life

Ray was born Raymond Nicholas Kienzle Jr. in Galesville, Wisconsin, and raised in La Crosse. After studying at the University of Chicago and briefly pursuing architecture under Frank Lloyd Wright at Taliesin, he turned to theatre, working with the Theatre of Action in New York and travelling with folk-music collectors Alan Lomax and John Houseman. Through Houseman and Elia Kazan he entered the film industry, making his directorial debut with They Live by Night (shot 1946, released 1948), a lyrical lovers-on-the-run story now regarded as a precursor of films such as Bonnie and Clyde.

Career

At RKO and in freelance work Ray directed a string of acclaimed pictures: the noir melodrama In a Lonely Place (1950) with Humphrey Bogart, the western Johnny Guitar (1954), and the youth drama Rebel Without a Cause (1955), which immortalised James Dean. Other notable works include Bigger Than Life (1956), Bitter Victory (1957), the epics King of Kings (1961) and 55 Days at Peking (1963). His later career was disrupted by alcoholism and conflict with studios; in the 1970s he taught filmmaking at Binghamton University and collaborated with Wim Wenders on Lightning Over Water (1980), filmed as he was dying of lung cancer.

Legacy

Ray's films, mixing emotional vulnerability with architectural, Cinemascope framing, influenced generations of directors from Martin Scorsese to Jim Jarmusch. Many of his once-overlooked works now rank among the most celebrated American films.
