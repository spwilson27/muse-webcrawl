Myosins are a superfamily of motor proteins that convert the chemical energy of ATP into mechanical work by moving along actin filaments. They are best known for their role in muscle contraction, but family members also drive cell division, vesicle transport, endocytosis, cell migration and the mechanosensory function of the inner ear.

Structure

A typical myosin consists of one or two heavy chains and associated light chains. The heavy chain has three regions: a globular head (motor domain) that binds actin and hydrolyses ATP; a neck that acts as a lever arm, amplified by bound light chains; and a tail that determines cargo and assembly. Conventional myosin II molecules dimerise through a long coiled-coil tail and assemble into the thick filaments of muscle.

Muscle contraction

In skeletal and cardiac muscle, myosin II thick filaments interdigitate with actin thin filaments in the sarcomere. In the cross-bridge cycle, ATP hydrolysis "cocks" the myosin head; binding to actin and release of phosphate trigger the power stroke that slides the filaments past each other, shortening the muscle. Calcium ions, acting through troponin and tropomyosin, control access to the actin binding sites. When ATP is absent after death, the heads remain locked to actin — the state of rigor mortis.

Diversity and disease

Humans possess around forty myosin genes across many classes. Unconventional myosins such as myosin V walk processively along actin to deliver cargo; myosin VIIa and others maintain the stereocilia of hair cells. Mutations in myosin genes cause hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathies (myosin heavy chain 7), hearing loss (myosins VIIa and XVa), and other disorders. The heart drug mavacamten, approved in 2022, works by inhibiting cardiac myosin ATPase.

History

Wilhelm Kühne isolated and named "myosin" from muscle in 1864. In the 1950s the sliding-filament model of contraction was proposed independently by Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke, and by Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson; subsequent crystallography of the myosin head revealed the lever-arm mechanism in atomic detail.
