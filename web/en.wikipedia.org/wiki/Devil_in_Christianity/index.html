In Christianity, the Devil — identified with Satan — is the central personification of evil: a fallen angel who rebelled against God and who tempts human beings into sin. Christian tradition holds that he was originally a high-ranking angel (often identified with Lucifer, "light-bearer") who was cast out of heaven with his followers after a war in heaven, as described in the Book of Revelation. He appears in the New Testament as the tempter of Jesus in the wilderness, the "prince of this world", and the adversary who will be defeated at the end of time.

Biblical basis

The Hebrew Bible's satan ("accuser") is largely a member of the divine council who tests humans, as in the Book of Job. In the New Testament, Satan becomes God's chief adversary: he tempts Christ (Matthew 4), is called Beelzebul and the father of lies, and appears in Revelation as the dragon who makes war on heaven and is thrown into the lake of fire. Later Christian theology identified the serpent of Genesis 3 and the "morning star" of Isaiah 14 with Satan as well.

Theology and doctrine

Mainstream Christian teaching, articulated by writers such as Augustine of Hippo, regards the Devil as a created being who is wholly evil but not equal to God — rejecting the dualism of heresies such as Manichaeism. Evil, for Augustine, is a privation of good rather than a substance. In Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant teaching, the Devil and his demons remain active tempters, but their power is limited and their final defeat is assured by Christ's death and resurrection. Views in modern liberal theology vary, with some interpreting Satan symbolically as the personification of evil.

In culture

The Devil is a major figure in Western literature and art, from Dante's Inferno and Milton's Paradise Lost — where Satan is a complex, defiant anti-hero — to Goethe's Mephistopheles in Faust. Medieval and early modern beliefs about the Devil fuelled witch trials and pact-with-the-Devil legends.
