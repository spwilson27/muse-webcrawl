Commemorative coins

Commemorative coins are coins issued to honour a particular person, place, event, or institution. Most modern commemoratives are struck for collectors rather than for general circulation and are sold by mints at a premium over face value, though some countries issue circulating commemoratives intended for everyday use. They are a major branch of numismatics.

Commemorative coins are often sold in presentation packaging to collectors.

History

The practice is ancient: Roman mints issued coins celebrating military victories, imperial anniversaries, and dynastic events, though these circulated as ordinary currency. Modern commemorative issues date largely from the late 19th century. The first United States commemorative coin, the Columbian half dollar of 1892–93, was sold at the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. Britain's Royal Mint and other national mints followed with issues marking coronations, jubilees, and national milestones.

Types

Commemoratives may be:

  • Circulating commemoratives — ordinary denominations issued into circulation with special designs, such as the U.S. 50 State quarters (1999–2008), Canadian commemorative issues, and the special €2 commemorative coins of the eurozone.
  • Non-circulating legal tender (NCLT) — coins sold to collectors in proof or uncirculated finishes, struck in base metal, silver, or gold, often with strictly limited mintages.
  • Bullion commemoratives — issues whose value rests mainly on precious-metal content combined with a themed design.

Themes

Common themes include anniversaries (of independence, battles, births and deaths of notable figures), sporting events such as the Olympic Games and FIFA World Cup, wildlife and conservation, royal occasions, and advances in science and exploration. Funds from sales sometimes support a related cause, as with the U.S. Mint's surcharge programmes that benefit museums and memorial foundations.

Controversies

Some countries — notoriously a few small island and microstate issuers — produce large numbers of commemoratives of questionable relevance, marketed aggressively to collectors; such issues rarely retain their purchase premium on the secondary market. Numismatists distinguish these from the restrained commemorative programmes of major mints.
