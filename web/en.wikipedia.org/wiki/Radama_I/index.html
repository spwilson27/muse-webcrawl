Overview

Radama I (c. 1793–1828), born Laidama, was the son and successor of Andrianampoinimerina, unifier of Imerina, and the king who extended Merina rule over most of Madagascar and opened the island to European contact. Succeeding in 1810, with British support — Governor Farquhar of Mauritius cultivated him as an ally against the French — Radama received arms, advisers and (from 1817) a treaty abolishing the slave trade in return for compensation and military aid.

Reign

London Missionary Society artisans and teachers followed (from 1820): David Jones and David Griffiths devised the Latin-alphabet orthography for Malagasy, schools spread, printing began, and a Malagasy Bible translation was completed shortly after Radama's death. His armies, drilled by the renegade sergeant James Hastie and others, conquered the Sakalava and Betsimisaraka coasts at heavy cost. Radama died in 1828 — of illness aggravated by alcohol, by most accounts — and was succeeded amid palace violence by his widow Ranavalona I, whose reign reversed his opening to the West.
