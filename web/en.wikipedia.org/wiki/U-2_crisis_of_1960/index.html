The U-2 incident, also known as the U-2 crisis of 1960, occurred on 1 May 1960, when an American Lockheed U-2 spy plane was shot down by Soviet air defences while performing photographic aerial reconnaissance deep inside Soviet territory. The pilot, Francis Gary Powers, ejected and was captured alive after initially failing to use his suicide pin.

Initially, the United States denied the plane's purpose and mission, claiming it was a weather aircraft that had strayed off course. The Soviet Union, led by Nikita Khrushchev, revealed that Powers was alive and unharmed, and that parts of the aircraft, including its surveillance camera and developed film, had been recovered largely intact.

The flight and shootdown

Powers took off from Peshawar, Pakistan, on a mission to photograph Soviet intercontinental ballistic missile sites at Baikonur and Plesetsk before landing in Bodø, Norway. Near Sverdlovsk (now Yekaterinburg), one of fourteen S-75 Dvina (SA-2 Guideline) surface-to-air missiles fired at the U-2 detonated close behind the aircraft, damaging it and forcing Powers to descend. He parachuted safely and was captured by the KGB.

Aftermath

The incident occurred less than two weeks before the scheduled opening of an east–west summit in Paris. Khrushchev demanded an apology from President Dwight D. Eisenhower; when Eisenhower acknowledged the flights but refused to apologize, the summit collapsed. Powers was convicted of espionage and sentenced to ten years' imprisonment, but was released in February 1962 in exchange for Soviet intelligence officer Rudolf Abel in the first-ever US–Soviet spy swap, which took place on the Glienicke Bridge in Berlin.

Legacy

The incident significantly damaged US–Soviet relations during the Cold War and ended a tentative period of improved relations sometimes called the "spirit of Camp David." The shootdown demonstrated that the U-2's extreme operating altitude no longer guaranteed immunity from Soviet defences, hastening development of faster aircraft and reconnaissance satellites.
