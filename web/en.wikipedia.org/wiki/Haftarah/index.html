Haftarah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The haftarah (Hebrew: הפטרה, "parting" or "conclusion"; plural haftarot) is a series of selections from the books of the Prophets (Nevi'im) of the Hebrew Bible that is publicly read in the synagogue as part of Jewish religious practice. The haftarah reading follows the Torah reading on the Sabbath (Saturday morning), on festivals, and on fast days; on Sabbath afternoons and some other occasions it is omitted or differs. The person called to read it is known as the maftir, and the portion customarily echoes a theme of that week's Torah portion, a connection sometimes as slight as a shared verse or phrase.

History

The origins of the practice are uncertain and debated. One long-standing theory holds that haftarah readings arose under Hellenistic or Roman persecution — when public Torah reading was forbidden, most famously under Antiochus IV — so that passages from the Prophets were substituted; differing customs among communities preserve traces of both one-year (Palestinian) and three-year reading cycles. Whatever its origin, the custom is already presupposed in sources of late antiquity, and the standard cycle of readings was fixed over the early medieval period, though Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Italian and Yemenite traditions still differ on some weeks.

Practice

Customs vary regarding the text used: in many congregations the haftarah is read from a printed book (a Chumash or a collection of all haftarot), while others use a parchment scroll containing only the relevant prophetic books, without the vowel points and cantillation marks of a Torah scroll. Special haftarot mark occasions such as the Sabbaths before Passover and the High Holy Days, the four special Sabbaths of late winter, and the haftarah for a groom (Haftarat Hatan). At a bar or bat mitzvah, the young person traditionally chants the haftarah. Distinctive prophetic passages, such as Isaiah's visions for the Sabbaths of consolation after Tisha B'Av, give several haftarot a prominent place in Jewish memory and preaching.
