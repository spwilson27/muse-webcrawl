Microtone

Quarter-tone accidentals in musical notation

A microtone is a musical interval smaller than a semitone. In Western music theory the semitone is the smallest standard interval, but many musical traditions — including Arabic maqam, Turkish makam, Indian classical music and Byzantine chant — systematically use smaller intervals, and 20th- and 21st-century composers have explored them extensively in the West. Music employing such intervals is called microtonal music.

Types and systems

The most commonly discussed microtones are the quarter tone (half a semitone, 50 cents), found in 24 equal temperament, and the intervals of other equal divisions such as 19-EDO, 31-EDO (which approximates meantone and contains diesis-sized steps), and 53-EDO. Composers such as Alois Hába wrote quarter-tone operas and commissioned quarter-tone pianos; Charles Ives composed pieces for two pianos tuned a quarter tone apart; Ivan Wyschnegradsky and Julián Carrillo (with his Sonido 13 theory) developed elaborate microtonal systems.

Practice

Microtones occur naturally in expressive performance: blues notes and string bends, the flexible intonation of fretless strings and voices, and the shruti of Indian music. Instruments such as the guitar can be refretted or the strings bent to reach microtones, while electronic instruments and software make arbitrary tuning systems practical. Notation uses extended accidentals, such as arrows or the half-sharp and half-flat signs systematised in notations like the Helmholtz–Ellis system.
