Pink and White Terraces

The White Terrace (Te Tarata), painted before its destruction

The Pink and White Terraces (Māori: Te Otukapuarangi, "the fountain of the clouded sky", and Te Tarata, "the tattooed rock") were natural travertine terraces on the shores of Lake Rotomahana, near Rotorua in New Zealand. Formed by geothermal silica deposits, they were considered a wonder of the natural world and were New Zealand's most famous tourist attraction of the nineteenth century.

Formation

Silica-rich hot water rose from two geysers at the lake's edge and spilled down hillsides, depositing siliceous sinter in great cascading steps and terraced pools. The White Terrace, Te Tarata, covered about 8 hectares and descended some 30 metres to the lake. The smaller Pink Terrace, Te Otukapuarangi, owed its rosy colour to mineral pigments — possibly antimony and other trace elements, or coloured microorganisms — in the sinter.

Tourism

From the 1870s the terraces drew visitors from around the world, who journeyed to Rotorua and were guided across the lake by Māori guides of Tūhourangi, most famously Guide Sophia Hinerangi. Visitors bathed in the terraced pools, whose temperatures varied by level.

Destruction

On 10 June 1886 the eruption of Mount Tarawera devastated the district. The eruption buried the terraces under ash and debris and transformed Lake Rotomahana, killing over 100 people and destroying the village of Te Wairoa. The terraces were long believed destroyed, but surveys in 2011 found substantial remains of the Pink Terrace on the lake bed, and a 2017 study based on surveyor Ferdinand von Hochstetter's rediscovered diaries argued that portions of both terraces may survive buried on land, a conclusion later researchers have debated.
