English landscape garden

The English garden, also called the English landscape park or jardin à l'anglaise, is a style of landscape garden that emerged in England in the early 18th century and spread across Europe, replacing the more formal, symmetrical French garden. It presents an idealised view of nature, with gently rolling lawns, groves of trees, lakes, and architectural set pieces such as classical temples, Gothic follies and ornamental bridges.

Origins and characteristics

Pioneers of the style, notably William Kent and Charles Bridgeman, broke with the geometric parterres and avenues of the Baroque garden in favour of naturalistic compositions inspired by landscape painting, especially the works of Claude Lorrain and Nicolas Poussin. The style was perfected by Lancelot "Capability" Brown, who reshaped the grounds of more than 170 estates, using sweeping turf, serpentine lakes created by damming streams, and clumps and belts of trees. A characteristic innovation was the ha-ha, a sunken fence that kept livestock away from the house while preserving an unbroken view.

Famous examples

Stowe and Stourhead are among the most celebrated surviving examples, combining lakes and woodland with temples, grottoes and statuary arranged as a sequence of picturesque scenes. Humphry Repton, Brown's successor, reintroduced terraces and flower gardens nearer the house. In the 19th century the style evolved into the gardenesque and the planting of the Victorian era, while the English garden itself was imitated across Europe and in public parks worldwide.

Influence

The English landscape garden expressed Enlightenment ideas about nature and liberty, contrasting with the absolutist symbolism of Versailles. Its principles underpin the design of many great urban parks, including Munich's Englischer Garten — one of the largest urban parks in the world — and influenced Frederick Law Olmsted's designs in North America.
