Sex-selective abortion

Sex-selective abortion is the practice of terminating a pregnancy based upon the predicted sex of the fetus. The selective abortion of female fetuses is most common where male children are valued over female children, especially in parts of East Asia, South Asia, the Caucasus, and the western Balkans, and is thought to have contributed to skewed sex ratios at birth in those regions.

Causes and extent

Demographers estimate the natural sex ratio at birth at about 105 boys per 100 girls. Ratios significantly above this range have been recorded in countries including China and India, where they rose following the spread of prenatal sex determination by ultrasound from the 1980s onward. Underlying causes commonly cited include patrilineal inheritance customs, dowry systems, reliance on sons for old-age support, and, in China, the interaction of son preference with the former one-child policy. Economist Amartya Sen drew attention to the phenomenon with his concept of "missing women," numbering over 100 million worldwide.

Responses

Many affected countries have enacted laws prohibiting prenatal sex determination and sex-selective termination — India's Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act (1994) is a prominent example — though enforcement varies. The practice is opposed by international bodies and women's rights advocates, and several countries' diaspora communities have also debated bans in countries such as Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States.

Consequences

Researchers have linked large cohorts of surplus males to social effects including a "marriage squeeze," bride trafficking, and increased migration for marriage, while noting that sex ratios in some countries, notably South Korea, have normalised as development, urbanisation, and changing norms reduced son preference.
