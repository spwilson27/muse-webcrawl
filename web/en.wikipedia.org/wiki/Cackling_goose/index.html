The cackling goose (Branta hutchinsii) is a species of goose native to North America. It closely resembles the Canada goose and was long considered part of that species, but it is noticeably smaller, with a shorter neck, a stubbier bill, and a higher-pitched, cackling call from which it takes its name.

Taxonomy

In 2004 the American Ornithologists' Union formally split the smaller forms of the Canada goose complex into a separate species, Branta hutchinsii, based on genetic and morphological evidence. Four subspecies are generally recognised:

  • B. h. hutchinsii — Richardson's cackling goose, breeding in north-central Canada
  • B. h. leucopareia — Aleutian cackling goose
  • B. h. minima — the smallest form, breeding in western Alaska
  • B. h. taverneri — Taverner's cackling goose

Description

The cackling goose has the same black head and neck, white cheek patch, and brown body as the Canada goose, but the largest cackling geese are barely the size of the smallest Canada geese; the tiny minima weighs little more than a mallard. The head is rounder, the bill shorter and more triangular, and the voice a high, yelping cackle rather than a deep honk.

Distribution and habitat

The species breeds on the tundra of northern Canada and Alaska and winters in the United States and Mexico, favouring open fields, wetlands, and agricultural land. Aleutian cackling geese winter mainly in California's Central Valley.

Conservation

The Aleutian cackling goose is a notable conservation success. Driven close to extinction by foxes introduced to its Aleutian breeding islands for the fur trade, it was listed under the US Endangered Species Act; after fox removal and reintroduction programmes, the population recovered from a few hundred birds to well over one hundred thousand, and the subspecies was delisted in 2001.
