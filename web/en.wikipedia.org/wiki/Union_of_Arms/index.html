Union of Arms

The Union of Arms sought a shared reserve army drawn from all the kingdoms of the Spanish monarchy.

The Union of Arms (Spanish: Unión de Armas) was a political and military programme proposed in 1625–1626 by the Count-Duke of Olivares, chief minister of Philip IV of Spain. It aimed to create a standing reserve force of 140,000 soldiers to which every kingdom and province of the Spanish monarchy — not only Castile — would contribute men and money in proportion to its population and wealth.

Background

By the 1620s Spain was fighting in the Netherlands, Germany and the Atlantic, and the burden of war fell overwhelmingly on Castile, whose population and treasury were exhausted. Olivares believed the monarchy's other realms — Aragon, Catalonia, Valencia, Portugal, Naples and Flanders — enjoyed the protection of Spanish arms while contributing little, and that sharing the burden was essential to preserve the empire.

The proposal and resistance

Under the plan, each territory would maintain a quota of paid reservists who could be called upon for the common defence. Although Olivares presented the scheme as mutual protection rather than centralisation, the non-Castilian kingdoms guarded their fueros (traditional privileges) jealously and suspected a Castilian power grab. The Cortes of the Crown of Aragon resisted, and the scheme was only ever partially and locally implemented.

Consequences

The attempt to enforce contributions — especially demands connected with the war against France after 1635 and the quartering of troops in Catalonia — helped provoke the Catalan Revolt (the Reapers' War) of 1640 and contributed to the Portuguese Restoration War of the same year, in which Portugal broke away from the monarchy. Historians regard the Union of Arms as emblematic of Olivares's reforming ambitions and of the structural tensions within the composite Spanish monarchy.
