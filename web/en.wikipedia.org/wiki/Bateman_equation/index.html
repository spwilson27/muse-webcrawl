Bateman equations

In nuclear physics, the Bateman equations are a set of first-order differential equations describing the abundances of nuclides as a function of time in a radioactive decay chain. The system was solved analytically by the English mathematician Harry Bateman in 1910, giving explicit expressions for the number of atoms of each member of a decay series.

Formulation

Consider a decay chain of n nuclides, where nuclide i decays into nuclide i+1 with decay constant λi. Letting Ni(t) denote the number of atoms of nuclide i at time t, the equations are:

dN1/dt = −λ1N1

dNi/dt = λi−1Ni−1 − λiNi   (for 1 < i < n)

dNn/dt = λn−1Nn−1

The last nuclide is assumed stable (λn = 0).

Solution

Bateman's solution for the abundance of the i-th nuclide, given an initial quantity N1(0) = N0 of the pure parent and zero initial quantities of the daughters, is a sum of exponential terms:

Ni(t) = (N0/λi) · Σj=1..i [ λj Cij exp(−λjt) ]

where the coefficients Cij are products of ratios of the decay constants, Cij = Πk=1, k≠j..i λk / (λk − λj). The solution fails in its simple form when two decay constants in the chain are equal, in which case limiting forms involving terms of the type t·exp(−λt) appear.

Applications

The Bateman equations underpin calculations of radioactive decay and transmutation in nuclear engineering, including nuclear fuel burnup, the ingrowth of decay products in spent fuel, fission-product yields, and radiometric dating. Modern depletion codes solve far larger coupled systems — including neutron-induced transmutation alongside decay — numerically, but the analytic Bateman solution remains the benchmark and the conceptual basis for the treatment of linear decay chains.

Closely related phenomena described by special cases of the equations include secular equilibrium, in which a long-lived parent maintains a nearly constant daughter activity, and transient equilibrium between parent and daughter of comparable half-life.
