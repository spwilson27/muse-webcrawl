Amitābha (Sanskrit: अमिताभ, "Infinite Light") is a celestial Buddha in Mahāyāna Buddhism, best known as the principal Buddha of Pure Land Buddhism. According to the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra, Amitābha was, in a past life, a monk named Dharmākara who made 48 vows, promising that beings who sincerely entrusted themselves to him and called his name would be reborn in his pure land, Sukhāvatī, the Land of Bliss in the west.

Role in Pure Land Buddhism

Pure Land practice centres on mindfulness of Amitābha, expressed through recitation of his name — the Chinese nianfo ("Nāmó Āmítuófó") and the Japanese nembutsu ("Namu Amida Butsu"). In the teachings of Hōnen and Shinran in Japan, rebirth in the Pure Land was held to depend entirely on Amitābha's "other-power" rather than the practitioner's own efforts. From Sukhāvatī, it is taught, a being can progress to enlightenment without falling back into the sufferings of samsara.

Iconography

Amitābha is usually depicted in meditation posture with hands in dhyāna mudrā holding an alms bowl, often flanked by the bodhisattvas Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta. Famous images include the great bronze Amida at Kōtoku-in in Kamakura, Japan. In esoteric Buddhism he is one of the Five Tathāgatas (Wisdom Buddhas), presiding over the west and the lotus family, associated with the transformation of desire into discriminating wisdom.

Origins

The earliest dated reference to Amitābha is an inscription from 2nd-century India found at Govindnagar, and the first Sukhāvatī sutras were translated into Chinese by the early 3rd century CE. Scholars have speculated about Iranian influences on the cult of the Buddha of Infinite Light, though this remains debated.
