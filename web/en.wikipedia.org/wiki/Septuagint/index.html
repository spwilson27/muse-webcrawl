Septuagint

Septuagint
Ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible
Produced: 3rd–2nd century BCE, Alexandria

The Septuagint (often abbreviated LXX) is the earliest extant Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, produced in Hellenistic Alexandria beginning in the 3rd century BCE. Its name, Latin for "seventy", derives from the legend in the Letter of Aristeas that seventy-two Jewish translators worked independently yet produced identical texts.

History

Commissioned, according to tradition, for the Library of Alexandria under Ptolemy II, the translation first covered the Torah and later extended to the Prophets, Writings, and additional books now classed as deuterocanonical. It became the scriptures of Greek-speaking Jews and then of the early Christian Church.

Significance

New Testament authors quote the Septuagint far more often than the Hebrew text. Its wording influenced Christian theology — notably the rendering of Hebrew almah as Greek parthenos ("virgin") in Isaiah 7:14. Major surviving codices include Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, and Alexandrinus.
