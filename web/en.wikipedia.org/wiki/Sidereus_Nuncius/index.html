Sidereus Nuncius (usually translated as Starry Messenger, also rendered Starry Message or Sidereal Messenger) is a short astronomical treatise, or pamphlet, published in New Latin by Galileo Galilei on March 13, 1610. It was the first published scientific work based on observations made through a telescope, and it contains the results of Galileo's early observations of the imperfect and mountainous Moon, of a multitude of stars too faint to be seen with the naked eye, and of the Medicean Stars (the four largest moons of Jupiter).

Contents

Galileo reported that the Moon's surface is rough and uneven, covered with mountains whose heights he estimated from the shadows at the terminator — contradicting the Aristotelian doctrine of perfect celestial spheres. He recorded far more stars in Orion and the Pleiades than were visible unaided, and observed that the Milky Way resolves into innumerable stars. Most consequentially, between 7 and 15 January 1610 he watched four "stars" that moved around Jupiter, concluding that they were moons of the planet — evidence that not everything in the heavens revolves around the Earth. He named them the Medicean Stars in honour of Cosimo II de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany, whose court mathematician he hoped to become.

Reception

The little book made Galileo famous across Europe; Johannes Kepler responded enthusiastically with his Conversation with the Starry Messenger. Sold out within a week, the work is regarded as a landmark of the Scientific Revolution.
