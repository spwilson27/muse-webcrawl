Shōji

A shōji (Japanese: 障子) is a door, window, or room divider in traditional Japanese architecture consisting of translucent washi paper stretched over a light wooden lattice frame. Shōji diffuse daylight softly through a room, provide visual privacy while remaining lightweight, and slide in wooden tracks so that interior space can be opened up or subdivided at will.

Construction

The frame (kumiko) is usually made of cedar, cypress, or bamboo, joined in geometric lattice patterns that range from simple rectangles to elaborate decorative designs. A single sheet of washi — traditionally handmade from mulberry fibres — is pasted across the back of the lattice with rice glue; damaged paper is traditionally renewed once a year before New Year. Modern shōji may use laminated paper or plastic sheets for durability, and some contemporary versions incorporate double layers or insulation to suit colder climates.

History

Interior screens developed in the Heian period from portable partitions; the sliding, lightweight form became standard in the shoin-zukuri residential style of the Muromachi and Momoyama periods, paired with tatami flooring, fusuma (opaque sliding panels), and tokobashira alcoves. Shoji's soft, shadowed light became a celebrated feature of Japanese aesthetics, famously praised by Jun'ichirō Tanizaki in his essay In Praise of Shadows (1933).

Use today

Shōji remain common in Japanese homes and ryokan inns, and shōji-style screens and lamps are widely used internationally as interior-design elements. Exterior shōji are protected by amado shutters or glass panes against weather.
