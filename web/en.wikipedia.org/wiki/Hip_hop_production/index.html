Hip hop production

Hip hop production is the creation of hip hop music in a recording studio environment. The term encompasses the methods used to create the backing tracks — commonly called beats — over which rappers perform, as well as the recording, mixing and engineering of the finished track. Although the term is sometimes used more loosely, hip hop production refers specifically to the craft of making the instrumental foundation of a hip hop song.

History

Hip hop production began in the Bronx in the 1970s, when DJs such as DJ Kool Herc isolated and extended the percussion breaks of funk and soul records using two turntables. Grandmaster Flash refined turntablism techniques such as cutting and backspinning, while Afrika Bambaataa broadened the palette of source material. In the 1980s, drum machines — most famously the Roland TR-808 — and samplers such as the E-mu SP-1200 and Akai MPC series transformed production, allowing producers to chop, loop and rearrange fragments of recorded music into new compositions.

Producers such as Marley Marl, Rick Rubin, Dr. Dre, DJ Premier, RZA, J Dilla and Kanye West each defined distinct regional and personal styles. The rise of digital audio workstations in the 2000s, and later of software-based beatmaking, made production widely accessible, while trap producers in Atlanta popularised hi-hat rolls, 808 bass slides and minimalist arrangements in the 2010s.

Techniques

Core techniques include sampling (reusing portions of existing recordings), looping, chopping (segmenting a sample into pieces that can be replayed in a new order), drum programming, synthesizer and live instrumentation, and arrangement. Producers typically work in a digital audio workstation such as FL Studio, Ableton Live, Logic Pro or Pro Tools, or with hardware samplers like the Akai MPC.

Equipment

Classic hip hop production tools include the Technics SL-1200 turntable, the Roland TR-808 and TR-909 drum machines, the E-mu SP-1200, the Akai MPC60, MPC2000 and MPC2000XL, the Ensoniq ASR-10, and a wide range of software instruments and sample libraries.

Legal issues

Sampling raised significant copyright questions. Landmark cases such as Grand Upright Music, Ltd. v. Warner Bros. Records Inc. (1991) and Bridgeport Music, Inc. v. Dimension Films (2005) established that samples generally must be licensed, reshaping production practices and encouraging interpolation and replayed samples.
