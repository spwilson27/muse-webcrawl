Housing discrimination in the United States

Housing discrimination in the United States refers to the unequal treatment of people seeking housing — in renting, buying, lending and insurance — historically and primarily on the basis of race, and also on grounds including ethnicity, religion, sex, disability and familial status. Such discrimination shaped the racial geography of American cities throughout the twentieth century.

Historical practices

Key mechanisms included racially restrictive covenants attached to deeds, which barred sales to Black, Asian, Jewish and other buyers and were enforced by courts until Shelley v. Kraemer (1948); "redlining", in which federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation maps graded neighbourhoods by racial composition and private lenders and the Federal Housing Administration denied mortgages in Black areas; steering by real estate agents; blockbusting; and exclusionary zoning. These practices suppressed Black homeownership and wealth accumulation while subsidizing white suburbanization.

Legal framework

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 (Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act), passed days after the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr., prohibited discrimination in the sale, rental and financing of housing based on race, colour, religion and national origin; sex was added in 1974 and disability and familial status in 1988. The Supreme Court upheld the doctrine of disparate impact under the Act in Texas Department of Housing v. Inclusive Communities Project (2015).

Contemporary issues

Enforcement relies on the Department of Housing and Urban Development, the Department of Justice, state agencies and private fair-housing organizations using paired testing. Studies continue to document steering, differential treatment in rental markets, appraisal bias and discriminatory effects of tenant screening and algorithmic pricing.
