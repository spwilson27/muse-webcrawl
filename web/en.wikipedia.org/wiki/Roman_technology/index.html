Roman technology


Fields: Civil engineering, hydraulic engineering, military engineering, materials
Signature works: Aqueducts, concrete, roads, baths, cranes

Roman technology is the collection of techniques, skills, methods, processes, and engineering practices used and developed by the civilisation of ancient Rome (753 BC – AD 476), which supported its economic and military expansion. Roman engineering was rarely theoretically innovative in the Greek manner but was outstanding in the practical adaptation, standardisation, and large-scale application of techniques across an empire.

Civil engineering

The Romans built some 84,000 km of paved roads, eleven aqueducts supplying Rome alone with roughly a billion litres of water per day, and monumental structures such as the Colosseum, the Baths of Caracalla, and the dome of the Pantheon — at 43.3 metres still the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. Their mastery of the arch, vault, and dome, combined with hydraulic concrete (opus caementicium) made with volcanic pozzolana ash that could set underwater, enabled harbours, bridges, and lighthouses from Britain to Mesopotamia.

Energy and industry

Water power was applied to milling grain — the Barbegal complex in Gaul, with sixteen overshot wheels, was the largest known milling installation of the ancient world — as well as to sawing stone and crushing ore. Mining used hydraulic techniques (ruina montium, described by Pliny) and sequences of waterwheels and Archimedes' screws for drainage. Glassblowing, invented in the Roman sphere in the first century BC, transformed glass from a luxury into an everyday material.

Military and mechanical technology

Roman arms included torsion artillery (ballistae and onagers), sophisticated siege machinery, and standardised fortification — each marching camp was laid out and ditched daily. Mechanical devices included treadwheel cranes depicted on the Haterii relief, odometers described by Vitruvius, geared astronomical mechanisms in the Greek tradition (the Antikythera mechanism), and Hero of Alexandria's aeolipile and automatic temple doors. Surgical instruments, central heating by hypocaust, and the Julian calendar reform further illustrate the breadth of Roman applied knowledge.
