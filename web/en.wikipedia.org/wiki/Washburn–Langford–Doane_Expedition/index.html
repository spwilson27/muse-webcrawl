Washburn–Langford–Doane Expedition

The Washburn–Langford–Doane Expedition of 1870 was the first detailed exploration of the region that later became Yellowstone National Park in the northwestern United States. Led by Montana surveyor-general Henry Washburn, with Nathaniel P. Langford and a United States Army escort under Lieutenant Gustavus Doane, the party spent about a month exploring the upper Yellowstone region in August and September 1870.

The expedition

The nine civilian members, mostly prominent Montana citizens, entered the region from the north, examined the Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone and its falls, and climbed the peak Langford named Mount Washburn. At Madison Junction, according to later accounts, the party discussed the commercial exploitation of the area and instead resolved that it should be preserved for the public — a conversation that helped inspire the national park idea. Member Truman Everts became separated and survived 37 days alone before rescue, an ordeal widely reported in the press.

Impact

Doane's official report and Langford's articles and lectures brought the wonders of the region to national attention and helped prompt the official Hayden Geological Survey of 1871. On 1 March 1872 President Ulysses S. Grant signed the act creating Yellowstone as the world's first national park; Langford became its first superintendent.
