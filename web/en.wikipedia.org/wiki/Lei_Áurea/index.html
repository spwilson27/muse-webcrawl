Lei Áurea

[Image: Signing of the Golden Law]

The Lei Áurea (Portuguese for "Golden Law"), officially Law No. 3,353 of 13 May 1888, is the law that abolished slavery in Brazil. It was signed by Princess Isabel, acting as regent for her father, Emperor Pedro II, and consisted of just two articles: the first declared slavery extinct in Brazil from the date of the law; the second revoked all contrary provisions. Brazil was the last country in the Western world to abolish slavery.

Background

Abolition came in stages. The slave trade was effectively suppressed by the Eusébio de Queirós Law of 1850 under British pressure. The Rio Branco Law (Law of the Free Womb, 1871) declared children born to enslaved women free, and the Sexagenarian Law of 1885 freed enslaved people over sixty. By the 1880s the abolitionist movement — led by figures such as Joaquim Nabuco, José do Patrocínio, Luís Gama and André Rebouças — had become a mass campaign; enslaved people increasingly fled plantations, the army declined to pursue fugitives, and several provinces abolished slavery locally before 1888.

Consequences

The law freed the remaining enslaved population, roughly 700,000 people, without compensation to slave owners and without any programme of land, education or assistance for the freed. Many landowners blamed the monarchy for abolition without indemnity and withdrew their support, contributing to the proclamation of the republic the following year, on 15 November 1889. The Lei Áurea's anniversary, 13 May, is commemorated in Brazil, though Afro-Brazilian movements note the incompleteness of emancipation without material reparations.
