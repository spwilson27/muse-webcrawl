Retinal artery occlusion

Retinal artery occlusion is a blockage of one of the arteries carrying blood to the retina, most often by an embolus. Occlusion of the central retinal artery (CRAO) causes sudden, painless, severe loss of vision in one eye, typically within seconds to hours. Blockage of a branch retinal artery (BRAO) causes loss of part of the visual field.

Signs and causes

On fundus examination the ischaemic retina appears pale and swollen, with a contrasting "cherry-red spot" at the fovea, where the underlying choroidal circulation shows through the thin foveola. The usual source of emboli is atherosclerotic plaque in the carotid arteries or fibrin-platelet emboli from the heart; in older patients, giant cell arteritis is an important alternative cause that must be excluded. Risk factors mirror those of stroke and cardiovascular disease, and retinal artery occlusion is regarded as an ocular analogue of cerebral infarction — patients are at elevated risk of subsequent stroke.

Treatment

There is no treatment of proven benefit once occlusion is established; emergency measures attempted include ocular massage, anterior chamber paracentesis, intraocular-pressure-lowering drugs and breathing carbogen, and intravenous thrombolysis has been studied within a very short window. Management therefore focuses on urgent stroke work-up and secondary prevention, including carotid imaging, antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation where indicated, and treatment of giant cell arteritis with corticosteroids when suspected.
