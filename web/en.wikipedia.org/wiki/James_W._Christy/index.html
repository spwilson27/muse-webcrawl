James W. Christy

James Walter Christy (born September 15, 1938) is an American astronomer known for discovering Charon, the largest moon of Pluto, in 1978 while working at the United States Naval Observatory. The discovery fundamentally revised estimates of Pluto's size and mass and revealed the Pluto–Charon system as a binary world.

Early life and career

Christy was born in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, and studied astronomy at the University of Arizona, graduating in 1965. He joined the United States Naval Observatory, where his work involved photographic astrometry — the precise measurement of star and planet positions on photographic plates.

Discovery of Charon

On June 22, 1978, while examining highly magnified photographic images of Pluto taken with the observatory's 1.55-metre telescope at Flagstaff, Arizona, Christy noticed that Pluto appeared periodically elongated, with a bulge that shifted from side to side. Rather than dismissing the bulge as a plate defect, he checked earlier plates and found the elongation recurring with a period of 6.387 days — exactly Pluto's rotation period, indicating a large companion in a tidally locked orbit.

Christy named the moon Charon, after the ferryman of the dead in Greek mythology who carries souls across the Styx — a name that also echoed the name of his wife, Charlene ("Char"). The discovery was announced on July 7, 1978. Because Charon is roughly half Pluto's diameter, the pair is often described as a double dwarf planet system, and the discovery showed Pluto to be far smaller and less massive than previously believed — a key step toward its eventual reclassification by the International Astronomical Union in 2006.

Later work

Christy continued his career in astrometry and later worked in private industry on satellite tracking and related fields. The asteroid 129564 Christy was named in his honour. In 2015 he attended the flyby of the Pluto system by NASA's New Horizons spacecraft, which imaged Charon in detail for the first time.
