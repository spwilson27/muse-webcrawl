Non-orientable surface

The Möbius strip is a non-orientable surface with a single side

In mathematics, a non-orientable surface is a surface (a two-dimensional manifold) that does not have a consistent choice of "side" or orientation. The concept of orientability is a property of a surface or, more generally, a manifold, that describes whether one can consistently choose a "clockwise" orientation at every point.

A surface is orientable if it has two distinct sides — informally, an ant walking on it can never return to its starting point mirror-reversed. On a non-orientable surface, such a journey is possible: a figure transported once around a suitable closed loop comes back as its own mirror image.

Examples

  • The Möbius strip — a strip of paper joined with a half-twist; it has only one side and one boundary edge.
  • The real projective plane — a closed non-orientable surface that cannot be embedded in three-dimensional space without self-intersection.
  • The Klein bottle — a closed non-orientable surface with no boundary, often depicted as a bottle whose neck passes through its side.

Classification

Every closed non-orientable surface can be obtained from a sphere by removing the interiors of some number of disks and gluing in Möbius strips (equivalently, by connected sums with real projective planes). The number of projective-plane summands is a complete topological invariant, known as the non-orientable genus. By contrast, orientable closed surfaces are classified by their genus — the number of "handles" — as the torus, double torus, and so on.

Orientation double cover

Every non-orientable manifold possesses an orientable double cover — an orientable manifold together with a two-to-one map onto the original. For the Möbius strip this cover is an ordinary cylinder; for the Klein bottle it is a torus.
