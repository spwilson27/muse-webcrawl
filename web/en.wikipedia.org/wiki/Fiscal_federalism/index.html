Fiscal federalism

FieldPublic economics
Key figuresRichard Musgrave, Wallace Oates, Charles Tiebout

Fiscal federalism is the branch of public economics concerned with the division of governmental functions and financial relations among levels of government — central, state or provincial, and local — in a federal system. It studies which level should tax, spend, and provide each public service, and how transfers between levels should be designed.

Theory

Richard Musgrave's framework assigned macroeconomic stabilization and income distribution chiefly to the central government, while the allocation of resources could be shared. Wallace Oates's decentralization theorem (1972) holds that, absent spillovers and scale economies, provision of a public good by the lowest level of government that can internalize its benefits is more efficient, because local governments can tailor output to local preferences. Charles Tiebout's model proposed that mobile citizens "vote with their feet," sorting among jurisdictions offering different tax-and-service bundles.

Issues

Central topics include intergovernmental grants, equalization payments to poorer regions, tax competition, the soft budget constraint and bailout expectations, and the mismatch known as vertical fiscal imbalance, in which spending responsibilities exceed subnational revenue-raising powers. The framework applies to federations such as the United States, Germany, Canada and India, and to fiscal arrangements within the European Union.
