Commission (remuneration)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A commission is a form of variable-pay remuneration for services rendered or products sold. Commissions are a common way to motivate and reward salespeople, agents and brokers: payment is awarded as a percentage of the value of a transaction, or sometimes as a flat sum per transaction, aligning the agent's income with the volume or value of business they generate. Professions commonly paid by commission include real estate agents, insurance agents, stockbrokers, travel agents, car salespeople and retail staff.

Structures

Commission plans range from "straight commission" (all pay variable) to salary-plus-commission blends, sometimes with draws against future commissions, tiered rates that rise with sales volume, residuals on recurring revenue, and clawbacks if a sale is cancelled. In real estate, customary total commissions on home sales have historically been around 5–6% of the price in the United States, split between the buyer's and seller's brokers — an arrangement challenged by antitrust litigation and a 2024 settlement by the National Association of Realtors that changed how commissions are advertised and negotiated.

Advantages and criticism

For employers, commission pay links labour cost directly to revenue and can attract high-performing sellers; for workers it offers high upside but unstable income. Economists note the principal–agent problems commissions create: incentives to maximise the agent's own transactions rather than the client's interests, which has driven regulation of commission disclosure and, in some markets such as UK retail investment advice (2013) and Australian financial advice, outright bans on certain product commissions.
