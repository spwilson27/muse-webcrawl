Ebola virus

Ebola virus (Zaire ebolavirus) is a virus in the family Filoviridae, genus Ebolavirus, and the principal cause of Ebola virus disease in humans. It is named after the Ebola River in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, near where the first recognised outbreak occurred in 1976. Ebola virus has one of the highest case fatality rates of any human pathogen, averaging around 50 percent in outbreaks.

Structure and genome

The virus particle is filamentous and pleomorphic — often shaped like a shepherd's crook or thread — about 80 nm in diameter and of variable length. It carries a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA genome of roughly 19 kilobases encoding seven proteins, including the nucleoprotein, the polymerase (L), and the surface glycoprotein (GP) responsible for cell entry.

Ecology

Fruit bats of the family Pteropodidae are considered the natural reservoir hosts, though the virus has not been conclusively isolated from wild bats. Spillover into humans occurs through contact with infected animals — alive or dead — after which the virus spreads between people through direct contact with blood and body fluids.

Species

The genus Ebolavirus includes six recognised species: Zaire, Sudan, Bundibugyo, Taï Forest, Reston and Bombali ebolaviruses. Of these, Zaire, Sudan and Bundibugyo have caused large human outbreaks; Reston virus infects non-human primates and can infect humans without known illness.
