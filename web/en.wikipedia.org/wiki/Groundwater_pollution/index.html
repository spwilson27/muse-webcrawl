Groundwater pollution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Groundwater pollution (or groundwater contamination) occurs when pollutants are released to the ground and make their way down into the water table, contaminating an aquifer. Pollutants are often released within an area above the aquifer and move through it following the groundwater flow paths. Because groundwater generally moves slowly and is hidden from view, contamination can spread over large areas before it is detected, and remediation is typically slow and expensive.

Sources and contaminants

Major sources include agricultural runoff of fertilisers and pesticides, leaking underground storage tanks and pipelines, landfills and waste dumps, septic systems, industrial spills, and mining operations. Notable contaminants include nitrates (a cause of methemoglobinemia, or "blue baby syndrome"), pathogens from faecal waste, petroleum hydrocarbons, chlorinated solvents such as trichloroethylene, heavy metals, and arsenic — which occurs naturally in many aquifers but is mobilised by pumping, causing mass poisoning in Bangladesh and West Bengal, described by the World Health Organization as the largest mass poisoning in history.

More recently, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), pharmaceuticals, and microplastics have emerged as contaminants of concern. Road salt and increased pumping near coasts can also cause saline intrusion.

Transport and fate

Contaminants move through aquifers by advection with flowing groundwater, dispersion, and diffusion, and may be retarded by sorption to soil particles or transformed by chemical and biological processes. Dense non-aqueous phase liquids (DNAPLs), such as chlorinated solvents, sink below the water table and can act as long-term contamination sources that are extremely difficult to remove.

Prevention and remediation

Protection strategies include wellhead protection zones, land-use controls, lining of waste facilities, and monitoring networks. Remediation techniques include pump-and-treat, permeable reactive barriers, in-situ bioremediation, and monitored natural attenuation. Because cleanup is so difficult, prevention is generally considered far more cost-effective than remediation.
