Rule of Faith

Rule of Faith
LatinRegula fidei
Also calledRule of truth (regula veritatis)
FieldChristian theology, patristics

The rule of faith (Latin: regula fidei) is the name given to the ultimate authority or standard in religious belief. In early Christianity it referred to the summary of apostolic teaching — the core confession of faith handed down from the apostles — against which the scriptures were to be interpreted and heretical teachings judged.

In the early church

Writers of the second and third centuries, notably Irenaeus of Lyons and Tertullian, appealed to the rule of faith (or "rule of truth") in controversies with Gnostics. Irenaeus argued that the apostolic tradition was preserved publicly and continuously in the churches founded by the apostles, and that the rule — confessing one God the Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth; one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, who became incarnate for our salvation; and the Holy Spirit — provided the fixed framework within which scripture was rightly read. The rule is closely related to the early baptismal creeds and is an ancestor of the Apostles' Creed.

Later usage

In Roman Catholic theology, the rule of faith came to denote the teaching of scripture and tradition as interpreted by the magisterium of the Church. In Protestant theology, especially under the principle of sola scriptura, scripture itself is identified as the "rule of faith and practice" — the supreme, and in some formulations the only infallible, standard — while creeds, confessions, and tradition function as subordinate ("ruled") standards. Reformed confessions such as the Westminster Confession describe Scripture as "the only rule of faith and obedience".

Theologians also distinguish between the remote rule of faith (scripture and tradition, the sources of revelation) and the proximate rule (the living teaching authority that applies them).
