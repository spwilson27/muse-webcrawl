Form criticism

Form criticism is a method of biblical criticism that classifies units of scripture by literary pattern (such as parables, legends or myths) and then attempts to trace each type to its period of oral transmission and its original setting in life (Sitz im Leben). The method was developed by German scholars in the early twentieth century, notably Karl Ludwig Schmidt, Martin Dibelius and Rudolf Bultmann, who applied it first to the synoptic Gospels.

Unlike source criticism, which studies how the written sources behind a text were combined, form criticism concerns the smaller units of tradition that circulated orally before being written down. It asks what function a particular kind of passage — a miracle story, a pronouncement story, a hymn, a parable — served in the worship, preaching, teaching or polemics of the early communities that preserved it.

Methodology

The form critic's procedure involves:

  1. Identifying the small, self-contained units of the text and classifying them by form or genre (Gattung) — e.g. miracle stories, legends, myths, parables, sayings.
  2. Determining the Sitz im Leben, the social setting in which that form typically arose and was used.
  3. Tracing the history of the tradition as it passed from its original setting into the written Gospel, noting how the community's needs shaped it.

Hermann Gunkel had earlier applied similar analysis to the Old Testament, classifying the Psalms into genres such as hymns, laments and thanksgiving songs, each tied to a setting in Israel's worship.

Key works

  • Karl Ludwig Schmidt, Der Rahmen der Geschichte Jesu (1919) — argued the connective framework of Mark's Gospel was editorial, while the individual scenes were separate traditional units.
  • Martin Dibelius, Die Formgeschichte des Evangeliums (1919) — classified Gospel material into paradigms, tales, legends, myths and sayings.
  • Rudolf Bultmann, The History of the Synoptic Tradition (1921) — the most comprehensive form-critical study, sceptical about recovering the historical Jesus from much of the tradition.

Criticism and legacy

Critics have questioned form criticism's assumptions: that traditions circulated in fixed forms governed by "laws", that communities rather than individuals shaped them anonymously, and that the forms can be neatly assigned to a single setting in life. Later methods — redaction criticism, narrative criticism and oral-tradition studies — built on or reacted against form criticism, which nonetheless remains foundational to the historical study of the Gospels.
