Raised-bed gardening

Vegetables growing in framed raised beds.

Raised-bed gardening is a form of gardening in which the soil is raised above ground level, usually enclosed in frames of wood, stone, or other materials, and planted more intensively than in conventional rows. Beds are typically about 1.2 m (4 ft) wide so they can be worked from the sides without stepping on and compacting the soil.

Advantages

Raised beds warm up earlier in spring, drain well, and allow the gardener to control soil composition — valuable where native soil is heavy clay, rocky, or contaminated. The contained structure reduces weed pressure, improves accessibility for gardeners with limited mobility, and suits intensive methods such as square foot gardening, which often yields more produce per area than row planting.

Disadvantages and construction

Beds cost more to build and fill than in-ground gardens and dry out faster, requiring more watering, especially in warm climates. Frames are made from rot-resistant or untreated timber, concrete blocks, corrugated metal, or recycled materials; treated lumber containing arsenic (CCA) is avoided for food gardens. Beds are commonly 15–30 cm deep, deeper for root crops. Related practices include hügelkultur and container gardening. See also square foot gardening.
