Twisted pair

Twisted pair cabling is a type of wiring in which two conductors of a single circuit are twisted together to cancel out electromagnetic interference (EMI) from external sources and to reduce crosstalk between neighbouring pairs. The invention is credited to Alexander Graham Bell, who received a patent in 1881. Twisted pair is the dominant medium for telephone networks and for Ethernet local area networks.

Why twisting works

When two wires carry equal and opposite signals (balanced transmission), interference picked up equally by both wires—a "common-mode" signal—can be rejected by a differential receiver. Twisting ensures each wire is exposed to nearly the same external field and swaps their positions regularly, averaging out interference and equalising radiation. Different pairs in a cable are twisted at different rates to minimise pair-to-pair crosstalk.

Categories

Performance categories are standardised by ANSI/TIA. Category 5e supports 1 Gbit/s Ethernet to 100 m; Category 6 supports 10 Gbit/s to 55 m; Category 6a extends 10 Gbit/s to 100 m; Category 8 targets 25/40 Gbit/s over 30 m in data centres. Shielded variants add foil or braid screens for noisy environments.

Applications

Beyond LANs, twisted pairs carry analogue telephone service, DSL broadband over legacy phone lines, and various industrial fieldbus systems. Its low cost and easy installation keep it ubiquitous despite competition from optical fibre at higher bandwidths.
