The Revolt of the Masses (Spanish: La rebelión de las masas) is a book by the Spanish philosopher José Ortega y Gasset, published in 1930 after appearing in serial form in the Madrid newspaper El Sol in 1929. It is Ortega's best-known work and one of the most influential works of social criticism of the twentieth century. An anonymous English translation appeared in 1932. In the book Ortega analyses the rise of what he calls the "mass-man" (hombre-masa) — a type, found in all classes, who is complacently mediocre, demands rights without obligations, and rejects the authority of reason, expertise and tradition.

Argument

Ortega begins from a visible fact of modern life: the accession of the masses to complete social power — crowds filling spaces (hotels, theatres, beaches, parliaments) once reserved to minorities. For Ortega, "mass" is not a social class but a psychological type: the mass-man feels himself "just like everybody," is not troubled by that feeling, and imposes his vulgarity as a right. He distinguishes this figure from the "noble" or select man, who demands much of himself and lives in self-discipline, regardless of wealth or birth.

The revolt of the title is the political consequence: mass-man, the "spoiled child of human history" produced by nineteenth-century liberal democracy, technical progress and population growth, takes the benefits of civilisation for granted as if they were rights of nature, feels no gratitude or responsibility toward the institutions sustaining them, and intervenes in every sphere — politics, art, science, morals — with unqualified opinion backed by violence. Ortega calls the direct imposition of mass desire "hyperdemocracy," and sees fascism and communism alike as specimens of mass rule. His warnings extend to the state itself, whose expansion he feared as the gravest danger to European civilisation, and to the specialist — the "learned ignoramus" (bárbaro especialista) who knows one corner of science and pontificates on all else. The essays close with a call for a united Europe under a shared project as the only remedy for continental decadence.

Influence

Published on the eve of the Spanish Civil War, the book was read worldwide as a prophecy of totalitarianism and remains a staple of political philosophy syllabi, frequently invoked in debates about populism, anti-intellectualism and mass media. Critics have accused Ortega of elitism; defenders note that his target is an attitude — complacent self-satisfaction — rather than any class of people, and that he wrote as a liberal defending democracy against its own pathologies.
