C. V. Raman

Born: 7 November 1888, Tiruchirappalli, British India
Died: 21 November 1970, Bangalore, India
Known for: Raman scattering
Awards: Nobel Prize in Physics (1930), Bharat Ratna (1954)

Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman (7 November 1888 – 21 November 1970) was an Indian physicist known for his work in the field of light scattering. Using a spectrograph that he developed, he and his student K. S. Krishnan discovered that when light traverses a transparent material, some of the deflected light changes its wavelength — an effect now called Raman scattering. For this discovery he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930, becoming the first Asian and the first non-white person to receive a Nobel Prize in the sciences.

Early life and career

Raman was born in Tiruchirappalli (Trichinopoly) in Tamil Nadu, the son of a physics lecturer. He published his first scientific papers while still a student at Presidency College, Madras. After a decade in the Indian civil service in Calcutta, he took the Palit Professorship of Physics at the University of Calcutta in 1917, and carried out the scattering experiments of February 1928 that led to the Raman effect, announced on 28 February — a date now celebrated in India as National Science Day.

Later work

In 1933 Raman became director of the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore, the first Indian to hold the post. He founded the Indian Academy of Sciences (1934) and, after independence, the Raman Research Institute (1948), which he funded in part from his personal resources. He worked on the acoustics of musical instruments, the optics of colloids and minerals, crystal dynamics, and the physiology of vision. His experiments on the colour of the sea during a 1921 voyage had earlier helped overturn the explanation proposed by Lord Rayleigh.

Honours

Raman was knighted in 1929, elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1924, and awarded India's highest civilian honour, the Bharat Ratna, in 1954, as well as the Lenin Peace Prize in 1957. Raman spectroscopy, the analytical technique based on his effect, is now used worldwide in chemistry, materials science, forensics and medicine.
