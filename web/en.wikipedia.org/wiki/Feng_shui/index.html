Feng shui (Chinese: 風水, pronounced "fung shway"), sometimes called Chinese geomancy, is a traditional practice that originated in ancient China and claims to use energy forces to harmonise individuals with their surrounding environment. The term means literally "wind-water".

Principles

Central to feng shui is qi (氣), the flow of energy through a space. Practitioners aim to arrange buildings, rooms and objects so that qi flows favourably. Tools include the luopan, a marked compass, and the bagua, an octagonal diagram dividing space into eight areas corresponding to aspects of life. The five elements (wood, fire, earth, metal, water), yin and yang, and the orientation of a site all figure in analysis.

History

Feng shui developed over more than three millennia, initially for siting tombs and dwellings — Yang Yunsong (9th century) is regarded as a founding master of the Form School. The Compass School, using the luopan and astrological calculations, flourished in Fujian. Imperial China employed feng shui in planning palaces and capitals such as Beijing.

Contemporary practice

Feng shui spread to the West in the late 20th century, where simplified schools (such as Black Sect Tantric Buddhist feng shui) adapted it for interior design. It remains influential in architecture and real estate in East and Southeast Asia. Critics regard feng shui as a pseudoscience, noting that its claims about qi have no empirical support, though its siting principles sometimes coincide with practical considerations of light, wind and water.
