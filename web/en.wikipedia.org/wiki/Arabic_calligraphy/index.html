Arabic calligraphy is the artistic practice of handwriting and calligraphy based on the Arabic alphabet. Known in Arabic as khaṭṭ ("line" or "handwriting"), it is regarded as one of the supreme art forms of Islamic civilisation: because the Qur'an is the word of God, the beautiful writing of Arabic — above all of scripture — carried profound religious prestige, and calligraphy adorned mosques, manuscripts, coins and everyday objects in place of figurative imagery.

History

The earliest formal script, Kufic, angular and monumental, dominated early Qur'ans. In the 10th century the vizier-calligrapher Ibn Muqla systematised cursive writing with a geometric system of proportions based on the rhombic dot, the alif and the circle. Ibn al-Bawwab refined the cursive hands, and Yaqut al-Musta'simi in 13th-century Baghdad brought them to classical perfection. Under the Ottomans, masters such as Sheikh Hamdullah and Hâfiz Osman refined sülüs (Thuluth) and nesih (Naskh); calligraphers trained for years under a master to earn an icazet (licence).

Principal styles

  • Kufic — angular, used for early Qur'ans and architecture.
  • Naskh — clear cursive; the standard book hand and basis of most Arabic typefaces.
  • Thuluth — large, elegant display script for mosque decoration and headings.
  • Diwani — dense, ornate Ottoman chancery script.
  • Maghrebi — rounded North and West African hand, still used in regional Qur'ans.
  • Nastaliq — the flowing Persian-derived script used for Persian, Urdu and Ottoman Turkish poetry.

Inscriptions in monumental calligraphy cover monuments from the Dome of the Rock to the Alhambra. In 2021 UNESCO inscribed Arabic calligraphy on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
