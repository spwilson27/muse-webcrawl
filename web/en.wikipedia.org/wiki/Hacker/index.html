Hacker

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The popular image of the lone hacker at a keyboard.

A hacker is a person skilled in information technology who achieves goals by non-standard means. The term is contested: within computing subcultures it originally denoted an enthusiastic and skillful programmer or hardware tinkerer, while in popular usage it has become associated with a security hacker — someone who seeks and exploits weaknesses in a computer system or network.

Senses of the term

Programmer subculture

The programmer subculture of hackers developed in academic and hobbyist computing from the 1960s, centred on places such as MIT's Tech Model Railroad Club and AI Lab, and later the homebrew computer scene. In this usage, a "hack" is a clever, playful, or exploratory solution, and the subculture's values — captured in documents such as the Jargon File — celebrate technical virtuosity, openness, and hands-on work. Adherents of this sense object to the media's use of "hacker" for criminals, preferring the term "cracker".

Security hackers

Security hackers are commonly classified by "hat" colours borrowed from Western films:

  • White hat hackers break security for non-malicious reasons, such as testing systems they own or are employed to protect, and include professional penetration testers.
  • Black hat hackers violate computer security for personal gain or malice.
  • Grey hat hackers fall between the two, sometimes violating laws or ethical standards without malicious intent, for example by disclosing vulnerabilities publicly.

Other figures include hacktivists, who use hacking for political ends, and script kiddies, unskilled users of tools written by others.

Other uses

"Life hack" has extended the word to mean any clever shortcut. A "hardware hacker" modifies electronics and other physical devices; related cultures include phone phreaking in the 1970s and the modern maker movement. In golf and tennis, "hacker" is a pejorative for an unskilled player.

Hacker culture and ethics

The so-called hacker ethic, articulated by Steven Levy in Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution (1984), asserts that access to computers should be unlimited, information should be free, authority should be mistrusted in favour of decentralization, and hackers should be judged by their work alone. This ethos underlies the free software and open-source movements.
