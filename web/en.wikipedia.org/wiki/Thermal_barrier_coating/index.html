Thermal barrier coating

FunctionInsulate hot-section components from high gas temperatures
Typical top coatYttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ)
Bond coatMCrAlY or platinum aluminide
ApplicationsGas turbines, jet engines, diesel engines, exhaust systems

A thermal barrier coating (TBC) is a multilayer materials system applied to metallic surfaces exposed to very hot gases, especially turbine blades, vanes, combustor liners and exhaust components. Its purpose is to sustain a temperature drop across a thin ceramic layer so the underlying superalloy can operate below its creep and oxidation limits even when gas temperatures exceed the metal’s melting range with the help of internal cooling.

Architecture

A conventional TBC has four elements: the load-bearing superalloy substrate; a metallic bond coat rich in aluminium, commonly MCrAlY (where M is nickel, cobalt or both) or a diffusion aluminide; a thermally grown oxide, mainly alumina, that forms at the bond-coat surface; and a ceramic top coat. The usual top coat is zirconia stabilized with about 7–8 wt% yttria (7YSZ), chosen for low thermal conductivity, relatively high thermal expansion for a ceramic and toughness produced by its metastable tetragonal phase.

Deposition methods

Air plasma spraying produces a lamellar microstructure of flattened splats with pores and microcracks that reduce conductivity and provide strain tolerance. Electron-beam physical vapour deposition (EB-PVD) creates columnar grains separated by gaps; the columns can flex as the substrate expands, giving excellent resistance to thermal cycling on rotating blades. Suspension and solution precursor plasma spraying, high-velocity oxy-fuel methods for bond coats, and newer vapour and slurry routes aim to control these architectures at lower cost.

How failure occurs

TBC life is governed less by the ceramic alone than by the evolving interface. As the thermally grown oxide thickens, stresses accumulate from growth, thermal-expansion mismatch and bond-coat phase changes. Rumpling of the bond coat, oxidation, sintering of the top coat at high temperature, attack by molten calcium-magnesium-aluminosilicate deposits (CMAS) from ingested sand and ash, and erosion can all lead to spallation. Once coating flakes off, the exposed metal overheats rapidly.

Designers therefore treat the coating as a system. Bond-coat chemistry, surface roughness, oxide growth rate, top-coat porosity and component cooling must be optimized together. Inspection and lifing models attempt to predict when local damage becomes unacceptable.

Research directions

Higher turbine inlet temperatures promise better fuel efficiency and lower emissions, but they push YSZ toward phase instability and sintering above roughly 1200 °C. Candidate ceramics include gadolinium zirconate and other rare-earth zirconates, tantalates and perovskite-related compositions with lower conductivity or better CMAS resistance. Many are less tough than YSZ, so multilayer and compositionally graded designs are explored. Environmental barrier coatings for silicon-carbide composites share ideas with TBCs but face water-vapour recession as a central problem.

Applications beyond turbines

Thermal barrier concepts appear in piston crowns and exhaust manifolds, rocket engines, industrial furnaces and re-entry or hypersonic structures. In each case the same bargain applies: a brittle, insulating layer can protect a stronger substrate only if interfaces, thermal cycling and chemical attack are managed for the whole service life.
