Creosote bush

The creosote bush (Larrea tridentata), called chaparral as a medicinal plant, is an evergreen flowering shrub of the family Zygophyllaceae. It is one of the most characteristic and dominant plants of the deserts of southwestern North America, including the Mojave, Sonoran, and Chihuahuan deserts.

Description

The plant grows 1–3 m tall, with small resinous leaves divided into two leaflets and bright yellow five-petalled flowers. The resin coating the leaves gives off a distinctive odour after rain, often described as the smell of desert rain, and reduces water loss.

Adaptations and longevity

Creosote bush is exceptionally drought-tolerant and long-lived. Clonal rings formed by asexual reproduction can persist for millennia; one ring in the Mojave Desert, known as "King Clone", is estimated to be around 11,700 years old, making it among the oldest living organisms on Earth.

Uses

Native peoples used chaparral preparations for numerous ailments, and the plant's resin contains nordihydroguaiaretic acid (NDGA), a powerful antioxidant. Commercial herbal use has been associated with liver toxicity concerns.
