Cádiz

Cádiz is a city and port in southwestern Spain, the capital of the Province of Cádiz, one of eight provinces of the autonomous community of Andalusia. Founded by the Phoenicians around 1100 BC as Gadir, it is often considered the oldest continuously inhabited city in Western Europe. Its old town occupies a narrow peninsula surrounded by the Atlantic Ocean and the Bay of Cádiz.

History

Phoenician Gadir became a major trading post for metals and other goods. It passed to Carthage, then to Rome (as Gades), where it prospered as a port famous for its fish sauces and its wealthy citizens such as the Balbi. After Visigothic and Moorish periods — when it was known as Qādis — it was conquered by Alfonso X of Castile in 1262.

Cádiz rose again in the Age of Exploration: Columbus sailed from nearby ports, and in the 18th century the city gained the monopoly of trade with Spanish America (1717), entering its golden age of merchant wealth, visible in its watchtowers and the neoclassical cathedral. In 1810–1812, besieged by Napoleon's forces, Cádiz hosted the Cortes of Cádiz, which promulgated the Spanish Constitution of 1812, the first in Spain.

Geography and landmarks

The old city is characterised by narrow streets, plazas such as San Juan de Dios and the Plaza de la Catedral, the Tavira Tower (the city's official watchtower, with a camera obscura), the Roman theatre, and the beaches of La Caleta and Victoria. The modern city extends along the isthmus toward San Fernando, connected to the mainland by the La Pepa Bridge (opened 2015).

Culture

Cádiz is famous for its Carnival, one of Spain's most celebrated, noted for satirical musical groups (chirigotas and comparsas). The city's inhabitants, gaditanos, are renowned in Spain for their wit.
