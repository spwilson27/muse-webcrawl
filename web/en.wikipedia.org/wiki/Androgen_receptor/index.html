The androgen receptor (AR), also known as NR3C4, is a type of nuclear receptor that is activated by binding any of the androgenic hormones, including testosterone and dihydrotestosterone, in the cytoplasm and then translocating into the nucleus. The androgen receptor is most closely related to the progesterone receptor, and progestins in higher dosages can block it.

Function

Upon binding an androgen, the receptor changes conformation, dissociates from heat-shock proteins, dimerises and enters the nucleus, where it binds to androgen response elements in DNA and regulates the transcription of target genes. This signalling drives the development and maintenance of male sexual characteristics, including the differentiation of the Wolffian ducts and external genitalia in the embryo, pubertal changes, spermatogenesis, prostate growth and patterns of muscle and hair growth.

Gene and structure

The AR gene is located on the long arm of the X chromosome. The receptor protein has the typical nuclear-receptor modular structure: an N-terminal transactivation domain (containing a variable polyglutamine tract encoded by a CAG repeat), a DNA-binding domain with two zinc fingers, a hinge region and a C-terminal ligand-binding domain.

Clinical significance

Loss-of-function mutations cause androgen insensitivity syndrome, in which XY individuals develop female or ambiguous external characteristics despite producing testosterone. Expansion of the CAG repeat beyond about 38 repeats causes spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (Kennedy's disease), a neuromuscular disorder. Because prostate cancer growth is driven by androgen signalling, the receptor is the main target of prostate cancer therapy: androgen deprivation, antiandrogens such as bicalutamide and enzalutamide, and androgen-synthesis inhibitors such as abiraterone.
