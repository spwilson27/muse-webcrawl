Electrowetting

Electrowetting is the modification of the wetting properties of a surface (which is typically hydrophobic) with an applied electric field. When a voltage is applied between a conductive droplet and an insulated electrode beneath it, the contact angle of the droplet decreases—it appears to wet the surface more—because the electrostatic energy stored in the system favours spreading. The effect is described by the Young–Lippmann equation, which predicts that the cosine of the contact angle increases with the square of the applied voltage.

History

The phenomenon was first described systematically by Gabriel Lippmann in 1875 in his work on electrocapillarity, and the term "electrowetting" was coined by Bruno Berge in 1993, when practical systems using thin insulating layers (electrowetting on dielectric, EWOD) made the effect robust and reversible.

Applications

Electrowetting drives digital microfluidics: arrays of electrodes shuttle, merge, split, and mix individual droplets for lab-on-a-chip chemical and biological assays. It also underlies variable-focus liquid lenses, in which two immiscible fluids change curvature with voltage (commercialized by Varioptic), and reflective electrowetting displays, where a coloured oil film is electrically withdrawn to expose a white substrate—a technology developed by Liquavista for low-power, video-capable e-paper.
