Palaeography

Palaeography (US spelling paleography; from Greek παλαιός, palaiós, "old", and γράφειν, gráphein, "to write") is the study of historic writing systems and the deciphering and dating of historical manuscripts. It involves analysing the letter-forms, abbreviations, punctuation and layout of handwritten texts — typically on papyrus, parchment and paper — in order to read them, to establish when and where they were produced, and to authenticate them.

History of the discipline

The discipline is usually traced to the Benedictine scholar Jean Mabillon, whose De re diplomatica (1681) established systematic criteria for judging the authenticity of medieval charters by their scripts. Bernard de Montfaucon's Palaeographia Graeca (1708) did the same for Greek manuscripts and gave the field its name. In the 19th and 20th centuries scholars such as Ludwig Traube developed detailed typologies of Latin scripts.

Major Latin scripts

Palaeographers trace the evolution of Latin handwriting from Roman capital and cursive hands through uncial and half-uncial, the regional "national hands" of the early Middle Ages (such as Insular and Beneventan script), the clear, standardised Carolingian minuscule promoted under Charlemagne, the compressed Gothic scripts (textualis and cursiva) of the later Middle Ages, and the humanistic hand of the Renaissance from which modern italic and roman type derive.

Methods

By comparing letter-forms, ligatures and abbreviation systems with dated examples, palaeographers can often date an undated manuscript to within a few decades and localise its scriptorium. The field works closely with codicology (the study of the physical book) and is essential to editors of medieval texts and to archivists reading early modern documents.
