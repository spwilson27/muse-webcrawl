A centaur (Ancient Greek: κένταυρος, kentauros) is a creature from Greek mythology with the upper body of a human and the lower body and legs of a horse. Centaurs were said to inhabit the mountains of Thessaly and were commonly depicted as wild, drunken, and lustful, embodying untamed nature — with the notable exception of the wise and kindly Chiron, tutor of heroes.

Origins

According to myth, the centaurs were born of Ixion, king of the Lapiths, and Nephele, a cloud fashioned in the likeness of Hera. Their offspring Centaurus mated with the mares of Mount Pelion, producing the centaur race. The image may have originated in the impression made on non-riding cultures by the first horsemen of the Thessalian plains; centaurs appear in Mycenaean-era art, including a terracotta figure from Ugarit and the famous "Centaur" from Lefkandi.

Myths

The best-known story is the Centauromachy: at the wedding of Pirithous, king of the Lapiths, the centaurs, inflamed by wine, attempted to carry off the bride Hippodamia and the other women, provoking a battle won by the Lapiths with the help of Theseus. The battle of Lapiths and centaurs became a favourite subject of Greek art — it decorates the west pediment at Olympia and the Parthenon metopes — symbolising civilisation's victory over barbarism.

Other centaurs include Pholus, who entertained Heracles; Nessus, whose poisoned blood caused Heracles' death; and Chiron, the immortal healer and teacher of Achilles, Asclepius, and Jason, who after death was placed among the stars as the constellation Centaurus (in some versions Sagittarius). Female centaurs, centaurides, appear in later art and literature.

Legacy

Centaurs recur in Roman literature, medieval bestiaries, Dante's Inferno, and modern fantasy, and gave their name to the astronomical centaurs — small bodies orbiting between Jupiter and Neptune.
