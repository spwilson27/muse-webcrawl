Paul Marlor Sweezy (April 10, 1910 – February 27, 2004) was an American Marxist economist, journalist, and the founder (with Leo Huberman) of the influential independent socialist magazine Monthly Review. He is remembered as one of the leading Marxian economists of the 20th century.

Early life and education

Sweezy was born in New York City, the youngest of three sons of Everett B. Sweezy, a vice-president of the First National Bank of New York. He attended Phillips Exeter Academy and Harvard University, graduating in 1931, and earned his Ph.D. from Harvard in 1937. A year of study at the London School of Economics exposed him to Marxist ideas, which shaped the rest of his career.

Academic career

At Harvard, Sweezy worked as Joseph Schumpeter's teaching assistant and produced one of the most cited early articles in modern economics, introducing the "kinked demand curve" explanation of price rigidity in oligopoly (1939). During the Second World War he served in the Office of Strategic Services. His book The Theory of Capitalist Development (1942) became the standard English-language exposition of Marxist economic theory.

Sweezy's academic career stalled amid the political climate of the McCarthy era; he left Harvard in 1946. In 1954 he was subpoenaed by the New Hampshire attorney general over a lecture at the University of New Hampshire; his refusal to answer questions about his political associations led to the landmark U.S. Supreme Court case Sweezy v. New Hampshire (1957), which upheld principles of academic freedom.

Monthly Review and Monopoly Capital

In 1949 Sweezy and Leo Huberman founded Monthly Review, which became the longest continuously published socialist magazine in the United States. With Paul Baran he wrote Monopoly Capital (1966), an influential analysis of how large corporations generate economic surplus and the ways it is absorbed through military spending, advertising, and finance.

Death and legacy

Sweezy died in 2004 at the age of 93. The economist Paul Samuelson described him as one of the most brilliant students Harvard produced, and his work remains central to Marxian political economy.
