Military deception

Military deception
DefinitionActions executed to deliberately mislead adversary decision-makers

Military deception (abbreviated MILDEC in US doctrine) is the deliberate attempt to mislead an adversary's decision-makers about one's own capabilities, intentions and actions, so as to induce the enemy to act in ways favourable to one's own mission. It is distinguished from simple concealment: deception actively feeds the enemy a false version of reality, exploiting the fact that commanders act on what they believe, not on what is true.

Methods

Common techniques include feints and demonstrations that draw attention to a false point of attack; decoys and dummy equipment, from inflatable tanks to mock airfields; false radio traffic and simulated unit deployments; camouflage and concealment; double agents and leaked "secret" documents; and displays of forces that do not exist. Deception planners aim to reinforce what the adversary already expects to see — the principle that it is far easier to make the enemy believe a plausible story than an implausible one.

Historical examples

Deception is as old as warfare: Sun Tzu taught that "all warfare is based on deception". Celebrated modern cases include the Second World War's Operation Mincemeat (1943), which planted false plans on a corpse to conceal the invasion of Sicily, and Operation Fortitude (1944), which — with a fictitious army group in Scotland and Kent, controlled double agents and fake radio traffic — convinced Germany that the main invasion of France would come at the Pas-de-Calais rather than Normandy. In 1991, coalition forces in the Gulf War used feints and an amphibious threat to pin Iraqi divisions while the real attack swept through the desert. Soviet and Russian doctrine developed the related concept of maskirovka, encompassing concealment, imitation and disinformation.
