Low-power electronics

Low-power electronics are electronics, such as notebook processors, that have been designed to use less electrical power than usual, often at some expense. In the case of notebook processors, this expense is processing power; notebook processors usually consume less power than their desktop counterparts, at the expense of lower processing power.

History

The earliest computers were room-sized machines powered by vacuum tubes that consumed enormous amounts of electricity. The invention of the transistor and later the integrated circuit steadily reduced power consumption. CMOS (complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor) technology, which became dominant in the 1980s and 1990s, consumes significant power mainly when transistors switch states, making it well suited to low-power design.

Motivations

Reducing power consumption extends battery life in portable devices, lowers heat dissipation (which reduces cooling requirements and increases reliability), and cuts energy costs and environmental impact in large installations such as data centres.

Techniques

Common techniques include dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS), clock gating, power gating, using multiple voltage domains, and putting idle circuits into sleep states. At the circuit level, designers reduce supply voltage, since dynamic power scales with the square of the voltage. Software approaches include energy-aware scheduling and compiler optimisations. Modern systems-on-chip integrate dedicated power-management units that coordinate these techniques.

Applications

Low-power design is essential in mobile phones, laptops, wearable devices, wireless sensor networks, implantable medical devices, and Internet of Things nodes, many of which must operate for years on a small battery or on harvested energy.
