Chief Gall (Lakota: Phizí, c. 1840 – 5 December 1894) was a battle leader of the Hunkpapa Lakota in the Native American resistance against the United States during the mid-19th century. He was one of the commanders at the Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876, and in his later years became a prominent figure on the Standing Rock Indian Reservation.

His name derives from an incident in his youth in which, starving, he ate the gall bladder of an animal killed by a neighbor. Orphaned early, Gall was raised in the circle of Sitting Bull, whose close associate and adopted brother he became.

Warfare on the Plains

Gall fought in Red Cloud's War and was wounded in engagements with U.S. troops in the 1860s. During the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, he was a principal war chief under Sitting Bull. At the Battle of the Little Bighorn on 25 June 1876, Gall led attacks against Major Marcus Reno's battalion and later against Captain Frederick Benteen, helping to pin down the surviving cavalry on the bluffs while other warriors overwhelmed George Armstrong Custer's command.

Two of Gall's wives and several of his children were killed in the opening attack on the village, a loss that reportedly hardened his fighting that day.

Later life

After the war Gall fled to Canada with Sitting Bull, but in 1881, facing starvation, he broke with Sitting Bull and surrendered at Fort Buford with several hundred followers. On the Standing Rock Reservation he worked as a farmer, served as a judge on the Court of Indian Offenses, and advocated for education, taking a more conciliatory position toward the U.S. government than Sitting Bull. He died in 1894 and was buried at Wakpala, South Dakota.
