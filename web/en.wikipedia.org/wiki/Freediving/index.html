Freediving

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freediving is underwater diving that relies on holding one's breath until resurfacing, rather than on breathing apparatus such as scuba gear. Practised for millennia by pearl divers, sponge divers, and spearfishers — including Japan's ama and Korea's haenyeo — freediving is today also a recreational activity and a competitive sport governed chiefly by AIDA International and CMAS, with disciplines for depth, distance, and static breath-holding.

Physiology

Freedivers exploit the mammalian dive reflex: on immersion, especially of the face in cool water, the heart rate slows (bradycardia), blood vessels in the limbs constrict, and blood shifts toward the vital organs, conserving oxygen. Trained divers can hold their breath for many minutes — the static apnea world record exceeds eleven minutes without prior oxygen breathing — while depth divers descend beyond 100 metres on a single breath, enduring lung compression to a fraction of surface volume. The urge to breathe is driven mainly by rising carbon dioxide rather than low oxygen, and training focuses on CO₂ tolerance, relaxation, and efficient movement.

Disciplines

Depth disciplines include constant weight (CWT, swimming down and up under one's own power, often with a monofin), free immersion (FIM, pulling along a rope), variable weight, and no-limits, in which a weighted sled carries the diver down and an inflatable device brings them up; the no-limits record, set by Herbert Nitsch in 2007, stands at 214 metres. Pool disciplines include static apnea (timed breath-hold) and dynamic apnea (horizontal distance with or without fins).

Safety

The sport's chief danger is hypoxic blackout, especially shallow-water blackout on ascent, which can occur without warning; hyperventilation before a dive increases the risk by lowering CO₂ and delaying the urge to breathe. The cardinal rule of freediving safety is to dive with a trained buddy who directly supervises each dive, and never to freedive alone.
