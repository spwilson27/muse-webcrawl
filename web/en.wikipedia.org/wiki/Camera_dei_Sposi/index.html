The Camera dei Sposi ("bridal chamber"), also known as the Camera picta ("painted chamber"), is a room in the Palazzo Ducale of Mantua, Italy, frescoed by the Renaissance artist Andrea Mantegna between about 1465 and 1474 for Ludovico III Gonzaga, Marquis of Mantua. It is celebrated as one of the most innovative fresco cycles of the Italian Renaissance, creating the illusion that the room's walls dissolve into open loggias and landscapes.

The frescoes

The Court Scene (north wall)

The north wall shows Ludovico Gonzaga enthroned among his family and courtiers, receiving a letter. It is among the most famous group portraits of the Renaissance, depicting the marquis's wife Barbara of Brandenburg, their children, court officials, dwarfs, and even the family dog Rubino, tucked under the chair.

The Meeting Scene (west wall)

The west wall depicts Ludovico meeting his son, Cardinal Francesco Gonzaga, returning from Rome in 1472, set against an idealised landscape with a classical city. The scene celebrates the family's rise — Francesco's cardinalate was the Gonzaga's greatest dynastic triumph.

The ceiling

The vaulted ceiling culminates in a painted oculus ringed by a balustrade, seen dramatically from below (di sotto in sù), with putti, courtiers, and a peacock peering down at the viewer, and a precariously balanced planter teasing the spectator. It is the first true quadratura ceiling of the Renaissance and a landmark of illusionistic painting, later echoed by Correggio and the Baroque ceiling painters.

Significance

The cycle is a supreme example of art in the service of dynastic representation: Mantegna, court painter to the Gonzaga for four decades, combined portraiture, landscape, antiquarian detail, and spatial illusion to glorify his patrons. The room — in the Castello di San Giorgio tower of the ducal palace — is now the highlight of the Palazzo Ducale museum.
