Hamzanama

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hamzanama (Persian/Urdu: حمزہ نامہ, Hamza Nama), or Dastan-e Amir Hamza, is a great epic of the fantastic adventures of Amir Hamza, the uncle of the Prophet Muhammad. The most famous version is the huge illustrated manuscript commissioned by the Mughal emperor Akbar, produced between about 1562 and 1577, which is considered one of the masterpieces of early Mughal painting.

The Akbar manuscript

Akbar, who was fond of the romance and had it recited to him, commissioned an unprecedentedly ambitious illustrated version. The project is said to have employed over a hundred artists, first under the Persian master Mir Sayyid Ali and then under Abd al-Samad, both of whom had helped found the Mughal court atelier. Production took roughly fifteen years.

The completed work comprised some 1,400 large folios of painted cloth, each about 70 by 55 centimetres, with the text written on the reverse so that the pictures could be held up while a storyteller read aloud. The paintings combine Persian, Indian and later European influences in bold, dramatic compositions of battles, monsters, sorcerers and courtly scenes.

Surviving folios

Only about 140 to 200 folios of the Akbar manuscript survive today, scattered among museums worldwide. The largest holdings are in the Museum of Applied Arts (MAK) in Vienna and the Victoria and Albert Museum in London, with others in the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Brooklyn Museum in New York. A major exhibition of the surviving paintings was held at the Sackler Gallery in Washington, D.C. in 2002.

The story

The Hamzanama recounts, in the oral dastan tradition, the heroic deeds of Hamza as he travels the world fighting kings, demons (divs), sorcerers and dragons in defence of the faith and of his beloved, the princess Mehr Nigar. The tale circulated for centuries across the Islamic world in Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Malay and other languages.
