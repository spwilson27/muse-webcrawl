Roman legion

A Roman legion (Latin: legio, "levy") was the largest formal unit of the Roman army, comprising roughly 5,000–5,200 heavy infantry (legionaries) organized into ten cohorts, supported by a small cavalry detachment and specialists such as engineers, surgeons and artillery crews. The legions formed the backbone of Rome's military power from the early Republic through the Empire.

Organization

The basic subunit was the century of 80 men commanded by a centurion; six centuries made a cohort, and ten cohorts a legion, the first cohort being double-strength. Overall command rested with a legate (legatus legionis), assisted by six tribunes and the camp prefect. Each legion had a number, a name, and a sacred golden eagle standard whose loss was a profound disgrace.

Equipment and tactics

The classical legionary carried a pilum (heavy javelin), a gladius short sword, a large curved shield (scutum), and wore mail or segmented plate armour. Legionaries were also engineers: they built a fortified camp every night on the march, plus roads, bridges and siege works. The reforms of Gaius Marius (c. 107 BC) professionalized the legions, opening service to the propertyless and standardizing equipment at state expense.

Service

Under Augustus soldiers served 20–25 years in return for pay, a discharge bonus of land or cash, and, for auxiliary counterparts, citizenship. At any time the empire maintained about 28–33 legions stationed along the frontiers.
