Homestead strike

The Homestead strike, also known as the Homestead steel strike or Homestead massacre, was an industrial lockout and strike that began on July 1, 1892, culminating in a violent battle between strikers and private security agents on July 6, 1892. It was one of the most serious disputes in U.S. labor history, centered on the Carnegie Steel Company's Homestead Steel Works in the Pittsburgh-area town of Homestead, Pennsylvania.

Background

The Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers (AA) was one of the strongest unions in the country, but Carnegie Steel's manager, Henry Clay Frick, was determined to break it. When the existing contract approached its June 30, 1892 expiration, Frick proposed wage cuts for many workers and, with Andrew Carnegie's approval from abroad, resolved to lock the union out rather than renew the agreement. Frick fortified the plant with barbed wire and sniper towers, earning it the nickname "Fort Frick," and hired 300 agents from the Pinkerton National Detective Agency as guards.

The battle of July 6

In the early hours of July 6, the Pinkertons attempted to land at the mill by barge on the Monongahela River. Strikers and townspeople discovered the plan and met them at the shore. A gun battle raged for roughly twelve hours; workers even used a cannon and attempted to set the barges afire with an oil-soaked raft and a flaming train of oil cars. By evening the Pinkertons surrendered and were marched through a gauntlet of enraged residents. At least seven strikers and three Pinkerton agents were killed, and many more were wounded.

Aftermath

Pennsylvania's governor ordered the state militia—some 8,500 men—to occupy the town. The plant reopened with strikebreakers, and on July 23 anarchist Alexander Berkman attempted to assassinate Frick, an act that badly damaged public sympathy for the strikers. The strike collapsed by November 20, 1892, and the AA was effectively destroyed at Homestead; Carnegie Steel remained non-union for decades. The defeat marked a turning point: the steel industry would not be successfully organized until the 1930s.

Not to be confused with the Homestead Acts, the federal land-distribution laws.
