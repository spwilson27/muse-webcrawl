Spandrel (biology)

In evolutionary biology, a spandrel is a phenotypic characteristic that is a byproduct of the evolution of some other characteristic, rather than a direct product of adaptive selection. The term was coined by Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin in their influential 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm".

The architectural metaphor

Spandrels are the roughly triangular spaces between the top of an arch and the ceiling or dome it supports, as in St Mark's Basilica in Venice. They are a necessary architectural byproduct of building a dome on rounded arches; although often beautifully decorated, they were not the reason the structure was designed as it was. Gould and Lewontin argued analogously that some biological traits exist not because they were selected for, but as side consequences of other features.

Critique of adaptationism

The paper criticized the "adaptationist programme" — the tendency to explain every trait as an adaptation shaped by natural selection — and urged biologists to consider alternative explanations such as developmental constraints, genetic drift, and byproducts. Traits that begin as spandrels may later be co-opted for a useful function, a process Gould and Elisabeth Vrba termed exaptation.

Examples

Frequently discussed candidate spandrels include the redness of blood, the human chin, and certain psychological traits whose adaptive status is debated.
