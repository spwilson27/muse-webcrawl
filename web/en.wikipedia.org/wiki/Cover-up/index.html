Cover-up

A cover-up is an attempt, whether successful or not, to conceal evidence of wrongdoing, error, incompetence or other embarrassing information. The expression dates to the 1920s and is usually applied to actions by people in positions of authority — governments, corporations and institutions — who suppress information, mislead investigators and the press, or falsely deny involvement.

Characteristics

When a scandal breaks, officials may attempt to minimise damage by withholding documents, issuing misleading statements, or sacrificing low-level figures. Cover-ups often become more damaging than the original offence: the archetypal case is the Watergate scandal, in which the attempt by the Nixon administration to conceal its connection to a burglary led to obstruction-of-justice charges and the resignation of President Richard Nixon in 1974, even though the underlying break-in was comparatively minor.

Examples

Well-documented cover-ups include the concealment by tobacco companies of evidence that smoking causes cancer, the Catholic Church's reassignment of priests accused of abuse, the delayed disclosure of defects in products by manufacturers, and official secrecy following disasters such as Chernobyl. Whistleblowers and investigative journalism are frequent mechanisms by which cover-ups are exposed.
