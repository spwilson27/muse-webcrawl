Reaction intermediate

Reaction intermediates occupy local energy minima along a reaction pathway.

A reaction intermediate, or simply intermediate, is a molecular entity that is formed from the reactants (or preceding intermediates) and reacts further to give the directly observed products of a chemical reaction. Most chemical reactions are stepwise — that is, they take more than one elementary step to complete — and an intermediate is any transient species that appears in the mechanism but not in the overall balanced equation.

Intermediates are typically highly reactive and short-lived, in contrast to transition states, which represent energy maxima and cannot be isolated. Common types include free radicals, carbocations, carbanions, carbenes, nitrenes, and aryne (benzyne) species. In catalytic cycles, catalyst-bound intermediates are central to the mechanism.

Detection

Evidence for intermediates comes from spectroscopy (including low-temperature matrix isolation and flash photolysis), trapping experiments that divert the intermediate to a stable product, kinetic studies, and isotopic labeling. Identifying intermediates is essential to establishing reaction mechanisms and to controlling selectivity in synthesis.
