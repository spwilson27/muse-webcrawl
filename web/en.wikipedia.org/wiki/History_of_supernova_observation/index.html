History of supernova observation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of supernova observation records the known instances in which astronomers — professional, amateur, and pre-telescopic observers — witnessed a supernova, a stellar explosion so luminous that it can briefly outshine an entire galaxy. The earliest recorded supernova was SN 185, noted by Chinese astronomers in AD 185. The brightest recorded supernova in human history was SN 1006, which appeared in AD 1006 and was described in records from China, Japan, the Islamic world, and Europe.

Pre-telescopic supernovae

Before the invention of the telescope, supernovae were recorded as "guest stars" (Chinese: kèxīng) that appeared suddenly in the sky and faded over weeks or months. Notable events include SN 393, SN 1006, SN 1054 — whose remnant is the Crab Nebula — SN 1181, SN 1572 (observed by Tycho Brahe, whose measurements helped demolish the Aristotelian doctrine of immutable heavens), and SN 1604 (Kepler's Supernova), the last supernova observed in the Milky Way.

Telescopic era

After 1604, no supernova in the Milky Way has been observed directly, though remnants such as Cassiopeia A indicate explosions that went unnoticed around 1680. Observations shifted to other galaxies: S Andromedae (SN 1885A) in the Andromeda Galaxy was the first extragalactic supernova recorded. In the 20th century, systematic photographic surveys raised the discovery rate dramatically; Fritz Zwicky and Walter Baade coined the term "super-nova" in the 1930s.

Modern surveys

Today, automated sky surveys such as the Zwicky Transient Facility and the All Sky Automated Survey for SuperNovae (ASAS-SN) discover thousands of supernovae per year. SN 1987A, in the Large Magellanic Cloud, was the closest observed supernova of the modern era and the first from which neutrinos were detected. The upcoming Vera C. Rubin Observatory is expected to increase discovery rates by an order of magnitude.
