Galois theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, Galois theory, originally introduced by Évariste Galois, is a branch of abstract algebra that provides a connection between field theory and group theory. This connection, the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, allows certain problems in field theory to be reduced to group theory, which in some sense is simpler and better understood.

Galois theory originated in the study of symmetric functions and the question of when a polynomial equation can be solved by radicals — by expressions involving only the coefficients, arithmetic operations and roots. Galois showed that this is possible exactly when a certain group attached to the polynomial, its Galois group, is a solvable group.

Fundamental theorem

For a finite Galois extension of fields E/F, the fundamental theorem establishes a one-to-one, inclusion-reversing correspondence between the intermediate fields of the extension and the subgroups of the Galois group Gal(E/F). Subgroups of index n correspond to subfields of degree n over F, and normal subgroups correspond precisely to intermediate fields that are themselves Galois over F, with quotient groups giving the Galois groups of those subfields.

Applications

Galois theory settles several classical problems. It proves that there is no general formula by radicals for polynomial equations of degree five or higher (the Abel–Ruffini theorem), explains why the ancient Greek problems of trisecting an angle and doubling the cube are impossible with straightedge and compass, and underlies modern approaches to algebraic number theory and the inverse Galois problem. The terminology of groups — soluble, simple, normal — largely originates in Galois's writings, published posthumously in 1846, fourteen years after his death in a duel at the age of twenty.
