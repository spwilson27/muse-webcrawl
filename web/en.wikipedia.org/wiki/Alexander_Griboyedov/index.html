Alexander Sergeyevich Griboyedov (15 January 1795 – 11 February 1829) was a Russian diplomat, playwright, poet and composer. He is best known for his verse comedy Woe from Wit (1825), one of the most celebrated works of Russian literature, and for his death at the hands of a mob that stormed the Russian embassy in Tehran in 1829.

Early life

Griboyedov was born in Moscow into an old noble family. He studied at Moscow University, where he took degrees in law and philology and showed a striking gift for languages, mastering French, English, German, Italian, Greek, Latin, and later Persian, Arabic and Turkish. He was also an accomplished pianist and composer; two of his waltzes survive.

Military and civil service

During the Napoleonic invasion of 1812 he volunteered for a hussar regiment but saw little action. In 1817 he entered the foreign service and was posted to Persia, then to Tiflis (Tbilisi) in Georgia, where he served under General Aleksey Yermolov. In 1828 he helped negotiate the Treaty of Turkmenchay, which ended the Russo-Persian War (1826–1828) on terms highly favourable to Russia, and he personally delivered the treaty to St Petersburg.

Woe from Wit

Woe from Wit (Gore ot uma), written in 1823–1824, is a satirical verse comedy about the young idealist Chatsky, who returns to Moscow society and is driven to feign madness by its gossip, servility and empty formality. Barred from full publication by the censors, the play circulated in manuscript copies; its aphorisms passed into everyday Russian speech. Griboyedov was briefly arrested and questioned in connection with the Decembrist revolt of 1825, many of whose participants were his friends, but he was exonerated.

Death

Appointed minister plenipotentiary to Persia, Griboyedov arrived in Tehran in 1828 to enforce the treaty's terms, including the repatriation of captives. When two Armenian women from the harem of a Persian notable and an Armenian eunuch took refuge in the Russian mission, an enraged crowd, incited by religious leaders, stormed the embassy on 11 February 1829. Griboyedov and nearly all his staff were killed. His mutilated body was identified, according to tradition, by an old duelling scar on his hand. Persia sent a formal apology mission to St Petersburg, bearing gifts including the Shah Diamond. Griboyedov was buried in Tiflis, beside the grave that his young widow, Nina Chavchavadze, marked with the inscription: "Your mind and your deeds are immortal in the memory of Russians, but why did my love outlive you?"

Legacy

Griboyedov's single great play secured him a permanent place in Russian culture; Pushkin quoted it extensively, and lines from Woe from Wit remain common Russian idioms. The Griboyedov Canal in St Petersburg and a monument in Moscow commemorate him.
