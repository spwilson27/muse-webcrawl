Bioethics

Bioethics is the study of the ethical issues emerging from advances in biology, medicine and technologies. It proposes the discussion about moral discernment in society and it is often related to medical policy and practice, but also to broader questions as environment, well-being and public health. Bioethics is concerned with the ethical questions that arise in the relationships among life sciences, biotechnology, medicine, politics, law, theology and philosophy. It includes the study of values relating to primary care, other branches of medicine ("the ethics of the ordinary"), ethical education in science, life and environmental ethics.

History

The term bioethics was coined in 1926 by Fritz Jahr, who "anticipated many of the arguments and discussions now current in biological research involving animals" in an article about the "bioethical imperative," as he called it, regarding scientific research. The term was independently reintroduced in 1970 by the American biochemist and oncologist Van Rensselaer Potter, who used it to describe a proposed "science of survival" that would bridge biology and human values. Potter's usage was broader and more ecological than the clinical sense that later became dominant.

In the 1970s, institutions such as the Hastings Center (founded 1969) and the Kennedy Institute of Ethics at Georgetown University helped establish bioethics as an academic discipline. The publication of the Belmont Report in 1979, following scandal over research abuses such as the Tuskegee syphilis study, laid out principles for the protection of human research subjects in the United States.

Principlism

One influential approach, developed by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress in their book Principles of Biomedical Ethics (first published 1979), identifies four prima facie principles:

  • Autonomy — respecting the decision-making capacities of autonomous persons;
  • Beneficence — providing benefits and balancing benefits against risks;
  • Non-maleficence — avoiding the causation of harm;
  • Justice — distributing benefits, risks and costs fairly.

Other values, such as dignity, sanctity of life, and solidarity, also figure in bioethical debate depending on cultural and religious traditions.

Areas of concern

Medical ethics

Bioethics overlaps substantially with medical ethics, addressing issues such as informed consent, confidentiality, euthanasia and assisted dying, abortion, organ transplantation, allocation of scarce medical resources, and end-of-life care.

Research ethics

The ethics of research involving human subjects and animals is regulated in many countries by institutional review boards (IRBs) and ethics committees, operating under frameworks such as the Declaration of Helsinki and the Nuremberg Code.

Genetics and biotechnology

Advances in genetics — including genetic testing, gene therapy, cloning, stem cell research, and genome editing technologies such as CRISPR — raise questions about genetic privacy, enhancement, designer babies, and the moral status of embryos.

Environmental and public health ethics

Following Potter's original vision, some bioethicists address environmental ethics, climate change, animal rights, and population-level questions in public health, including vaccination policy and pandemic response.
