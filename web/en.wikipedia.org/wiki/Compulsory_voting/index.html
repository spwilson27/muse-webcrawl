Compulsory voting

[Image: An Australian polling place with a long queue of voters on a Saturday election day]

Compulsory voting, also called universal civic duty voting or mandatory voting, is the requirement that eligible citizens register (where applicable) and vote in elections, usually enforced by sanctions on those who fail to do so without a valid excuse. It is in force in roughly two dozen countries, and is generally credited with producing very high turnout where it is enforced.

Countries

Australia is the best-known example: compulsory enrolment and attendance at federal elections were introduced in 1924, after turnout had fallen below 60 per cent, and turnout has typically exceeded 90 per cent since. Voting is held on Saturdays, and non-voters without a valid reason face a modest administrative fine. Belgium (since 1893), Brazil, Argentina, Peru, Ecuador, Uruguay, Singapore, Luxembourg and Greece are among other states with compulsory voting laws, though enforcement varies widely — in several countries the law is rarely applied in practice. The Netherlands abolished compulsion in 1970 and Italy in 1993; turnout fell in both afterwards.

Arguments

Supporters argue that voting is a civic duty like jury service or paying taxes; that compulsion produces a more representative electorate and reduces the ability of wealthy interests to mobilise only their supporters; that it forces parties to appeal to the whole electorate rather than turning out a base; and that it increases the legitimacy of outcomes. Opponents argue that compelled voting infringes liberty and freedom of conscience; that it adds uninformed or random votes (in Australia, the informal and so-called "donkey vote" are recurring topics of debate); and that high turnout can be achieved by making voting easier instead. Political science research generally finds that compulsory rules raise turnout substantially, with effects on policy and party strategy that remain debated.

Design

Systems differ in what is compulsory — enrolling, attending a polling place, or actually marking a ballot (the secret ballot means the last cannot be verified; a voter may cast a blank or spoiled vote). Sanctions range from small fines to, in a few countries, restrictions on access to public services. See also Political participation.
