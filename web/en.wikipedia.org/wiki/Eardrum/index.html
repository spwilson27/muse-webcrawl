Eardrum

The eardrum, or tympanic membrane, is a thin, cone-shaped membrane that separates the external ear from the middle ear in humans and other tetrapods. Its function is to transmit sound vibrations from the air to the ossicles inside the middle ear, and then to the oval window in the fluid-filled cochlea. Hence, it ultimately converts and amplifies vibration in air to vibration in fluid.

Structure

The tympanic membrane is roughly oval, about 8–10 mm in diameter, and consists of three layers: an outer cutaneous layer, a middle fibrous layer, and an inner mucous layer. Most of its area is the taut pars tensa; the small superior portion lacking the fibrous layer is the pars flaccida. The handle of the malleus, the first ossicle, is attached to its inner surface, and the membrane's conical apex (the umbo) points into the middle ear.

Clinical significance

The eardrum can rupture from trauma, infection (otitis media), sudden pressure changes (barotrauma), or foreign objects; small perforations often heal spontaneously, while persistent ones may be repaired surgically by tympanoplasty. Examination with an otoscope reveals the membrane's pearly-grey surface and a characteristic cone of light reflection.
