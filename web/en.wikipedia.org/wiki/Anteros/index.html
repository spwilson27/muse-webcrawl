In Greek mythology, Anteros (Ancient Greek: Ἀντέρως, "love returned" or "counter-love") is the god of requited love — literally "anti-Eros" — and the punisher of those who scorn love or fail to return the love of others. He is the son of Ares and Aphrodite and the brother of Eros, and belongs to the group of winged love gods known as the Erotes.

According to the myth, Aphrodite complained to the goddess Themis that Eros remained a perpetual child. Themis advised that Eros would grow only if he had a companion to love in return, so Aphrodite bore Anteros; whenever Anteros was near, Eros grew, but he shrank back when Anteros left. The story was read as an allegory that love must be answered to flourish.

Cult and iconography

Anteros had an altar at Athens, said to have been erected by metics (resident foreigners) after the Athenian Timagoras, ordered by the object of his love, Meles, to throw himself from the rock of the Acropolis, did so — whereupon Meles, stricken with remorse, threw himself down in turn. In art Anteros closely resembles Eros and is sometimes shown with a leaden club or with butterfly wings. The two brothers appear together wrestling on a gem base that stood in the palaestra at Elis, a symbol of the struggle between love and its return.

Legacy

The famous aluminium statue at Piccadilly Circus in London, popularly called "Eros", in fact represents Anteros, the god of selfless and requited love, chosen by its sculptor Alfred Gilbert as a memorial to the philanthropic Earl of Shaftesbury. The name Anteros also appears in Plato's Phaedrus, where it describes the reflection of love felt by the beloved in return — an "image of love" (eidolon erotos) answering the lover's desire.
