Refugee law

Key instruments: 1951 Refugee Convention, 1967 Protocol
Core principle: Non-refoulement
Oversight: UNHCR

Refugee law is the branch of international law concerned with the rights and protection of refugees — people who are outside their country of nationality and unable or unwilling to return owing to a well-founded fear of persecution. Its principal instruments are the 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol, supplemented by regional agreements and human rights law.

The refugee definition

The Convention defines a refugee as a person with a well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. Regional instruments, such as the 1969 OAU Convention and the 1984 Cartagena Declaration, extend the definition to people fleeing armed conflict and generalised violence.

Non-refoulement

The cornerstone of refugee law is the principle of non-refoulement: no state may return a refugee to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened on a Convention ground. The principle is widely regarded as part of customary international law and also appears in human rights treaties prohibiting torture and ill-treatment.

Institutions

The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), created in 1950, supervises the application of the Convention and provides international protection and assistance. States parties operate national asylum procedures to determine refugee status.
