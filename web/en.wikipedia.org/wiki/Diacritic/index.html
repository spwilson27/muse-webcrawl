Diacritic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Latin letters with common diacritics]

A diacritic (also diacritical mark, diacritical point, diacritical sign, or accent) is a glyph added to a letter or to a basic glyph. The term derives from the Ancient Greek διακριτικός (diakritikós, "distinguishing"), from διακρίνω (diakrínō, "to distinguish"). A diacritic changes the sound-value of the letter to which it is added, or distinguishes between two otherwise identical words, or one of both.

Diacritics can be placed above a letter (as in á), below it (ç), through it (ø), or between two letters. They are a central feature of many writing systems, and their use varies greatly from language to language: some orthographies, such as Vietnamese, rely on them heavily, while others, such as English, use them only in loanwords.

Types

Among the most common diacritics in the Latin script are the acute accent (´), grave accent (`), circumflex (ˆ), tilde (˜), umlaut or diaeresis (¨), macron (¯), breve (˘), caron (ˇ), cedilla (¸), ogonek (˛), and the dot above and below. In some languages what appears to be a diacritic is treated as part of the letter itself: the å, ä and ö of Swedish and the ø of Danish and Norwegian are letters in their own right.

Uses

Diacritics may mark vowel quality or length, nasalisation, tone (as in the four tones of Mandarin Chinese written in pinyin), stress, palatalisation, or retroflexion. In Semitic scripts, vowel points such as the Arabic ḥarakāt and Hebrew niqqud function similarly. A diacritic may also be the sole distinguishing mark between words, as with Spanish sí ("yes") and si ("if").

In computing

In Unicode, diacritics can be encoded either as precomposed characters (such as U+00E1, á) or as a base letter followed by a combining diacritical mark (such as U+0301, the combining acute accent). The International Phonetic Alphabet uses a large inventory of diacritics to record fine phonetic detail.
