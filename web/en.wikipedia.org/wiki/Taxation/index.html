Taxation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Taxation is the means by which governments finance their expenditure by imposing charges on citizens and corporate entities. Taxes consist of direct or indirect taxes and may be paid in money or (historically) in labour or kind. Tax systems are a central instrument of public policy — funding public goods and welfare states, redistributing income, stabilising economies and steering behaviour (for example, through carbon or tobacco taxes).

Types

The main bases are income (personal and corporate), consumption (VAT/GST and excises), property and wealth, payroll (social contributions), and trade (customs duties). Structures may be progressive, proportional or regressive in incidence. Modern states raise revenue-to-GDP ratios from about 15% to over 45% (the OECD range), the highest ratios occurring in European welfare states; collection relies heavily on employer withholding and third-party reporting.

History and politics

Taxation is as old as states — ancient Egypt, Rome's tributum, medieval feudal dues and early-modern excises — and its consent has been explosive political material: "no taxation without representation" was the American revolutionary slogan, and tax revolts and reforms (income tax adoption in wars, VAT's spread after 1954) mark fiscal history. Constitutional practice commonly reserves tax legislation to elected legislatures. Contemporary debates cover tax competition and havens, the OECD/G20 global minimum corporate tax (agreed 2021, 15%), digital-services taxation, wealth taxes, and carbon pricing.
