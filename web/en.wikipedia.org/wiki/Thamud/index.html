Thamud

The Thamud (Arabic: ثَمُود, romanized: Ṯamūd) were an ancient Arab tribe or confederation of tribes that occupied the northwestern Arabian Peninsula, in the region of the Hejaz around Hegra (Mada'in Salih). They are attested in Assyrian, Roman and Arabic sources between roughly the 8th century BC and the 5th century AD, and they figure prominently in the Quran as a people punished for their arrogance and rejection of the prophet Salih.

Rock-cut tombs at Hegra, associated with the Thamud in Islamic tradition

Historical sources

The earliest secure attestation is in the annals of the Assyrian king Sargon II (late 8th century BC), which mention the "Tamudi" among the peoples of the Arabian desert. Thamudic inscriptions — graffiti and carvings in several related Ancient North Arabian scripts — are found across a wide area of Arabia. Greek and Roman authors list the Thamudaei among the peoples of Arabia, and under Roman rule Thamudic soldiers were recruited into the Roman army as federate troops in Syria.

In Islamic tradition

In the Quran, the Thamud demanded a sign from the prophet Salih and were given a miraculous she-camel, which they were commanded not to harm. When they hamstrung and killed the camel, they were destroyed by a catastrophic punishment — described variously as an earthquake, a blast, or a shout. Their fate is presented as a warning to later generations, and the rock-cut dwellings at al-Hijr were pointed out to travellers as their remains, though the surviving tombs at Hegra are actually Nabataean.
