Carbonate rocks are a class of sedimentary rocks composed mainly of carbonate minerals. The two major types are limestone, composed mainly of calcite (and originally aragonite), and dolomite rock (dolostone), composed mainly of the mineral dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate). Carbonate rocks make up roughly 15–20% of sedimentary rocks and are of great economic importance as building stones, raw material for cement, and reservoirs for oil, gas and groundwater.

Formation

Most carbonate rocks form in warm, shallow marine environments where organisms secrete calcium carbonate shells and skeletons. Accumulations of shell fragments, coral and algal debris, ooids, and carbonate mud (lime mud) lithify into limestone through compaction and cementation. Much of the carbonate production of the oceans comes from reefs and carbonate platforms; the Bahamas Banks are a classic modern analogue. Some carbonates precipitate directly from water, as in travertine deposits around hot springs, or form in lakes.

Dolomitisation

Dolostone commonly arises when magnesium-bearing waters alter limestone after deposition, replacing calcite with dolomite — a process whose precise mechanisms (the "dolomite problem") long puzzled geologists. Dolomitisation can enhance porosity, making dolostones important petroleum reservoirs.

Weathering and karst

Because carbonate minerals dissolve in slightly acidic water, carbonate terrains develop distinctive karst landscapes of caves, sinkholes and disappearing streams. Dissolution also enlarges fractures, giving many carbonate aquifers high but unpredictable permeability — a challenge for water supply and engineering.

Classification

Sedimentologists classify carbonate rocks using schemes such as Folk's classification (based on grain types and matrix) and Dunham's classification (based on depositional texture: mudstone, wackestone, packstone, grainstone, boundstone), which relate texture to the energy of the depositional environment.
