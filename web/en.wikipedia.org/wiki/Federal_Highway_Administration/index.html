The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) is a division of the United States Department of Transportation that specializes in highway transportation. The agency's major activities are grouped into two primary programs: the Federal-aid Highway Program, which provides financial assistance to state and local governments for constructing, preserving, and improving highways, and the Federal Lands Highway Program, which provides access to and within national forests, national parks, Indian reservations, and other federal lands.

History

The FHWA's origins lie in the Office of Road Inquiry, created in the Department of Agriculture in 1893, and its successor the Bureau of Public Roads, which administered the first federal-aid highway legislation of 1916 and, after 1956, the construction of the Interstate Highway System under the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956. When the Department of Transportation was created in 1966, the bureau became the Federal Highway Administration.

The FHWA does not, for the most part, build or own highways itself. Instead, it apportions funds from the Highway Trust Fund — financed chiefly by the federal motor fuel tax — to state departments of transportation according to formulas set by Congress in periodic surface transportation authorization acts, and it sets design, safety, and engineering standards. Each state has an FHWA division office that oversees federal-aid projects jointly with the state DOT.

Functions

Beyond funding, the FHWA conducts research at the Turner-Fairbank Highway Research Center in McLean, Virginia; develops the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD), which standardises road signs and markings nationwide; administers programs for bridge inspection, highway safety, freight, and intelligent transportation systems; and promotes deployment of innovations through its Every Day Counts initiative. The Federal Highway Administrator is appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate.
