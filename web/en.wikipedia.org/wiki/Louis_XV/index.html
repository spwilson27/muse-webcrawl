Louis XV (15 February 1710 – 10 May 1774), known as Louis the Beloved (Louis le Bien-Aimé), was King of France from 1715 until his death in 1774. He succeeded his great-grandfather Louis XIV at the age of five. His reign of nearly 59 years was the second-longest in French history, exceeded only by that of Louis XIV.

Early reign and regency

Until 1723 France was governed by the Regent, Philippe II, Duke of Orléans. Louis assumed personal rule in 1743 after the death of his chief minister, Cardinal Fleury. In 1725 he married Marie Leszczyńska of Poland, with whom he had ten children, including the Dauphin Louis, father of Louis XVI.

Wars and diplomacy

Louis's reign was marked by major conflicts: the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748), in which French arms won distinction at Fontenoy, and the disastrous Seven Years' War (1756–1763), which cost France most of its colonial empire in Canada and India. The "Diplomatic Revolution" of 1756 allied France with its old enemy Austria against Prussia and Britain. France later regained some prestige by annexing Lorraine (1766) and Corsica (1768).

Court and culture

The court at Versailles was a centre of art and learning, and Louis's mistresses — notably Madame de Pompadour and later Madame du Barry — wielded great cultural and political influence. The king patronised the Rococo style and the building of the Panthéon and Place Louis XV (now Place de la Concorde) in Paris.

Decline and legacy

Chronic fiscal crisis, resistance from the parlements, and the king's perceived indifference — summed up in the attributed phrase après moi, le déluge — eroded the monarchy's prestige. Louis died of smallpox in 1774, succeeded by his grandson Louis XVI, leaving unresolved financial problems that contributed to the French Revolution.
