Migraine

Migraine
SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsModerate-to-severe throbbing headache, often one-sided; nausea; sensitivity to light and sound; aura in some patients

PrevalenceAbout 15% of people worldwide; more common in women

Migraine is a neurological condition characterised by recurrent headaches of moderate to severe intensity, typically throbbing and often affecting one side of the head, accompanied by nausea and increased sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia). Around a third of people with migraine experience an aura — transient visual or sensory disturbances preceding the headache. Attacks usually last between 4 and 72 hours. Migraine is one of the most common causes of disability worldwide.

Signs and symptoms

A migraine attack often progresses through phases: a prodrome (mood change, food cravings, neck stiffness) hours to days before; an aura phase in a minority; the headache phase itself; and a postdrome of fatigue or elation. Pain is typically pulsating, aggravated by routine physical activity, and accompanied by nausea and sometimes vomiting. Triggers reported by patients include stress and its release, hormonal changes, skipped meals, sleep disturbance, weather changes and certain foods.

Causes and mechanism

Migraine is believed to result from a genetically influenced state of neuronal hyperexcitability. The aura is attributed to cortical spreading depression, a wave of neuronal depolarisation across the cortex. Headache pain involves activation of the trigeminovascular system and release of neuropeptides such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which has become a major drug target.

Management

Acute treatment includes simple analgesics (paracetamol, NSAIDs) and triptans — serotonin receptor agonists specific to migraine — as well as newer gepants and ditans. Preventive treatment for frequent attacks includes beta blockers, topiramate, amitriptyline, and monoclonal antibodies against CGRP or its receptor. Lifestyle measures and trigger management are also recommended.
