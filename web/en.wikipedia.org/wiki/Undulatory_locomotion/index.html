Undulatory locomotion

Undulatory locomotion is the type of motion characterized by wave-like movement patterns that act to propel an animal forward. It is used by animals lacking limbs or moving in fluids, and is considered one of the most ancient forms of locomotion. Examples range from the swimming of fish and eels to the crawling of snakes, worms, and microscopic nematodes.

Forms

  • Lateral undulation — waves pass side to side along the body, as in most fish swimming (anguilliform, subcarangiform, carangiform, and thunniform modes depending on how much of the body flexes) and in snakes on land, where body bends push against surface irregularities.
  • Anguilliform swimming — the whole body flexes in large-amplitude waves, as in eels and lampreys.
  • Dorsoventral undulation — waves pass vertically, used by many dolphins and whales (with vertical fluke motion) and by some mammals and caterpillars.
  • Median and paired fin swimming — waves pass along fins, as in cuttlefish, knifefish, and skates.

Mechanics

Undulatory swimmers generate thrust by accelerating water backward with travelling waves that increase in amplitude toward the tail; efficient swimmers keep the Strouhal number in a narrow band around 0.2–0.4. On land, limbless undulators rely on anisotropic friction — belly scales resist sideways slipping more than forward sliding — so that lateral pushes convert into forward motion. Related wave-like internal processes, such as peristalsis in earthworms and in the gut, move material by sequential contraction, and undulation has inspired numerous bio-inspired swimming and snake robots.
