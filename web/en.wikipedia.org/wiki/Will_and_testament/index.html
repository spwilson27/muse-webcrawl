A will, or testament, is a legal document that expresses a person's (the testator) wishes as to how their property (estate) is to be distributed after their death and as to which person (the executor) is to manage the property until its final distribution. For the distribution (devolution) of property not determined by a will, see intestacy and probate.

Historically the word "will" applied only to real property (land) while "testament" applied only to personal property, giving rise to the combined phrase "last will and testament". Today the terms are generally used interchangeably.

Requirements for creation

Most jurisdictions require that a valid will be made by a person of sound mind who has reached the age of majority (usually 18), that it be in writing, that it be signed by the testator, and that the signature be witnessed by two disinterested witnesses. Some jurisdictions recognise holographic (entirely handwritten) wills, which in some cases need not be witnessed.

Types of wills

  • Holographic will — written wholly in the testator's handwriting.
  • Joint will — a single document executed by two or more testators, typically spouses.
  • Living will — not a will in the distributive sense, but a directive about medical treatment; see advance healthcare directive.
  • Testamentary trust — a trust created by the terms of the will itself, taking effect on death.

Revocation and alteration

A will may be revoked by the execution of a later will, by a written declaration of revocation, or by physical destruction of the document with intent to revoke. Minor changes can be made by a codicil, a supplementary document executed with the same formalities as the will itself.

Probate

After death, the will is submitted to a probate court, which determines its authenticity and supervises the executor in collecting the estate's assets, paying debts and taxes, and distributing the remainder to the beneficiaries named in the will.
