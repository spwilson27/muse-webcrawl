The Novgorod Republic (Russian: Новгородская республика) was a medieval state in northwestern Rus' centred on Novgorod the Great, existing from 1136 until 1478. One of the largest states of medieval Europe by territory — its fur-trading empire stretched from the Baltic to the Ural Mountains — it was governed through a popular assembly, the veche, which elected officials and invited, constrained, and sometimes expelled its princes. The republic was a major trading partner of the Hanseatic League and a celebrated centre of literacy, icon painting, and chronicle writing.

Government

The turning point came in 1136, when the Novgorodians expelled Prince Vsevolod Mstislavich and thereafter claimed the right to choose their own rulers. Supreme authority formally rested with the veche, an assembly of the city's free male population summoned by bell, which elected the posadnik (mayor) and the tysyatsky (commander of the militia) and decided questions of war, peace, and the invitation of a prince. Princes, drawn from the Rurikid dynasties and later also from Lithuania, ruled under strict contractual terms (ryad): they could not own land in Novgorod territory or dismiss officials without trial. The Archbishop of Novgorod, also elected, was a central political figure and custodian of the treasury. In practice, power was contested among great boyar families, and the city was divided into boroughs and streets with their own assemblies.

Trade and territory

Novgorod's wealth rested on the fur trade. Its agents and tributary expeditions reached the White Sea, the Pechora, and "Yugra" beyond the Urals, exchanging furs, wax, honey, and flax for Flemish cloth, silver, wine, and herring through the Hanseatic kontor at the Peterhof in Novgorod and links with Gotland and Lübeck. The city escaped destruction in the Mongol invasion of 1237–1240 — spring mud halted the invaders — but thereafter paid tribute to the Golden Horde.

Wars and the fall of the republic

Novgorod fought repeatedly with Sweden and the German crusading orders over Karelia, Estonia, and the Neva region; Prince Alexander Nevsky's victories over the Swedes on the Neva (1240) and the Teutonic Knights on the ice of Lake Peipus (1242) became legendary. During the 14th and 15th centuries Novgorod's growing rival was Moscow, whose princes sought to absorb the republic's lands. After the Battle of the Shelon River (1471), Grand Prince Ivan III imposed a harsh treaty; in 1478 he annexed Novgorod outright, removed the veche bell to Moscow, and deported many leading families. The republic's sister city, Pskov, survived until 1510.

Culture

Archaeology has made Novgorod famous for its birch-bark letters — over a thousand everyday documents, from business records to a child's alphabet exercises, preserved in the waterlogged clay — which testify to unusually widespread literacy. The 11th-century Cathedral of St Sophia, with its bronze Magdeburg doors, is among the oldest buildings in Russia.
