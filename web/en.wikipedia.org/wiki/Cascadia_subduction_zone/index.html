Cascadia subduction zone

The Cascadia subduction zone extends from northern California to Vancouver Island.

The Cascadia subduction zone is a convergent plate boundary off the Pacific Northwest coast of North America, extending roughly 900 kilometres (560 miles) from Cape Mendocino, California, to Vancouver Island, British Columbia. Here the Juan de Fuca Plate and the smaller Explorer and Gorda plates subduct beneath the North American Plate.

Geology

The subduction fuels the Cascade Volcanic Arc, including Mount St. Helens, Mount Rainier, and Mount Hood. Offshore, deformation builds strain along the locked megathrust fault. Subtle “episodic tremor and slip” events periodically release strain on the deeper part of the fault.

Megathrust earthquakes

The zone can produce megathrust earthquakes of magnitude 9 or greater. The most recent full-margin rupture occurred on January 26, 1700, an event inferred from coastal subsidence, tsunami deposits, Indigenous oral traditions, and Japanese records of an “orphan tsunami.” Geological evidence shows that such earthquakes recur at intervals of a few hundred years, accompanied by large tsunamis that can reach the coast within minutes to tens of minutes.

Preparedness

Because major cities including Seattle, Portland, and Vancouver lie in the affected region, governments and scientists study building resilience, tsunami evacuation, and early-warning systems such as ShakeAlert.
