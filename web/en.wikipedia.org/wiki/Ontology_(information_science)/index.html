Ontology (information science)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In information science, an ontology encompasses a representation, formal naming, and definitions of the categories, properties, and relations between the concepts, data, or entities that pertain to one, many, or all domains of discourse. More simply, an ontology is a way of showing the properties of a subject area and how they are related, by defining a set of terms and relational expressions that represent the entities in that subject area. The field which studies ontologies so conceived is sometimes referred to as applied ontology.

Every academic discipline or field creates ontological assumptions — explicit or implicit — that frame what is studied and how. In information science, ontologies are formalised using ontology languages such as the Resource Description Framework (RDF) and the Web Ontology Language (OWL), enabling machines to process and reason over the knowledge they encode.

Definition

The computer scientist Tom Gruber gave a widely cited definition: "An ontology is an explicit specification of a conceptualization." A later refinement by Studer, Benjamins and Fensel (1998) states that "an ontology is a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualisation." The definition emphasises four elements: a conceptualisation (an abstract model of some phenomenon), being explicit (concepts and constraints are clearly defined), formal (machine-readable), and shared (capturing consensual knowledge).

Components

Modern ontologies share common structural components regardless of the language in which they are expressed:

  • Individuals (instances or objects) — the basic, ground-level components.
  • Classes — sets, collections, or types of objects.
  • Attributes — aspects, properties, features, or parameters objects can have.
  • Relations — ways in which classes and individuals can be related to one another.
  • Function terms, restrictions, rules, axioms, and events in more expressive formalisms.

Types

Ontologies are commonly categorised by their level of generality. Upper ontologies (or foundational ontologies) model very general concepts common across all domains — examples include BFO (Basic Formal Ontology), DOLCE, and SUMO. Domain ontologies model concepts specific to a particular area, such as the Gene Ontology in biology or SNOMED CT in clinical medicine. Task and application ontologies describe concepts dependent on a particular activity or application.

Ontology engineering

Ontology engineering is the field concerned with the methods and methodologies for building ontologies, including ontology learning (automated or semi-automated extraction from data), ontology alignment and merging, and ontology evaluation. Editors such as Protégé are widely used to construct and maintain ontologies.

Applications

Ontologies underpin the Semantic Web, knowledge graphs, natural-language processing, biomedical informatics, digital libraries, and enterprise data integration. By providing a shared vocabulary with machine-processable semantics, they allow heterogeneous systems to interoperate and support automated reasoning, for example checking class consistency or inferring new facts via description-logic reasoners.
