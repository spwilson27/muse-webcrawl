The National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) is a nonprofit organisation that regulates student athletics among about 1,100 schools in the United States, Canada, and Puerto Rico. It also organises the athletic programmes of colleges and universities and helps over 500,000 college student-athletes who compete annually in college sports. The headquarters are located in Indianapolis, Indiana.

The NCAA is divided into three levels of competition: Division I, Division II, and Division III. Under NCAA rules, Division I and Division II schools can offer scholarships to athletes for playing a sport, while Division III schools may not offer athletic scholarships. The NCAA's most lucrative and best-known events are the Division I men's and women's basketball tournaments ("March Madness"), whose media contracts fund most of the association's budget and distributions to member schools.

History

The association was founded in 1906 as the Intercollegiate Athletic Association of the United States (IAAUS), largely in response to President Theodore Roosevelt's concern over deaths and brutal injuries in college football; it adopted the NCAA name in 1910. Walter Byers became the first full-time executive director in 1951 and built the association's modern enforcement and television apparatus. In 1973 the membership split into the present three divisions. Women's championships were added in the early 1980s after the NCAA absorbed the AIAW. Headquarters moved from Kansas City to Indianapolis in 1999.

Structure and governance

The NCAA is a member-led association: legislation is adopted through divisional governance structures in which schools and conferences vote. The national office in Indianapolis administers championships, eligibility, and enforcement. The president serves as the association's chief executive; former Massachusetts governor Charlie Baker succeeded Mark Emmert in March 2023.

Championships and sports

The NCAA administers 90 national championships in 24 sports, including basketball, baseball, ice hockey, lacrosse, soccer, swimming and diving, track and field, volleyball, and wrestling. Notably, it does not determine a champion in Division I FBS football, where the College Football Playoff is run separately, though it governs the FCS championship.

Eligibility, NIL, and legal challenges

For most of its history the NCAA enforced strict amateurism rules barring athlete pay. Legal and legislative pressure dismantled much of this model: the Supreme Court's decision in NCAA v. Alston (2021) struck down limits on education-related benefits, and from July 2021 athletes have been permitted to earn money from their name, image, and likeness (NIL). The House v. NCAA settlement, approved in 2025, introduced direct revenue sharing between schools and athletes, further transforming the economics of college sports.

Criticism

The NCAA has long been criticised over amateurism, the distribution of tournament revenues, inconsistent enforcement, and athlete welfare. Defenders argue it preserves an educational model of sport distinct from professional leagues and funds broad-based athletic opportunities, including women's and Olympic sports.
