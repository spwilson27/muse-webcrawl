Category (philosophy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In philosophy, a category (from the Greek katēgoria, "accusation" or "predication") is a highest kind or genus under which entities can be classified. Categories are the most general sorts of things that can be said of anything; a theory of categories aims to provide an exhaustive inventory of the basic structures of reality or of thought.

Aristotle

Aristotle, in his treatise Categories and in the Topics, listed ten categories: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, posture, having (state), action, and being affected (passion). Substance — what a thing is in itself — is primary; the other nine are accidents that inhere in substances. This scheme profoundly influenced medieval scholasticism.

Kant

Immanuel Kant argued that categories are not derived from the world but are a priori concepts of the understanding that structure all experience. In the Critique of Pure Reason (1781) he derived twelve categories from the forms of judgment, grouped under quantity (unity, plurality, totality), quality (reality, negation, limitation), relation (substance and accident, cause and effect, community) and modality (possibility, existence, necessity).

Later theories

C. S. Peirce proposed three universal categories — Firstness, Secondness and Thirdness. In the 20th century, Gilbert Ryle used "category mistake" for the error of treating something as belonging to the wrong logical type, while ontologists such as E. J. Lowe proposed four fundamental categories: objects, kinds, attributes and modes. Debates continue over whether categories are features of reality itself or of human conceptual schemes.
