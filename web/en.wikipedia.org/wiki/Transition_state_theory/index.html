Transition state theory

A reaction coordinate diagram; the transition state sits at the energy maximum.

Transition state theory (TST) explains the reaction rates of elementary chemical reactions. The theory assumes a special type of equilibrium (quasi-equilibrium) between reactants and the activated transition state complex. It was developed simultaneously in 1935 by Henry Eyring at Princeton and by Meredith Gwynne Evans and Michael Polanyi in Manchester.

The Eyring equation

TST yields the Eyring equation for the rate constant:

k = κ (kBT / h) exp(−ΔG‡ / RT)

where ΔG‡ is the Gibbs energy of activation, kB is Boltzmann's constant, h is Planck's constant, and κ is a transmission coefficient, usually taken as 1. The prefactor kBT/h sets a universal frequency scale of about 6 × 1012 s−1 at room temperature.

Assumptions and limitations

The theory rests on the Born–Oppenheimer separation of electronic and nuclear motion, the assumption that activated complexes crossing the barrier proceed to products without recrossing, and quasi-equilibrium between reactants and the transition state. It improves on the empirical Arrhenius equation by giving a molecular interpretation of the pre-exponential factor in terms of activation entropy. Extensions such as variational TST and tunnelling corrections address reactions where the basic assumptions fail, notably light-atom transfers at low temperature and enzymatic reactions.

References: Eyring, H., "The Activated Complex in Chemical Reactions", Journal of Chemical Physics 3 (1935); Laidler, K. J., Chemical Kinetics.
