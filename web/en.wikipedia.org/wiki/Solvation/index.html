Solvation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Solvation describes the interaction of a solvent with dissolved molecules or ions. In the process of solvation, ions and molecules are surrounded by solvent molecules arranged in a specific manner, forming a solvation shell (or solvation sphere). For water as the solvent, the process is called hydration. Solvation is the kinetic and thermodynamic process by which solvent and solute molecules reorganize into solvation complexes, and it underlies the solubility of substances and much of solution chemistry.

Mechanism

When an ionic compound such as sodium chloride dissolves in water, the polar water molecules surround each ion: oxygen ends face sodium cations and hydrogen ends face chloride anions. The attraction between ions and solvent must overcome the lattice energy of the crystal for dissolution to occur. Polar solutes dissolve through hydrogen bonding and dipole interactions, while nonpolar solutes are accommodated through weaker van der Waals forces and often with an unfavourable ordering of surrounding solvent — the hydrophobic effect.

Thermodynamics

The solvation energy (Gibbs free energy of solvation) combines an enthalpic contribution from solvent–solute attractions with an entropic contribution from the ordering of solvent around the solute. Solvation energies are large for small, highly charged ions and explain the energy released when ions are hydrated. Models of solvation range from continuum treatments such as the Born model and polarizable continuum models to explicit molecular dynamics simulations.

Effects and applications

Solvation determines reaction rates and equilibria in solution: solvent polarity can stabilize or destabilize transition states, shift acid–base behaviour, and change spectroscopic properties (solvatochromism). In biology, hydration shells govern protein folding and molecular recognition; in technology, solvation controls electrolyte performance in batteries and the behaviour of pharmaceuticals in the body.
