The Lehmann discontinuity is an abrupt increase in the velocities of P-waves and S-waves at a depth of about 5,150 kilometres (3,200 mi) within the Earth, marking the boundary between the liquid outer core and the solid inner core. It is named for the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann, who inferred the existence of the inner core in 1936.

Discovery

By the 1930s it was known from seismic shadow zones that the Earth has a core through which S-waves do not pass and which refracts P-waves. However, seismograms recorded at distances where P-waves should have been absent showed weak arrivals, labelled P′. In a landmark 1936 paper (titled simply "P′"), Lehmann proposed that these arrivals were explained if the core contained a smaller, solid inner core from which the waves were reflected and refracted. Her model was confirmed as data improved, and the existence of the inner core became accepted in the following decades.

Properties

The inner core boundary (ICB) lies at a radius of about 1,220 km. Across it, P-wave velocity rises sharply from about 10.3 km/s to about 11.0 km/s, and S-waves, which cannot propagate through the liquid outer core, reappear because the inner core is solid. The boundary is thought to be sharp — a few kilometres at most — though studies of reflected waves suggest a gradational zone of tens of kilometres may exist at its top, where iron alloy is freezing onto the growing inner core. Slight differences in temperature, rotation, and hemispherical structure of the inner core remain active topics of seismological research.

Other use of the name

The term "Lehmann discontinuity" is also applied to a less pronounced seismic discontinuity at roughly 220 km depth in the upper mantle, detected under continents, which may mark the base of a low-velocity or anisotropic layer. This dual usage can cause confusion in the literature.
