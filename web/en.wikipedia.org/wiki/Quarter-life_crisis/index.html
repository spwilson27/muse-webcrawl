Quarter-life crisis

A quarter-life crisis is a period of stress and anxiety affecting people in their twenties and early thirties, centred on the direction and quality of one's life. Typically experienced around age 25 to 30, it involves uncertainty over career, relationships and the mismatch between adolescent ambitions and adult reality. The term parallels the midlife crisis, placed at the midpoint of the working life.

Origins

The concept was popularised by the 2001 book Quarterlife Crisis: The Unique Challenges of Life in Your Twenties by Alexandra Robbins and Abby Wilner, based on interviews with recent graduates. It drew on developmental psychology's long-standing "emerging adulthood" research — Jeffrey Arnett's characterisation of the twenties as a distinct life stage of identity exploration and instability — and on Erikson's stages, where young adulthood confronts intimacy versus isolation.

Symptoms and causes

Described features include anxiety about "not knowing what to do", career regret, social comparison (amplified by social media), financial strain, and a sense of being trapped between youth and settled adulthood. Therapists note that it frequently follows transitions — leaving education, first full-time work, the end of a first serious relationship — and that reframing the period as normal development rather than failure aids recovery. Critics argue the term medicalises an ordinary life stage.
