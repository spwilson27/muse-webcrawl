Rhinoplasty (Greek: ῥίς, romanized: rhīs, "nose" + πλάσσειν, plássein, "to shape"), commonly called a nose job, is a plastic surgery procedure for altering and reconstructing the nose. There are two types of plastic surgery used: reconstructive surgery, which restores the form and functions of the nose, and cosmetic surgery, which changes the appearance of the nose. Reconstructive surgery seeks to resolve nasal injuries caused by various traumas, including blunt and penetrating trauma, as well as trauma caused by blast injury. Reconstructive surgery can also treat birth defects, breathing problems, and failed primary rhinoplasties. Rhinoplasty may remove a bump, narrow the nostril width, change the angle between the nose and the mouth, and address injuries, birth defects, or other problems that affect breathing, such as a deviated nasal septum or a sinus condition.

In closed rhinoplasty and open rhinoplasty surgeries, a plastic surgeon, an otolaryngologist (ear, nose, and throat specialist), or an oral and maxillofacial surgeon (jaw, face, and neck specialist) creates a functional, aesthetic, and facially proportionate nose by separating the nasal skin and the soft tissues from the nasal framework, altering them as required for form and function, then suturing the incisions and applying either a package or a stent, or both, to immobilize the altered nose and ensure proper healing.

History

Treatments for the plastic repair of a broken nose are first mentioned in the Edwin Smith Papyrus, a transcription of text dated to the Old Kingdom of ancient Egypt, from 3000 to 2500 BC. The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BC) describes rhinoplasty as the ancient Egyptian surgical operation for reconstructing a nose destroyed by rhinectomy, an amputation performed as a criminal, religious, political, or military punishment in that era.

The Indian physician Sushruta described the surgery in the Sushruta Samhita (c. 500 BC), where a nasal reconstruction used a flap of skin taken from the cheek. This "Indian method" was later described in the West and refined over centuries. In the 19th century, surgeons such as Joseph Constantine Carpue in England popularised the forehead-flap technique in Europe. Modern rhinoplasty was further developed in the early 20th century by surgeons including John Orlando Roe, who in 1887 described an intranasal approach for correcting a "pug nose", and Jacques Joseph in Berlin, regarded by many as the father of modern facial plastic surgery.

Anatomy of the human nose

The nose consists of skin, soft tissue, and a framework of bone and cartilage. The upper third of the nose is supported by the paired nasal bones, while the middle and lower thirds are formed by cartilages: the upper lateral cartilages, the lower lateral (alar) cartilages, and the quadrangular cartilage of the septum. Internally, the nasal septum divides the airway into two passages; deviation of the septum can obstruct breathing and is often corrected during functional rhinoplasty (septorhinoplasty).

Patient evaluation

Before surgery, the surgeon evaluates the patient's medical history, nasal anatomy, skin thickness, facial proportions, and breathing function. Photographs are taken from multiple angles to plan the changes. Realistic expectations are important: the goal is a nose in proportion with the rest of the face rather than a copy of another person's nose. Patients are advised to stop smoking and avoid medications that increase bleeding before the procedure.

Approaches: closed and open rhinoplasty

Closed rhinoplasty

In the closed (endonasal) approach, all incisions are made inside the nostrils, leaving no external scar. The surgeon works through limited openings, which can mean less dissection and a shorter operating time, but provides a more restricted view of the nasal framework.

Open rhinoplasty

In the open (external) approach, an additional incision is made across the columella, the narrow strip of tissue between the nostrils. This allows the nasal skin to be lifted and the underlying structures to be seen directly, which many surgeons prefer for complex revisions, major tip work, and graft placement. The resulting scar is usually small and fades well.

Techniques used in both approaches include removing or reshaping cartilage and bone (such as a dorsal hump), osteotomies (controlled fractures of the nasal bones) to narrow the bridge, tip sutures, and grafts of cartilage taken from the septum, ear, or rib.

Non-surgical rhinoplasty

Non-surgical rhinoplasty uses injectable fillers, such as hyaluronic acid, to temporarily smooth a dorsal hump, lift a drooping tip, or correct minor asymmetries. The results last months rather than years, and the procedure cannot reduce the size of the nose. Because the nose has a complex blood supply, filler injections carry rare but serious risks, including vascular occlusion and, in exceptional cases, vision loss.

Recovery

After surgery, a splint is typically worn on the nose for about a week. Bruising and swelling around the eyes are common and subside over two to three weeks, though subtle swelling of the tip can take a year or more to resolve fully. Most patients return to sedentary work within one to two weeks and avoid strenuous exercise and contact sports for several weeks.

Risks and complications

Possible complications include bleeding, infection, adverse reactions to anaesthesia, persistent asymmetry, perforation of the septum, difficulty breathing, and dissatisfaction with the cosmetic result. A proportion of patients—often cited in the range of 5 to 15 percent—undergo revision rhinoplasty to refine the outcome.
