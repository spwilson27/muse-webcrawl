Stromatolites are layered sedimentary formations created by the growth of microbial mats, principally photosynthetic cyanobacteria, which trap, bind, and precipitate sediment. They are among the oldest evidence of life on Earth, with fossil examples dating back about 3.5 billion years.

Significance

For most of Earth's history, stromatolite-forming microbes dominated shallow marine environments. Their photosynthesis gradually released oxygen into the atmosphere, driving the Great Oxidation Event roughly 2.4 billion years ago and transforming the planet's chemistry and the course of evolution. Stromatolites declined in the late Proterozoic, plausibly because of grazing and burrowing animals.

Modern stromatolites

Living stromatolites survive in a few refuges where conditions exclude most grazing animals — notably the hypersaline Hamelin Pool in Shark Bay, Western Australia, and subtidal channels in the Exuma Cays of the Bahamas.
