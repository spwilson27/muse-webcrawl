Ajax the Lesser

Ajax the Lesser, also called Ajax son of Oileus (Ancient Greek: Αἴας ὁ Μικρός), is a hero of Greek mythology, king of Locris and a leader of the Greek forces in the Trojan War. He is distinguished from the greater Ajax son of Telamon by his smaller stature, speed and — above all — his impiety and violent death.

In the Iliad

In Homer's Iliad, Ajax son of Oileus leads forty ships of Locrians. Small, swift-footed and expert with the spear and the linen breastplate, he often fights alongside his namesake, the two Ajaxes forming a famous pair. He competes in the funeral games of Patroclus, slipping in the footrace when Athena trips him into dung, to the amusement of the army.

The sacrilege and death

During the sack of Troy, Ajax dragged the prophetess Cassandra from the temple of Athena, where she had taken sanctuary clinging to the goddess's statue (the Palladium) — in some versions raping her. The sacrilege polluted the Greek fleet: Athena and Poseidon wrecked the returning ships, and Ajax's vessel was shattered on the Gyraean rocks. He briefly survived, clinging to a rock and boasting that he had escaped despite the gods, whereupon Poseidon split the rock and drowned him. His corpse was washed ashore at Mykonos and buried by Thetis.

Aftermath and cult

The Locrians long expiated the crime by sending an annual tribute of two maidens to serve in the temple of Athena at Troy — the "Locrian maidens" — a ritual said to have lasted for centuries. Ajax received heroic cult in Locris, and his image appeared on Locrian coinage.
