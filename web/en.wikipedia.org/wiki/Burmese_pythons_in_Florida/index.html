Burmese pythons in Florida

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Burmese pythons (Python bivittatus) are an invasive species in the U.S. state of Florida, where a breeding population became established in the Everglades, largely as a result of escaped or released pets and the destruction of a breeding facility by Hurricane Andrew in 1992. The snakes have caused severe declines in native mammal populations and are considered one of the most concerning invasive species in Everglades National Park.

Establishment

Burmese pythons are native to Southeast Asia and were widely imported for the pet trade. Individuals sighted in the Everglades from the 1980s onward were initially presumed to be escaped pets, but by around 2000 scientists recognised that a self-sustaining breeding population existed. Genetic studies suggest the Florida population is relatively uniform, consistent with a limited founder population.

Impact

A 2012 study documented dramatic declines — in some cases over 90 percent — in sightings of raccoons, opossums, bobcats, and rabbits in areas where pythons were established. Pythons prey on a wide range of mammals and birds, including threatened species, and compete with native predators.

Control efforts

Florida has banned ownership of Burmese pythons as pets, runs the annual Florida Python Challenge hunting competition, and employs contracted python removal agents. Tens of thousands of snakes have been removed, yet eradication is considered unlikely because the snakes are cryptic and the habitat vast.
