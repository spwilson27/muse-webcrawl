Genevan Psalter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Genevan Psalter, also known as the Huguenot Psalter or the French Psalter, is a metrical psalter in French created under the supervision of John Calvin for liturgical use by the Reformed churches of the city of Geneva in the sixteenth century. The complete psalter, containing melodies for all 150 psalms, was published in 1562 as Les Pseaumes mis en rime françoise par Clément Marot et Théodore de Bèze.

History

When Calvin returned to Geneva in 1541, he insisted on congregational psalm singing as a central part of worship. The first collection appeared in Strasbourg in 1539, with versifications by Clément Marot. In Geneva, the work was continued by Théodore de Bèze, who completed the remaining psalms after Marot's death. Composers including Loys Bourgeois, Guillaume Franc, and Claude Goudimel supplied the tunes, which are mostly syllabic and written in the church modes.

Characteristics

Distinctive features of the Genevan Psalter include a separate melody for nearly every psalm, extensive use of varied metrical patterns, and unison congregational singing without instrumental accompaniment. The tune for Psalm 134, known in English as "Old Hundredth" when adapted to Psalm 100, became one of the most widely sung melodies in Protestantism.

Influence

Translations and adaptations of the Genevan tunes spread through Reformed churches in France, the Netherlands, Scotland, Hungary, and beyond, and later to North America and South Africa. Dutch versions became the standard psalmody of the Dutch Reformed Church for centuries. The melodies were harmonized by composers such as Claude Goudimel and Jan Pieterszoon Sweelinck and remain in use in many hymnals today.
