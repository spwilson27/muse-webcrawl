Culture jamming

Culture jamming (sometimes also guerrilla communication) is a form of protest used by many anti-consumerist social movements to disrupt or subvert media culture and its mainstream cultural institutions, including corporate advertising. It attempts to "expose the methods of domination" of mass society.

Culture jamming employs techniques that remix, parody, or hijack existing media — advertisements, logos, slogans, news formats — in order to carry a contrasting message, usually ridiculing the original. Practitioners describe their work as a kind of public "jujitsu": turning the weight of corporate media against itself.

Origins

The term was coined in 1984 by Don Joyce of the sound collage band Negativland, who drew an analogy with radio jamming. Its intellectual roots lie in the Situationist International's concept of détournement — rerouting spectacular images and language — and in Dada and surrealist collage. The Canadian magazine Adbusters, founded in 1989 by Kalle Lasn, became the movement's most visible platform; Lasn's book Culture Jam (1999) popularized the concept.

Tactics

Common tactics include subvertising (spoof advertisements), billboard banditry (altering outdoor ads), flash mobs, performance art, brandalism, hoax press releases, and media pranks. Groups such as the Billboard Liberation Front, RTMark, and The Yes Men have impersonated corporate and government spokespeople to expose what they see as institutional hypocrisy.

Notable practitioners

Banksy and other street artists are often described as culture jammers, as are the Cacophony Society, the Barbie Liberation Organization — which swapped voice boxes in talking Barbie and G.I. Joe dolls in 1993 — and the Italian Luther Blissett Project. Adbusters also helped launch Buy Nothing Day and, through a 2011 call to protest, the Occupy movement.

Criticism

Critics argue that culture jamming is easily absorbed by the marketing it mocks — "cool" anti-advertising is itself a proven advertising style — and that its gestures substitute symbolic play for material political organization. Defenders reply that jamming's purpose is consciousness-raising: to make the manufactured character of consumer culture visible.
