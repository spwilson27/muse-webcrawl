Gerald "Jerry" Wexler (January 10, 1917 – August 15, 2008) was an American record producer and executive who, as a partner in Atlantic Records from 1953, helped transform rhythm and blues and soul music into central currents of American popular music. A former Billboard journalist, he is credited with popularising the term "rhythm and blues" in place of "race records". He produced landmark recordings by Ray Charles, Aretha Franklin, Wilson Pickett, the Drifters and Dusty Springfield, and signed Led Zeppelin to Atlantic.

Early career and Atlantic

Wexler was born in the Bronx and, after war service, became a reporter and editor at Billboard, where in 1949 the magazine adopted "Rhythm & Blues" as the name of its black-music chart — a change he championed. In 1953 Ahmet Ertegun invited him to buy into Atlantic Records, the independent label Ertegun had founded with Herb Abramson. With engineer Tom Dowd and arranger-producers such as Jesse Stone, Wexler and Ertegun built Atlantic into the leading R&B label, recording Ray Charles (whose "I Got a Woman" Wexler co-wrote, uncredited at the time), the Drifters, Ruth Brown, Big Joe Turner and LaVern Baker.

The soul era

In the 1960s Wexler drove Atlantic's expansion into Southern soul, recording at Stax in Memphis and at FAME Studios in Muscle Shoals, Alabama, where he produced Wilson Pickett's "In the Midnight Hour" (1965) and Percy Sledge's "When a Man Loves a Woman" (1966). His greatest coup was signing Aretha Franklin in 1966 and recording her at FAME: "I Never Loved a Man (The Way I Love You)" and "Respect" (1967) made her the "Queen of Soul". He also produced Dusty Springfield's Dusty in Memphis (1969) and worked with the Allman Brothers Band, Dr. John and Bob Dylan, producing Dylan's Slow Train Coming (1979). Atlantic was sold to Warner Bros.-Seven Arts in 1967, and Wexler left the company in the mid-1970s.

Honours and legacy

Wexler was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1987 in the non-performer category. His memoir, Rhythm and the Blues (1993, with David Ritz), is regarded as one of the essential accounts of the post-war record business. He died of congestive heart failure at his home in Sarasota, Florida, aged 91.
