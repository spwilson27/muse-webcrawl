Aftermath of the September 11 attacks

The aftermath of the September 11 attacks encompasses the wide-ranging consequences of the terrorist attacks carried out by al-Qaeda on 11 September 2001, in which 2,977 victims were killed in New York City, at the Pentagon, and in Pennsylvania. The attacks reshaped United States domestic policy, foreign policy, aviation security, and public life for decades.

Immediate response

US airspace was closed and the North American Aerospace Defense Command raised its alert level. Rescue and recovery at the World Trade Center site continued for months; fires at Ground Zero burned for 99 days. The stock markets closed for four trading days, and airlines suffered severe financial losses.

Domestic consequences

Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act expanding surveillance powers, and created the Department of Homeland Security and the Transportation Security Administration, which federalized airport screening. The September 11th Victim Compensation Fund compensated families of the dead and injured. Rescue workers and residents exposed to toxic dust at Ground Zero developed respiratory illnesses and cancers, leading to the James Zadroga 9/11 Health and Compensation Act of 2010.

International consequences

The United States, invoking the "war on terror", invaded Afghanistan in October 2001 to dismantle al-Qaeda and remove the Taliban, beginning the longest war in US history, and invaded Iraq in 2003. NATO invoked Article 5 for the first time. Osama bin Laden was killed by US special forces in Pakistan in 2011.

Memorials

The National September 11 Memorial & Museum opened at the World Trade Center site in 2011–2014, alongside memorials at the Pentagon and in Shanksville, Pennsylvania.
