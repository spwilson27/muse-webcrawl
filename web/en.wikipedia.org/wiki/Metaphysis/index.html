The metaphysis is the narrow portion of a long bone between the epiphysis (the rounded end) and the diaphysis (the central shaft). It contains the growth plate (epiphyseal plate) in growing bones and is composed largely of spongy (trabecular) bone richly supplied with blood vessels.

Structure and function

During childhood and adolescence, the metaphysis is the region where the cartilage of the growth plate is converted into new bone, lengthening the bone — a process called endochondral ossification. When growth ceases, the growth plate ossifies into the epiphyseal line and the metaphysis merges with the epiphysis. The metaphysis also transfers loads from the joint surfaces to the shaft of the bone.

Clinical significance

The rich blood supply and relatively sluggish circulation of the metaphysis make it a common site of hematogenous osteomyelitis in children. Metaphyseal fractures are among the most frequent childhood fractures, and certain bone tumors, such as osteosarcoma, characteristically arise in the metaphysis of long bones in adolescents. Rickets and other metabolic bone diseases produce characteristic widening and cupping of the metaphysis visible on radiographs.
