Central Valley Project

The Central Valley Project (CVP) is a federal water management project in the U.S. state of California, operated by the United States Bureau of Reclamation. One of the largest water systems in the world, it comprises 20 dams and reservoirs with a combined capacity of about 11 million acre-feet, some 800 kilometres of canals, and associated power plants; it delivers water to irrigate roughly 3 million acres of farmland in the Central Valley and to supply municipal users in the Sacramento and San Joaquin valleys and the eastern San Francisco Bay Area.

History

California voters approved the project in 1933, but the state could not finance it during the Great Depression, and the federal government took it over in 1935 as a New Deal public works programme. Construction of Shasta Dam (1938–1945), Friant Dam, and the Delta-Mendota and Friant-Kern canals followed. The project's purpose was to move abundant Sacramento River water south to the water-scarce but fertile San Joaquin Valley, while controlling floods and generating hydroelectric power.

Facilities and issues

Major facilities include Shasta, Trinity, Folsom, New Melones and Friant dams, and the California Aqueduct is part of the separate State Water Project. The CVP has long been criticised for harm to salmon runs and the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta ecosystem, prompting reform under the Central Valley Project Improvement Act of 1992, which dedicated water to environmental purposes. Deliveries are sharply curtailed in drought years, as in 2014–2015 and 2021–2022.
