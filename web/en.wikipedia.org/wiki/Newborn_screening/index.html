Newborn screening (NBS) is a public health programme in which infants are tested shortly after birth for a panel of conditions that are treatable but not clinically evident in the newborn period. Early detection allows interventions — dietary, hormonal or otherwise — that prevent intellectual disability, serious illness or death.

History

Screening began with phenylketonuria (PKU) in the early 1960s, following Robert Guthrie's development of a bacterial inhibition assay using dried blood spots collected on filter paper — still called "Guthrie cards". The introduction of tandem mass spectrometry in the 1990s allowed dozens of metabolic disorders to be detected from a single blood spot.

Conditions screened

  • Phenylketonuria (PKU)
  • Congenital hypothyroidism
  • Cystic fibrosis
  • Sickle cell disease and other haemoglobinopathies
  • Medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MCADD)
  • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia
  • Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)
  • Spinal muscular atrophy (in expanding panels)

Panels vary by country and, in the United States, by state; the US Recommended Uniform Screening Panel (RUSP) lists over 35 core conditions. Screening also commonly includes hearing tests and pulse oximetry for critical congenital heart disease.

Criteria

Conditions are selected using principles derived from the Wilson and Jungner criteria (1968): the condition should be an important health problem, have a recognisable latent stage, a suitable test, and an accepted treatment whose early application improves outcomes.
