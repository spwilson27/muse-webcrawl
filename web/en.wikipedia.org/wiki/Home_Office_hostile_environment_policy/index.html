Home Office hostile environment policy

The hostile environment policy is a set of administrative and legislative measures pursued by the British Home Office, announced in 2012 by Home Secretary Theresa May, with the aim of making staying in the United Kingdom as difficult as possible for people without leave to remain, so as to encourage them to depart voluntarily. May stated the goal explicitly: "The aim is to create, here in Britain, a really hostile environment for illegal immigrants."

Measures

The policy, building on earlier laws and substantially extended by the Immigration Acts of 2014 and 2016, enlisted employers, landlords, banks, the National Health Service, and the Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency as de facto immigration checkers. Landlords were required to check tenants' immigration status under the "Right to Rent" scheme, employers faced penalties for hiring people without status, and people without leave were barred from opening bank accounts or holding driving licences, with data shared between public services and the Home Office for enforcement purposes.

Criticism and the Windrush scandal

Critics, including parliamentary committees, the UN special rapporteur on racism, and the Equality and Human Rights Commission, argued the measures fostered discrimination and pushed people into destitution. The policy came to prominence in 2018 with the Windrush scandal, in which long-term residents from the Caribbean — including people who had arrived as children with legal status — were wrongly detained, denied healthcare and housing, and in some cases deported, after being unable to document their status. The scandal led to the resignation of Home Secretary Amber Rudd, a formal government apology, a compensation scheme, and the Windrush Lessons Learned Review (2020) by Wendy Williams, which found institutional ignorance and thoughtlessness towards race within the Home Office. The government subsequently renamed its approach the "compliant environment."
