Robert Koch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Robert Koch
Born: 11 December 1843, Clausthal, Germany
Died: 27 May 1910
Known for: Identifying the bacteria causing anthrax, tuberculosis and cholera; Nobel Prize 1905

Robert Koch (1843–1910) was a German physician and microbiologist who, with Louis Pasteur, founded the science of bacteriology and the germ theory of disease.

Working as a district doctor, Koch demonstrated in 1876 that the bacterium Bacillus anthracis causes anthrax — the first conclusive proof that a specific microbe causes a specific disease. In Berlin he developed the laboratory toolkit of the new science: solid culture media, agar (suggested by Fanny Hesse), the Petri dish (from his assistant Julius Petri), and staining and photomicrography techniques.

In 1882 he announced the discovery of the tuberculosis bacillus, and in 1883–84 identified the cholera vibrio in Egypt and India. His Koch's postulates — criteria for proving a microbe causes a disease — became a methodological standard. He received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1905 for his work on tuberculosis, though his claimed tuberculosis cure, tuberculin (1890), proved a failure as a therapy (it later found use as a diagnostic). The Robert Koch Institute in Berlin, Germany's national public-health institute, bears his name.
