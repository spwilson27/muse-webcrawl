Long slow distance

Long slow distance (LSD) is a form of aerobic endurance training used in sports including running, rowing, skiing and cycling. It is characterised by training at a low to moderate intensity over an extended distance or duration.

The physiological adaptations of LSD training include improved cardiovascular function, improved thermoregulatory function, improved mitochondrial energy production, increased oxidative capacity of skeletal muscle, and increased utilisation of fat for fuel. Ernst van Aaken, a German physician and coach, is believed to have been the first to introduce long slow distance as a training method for runners.

History

The method was popularised in the late 1960s and 1970s, notably by American runner and writer Joe Henderson, who coined and promoted the term "long slow distance". It was a reaction against the dominance of high-intensity interval training and argued that enjoyable, conversational-pace running over long distances could build endurance and keep runners healthy and injury-free.

Practice

An LSD run is usually performed at a pace at which the athlete can hold a conversation, roughly 60–70% of maximum heart rate, and lasting from 30 minutes to two hours or more depending on the event being trained for. In marathon training, the weekly long run is typically the centrepiece of the programme and builds toward 30–35 km.

Criticism

Critics note that training only at slow paces leaves runners unprepared for race-pace demands, so most modern training systems combine a high volume of slow running with smaller amounts of faster work (tempo runs and intervals) — an approach often summarised as polarised training.
