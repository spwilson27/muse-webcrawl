Government budget

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A government budget is a projection of a government's revenues and expenditures for a specified period, usually a fiscal year, presented as a law or plan requiring approval by the legislature. Budgets are the state's most concrete policy act: they translate programmes into money, fix tax burdens and borrowing, and (because legislatures guard the power of the purse as their historic core) provide the main recurring occasion for executive accountability.

Process and politics

Typical cycles run from executive preparation (treasury/finance ministry collecting departmental bids) through legislative authorisation — strong in the US congressional model (authorisations and appropriations, continuing resolutions and shutdowns when they fail) to weak in Westminster systems where budget defeat equals loss of confidence and amendments are constrained — then execution, audit and evaluation. Budgetary institutions condition fiscal outcomes: fiscal rules and councils, medium-term frameworks, programme and performance budgeting reforms, and transparency standards. The politics are permanent: budgeting is incremental (Lindblom, Wildavsky), distributive (concentrated benefits, diffuse costs), and cyclical (deficits in recessions and wars, contested consolidation in booms) — which is why budget offices and independent fiscal institutions have multiplied as legislatures' corrective.
