Parasitic twin

A parasitic twin, also known as an asymmetrical twin or unequal conjoined twin, occurs when a twin embryo begins developing in utero but the pair does not fully separate, and one embryo maintains dominant development at the expense of the other. The underdeveloped twin is wholly dependent on the bodily functions of the complete foetus, known as the autosite. The parasitic twin is known as a heteropagus when attached externally to the autosite, or as a fetus in fetu when enclosed within the autosite's body.

Development

Parasitic twins arise from the same processes as conjoined twins and vanishing twins, in which a fertilised egg fails to separate completely. Unlike ordinary conjoined twins, one twin ceases development during gestation and becomes vestigial to the fully formed dominant twin. The rudimentary twin may consist of limbs, organs or other tissue structures attached to the autosite.

Variants

Heteropagus twins are parasitic twins attached externally, often at the torso or pelvis. Fetus in fetu is an enclosed variant in which a malformed parasitic foetus grows inside the body of its normally developed twin, most often in the abdomen. Another variant, the teratoma, is sometimes discussed in the same context, although its origin is debated.

Treatment

Management is usually surgical: the parasitic tissue is removed to relieve the burden on the autosite and to prevent complications. Prognosis after successful separation is generally good for the autosite.
