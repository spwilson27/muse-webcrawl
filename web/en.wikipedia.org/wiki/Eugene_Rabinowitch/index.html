Eugene Rabinowitch

Eugene I. Rabinowitch (April 27, 1901 – May 15, 1973) was a Russian-born American biophysicist and a leading figure in scientists' efforts to control nuclear weapons. He worked on the Manhattan Project, co-authored the Franck Report, and founded the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, whose Doomsday Clock became a famous symbol of the nuclear danger. His scientific career centred on photosynthesis, on which he wrote the standard multi-volume treatise.

Early life and career

Born in Saint Petersburg, Rabinowitch emigrated after the Russian Revolution, studied chemistry in Berlin, and worked with James Franck in Göttingen before fleeing Nazi Germany in 1933. After posts in Copenhagen and London he settled in the United States, teaching at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and later at the University of Illinois, where his laboratory advanced understanding of the photochemistry of chlorophyll and the kinetics of photosynthesis.

The atomic scientists' movement

During the Second World War Rabinowitch worked at the Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago on the chemistry of plutonium. In June 1945 he helped draft the Franck Report, which urged that the atomic bomb be demonstrated before an unannounced use on a Japanese city. After the war he co-founded the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists (1945) with Hyman Goldsmith and edited it for many years, writing much of its early content himself. He was a central organiser of the Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs, bringing together scientists from both sides of the Cold War divide.
