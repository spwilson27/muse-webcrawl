Utopia is an Aboriginal homelands region in the Northern Territory of Australia, located about 270 kilometres (170 mi) north-east of Alice Springs. It is famous as the home of a community of Alyawarre and Anmatyerre artists whose paintings—most notably those of Emily Kame Kngwarreye—achieved international recognition from the late 1980s onward.

History

The area was originally part of the Utopia pastoral lease, a cattle station established in the 1920s. Its name is said to derive from the abundance of rabbits in the area, which early settlers jokingly described as a "utopia" for the animals. In 1976, following changes to Australian land rights law, the lease was purchased on behalf of its Aboriginal residents, and in 1979 the land was formally handed back under the Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act, becoming one of the first successful Aboriginal land claims in Australia.

The community is not a single settlement but a network of outstations (homelands) spread across roughly 3,500 square kilometres of desert country, with families living on their traditional clan estates.

Art

Utopia is best known for its art movement. In the late 1970s, women of the community began producing batik under the guidance of adult educators, and in 1988–89 the community shifted to acrylic painting on canvas. The resulting body of work—characterised by layered fields of dots and gestural line work depicting ancestral stories and bush foods—brought Utopia artists to national and international attention. Emily Kame Kngwarreye (c. 1910–1996), who began painting in her late seventies, became one of Australia's most celebrated artists. Other notable Utopia artists include Gloria Petyarre, Kathleen Petyarre, Minnie Pwerle and the Pwerle sisters.

Health study

Utopia was also the subject of a long-running epidemiological study which found that Aboriginal people living on the Utopia homelands had significantly lower mortality and hospitalisation rates than comparable communities in centralised settlements, a result attributed in part to continued connection to country, traditional diet and physical activity.
