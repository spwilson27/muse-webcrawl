Coronagraph

Coronagraph
TypeTelescopic attachment / instrument
Invented1930, by Bernard Lyot
PurposeBlocking direct starlight to image faint surroundings

A coronagraph is a telescopic attachment designed to block out the direct light of a star or the Sun so that nearby objects — otherwise hidden in the glare — can be resolved. Originally invented to observe the Sun's corona outside of solar eclipses, coronagraphs are now a central technology in the direct imaging of exoplanets and circumstellar discs.

History

The French astronomer Bernard Lyot invented the coronagraph in 1930 and first used it at the Pic du Midi Observatory in the Pyrenees, where the clean mountain air reduced scattered light. Lyot's design placed an opaque occulting disc at the focal plane to mask the solar disc, with carefully designed optics and a Lyot stop to suppress diffracted light. Coronagraphs were later flown in space, where the absence of atmospheric scattering made far cleaner observations possible.

Solar coronagraphs

Spaceborne solar coronagraphs such as LASCO aboard the SOHO observatory continuously monitor the corona and detect coronal mass ejections, which are important for space-weather forecasting. Ground-based coronagraphs remain in use at high-altitude observatories.

Exoplanet imaging

Modern stellar coronagraphs use apodised pupils, phase masks (such as the four-quadrant and vortex coronagraphs), deformable mirrors and sophisticated image processing to suppress starlight by factors of millions or more. Instruments including SPHERE at the Very Large Telescope, GPI at Gemini South, and the coronagraphs on the Hubble and James Webb space telescopes have directly imaged young giant planets and debris discs. A high-performance coronagraph is a key technology planned for future missions aimed at imaging Earth-like exoplanets.
