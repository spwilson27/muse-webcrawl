Albendazole is a broad-spectrum anthelmintic (anti-worm) medication of the benzimidazole class, used to treat a wide variety of parasitic worm infections. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is used in mass drug administration campaigns against soil-transmitted helminthiases and lymphatic filariasis.

Medical uses

Albendazole is effective against roundworms, whipworms, hookworms, threadworms, and pinworms, and is a drug of choice for tissue infections including neurocysticercosis (larval pork tapeworm infection of the brain) and hydatid disease (echinococcosis) caused by the dog tapeworm Echinococcus granulosus, where it is used before and after surgery. It is also used for giardiasis, trichuriasis, filariasis (with ivermectin or diethylcarbamazine), and cutaneous larva migrans.

Mechanism of action

Albendazole is converted in the liver to its active metabolite, albendazole sulfoxide. The drug binds selectively to parasite β-tubulin, inhibiting microtubule polymerization; this blocks glucose uptake by the worm, depleting its energy stores and causing immobilization and death. Its greater affinity for parasite than mammalian tubulin accounts for its therapeutic margin.

Side effects

Short courses are generally well tolerated; common effects include headache, nausea, and abdominal pain. Prolonged high-dose therapy, as for hydatid disease, can cause reversible liver enzyme elevation, bone marrow suppression, and hair loss, and requires monitoring of blood counts and liver function. It is avoided in pregnancy because of embryotoxicity in animals.

History and access

Developed in the 1970s and introduced for human use in the early 1980s, albendazole became a mainstay of global deworming; hundreds of millions of tablets are donated annually for school-based and community programmes. Controversy arose in the United States in 2013 when rights changed hands and the price per tablet rose sharply.
