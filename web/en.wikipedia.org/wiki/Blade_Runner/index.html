Blade Runner

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Deckard in the rain-drowned neon streets of 2019 Los Angeles, in the film's vision of a polyglot, smoke-hazed future city]

Blade Runner is a 1982 American science fiction film directed by Ridley Scott, adapted from Philip K. Dick's novel Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? (1968). Harrison Ford plays Rick Deckard, a burnt-out 'blade runner' hunting four escaped replicants — bioengineered humans — through a decaying, rain-soaked Los Angeles of 2019. A commercial failure on release, it became, through video and its director's cuts, one of the most influential films ever made: the founding text of cyberpunk and of tech-noir design.

The film exists in at least seven versions; the 1992 Director's Cut and 2007 Final Cut removed the studio-imposed voiceover and happy ending and left Deckard's own humanity (the unicorn dream) teasingly open. Its production design — Syd Mead's 'retro-fitted' future, Douglas Trumbull's effects, Vangelis's score — defined the look of the future for forty years.

The replicants

Rutger Hauer's Roy Batty — the film's dying antagonist, whose final speech (written largely by Hauer himself the night before shooting, and paraphrased in every account rather than reproduced here) is among cinema's most famous — reverses the film's sympathies: the manufactured man clings to life while the human hunter sleepwalks. Sean Young's Rachael, who does not know what she is, carries the film's question: what memory, what empathy, makes a person?

Legacy

Blade Runner's city — Hong Kong density, Japanese signage, perpetual rain and smoke — became the default future of anime (Ghost in the Shell), games and a hundred films. Its sequel Blade Runner 2049 (2017), directed by Denis Villeneuve, won Oscars for cinematography and effects while honouring the original's pace and ambiguity. Scholars now teach it as philosophy's favourite film.
