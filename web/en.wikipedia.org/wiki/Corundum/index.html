Corundum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Corundum
Composition: Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃)
Hardness: 9 on the Mohs scale (reference mineral)
Gem varieties: Ruby (red) and sapphire (all other colours)

Corundum is a crystalline form of aluminium oxide, the second-hardest natural mineral after diamond — it defines hardness 9 on the Mohs scale. Its transparent gem varieties are among the most valuable of all stones: red corundum is ruby; every other colour, including the classic blue, is sapphire.

Colour comes from trace impurities: chromium makes ruby glow (and fluoresce under ultraviolet); iron and titanium make blue sapphire; the rarest, padparadscha, is pink-orange. The finest rubies — "pigeon's blood" stones from Mogok, Burma — rival diamonds in price per carat. Because corundum is so hard, its opaque industrial form (emery) is used as an abrasive, and synthetic corundum (grown in bulk since the Verneuil process of 1902) serves in watch bearings, lasers (the first laser, in 1960, was a ruby rod) and scratch-proof watch glasses and phone screens in its sapphire form.

Corundum forms in aluminium-rich, silica-poor rocks and survives weathering into river gravels — the gem gravels of Burma, Sri Lanka and Montana — outlasting the mountains that bore it, as the reference minerals from talc to topaz each outlast their eras in the geologist's kit.
