Schistosoma

KingdomAnimalia
PhylumPlatyhelminthes
ClassTrematoda
FamilySchistosomatidae
DiseaseSchistosomiasis (bilharzia)

Schistosoma is a genus of parasitic flatworms (blood flukes) responsible for schistosomiasis, a disease affecting over 200 million people, mainly in sub-Saharan Africa. Unlike other trematodes, schistosomes are dioecious — males and females are separate — and adult pairs live for years in the blood vessels of their human host, the thicker male cradling the slender female in a gynaecophoric canal.

Life cycle

Eggs laid in blood vessels are shed in urine or faeces and hatch in fresh water into miracidia, which infect particular species of freshwater snails. Inside the snail, asexual multiplication produces thousands of fork-tailed cercariae that are released into the water and penetrate human skin during bathing, farming or fishing. The young worms (schistosomula) migrate through the lungs to the liver, mature, pair up, and settle in the veins of the bladder (S. haematobium) or the intestines (S. mansoni, S. japonicum and others). Much of the disease is caused not by the worms but by the immune reaction to eggs trapped in tissues.

Major species

Schistosoma haematobium causes urinary schistosomiasis and is a recognized carcinogen for bladder cancer; S. mansoni (Africa and South America) and S. japonicum (East and Southeast Asia) cause intestinal and hepatosplenic disease. The genus was long known as Bilharzia after Theodor Bilharz, who described the worm in Cairo in 1851. Treatment relies on the drug praziquantel, and control combines mass drug administration with snail control and improved sanitation.
