Eskimo roll

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An Eskimo roll, also called a kayak roll, is the act of righting a capsized kayak or canoe by use of body motion and/or a paddle. Typically this is done by quickly applying leverage through the paddle or hands while performing a coordinated "hip snap" to rotate the boat upright while the paddler remains seated. A successful roll allows a capsized paddler to recover without exiting the boat — an essential skill in whitewater kayaking, sea kayaking, and surf kayaking.

History

The technique was developed by the Inuit and Yupik peoples of the Arctic, for whom a wet exit into freezing water could be fatal; Greenland hunters mastered dozens of rolling variations, including rolls performed without a paddle, using a harpoon, or with arms crossed. European explorers described the manoeuvre in the 18th and 19th centuries. Austrian kayaker Edi Hans Pawlata brought the technique to Europe in 1927, performing the first documented European roll and teaching the "Pawlata roll", an extended-paddle method still taught to beginners.

Techniques

Dozens of named rolls exist. The most commonly taught include:

  • Sweep roll — the paddle is swept in a wide arc across the surface while the hips snap the boat upright.
  • C-to-C roll — the body forms a "C" toward the paddle side, sets the paddle perpendicular, then curves into a reverse "C" as the hip snap is executed.
  • Combat roll — any roll performed in real conditions, such as in rapids, rather than in practice.
  • Hand roll — righting the boat without a paddle, used in freestyle kayaking and as an emergency skill.
  • Greenland rolls — a large traditional repertoire, contested at Greenland's national kayaking championships, including rolls with a throwing stick, with a brick, and the "under-the-hull" sculling rolls.

Learning and safety

Rolls are typically first learned in a swimming pool or calm water with an instructor. Key elements are keeping the head down and letting it come up last, snapping the hips rather than lifting the body, and keeping the paddle blade at the surface. A reliable roll greatly reduces the need for a "wet exit" and swimming in hazardous water, and is considered a fundamental safety skill for whitewater paddlers.
