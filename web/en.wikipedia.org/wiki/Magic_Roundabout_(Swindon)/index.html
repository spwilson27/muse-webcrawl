The Magic Roundabout in Swindon, England, is a complex road junction constructed in 1972 consisting of five mini-roundabouts arranged in a ring around a central island. Traffic circulates clockwise around each mini-roundabout (as normal in the United Kingdom) but anticlockwise around the central ring — the opposite of a conventional roundabout — allowing drivers to choose from several paths across the junction. It takes its name from the children's television programme The Magic Roundabout.

Design and operation

The junction, originally called County Islands, was designed under the influence of the Transport and Road Research Laboratory's experiments with "ring junctions", with input from roundabout pioneer Frank Blackmore. Each of the five entries leads onto a mini-roundabout; a driver may proceed around the outer ring visiting successive mini-roundabouts, or cut across using the inner circulatory carriageway in the reverse direction. Because flows are distributed among many small conflict points rather than one large gyratory, capacity is high and speeds are low. The junction was officially renamed the Magic Roundabout in the early 1980s following a public vote; before that, locals had already adopted the nickname.

Reputation and safety

The Magic Roundabout regularly appears in polls of Britain's most intimidating junctions, and unfamiliar drivers often find it confusing at first sight. Its safety record is, however, excellent: the compact geometry forces low speeds, and serious accidents have been rare over five decades of heavy use. Similar ring junctions were later built elsewhere in England, including at Hemel Hempstead (the "Plough" roundabout) and High Wycombe, and the design has been studied internationally as an example of a junction that works better in practice than it looks on paper.
