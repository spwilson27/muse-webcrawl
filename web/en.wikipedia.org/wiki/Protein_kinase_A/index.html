Protein kinase A

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
PKA is a tetramer of two regulatory and two catalytic subunits; binding of cAMP releases the active catalytic subunits.

In cell biology, protein kinase A (PKA) is a family of enzymes whose activity is dependent on cellular levels of cyclic AMP (cAMP). PKA is also known as cAMP-dependent protein kinase (EC 2.7.11.11). PKA has several functions in the cell, including regulation of glycogen, sugar, and lipid metabolism, and it is a central effector of signalling by many hormones and neurotransmitters that act through G protein-coupled receptors.

Mechanism

The inactive PKA holoenzyme is a tetramer of two regulatory (R) subunits and two catalytic (C) subunits. When a stimulus—such as adrenaline acting through a β-adrenergic receptor—activates adenylyl cyclase via a Gs protein, cAMP levels rise and four cAMP molecules bind the regulatory subunits, causing them to release the catalytic subunits. The free catalytic subunits then phosphorylate serine and threonine residues on target proteins, typically within the consensus sequence Arg-Arg-X-Ser/Thr. Activity is terminated when phosphodiesterases degrade cAMP and protein phosphatases remove the phosphates.

Targets and effects

PKA substrates include phosphorylase kinase and hormone-sensitive lipase in metabolic pathways, ion channels controlling cardiac contractility, and the transcription factor CREB, whose phosphorylation (at Ser133) recruits co-activators and changes gene expression—one mechanism linking PKA to learning and memory. Localisation is refined by A-kinase anchoring proteins (AKAPs), which tether PKA to specific subcellular compartments near its substrates.

History and disease

The cAMP–PKA pathway was the original "second messenger" system, arising from Earl Sutherland's work on adrenaline and glycogen breakdown; the enzyme itself was characterized by Edwin Krebs, Edmond Fischer, and Donal Walsh in the late 1960s. Mutations in PKA subunit genes are associated with disease: activating variants in the catalytic subunit cause a form of Cushing syndrome, and inactivating mutations in the regulatory subunit PRKAR1A cause Carney complex.
