Electric stove

An electric stove or electric range is a kitchen appliance that cooks food using electrical heating elements instead of combustion fuel. It typically combines a cooktop (hob) with an oven in a single unit, and is one of the principal alternatives to the gas stove.

Heating elements

Cooktop elements take several forms. Coil elements expose a resistive wire sheathed in metal tubing, which heats cookware by direct contact and radiation. Solid disk elements embed the heater in a cast-iron plate. Smooth-top designs place radiant elements beneath a glass-ceramic surface, which is easy to clean but slower to respond. Induction cooktops, sometimes classed separately, heat the pan directly through electromagnetic induction and are the fastest and most efficient electric option. Oven compartments use exposed or concealed resistive elements for baking and broiling, often combined with a convection fan.

History

Experimental electric cookers appeared in the late nineteenth century after the spread of electricity generation; a full-size electric range was demonstrated at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. Adoption grew through the twentieth century as domestic electricity became reliable, and electric ranges became standard in many markets.

Advantages and disadvantages

Electric stoves avoid indoor combustion pollutants and the need for a gas supply, and smooth-tops are easy to clean. Traditional resistive cooktops respond more slowly to temperature changes than gas, while induction largely removes that disadvantage but requires magnetic cookware.
