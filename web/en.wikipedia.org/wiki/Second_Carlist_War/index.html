The Second Carlist War (1846–1849) was a civil war in Spain, the second of the Carlist Wars fought between supporters of the traditionalist claimant Don Carlos and his heirs and the liberal government of Isabella II. Unlike the First Carlist War (1833–1840), it was largely confined to Catalonia, where it is known as the War of the Matiners (Guerra dels Matiners, "War of the Early Risers", from the guerrillas' habit of striking at dawn).

Causes

The war grew out of the unresolved settlement of the First Carlist War and the Convention of Vergara (1839), which had left many Carlist grievances untouched. Its immediate trigger was the failed project of a dynastic marriage between Isabella II and the Carlist claimant Carlos Luis de Borbón, Count of Montemolín (Carlos VI), which would have united the two branches; when Isabella instead married Francisco de Asís in October 1846, Montemolín issued a manifesto and Carlists rose in arms. In Catalonia, discontent over conscription (the quintas), taxation, unemployment among textile workers and the defence of traditional privileges gave the revolt a broader social base; even some republicans and progressives joined the insurgents against the moderate government.

Course of the war

The rising was waged as guerrilla warfare by partidas operating in the Catalan mountains under commanders such as Benet Tristany, José Borges and Marcel·lí Gonfaus ("Marsal"). The veteran general Ramón Cabrera, hero of the First War, entered Spain in 1848 to take command, but the rebels could not take any major city or coordinate a nationwide movement. Government forces under Manuel Pavía and then Fernando Fernández de Córdova, combining columns, amnesties and economic measures, gradually wore the partidas down. Cabrera crossed into France in April 1849, and the last bands were dispersed or surrendered later that year.

Consequences

The defeat drove thousands of Carlists into exile and left the movement dormant until the revolution of 1868 gave it a new pretender and a national stage in the Third Carlist War (1872–1876). The Catalan war also revealed how Carlist legitimism could absorb wider social and regional protest — a pattern that recurred in later conflicts.
