Glyptics


Engraved gems: products of the glyptic art.

Glyptics, or the glyptic arts, is the art of carving or engraving on small objects, especially gemstones, and by extension the study of carved gems, cylinder seals, stamp seals and related small relief works of the ancient and classical world. The term derives from the Greek glyphein, "to carve".

Techniques and forms

Glyptic works take two principal forms: the intaglio, in which the design is cut into the stone so that it appears in relief when pressed into clay or wax to make a seal impression, and the cameo, in which the design stands in relief above a contrasting background, exploiting the layered colours of stones such as sardonyx and agate. Carvers worked with bow-driven drills, wheels and abrasive powders of emery and diamond dust, using hardstones — carnelian, jasper, rock crystal, amethyst, lapis lazuli — as well as glass pastes imitating them. In Mesopotamia the cylinder seal, rolled across wet clay, served administrative and legal functions for millennia; scarab seals performed a similar role in Egypt.

History

Gem engraving flourished in Minoan and Mycenaean Greece, reached a peak of refinement in Classical and Hellenistic Greece and in Rome, where engraved portraits of emperors were cut in precious sardonyx — most famously in the Gemma Augustea. The art survived in Byzantium and the Islamic world, was revived with antiquarian enthusiasm in Renaissance Italy, and enjoyed a neoclassical golden age in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries with carvers such as the Pichler family. Major collections are held by the British Museum, the Cabinet des Médailles in Paris and the Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna. Glyptics remains an important source for ancient iconography, portraiture and the identification of lost works of monumental sculpture.
