Americo-Liberians

Americo-Liberians (or Americo-Liberian people) are a Liberian ethnic group descended from African-American, Afro-Caribbean and liberated African ("Congo") settlers who founded Liberia beginning in 1822 under the auspices of the American Colonization Society. Although never more than a small minority of the population, Americo-Liberians dominated Liberian politics, commerce and society from independence in 1847 until the military coup of 1980.

History

The first settlers landed at Cape Mesurado in 1822, founding Monrovia, named for US President James Monroe. In 1847 Liberia declared independence, with Joseph Jenkins Roberts, born free in Virginia, as its first president. The settlers reproduced many institutions of the United States — a constitution modelled on the American one, a flag resembling the Stars and Stripes, English language, Protestant churches and Southern-style architecture — while relations with the indigenous peoples of the interior were frequently marked by conflict, land disputes and political exclusion; indigenous Liberians were not granted full citizenship and voting rights until the mid-20th century.

The True Whig Party, closely associated with the Americo-Liberian elite, ruled continuously from 1878 to 1980, one of the longest periods of single-party rule in history. Presidents such as William Tubman (1944–1971) promoted "unification" policies and an economic Open Door, continued under William Tolbert.

End of dominance

Americo-Liberian political dominance ended abruptly on 12 April 1980, when Master Sergeant Samuel Doe and other indigenous soldiers overthrew and killed President Tolbert. In the ensuing decades, including the Liberian civil wars (1989–2003), the distinct settler elite lost its former position, although Americo-Liberians remain prominent in business, law, education and public life, and many emigrated to the United States.

Culture

Americo-Liberian culture blended African-American traditions with West African influences: Liberian English and Kreyol speech, quilting, soul food adapted to local ingredients, Masonic lodges, and church-centred social life. Within Liberia, settlers were sometimes called "Congo people", a label extended to the recaptive Africans landed by anti-slave-trade patrols.
