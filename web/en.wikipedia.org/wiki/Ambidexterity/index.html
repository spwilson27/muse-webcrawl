Ambidexterity

Ambidexterity is the ability to use both the right and left hand equally well. When referring to objects, the term indicates that the object is equally suitable for right-handed and left-handed people. When referring to humans, it indicates that a person has no marked preference for the use of the right or left hand.

Prevalence

Only about one percent of people are naturally ambidextrous, meaning they display no consistent preference for one hand across tasks. More common is mixed-handedness, in which a person prefers one hand for some tasks and the other hand for others — for example, writing with the right hand but throwing with the left. Most people have a dominant hand, with roughly 90 percent of the population right-handed.

Development and training

Hand preference typically emerges in early childhood and is influenced by genetics, prenatal development, and cultural factors. Historically, many left-handed children were forced to write with their right hands. Ambidexterity can also be deliberately trained: athletes, musicians, and jugglers often practise with the non-dominant hand, and some degree of skill can be acquired, although trained ambidexterity rarely matches the innate ability of the naturally ambidextrous.

In sports and arts

Ambidexterity is advantageous in many sports. In baseball, switch hitters bat from both sides of the plate; in basketball and football (soccer), players who can shoot or pass with either hand or foot are highly valued. Notable historical ambidextrous figures include Leonardo da Vinci, who was said to write and draw with both hands, and Nikola Tesla.

Relation to the brain

Handedness is linked to the lateralisation of brain function. Studies have suggested that ambidextrous and mixed-handed people may show less strongly lateralised language processing, and some research has explored associations — largely inconclusive — between ambidexterity and cognitive or developmental traits.
