Notch signaling pathway

The Notch signaling pathway is a highly conserved cell signalling system present in most multicellular organisms. It regulates cell-fate decisions during embryonic and adult development through direct cell-to-cell communication, influencing processes such as neurogenesis, angiogenesis, and the maintenance of stem cells. It is named after the Notch gene, first identified in the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, where mutations cause notches in the wing margins.

Mechanism

Notch receptors (NOTCH1–4 in mammals) are single-pass transmembrane proteins on the receiving cell. They bind ligands of the Delta-like and Jagged families on a neighbouring cell. Ligand binding triggers two proteolytic cleavages of the receptor — first by an ADAM metalloprotease, then by the γ-secretase complex — releasing the Notch intracellular domain (NICD). The NICD travels to the nucleus, where it binds the transcription factor CSL (RBPJ) and coactivator Mastermind, converting CSL from a repressor to an activator and switching on target genes such as the Hes and Hey families.

Roles and disease

Because signalling requires direct contact, Notch mediates patterning mechanisms such as lateral inhibition, in which one cell's fate suppresses the same fate in its neighbours. Dysregulation is implicated in many diseases: activating NOTCH1 mutations occur in most cases of T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, while loss-of-function mutations cause conditions including Alagille syndrome and CADASIL. γ-secretase inhibitors and other Notch-targeting drugs have been explored as cancer therapeutics.
