Rhinoceros beetle

Rhinoceros beetles are a group of scarab beetles in the subfamily Dynastinae, named for the prominent horns borne by males of many species. The group includes some of the largest and strongest insects, such as Hercules beetles, elephant beetles and the Japanese rhinoceros beetle.

Family Scarabaeidae
Subfamily Dynastinae
Distribution Worldwide, especially tropical and subtropical regions
Diet Larvae often feed on decaying wood or plant matter; adults may eat fruit, nectar or sap

Horns and sexual dimorphism

Male horns can project from the head and thorax, forming a pincer-like apparatus used to pry or lift rivals during contests over food, sap sites or mates. Horn size often depends strongly on larval nutrition. Females usually have much smaller horns or none. Despite their weapon-like appearance, the horns are not primarily for defense against predators.

Life cycle

Females lay eggs in soil, compost, rotting logs or other decaying organic matter. The C-shaped larvae may develop for months or several years in large species before pupating. Adults are frequently nocturnal and attracted to lights. Some adults feed little; others visit fermenting fruit and tree wounds.

Strength

Rhinoceros beetles are famous for carrying loads many times their body mass. Popular claims vary because strength depends on whether the beetle is lifting, dragging or supporting a load and on the experimental setup. Their robust bodies and traction make them disproportionately powerful rather than literally unmatched among animals by every measure.

Human interactions

Large species are popular in insect keeping and children’s media in Japan and elsewhere. Most are ecologically useful decomposers as larvae, but the coconut rhinoceros beetle is a serious pest of palms in parts of Asia and the Pacific, boring into crowns and damaging young fronds.
