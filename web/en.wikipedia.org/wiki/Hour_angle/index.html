Hour angle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In astronomy and celestial navigation, the hour angle is the angle between two planes: one containing Earth's axis and the zenith (the meridian plane), and the other containing Earth's axis and a given point of interest on the celestial sphere (the hour circle). It is a measure of the angular distance of an object west of the local meridian, expressed in hours, minutes and seconds, where 24 hours correspond to a full 360° rotation. One hour of hour angle is equal to 15°.

Definition

The hour angle of a point is the angle between the meridian plane and the hour circle plane passing through the point. It is measured westward from the meridian: an object on the observer's meridian has an hour angle of zero; an object six hours west of the meridian has an hour angle of +6h, and one six hours east has −6h, or equivalently 18h. The hour angle is directly related to the time elapsed since the object last crossed (transited) the meridian.

Relation to right ascension

The local hour angle (LHA) of an object in the observer's sky is related to the object's right ascension and the local sidereal time:

LHA = LST − RA

where LST is local sidereal time and RA is the object's right ascension. If the result is negative, 24 hours are added. Together with the object's declination, the hour angle fully specifies its position in the equatorial coordinate system relative to the observer's location and time, and can be converted into altitude and azimuth in the horizontal coordinate system.

Applications

Hour angle is fundamental to equatorial mount telescope operation, where the polar axis is rotated at sidereal rate to track the sky, and to celestial navigation, where the Greenwich hour angle (GHA) of a body — measured westward from the Greenwich meridian — is tabulated in nautical almanacs. In solar engineering, the hour angle of the Sun is used in calculating solar position and the performance of solar collectors; it is zero at solar noon and increases by 15° per hour.
