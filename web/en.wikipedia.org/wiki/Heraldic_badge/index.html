A heraldic badge, in the terminology of English heraldry, is an emblem — often simple and bold — borne by a king or great noble as a personal device and given to retainers, servants, and supporters to wear as a mark of allegiance. Badges flourished in England from the reign of Richard II in the late 14th century until the Tudor period. Unlike a coat of arms, a badge was not inherited according to strict rules and did not identify its bearer uniquely; it identified whose man the wearer was. Badges were displayed on livery collars and clothing, on standards and banners, on buildings, plate, and horse trappers, and as carved or painted ornament.

History

The use of badges developed out of the tournament and household devices of the 13th century and reached its height under Richard II, who distributed his badge of the White Hart to his courtiers and household servants; his deposition in 1399 was accompanied by the ritual removal of the hart badges of his retainers. During the Wars of the Roses the rival royal houses made their badges famous political symbols: the red rose of Lancaster and the white rose of York. After his victory at Bosworth in 1485, Henry VII combined the two in the Tudor rose, uniting the claims of both houses in one device. The portcullis badge of his mother, Lady Margaret Beaufort, remains in use today, notably as the emblem of the Palace of Westminster and UK Parliament.

Other famous badges include the Stafford knot of the Dukes of Buckingham, the ragged staff of the Earls of Warwick ("the Kingmaker"), the antelope and swan of Henry V, the falcon and fetterlock of the House of York, the sun in splendour of Edward IV, and the three feathers of the Prince of Wales, which descend from the badge attributed to Edward, the Black Prince. Scottish heraldry uses a related concept, the clan badge or plant badge.

Badges, arms, and crests

Three systems overlapped in late medieval display. The coat of arms (the shield) identified an individual and descended hereditarily. The crest, worn atop the helm, was also personal and hereditary. The badge, by contrast, could be shared: a magnate distributed it to his affinity, and it could be used more freely in architecture and decoration, where its repeatability suited patterned ornament. A fourth element, the heraldic standard, was a long tapering flag bearing the owner's badges, motto, and livery colours (but not his shield).

Modern use

The sovereign and members of the British royal family continue to use personal and national badges, regulated by the College of Arms and the Court of the Lord Lyon. Royal badges appear on coinage, official buildings, and regimental insignia of the British armed forces; examples include the Tudor rose, the Scottish thistle, the Welsh leek and dragon, and the feathers of the Prince of Wales worn by Welsh regiments.
