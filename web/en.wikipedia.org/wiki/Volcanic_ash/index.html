Volcanic ash

Volcanic ash consists of fragments of rock, mineral crystals, and volcanic glass, created during volcanic eruptions and measuring less than 2 mm (0.079 inches) in diameter. The term is also often used to refer to all explosive eruption products, including particles larger than 2 mm. Volcanic ash is formed during explosive volcanic eruptions when dissolved gases in magma expand and escape violently into the atmosphere.

An ash plume from an explosive eruption; ash can be carried thousands of kilometres downwind.

Formation and properties

Ash particles are angular, abrasive and mildly corrosive; unlike the soft ash of a wood fire, volcanic ash is hard, does not dissolve in water, and becomes conductive when wet. Ashfall can collapse roofs, contaminate water supplies and damage machinery.

Hazards to aviation

Ingested ash melts inside jet engines and re-solidifies on turbine blades, potentially causing flameout — as happened to British Airways Flight 9 over Indonesia in 1982. The 2010 eruptions of Eyjafjallajökull in Iceland closed most of European airspace for six days, the largest air-traffic shutdown since the Second World War.

Benefits

Weathered ash forms exceptionally fertile soils (andisols), supporting intensive agriculture in regions such as Java and the slopes of Mount Etna.
