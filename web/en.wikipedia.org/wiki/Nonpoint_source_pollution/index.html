Nonpoint source pollution

Nonpoint source (NPS) pollution is pollution resulting from many diffuse sources, in direct contrast to point source pollution, which results from a single source. Nonpoint source pollution generally comes from land runoff, precipitation, atmospheric deposition, drainage, seepage or hydrological modification (rainfall and snowmelt), where tracing pollution back to a single source is difficult.

Sources

Major sources of nonpoint source pollution include:

  • Agriculture — fertilisers, herbicides, insecticides, animal waste, and sediment from erosion of cropland and pasture.
  • Urban runoff — oil, grease, toxic chemicals and sediment washed from roads, parking lots and construction sites into storm drains and waterways.
  • Forestry and mining — sediment and acid drainage from logging roads, surface mines and abandoned mine sites.
  • Atmospheric deposition — pollutants such as nitrogen compounds and mercury deposited from the air onto land and water surfaces.
  • Hydromodification — channelisation, dams and the removal of riparian vegetation, which increase erosion and alter temperature and sediment loads.

Effects

Nonpoint source pollution is the leading remaining cause of water quality problems in many countries. Nutrient runoff (nitrogen and phosphorus) drives eutrophication and harmful algal blooms in lakes, estuaries and coastal waters, including large hypoxic "dead zones" such as the one in the Gulf of Mexico at the mouth of the Mississippi River. Sediment clouds water, smothering aquatic habitat, while pathogens from animal and human waste can close beaches and shellfish beds.

Regulation and control

Because it cannot be controlled at a single discharge point, NPS pollution is managed through land-use practices known as best management practices (BMPs): buffer strips of vegetation along waterways, conservation tillage, nutrient management planning, constructed wetlands, green infrastructure in cities such as permeable pavement and rain gardens, and erosion controls at construction sites. In the United States, Section 319 of the Clean Water Act provides funding for state nonpoint source management programmes.
