Arthouse cinema

Arthouse cinema refers to films — and the specialised cinemas that show them — aimed at a niche, cinephile audience rather than the mass market. Arthouse films typically emphasise artistic expression, experimentation and serious themes over commercial entertainment formulas.

History

Dedicated venues for artistic film emerged in Paris in the 1920s, and after the Second World War the art-house circuit expanded in Europe and North America, screening foreign-language and independent films. Directors associated with the post-war art cinema include Ingmar Bergman, Federico Fellini, Akira Kurosawa, Satyajit Ray, Michelangelo Antonioni and the French New Wave filmmakers.

Characteristics

Art films often feature ambiguous narratives, open endings, extended duration, non-professional or ensemble casts, and a strong authorial voice associated with the auteur director. Film festivals such as Cannes, Venice and Berlin serve as the primary launchpads for arthouse distribution.

Modern era

The decline of repertory cinemas and the rise of streaming have shifted arthouse viewing to platforms and festival circuits, while distributors such as A24 and Neon have brought auteur-driven films to wider audiences.
