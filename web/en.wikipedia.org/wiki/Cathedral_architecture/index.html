Cathedral architecture

Cathedral architecture refers to the design and construction of cathedrals — churches that contain the seat (cathedra) of a bishop — which have been among the largest and most ambitious buildings of their cultures for nearly two millennia. Cathedral architecture evolved from early Christian basilicas through Byzantine, Romanesque, Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque and modern forms, and its study encompasses structure, liturgical planning, decoration and symbolism.

Plan and elements

Large cathedrals are traditionally cruciform in plan, with a long nave flanked by aisles, transepts crossing near the eastern arm, and a chancel containing the choir and sanctuary. Features commonly include a west front with twin towers, a crossing tower or spire, an ambulatory with radiating chapels around the apse, a cloister, and a chapter house. Orientation places the altar at the east end, so that congregation and priest face the rising sun.

Historical development

Early Christian and Byzantine cathedrals adapted the Roman basilica, while Byzantine builders achieved great domes on pendentives, exemplified by Hagia Sophia in Constantinople. Romanesque cathedrals (c. 1000–1200) used massive walls, round arches and barrel or groin vaults. Gothic architecture, beginning at Saint-Denis near Paris in the 1140s, combined the pointed arch, rib vault and flying buttress to achieve unprecedented height and luminous stained-glass walls, as at Chartres, Amiens and Cologne. The Renaissance returned to classical orders and centralised domes (Florence, St Peter's in Rome), and the Baroque added dramatic movement and decoration. Gothic Revival movements of the 19th century produced cathedrals worldwide, while 20th- and 21st-century examples, such as Coventry, Liverpool Metropolitan and Gaudí's Sagrada Família in Barcelona, employ modern materials and forms.

Construction

Cathedrals were financed by bishops, chapters, monarchs, guilds and popular devotion, and often built over generations or centuries by hereditary lodges of masons guided by master builders. Their construction drove advances in geometry, scaffolding, glassmaking and engineering, and the buildings embodied civic as much as religious pride.
