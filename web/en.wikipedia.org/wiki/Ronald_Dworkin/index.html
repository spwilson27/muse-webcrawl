Ronald Dworkin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ronald Myles Dworkin (11 December 1931 – 14 February 2013) was an American legal philosopher and constitutional-law scholar, successor to H. L. A. Hart at Oxford and professor at Yale and NYU. His Taking Rights Seriously (1977) attacked positivism and utilitarianism together: rights are “trump” against collective goals, and adjudication in hard cases is governed not by judicial discretion but by principles already embedded in legal practice, which the ideal judge (“Hercules”) discovers by constructing the interpretation that best fits and justifies the law as a whole.

In Law's Empire (1986) the theory became “law as integrity”: law is an interpretive, chain-novel enterprise, and there are (usually) right answers in hard cases — the “one right answer” thesis that made him positivism's great antagonist in the Hart–Dworkin debate. Later works extended the system to equality (Sovereign Virtue, 2000; luck egalitarianism), to freedom and bioethics, and to a unified theory of value (Justice for Hedgehogs, 2011). As a public intellectual (chiefly in the New York Review of Books) Dworkin was American liberalism's legal voice — on abortion, campaign finance, torture and the Supreme Court's legitimacy.
