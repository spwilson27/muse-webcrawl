Non-associative algebra

A non-associative algebra (or distributive algebra) is an algebra over a field where the binary multiplication operation is not assumed to be associative. That is, an algebraic structure A is a non-associative algebra over a field K if it is a vector space over K and has a K-bilinear vector multiplication operation, without any requirement that the multiplication be associative.

Examples

  • Lie algebras, in which multiplication (the bracket) satisfies the Jacobi identity and anticommutativity; fundamental in geometry and physics.
  • Jordan algebras, commutative algebras satisfying the Jordan identity (x² · y) · x = x² · (y · x), arising in quantum mechanics and projective geometry.
  • Octonions and more generally Cayley–Dickson constructions, which are alternative but not associative.
  • Cross products of vectors in three and seven dimensions, and Poisson algebras, have non-associative multiplication.

Weaker conditions

Because the general theory is very broad, particular classes are studied under additional identities that weaken associativity rather than abandon it entirely: flexibility, (x · y) · x = x · (y · x); alternativity, in which any subalgebra generated by two elements is associative; and power associativity, in which powers of a single element are well defined. The associator,

[x, y, z] = (x · y) · z − x · (y · z),

measures the failure of associativity, much as the commutator measures the failure of commutativity. Non-associative structures appear in differential geometry, mathematical physics (including string theory and quantum mechanics), genetics algebras in mathematical biology, and the theory of Moufang loops and quasigroups.
