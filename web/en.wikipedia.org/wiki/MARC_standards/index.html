MARC standards

MARC (MAchine-Readable Cataloging) is a set of digital standards for the representation and exchange of bibliographic and related information in machine-readable form. Developed at the Library of Congress in the 1960s under the leadership of Henriette Avram, MARC made it possible for libraries to share catalogue records electronically and became the foundation of modern library automation. The current format, MARC 21, harmonised the American and Canadian national formats in 1997 and is maintained by the Library of Congress.

Structure of a MARC record

A MARC record consists of a leader (fixed-length data describing the record), a directory (an index of the fields present), and variable fields. Content is organised into numbered tags of three digits: control fields (001–009) hold data such as the record number, while data fields use two indicator characters and subfields delimited by codes (e.g. $a). Key fields include 100 for the main author entry, 245 for the title and statement of responsibility, 260/264 for publication data, 300 for physical description, and 650 for topical subject headings.

MARC formats

MARC 21 defines five communication formats: bibliographic data (describing books, serials, maps, music, and other resources), authority data (standardised names and subjects), holdings data, classification data, and community information. Historically, national variants such as USMARC, CAN/MARC and UNIMARC (maintained by IFLA and widely used in Europe) developed in parallel; MARC 21 is now dominant in North America and many other countries.

History

Work began at the Library of Congress in 1965–66; a pilot project distributed catalogue records on magnetic tape to participating libraries, and by 1969 the MARC Distribution Service was operating routinely. MARC records enabled union catalogues and cooperative cataloguing networks such as OCLC's WorldCat, transforming cataloguing from a local into a shared enterprise. In 2002 the MARCXML schema provided an XML expression of the format.

Criticism and future

MARC has been criticised for its arcane encoding, its origins in card-catalogue practice, and its limited compatibility with web technologies and linked data. The Library of Congress's BIBFRAME initiative aims to provide a linked-data replacement, but the enormous installed base of MARC records — hundreds of millions worldwide — means MARC is expected to remain in use for many years.
