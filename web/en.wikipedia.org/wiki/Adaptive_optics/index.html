Adaptive optics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Adaptive optics (AO) is a technology that improves the performance of optical systems by reducing the effect of wavefront distortions — in astronomy, the blurring caused by turbulence in Earth's atmosphere, which makes stars twinkle and normally limits a large ground-based telescope to the resolution of a much smaller one. An AO system measures the distortion hundreds or thousands of times per second, usually with a wavefront sensor analysing light from a bright reference star, and cancels it in real time with a deformable mirror whose shape is adjusted by many small actuators.

How it works

Light from the target is split: part goes to a Shack–Hartmann wavefront sensor, an array of lenslets that measures local wavefront slopes, and a computer reconstructs the distortion and commands the deformable mirror to apply the opposite shape. Because few suitably bright natural guide stars lie close to arbitrary targets, many systems create an artificial laser guide star by exciting sodium atoms in the mesosphere at about 90 km altitude (or by Rayleigh scattering at lower altitude), allowing AO correction over most of the sky. The corrected field of view is small (the isoplanatic patch), and correction is best in the infrared, where atmospheric effects are weaker — in the near-infrared, 8–10 m telescopes with AO routinely reach their diffraction limit and can out-resolve the Hubble Space Telescope.

History and applications

The concept was proposed by Horace Babcock in 1953 but became practical only with fast computers and deformable mirrors; it was developed in classified military programmes in the United States in the 1970s–80s and declassified in 1991, after which it spread rapidly to major observatories such as Keck, the Very Large Telescope and Gemini. AO has enabled direct imaging of exoplanets, sharp views of the Galactic Centre's orbiting stars, and resolved studies of solar system bodies. Outside astronomy, related methods are used in retinal imaging (revealing individual photoreceptor cells in the living eye), microscopy, and free-space laser communications.
