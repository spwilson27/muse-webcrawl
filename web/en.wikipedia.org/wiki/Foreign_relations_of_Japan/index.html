Foreign relations of Japan

The foreign relations of Japan are conducted by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Since the end of the Second World War, Japanese foreign policy has been anchored in the alliance with the United States, membership in international institutions, and an emphasis on economic diplomacy, while Article 9 of the Japanese constitution renounces war as a sovereign right.

United States and security policy

The Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security between the United States and Japan (1960) underpins Japanese security; roughly 50,000 U.S. troops are stationed in Japan, mostly in Okinawa. In the 2010s and 2020s Japan moved toward a more proactive defense posture, including a 2015 reinterpretation permitting limited collective self-defense, a plan to raise defense spending toward 2 percent of GDP, and the "Free and Open Indo-Pacific" strategy, which also underlies the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad) with the United States, India, and Australia.

Regional relations

Relations with China combine deep economic interdependence with strategic rivalry and disputes over the Senkaku/Diaoyu Islands and wartime history. Relations with South Korea have fluctuated over historical issues including comfort women and forced labor. Japan has no peace treaty formally ending the Second World War with Russia, owing to the Kuril Islands (Northern Territories) dispute, and supports sanctions on North Korea over its nuclear program and the unresolved abduction of Japanese citizens.

Multilateral role

Japan is a member of the United Nations (a frequent non-permanent Security Council member and long-standing candidate for a permanent seat), the G7, G20, APEC, and the World Trade Organization, and has been one of the world's largest providers of official development assistance.
