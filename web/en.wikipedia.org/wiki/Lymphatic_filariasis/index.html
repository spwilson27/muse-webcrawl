Lymphatic filariasis

Lymphatic filariasis is a parasitic disease caused by infection with nematode worms of the family Filariidae and transmitted to humans through the bites of infected mosquitoes. It is classified as a neglected tropical disease. In severe cases, damage to the lymphatic system leads to the painful and profoundly disfiguring swelling of body parts known as elephantiasis, as well as lymphoedema and, in men, hydrocele (swelling of the scrotum).

Cause and transmission

Three species of thread-like parasitic worms cause the disease: Wuchereria bancrofti, responsible for about 90% of cases, and Brugia malayi and Brugia timori. The adult worms live in the human lymphatic system and produce microscopic larvae called microfilariae, which circulate in the blood. When a mosquito of genera such as Culex, Anopheles, Aedes or Mansonia feeds on an infected person, the larvae develop inside the insect and can be passed to another human host. Infection is usually acquired in childhood, and repeated exposure over months to years is typically required.

Signs and symptoms

Most infections are asymptomatic, although the lymphatic system is still damaged. Symptomatic disease includes acute episodes of local inflammation of the skin, lymph nodes and lymphatic vessels, often accompanied by fever, which may be triggered by secondary bacterial infection. Over time, chronic lymphoedema develops; the gross enlargement of limbs or other body parts in elephantiasis can cause disability, stigma and mental-health problems. In men, hydrocele can become very large. Damage to the kidneys (chyluria) may also occur.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis historically relied on finding microfilariae in a blood smear, with blood ideally drawn at night because of the parasites' nocturnal periodicity. Antigen detection tests, such as the filarial test strip, can identify W. bancrofti infection without microscopy. Ultrasound may reveal adult worms in the lymphatics (the "filarial dance sign").

Preventive chemotherapy through mass drug administration is the mainstay of control: whole at-risk populations are given annual single doses of combinations of albendazole with ivermectin or diethylcarbamazine (DEC). Individual care includes meticulous hygiene of swollen limbs, skin care, exercise and elevation to reduce lymphoedema, and surgery for hydrocele.

Elimination efforts

The World Health Organization launched the Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis in 2000, aiming to interrupt transmission through mass drug administration and to alleviate suffering through morbidity management. Hundreds of millions of people have been treated, and a number of countries have been validated as having eliminated the disease as a public health problem, though it remains endemic in parts of Africa and Asia.
