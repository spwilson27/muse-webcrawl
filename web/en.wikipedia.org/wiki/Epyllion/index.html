Epyllion

An epyllion (Greek: ἐπύλλιον, plural: epyllia) is a brief narrative poem in dactylic hexameters or elegiac couplets, popular in the Hellenistic period and later in Roman poetry. The term, meaning "little epic", is modern — its use by ancient critics is uncertain — but the form is recognisable: a short mythological tale, often with a story-within-a-story, learned allusion, and focus on pathos and lesser-known episodes.

FormShort narrative poem
EraHellenistic, Roman

Characteristics

Epyllia typically run a few hundred lines, treat obscure myths with scholarly detail (aetiology), pause for extended ekphrasis — such as the coverlet depicting Ariadne in Catullus 64 — and favour emotional, often female, perspectives over heroic action.

Examples

Callimachus' Hecale told of Theseus sheltered by a poor old woman; Moschus' Europa retells Zeus's abduction of Europa. In Latin, the neoteric poets made the form fashionable: Catullus' Poem 64, on Peleus and Thetis with the inset tale of Ariadne, is the supreme surviving example, influencing Virgil's inset narratives and Ovid.

Scholarship

Whether "epyllion" names a true ancient genre or a modern convenience is debated, but the cluster of shared features — brevity, learning, digression, pathos — remains a useful description of Hellenistic taste against grand epic.
