Architecture of Chicago


The Chicago skyline along the Chicago River.

The architecture of Chicago has influenced and been reflected in the development of American architecture. The city of Chicago, Illinois features prominent buildings in a variety of styles by many important architects. Since most structures in the central city were destroyed by the Great Chicago Fire in 1871, Chicago buildings are noted for their originality rather than their antiquity.

Chicago is world-famous for its role in the birth of the skyscraper. The rebuilding after the fire, combined with soaring land values in the Loop, encouraged steel-frame high-rise construction, fireproofing and large plate-glass windows.

The Chicago School

The first "Chicago School" flourished in the 1880s and 1890s. Its architects — including William Le Baron Jenney, Daniel Burnham, John Wellborn Root, William Holabird, Martin Roche and Louis Sullivan — pioneered the steel skeleton frame, exemplified by Jenney's Home Insurance Building (1885), often called the first skyscraper. Sullivan's Carson, Pirie, Scott Building and his dictum "form follows function" epitomized the school's commercial classicism.

The World's Columbian Exposition

The World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, directed by Burnham, showcased the neoclassical "White City" and helped launch the City Beautiful movement, though it also marked a turn away from the Chicago School's structural functionalism toward Beaux-Arts historicism, visible in many later civic buildings.

Prairie School

Around the turn of the twentieth century, Frank Lloyd Wright and his contemporaries developed the Prairie School of residential architecture: low, horizontal houses with overhanging eaves, open plans and integration with the flat Midwestern landscape. Wright's Robie House (1910) in Hyde Park is a landmark of the style.

Second Chicago School and modernism

After the Second World War, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, teaching at the Illinois Institute of Technology, defined a "Second Chicago School" of minimalist steel-and-glass towers, such as the Lake Shore Drive apartments (1951). Firms such as Skidmore, Owings & Merrill produced landmarks including the John Hancock Center (1969), with its distinctive X-bracing, and the Willis Tower (1973, formerly Sears Tower), for decades the world's tallest building, designed by Fazlur Rahman Khan and Bruce Graham.
