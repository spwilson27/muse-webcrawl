Private Finance Initiative

The Private Finance Initiative (PFI) was a United Kingdom government procurement policy aimed at creating "public–private partnerships" (PPPs) where private firms are contracted to complete and manage public projects. Initially launched in 1992 by the Conservative government of Prime Minister John Major, and expanded considerably by the Labour government of Tony Blair, PFI was part of the wider programme of privatisation and macroeconomic reform favoured by the governments of the 1990s and 2000s.


Many schools and hospitals in the UK were built under PFI contracts.

Under PFI, a private sector consortium typically forms a special purpose vehicle (SPV) to design, build, finance, and operate a facility (such as a hospital or school) for a concession period, commonly 25 to 30 years. The public authority pays an annual "unitary charge" covering capital costs, maintenance, and services. At the end of the contract, ownership typically transfers to the public sector.

Development

PFI was introduced following earlier measures such as the Ryrie Rules, which had constrained private finance for public projects. The Blair government expanded PFI to cover hundreds of projects, including schools, hospitals, roads, prisons, and defence facilities. By the late 2000s there were over 700 operational PFI contracts with a combined capital value in the tens of billions of pounds.

Criticism

The scheme has been widely criticised. Critics argue that borrowing through PFI is more expensive than conventional government borrowing, that contracts are inflexible, and that the policy was used to keep debt off the government's balance sheet. The National Audit Office and parliamentary committees published several critical reports. Following the collapse of the construction and services firm Carillion in 2018, the Chancellor announced in that year's Budget that the government would no longer use PFI or its successor model, PF2.
