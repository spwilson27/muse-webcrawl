Angle of attack

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fluid dynamics, angle of attack (AOA, Greek letter α) is the angle between a reference line on a body (often the chord line of an airfoil) and the vector representing the relative motion between the body and the fluid through which it is moving. Angle of attack is the angle between the body's reference line and the oncoming flow. This article is about the general concept in fluid mechanics; in aeronautics the term usually refers to the angle between an aircraft wing's chord and the relative wind.

Effect on lift

For a fixed-wing aircraft, lift increases roughly linearly with angle of attack over the normal operating range. The lift coefficient CL rises with slope about 0.1 per degree (2π per radian for an ideal thin airfoil) until the wing approaches the critical angle of attack — typically around 15–18 degrees for a simple airfoil — beyond which airflow separates from the upper surface, lift falls away, and the wing stalls. Angle of attack, not airspeed alone, determines whether a wing stalls: a stall can occur at any speed if the critical angle is exceeded, a fact central to pilot training.

Terminology and related concepts

Angle of attack must be distinguished from the pitch angle (the orientation of the aircraft relative to the horizon) and from the angle of incidence (the fixed angle between the wing chord and the aircraft's longitudinal axis). In manoeuvring flight the three angles differ. Helicopters, propellers, and wind-turbine blades have their own blade angles of attack, and wind turbines actively control blade pitch to regulate them. High-performance fighters can operate at extreme ("high-alpha") angles beyond 30 degrees using thrust vectoring and vortex lift, as in the Pugachev's Cobra manoeuvre.

Measurement

Modern aircraft measure angle of attack with vanes or probes on the fuselage and display it to pilots directly or feed it to flight-control and stall-warning systems (stick shakers and stick pushers). Erroneous AOA data has been implicated in several accidents, which has driven requirements for multiple independent sensors.

Sailing and other applications

The term is also used in sailing (angle between sail chord and apparent wind), in the design of hydrofoils, keels, and racing-car wings, and for the attitude of re-entering spacecraft, where a high angle of attack uses atmospheric drag to shed speed.
