Cuprate superconductor

Cuprates are layered perovskite-like materials built around copper–oxygen planes.

Cuprate superconductors are a family of copper-oxide ceramics that exhibit high-temperature superconductivity, with critical temperatures far above those of conventional superconductors. Their discovery in 1986 by Georg Bednorz and K. Alex Müller revolutionized condensed-matter physics and earned the pair the 1987 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Structure and families

All cuprates share sheets of copper and oxygen atoms (CuO2 planes) separated by layers containing other elements that act as charge reservoirs. Major families include lanthanum barium copper oxide (the original 1986 material, Tc ≈ 35 K), yttrium barium copper oxide YBa2Cu3O7 (YBCO, Tc ≈ 93 K — the first superconductor above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen), bismuth strontium calcium copper oxide (BSCCO), thallium- and mercury-based cuprates, the last reaching about 133 K at ambient pressure and ~164 K under pressure.

Physics

The undoped parent compounds are Mott insulators and antiferromagnets, not metals. Superconductivity appears when they are doped with holes or electrons, producing a celebrated phase diagram with a "pseudogap" regime and a dome of superconductivity. The pairing symmetry is predominantly d-wave, and the pairing mechanism remains one of the great unsolved problems of physics.

Applications

YBCO and BSCCO are manufactured as tapes and wires for high-field magnets, power cables, fault-current limiters and precision instruments, though their brittleness and cost limit wider use.
