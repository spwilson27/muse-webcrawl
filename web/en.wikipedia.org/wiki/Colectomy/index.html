Colectomy

Colectomy removes part or all of the colon; the type is named for the segment resected.

Colectomy is the surgical removal of any extent of the colon (large intestine). Removal of the entire colon is a total colectomy; removal of part is a partial or segmental colectomy, named by region—right or left hemicolectomy, sigmoid colectomy, and so on. Colectomy combined with removal of the rectum is a proctocolectomy.

Indications

Common indications include colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease) that fails medical therapy or develops dysplasia, diverticulitis with complications, bowel obstruction or perforation, massive lower gastrointestinal bleeding, ischaemic colitis, and inherited polyposis syndromes such as familial adenomatous polyposis, for which prophylactic colectomy may be recommended.

Procedure

The operation is performed by open laparotomy or laparoscopically/robotically. After the diseased segment is resected, the remaining bowel ends may be joined (anastomosis) or brought out as a stoma—an ileostomy or colostomy—which may be temporary or permanent. In total proctocolectomy for ulcerative colitis, an ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (J-pouch) can avoid a permanent stoma.

Risks and recovery

Risks include anastomotic leak, infection, bleeding, injury to adjacent organs and later adhesions or hernia. Recovery typically involves several days in hospital and gradual return to normal diet; after extensive resection, bowel frequency increases.
