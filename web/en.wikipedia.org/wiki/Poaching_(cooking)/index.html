Poaching (cooking)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Eggs poaching in swirling water in a shallow pan]

Poaching is a moist-heat cooking technique in which food is submerged in liquid — water, stock, milk or wine — held at a temperature well below boiling, roughly 70–85 °C (160–180 °F), so the liquid barely trembles. The gentleness of poaching suits delicate foods that would break apart or toughen at a boil: eggs, fish, shellfish, chicken breast and fruit.

The most famous poached dish is the poached egg, with its set white and runny yolk, served on toast or as the centrepiece of eggs Benedict. Classic French cooking poaches whole fish in a flavoured court bouillon, and fruit poached in spiced syrup or wine is a traditional dessert.

Method

The liquid should shiver but not bubble; a thermometer removes the guesswork. Food may be poached shallow (partly covered, in a pan) or deep (fully submerged). Buttered paper or a lid helps hold heat evenly. Because no browning occurs, poaching gives clean, delicate flavours and moist results, especially when the cooking liquid is itself seasoned and later used as a sauce base.

Poached eggs

For poached eggs, fresh eggs are lowered into barely simmering water, sometimes with a splash of vinegar to help the white cohere, and cooked about three minutes. A swirl in the water wraps the white around the yolk. The technique is compared with boiling (in-shell) and frying in the family of egg cookery.

Other poached foods

Salmon, chicken, quenelles, pears in red wine, and Chinese "white cut" chicken are all traditionally poached. Poaching in oil or butter at low temperature is a related confit technique.
