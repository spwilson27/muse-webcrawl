Milk can escape

The milk can escape is a famous escape act in the repertoire of escapology, popularized by the Hungarian-American magician Harry Houdini. In the act, the performer is handcuffed and sealed inside an oversized milk can filled with water, then hidden from view by a curtain while he effects his escape.

Origins

Houdini first performed the milk can escape in St. Louis, Missouri, in January 1908, and it remained in his repertoire for much of his career. The can was a galvanized steel vessel slightly larger than a standard milk churn, filled to the brim with water. Houdini, wearing handcuffs, was submerged in the can; the lid, onto which several padlocks could be fastened, was secured, and assistants drew a curtain around the apparatus.

Performance

Before the performance, audience members were invited to hold their breath along with Houdini to appreciate the duration of the submersion. Assistants stood by with axes, ostensibly ready to smash the can open in an emergency. After a period that sometimes exceeded three minutes, Houdini would emerge from behind the curtain, dripping wet, with the can still apparently sealed and padlocked.

Method and legacy

The secret of the escape lay in the construction of the can: the neck and lid were designed so that, once the outer padlocked hasps were engaged, the performer could rotate the inner collar from within and free himself, since the locks secured only an outer ring rather than the lid itself. As with many of Houdini's methods, the details were guarded secrets during his lifetime. The milk can escape became one of the most imitated acts in magic and remains a standard reference point in the history of escape artistry.
