Claudication

Intermittent claudication is leg pain on walking caused by narrowed arteries, most often due to peripheral artery disease.

Claudication is a medical term usually referring to impairment in walking, or pain, discomfort, numbness, or tiredness in the legs that occurs during walking and is relieved by rest. The perceived level of pain from claudication can be mild to extremely severe. Claudication is most common in the calves but can also affect the feet, thighs, hips, buttocks, or arms. The word claudication comes from the Latin claudicare, meaning "to limp".

Claudication that appears after a short walking distance and eases within minutes of standing still is called intermittent claudication and is a classic symptom of peripheral artery disease (PAD), in which atherosclerotic narrowing of the leg arteries fails to meet the increased oxygen demand of exercising muscle. It is conventionally described using the Fontaine or Rutherford classifications of limb ischaemia.

Causes

The great majority of cases result from atherosclerosis; risk factors include smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia and advancing age. Claudication-like symptoms may also arise from spinal stenosis (neurogenic or pseudoclaudication), in which pain is provoked by walking or standing and relieved by sitting or flexing the spine, as well as from popliteal artery entrapment, chronic compartment syndrome, venous insufficiency and musculoskeletal disease.

Diagnosis

Assessment includes palpation of pulses and measurement of the ankle–brachial pressure index (ABPI): a resting ABPI below 0.9 supports a diagnosis of PAD. Duplex ultrasound, CT angiography or MR angiography may localise the disease; exercise ABPI testing helps distinguish vascular from neurogenic claudication.

Treatment

Management centres on cardiovascular risk reduction — smoking cessation, antiplatelet therapy, statins, and control of blood pressure and diabetes — together with supervised exercise therapy, which substantially improves walking distance. The drug cilostazol may be used in selected patients. For limiting symptoms despite conservative treatment, endovascular angioplasty or surgical bypass may be offered. Claudication itself rarely progresses to limb loss, but it is a marker of systemic atherosclerosis and elevated risk of myocardial infarction and stroke.
