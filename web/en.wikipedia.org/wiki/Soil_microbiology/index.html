Soil microbiology

Soil microbiology is the study of microorganisms in soil, their functions, and how they affect soil properties. It is believed that between two and four billion years ago, the first ancient bacteria and microorganisms came about on Earth's oceans. These bacteria could fix nitrogen, in time multiplied, and as a result released oxygen into the atmosphere. This led to more advanced microorganisms, which were important because they affect soil structure and fertility. Soil microorganisms can be classified as bacteria, actinomycetes, fungi, algae and protozoa. Each of these groups has characteristics that define them and their functions in soil.

Up to 10 billion bacterial cells inhabit each gram of soil in and around plant roots, a region known as the rhizosphere. In 2011, a team detected more than 33,000 bacterial and archaeal species on sugar beet roots.

Bacteria

Bacteria are the smallest and most numerous organisms in soil. They perform many essential functions, including decomposition of organic matter, nitrogen fixation, nitrification, and denitrification. Free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as Azotobacter and symbiotic fixers such as Rhizobium, which live in the root nodules of leguminous plants, convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms usable by plants.

Fungi

Soil fungi include yeasts, moulds and mushrooms. They are important decomposers of tough plant materials such as cellulose and lignin. Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, extending the root system through networks of hyphae and helping plants absorb water and nutrients, particularly phosphorus, in exchange for sugars.

Actinomycetes

Actinomycetes are filamentous bacteria that give freshly turned soil its characteristic earthy smell, largely through the compound geosmin. Many actinomycetes, especially members of the genus Streptomyces, produce antibiotics of major medical importance.

Algae and protozoa

Photosynthetic algae live near the soil surface and contribute organic matter. Protozoa feed on bacteria and other microorganisms, releasing nutrients such as nitrogen in forms plants can use, and helping regulate microbial populations.

Importance

Soil microorganisms drive nutrient cycling, maintain soil structure, suppress plant diseases, and influence the global carbon cycle. Their study underpins sustainable agriculture, bioremediation and the understanding of soil health.
