Polio

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polio (poliomyelitis) is a highly infectious viral disease caused by poliovirus, which spreads mainly through contaminated water and food. Most infections cause no symptoms or a mild fever, but in a minority the virus invades the nervous system and destroys motor neurons, causing irreversible paralysis — historically most feared in children, hence its older name 'infantile paralysis'.

Disease and history

Paralysis typically affects the legs asymmetrically; involvement of breathing muscles could be fatal, and severely affected patients were sustained in 'iron lung' negative-pressure ventilators. Epidemics struck industrialised countries each summer in the early 20th century, peaking in the United States in 1952.

Vaccination and eradication

Jonas Salk's injectable inactivated vaccine (1955) and Albert Sabin's oral vaccine (early 1960s) transformed control. The Global Polio Eradication Initiative, launched in 1988 when polio paralysed an estimated 350,000 children a year, has cut cases by over 99%; wild poliovirus remains endemic only in Afghanistan and Pakistan, though vaccine-derived outbreaks require continuing campaigns. Some survivors develop post-polio syndrome decades later.
