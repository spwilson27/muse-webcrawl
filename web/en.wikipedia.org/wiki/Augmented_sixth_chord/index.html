Augmented sixth chord

In music theory, an augmented sixth chord is a chord that contains the interval of an augmented sixth, usually above its bass tone. Conventionally, the augmented sixth is built on the submediant (scale degree 6) in a major or minor key and resolves outward to the dominant. Augmented sixth chords are characteristic of the music of the Classical and Romantic eras and were typically used to intensify the approach to the dominant at cadences.

The most common forms contain the lowered submediant (♭6) and the raised subdominant (#4), which form the augmented sixth interval. The chord normally resolves with ♭6 falling to 5 and #4 rising to 5, producing an octave on the dominant scale degree.

Types

Three varieties are conventionally distinguished, named—somewhat fancifully—after national "schools" in 19th-century theory:

Italian augmented sixth

The Italian sixth (It+6) consists of ♭6, 1, and #4—three distinct pitch classes, with the tonic usually doubled. In C major/minor: A♭–C–F#. It may be analysed as a modified subdominant chord.

French augmented sixth

The French sixth (Fr+6) adds the raised supertonic (#2) to the Italian: ♭6, 1, #2, and #4. In C: A♭–C–D#–F#. Its pitch content resembles an altered dominant seventh chord and it has a distinctive whole-tone flavour.

German augmented sixth

The German sixth (Ger+6) adds the flattened mediant (♭3 in major; the ordinary third degree in minor): ♭6, 1, ♭3, #4. In C: A♭–C–E♭–F#. It is enharmonically equivalent to a dominant seventh chord. When resolving to the dominant, the German sixth usually passes through a cadential six-four chord to avoid parallel fifths.

Resolution

The augmented sixth interval resolves outward by semitone to an octave on the dominant. The chords most often precede V, either directly or via the cadential I6/4–V progression. Composers from Mozart and Beethoven to Chopin and Wagner exploited the chord's chromatic tension; in later Romantic music it became a springboard for modulation and increasingly free chromatic harmony.
