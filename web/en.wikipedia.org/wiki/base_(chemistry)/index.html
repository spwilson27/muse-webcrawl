Base (chemistry)

In chemistry, there are three definitions in common use of the word base: Arrhenius bases, Brønsted bases, and Lewis bases. All definitions agree that bases are substances that react with acids, as proposed by Guillaume-François Rouelle in the mid-18th century. An Arrhenius base increases the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH−) in aqueous solution; a Brønsted base is a proton acceptor; a Lewis base donates a pair of electrons.

Properties

Bases feel slippery to the touch (as in soap), taste bitter, turn red litmus paper blue, and react with acids in neutralization reactions to form salts and water. Bases with a pH greater than 7 are more basic the higher the pH. Bases can be either strong or weak: strong bases such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) dissociate completely in water, whereas weak bases such as ammonia (NH3) ionize only partially.

Metal oxides and alkalis

Metal oxides and hydroxides are typically basic; soluble metal hydroxides are called alkalis. Common examples include sodium hydroxide (caustic soda), potassium hydroxide, and calcium hydroxide (slaked lime). Ammonia solution is a widely used weak alkali, even though ammonia contains no hydroxide ion itself — it generates hydroxide by reacting with water.

Uses

Bases have many applications: sodium hydroxide is used in soap-making, paper production, and drain cleaners; calcium hydroxide in construction and agriculture; sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) as a leavening agent and mild antacid; magnesium hydroxide as an antacid and laxative. In laboratories, bases are essential reagents and are used to standardize titration procedures alongside acids.

Bases in organic chemistry

Organic bases include amines, pyridine, and strong non-nucleophilic bases such as LDA (lithium diisopropylamide) used to deprotonate substrates in synthesis. Superbases, such as organolithium compounds and phosphazene bases, are far stronger than hydroxide and enable otherwise difficult transformations.
