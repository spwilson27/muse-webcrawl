Al-Azhar Mosque

Al-Azhar Mosque (Arabic: الجامع الأزهر, al-Jāmiʿ al-ʾAzhar, "the resplendent mosque") is a congregational mosque in Islamic Cairo, Egypt, founded by the Fatimid general Jawhar al-Siqilli shortly after the Fatimid conquest of Egypt. The first mosque built in the new city of Cairo, it held its first Friday prayer in 972 CE. Its name is generally thought to derive from az-Zahrāʾ ("the radiant"), an epithet of Fatimah, daughter of Muhammad. Closely attached to the mosque, Al-Azhar University grew into the world's foremost centre of Sunni Islamic learning.

Al-Azhar Mosque, Cairo — founded 970–972 CE.

Architecture and history

The original mosque consisted of a courtyard surrounded by arcades; successive dynasties — Fatimid, Ayyubid, Mamluk and Ottoman — enlarged it, adding the minarets that now give it a distinctive skyline, including the minaret of Sultan Qaitbay (1510) and the double-finial minaret of Abd el-Rahman Katkhuda (1753). Under Saladin the mosque lost its role as a centre of Ismaili teaching and was restored to prominence under the Mamluks, when its madrasas made it a hub of Sunni scholarship. Teaching at al-Azhar dates to 975 CE; the institution was formally chartered as a university in 1961, adding secular faculties to its religious core.
