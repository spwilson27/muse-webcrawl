Edward Condon

Edward U. Condon

Born: March 2, 1902, Alamogordo, New Mexico, U.S.

Died: March 26, 1974 (aged 72), Boulder, Colorado, U.S.

Fields: Nuclear physics, quantum mechanics

Known for: Franck–Condon principle; director of the National Bureau of Standards; Condon Report on UFOs

Edward Uhler Condon (March 2, 1902 – March 26, 1974) was an American nuclear physicist, a pioneer in quantum mechanics, and a participant in the development of radar and nuclear weapons during World War II as part of the Manhattan Project. The Franck–Condon principle and the Slater–Condon rules are named after him, as is the lunar crater Condon.

Early life and education

Condon was born in Alamogordo, New Mexico, and raised largely in California. He worked as a journalist before studying physics at the University of California, Berkeley, where he received his PhD in 1926 under Raymond T. Birge. He held postdoctoral positions in Göttingen and Munich, where he worked in the formative milieu of quantum mechanics, and co-authored an early English-language treatise on the subject.

Career

Condon taught at Princeton University and the University of Minnesota, then moved into industrial research at Bell Telephone Laboratories and Westinghouse Electric, where he became associate director of research. During the Second World War he advised on radar and worked briefly at Los Alamos before resigning over security concerns. After the war he served as director of the National Bureau of Standards (now NIST) from 1945 to 1951, and was an influential advocate of civilian control of atomic energy.

Security controversies

Condon was repeatedly targeted during the early Cold War: in 1948 the House Un-American Activities Committee called him "one of the weakest links in our atomic security", and his security clearance was revoked and restored several times amid political controversy. He resigned from government service and later became a professor at Washington University in St. Louis and then the University of Colorado Boulder.

Condon Report

From 1966 to 1968 Condon directed the University of Colorado UFO Project, funded by the U.S. Air Force. Its final report, the Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects (the "Condon Report"), concluded that further scientific study of UFOs was unlikely to yield significant results, a conclusion that contributed to the closure of Project Blue Book, though the report's findings were contested by some researchers.
