Genetic diversity

Genetic diversity
FieldPopulation genetics, conservation biology
MeasuresHeterozygosity, allelic richness, nucleotide diversity
SourcesMutation, recombination, gene flow
ThreatsDrift, inbreeding, population bottlenecks, habitat fragmentation

Genetic diversity is the total number of genetic characteristics in the genetic makeup of a species or population. It is the variation in alleles and genotypes present in a group, and it serves as a way for populations to adapt to changing environments: with more variation, it is more likely that some individuals will possess variants suited to new conditions.

Diverse maize varieties illustrate within-species genetic diversity.

Importance

Genetic diversity underpins the survival and adaptability of species. Populations with low diversity are vulnerable to inbreeding depression, accumulated harmful recessive alleles, and uniform susceptibility to disease — as seen when genetically uniform crops succumb to epidemics. The cheetah is a classic wildlife example: a historical population bottleneck left the species with exceptionally low diversity. Genetic diversity is measured by statistics such as observed and expected heterozygosity and nucleotide diversity (π).

Loss and conservation

Diversity is generated by mutation and recombination and is eroded by genetic drift, especially in small populations, and by bottlenecks and founder effects. Conservation genetics seeks to preserve diversity through habitat protection, wildlife corridors, captive breeding managed to avoid inbreeding, and seed banks and gene banks that store the diversity of crops and their wild relatives.
