Mandated reporter

A mandated reporter is a person who is legally required to report suspected child abuse or neglect (and in some jurisdictions, abuse of elderly or dependent adults) to the appropriate authorities. Mandated reporting laws exist in the United States, Australia, Canada and a number of other countries.

Who is covered

The categories of mandated reporters vary by jurisdiction but commonly include teachers and school staff, physicians and other health care workers, nurses, mental health professionals, social workers, police officers, childcare providers and clergy. In some US states, the duty extends to all adults, making every citizen a mandated reporter.

The duty to report

The obligation is generally triggered by a "reasonable suspicion" or "reasonable cause to believe" that abuse or neglect has occurred — a lower standard than certainty, and the reporter is not expected to investigate. Reports are usually made to child protective services or a designated hotline, and most laws grant immunity from civil and criminal liability for reports made in good faith. Failure to report can result in fines, imprisonment, or loss of a professional licence.

History and debate

Mandatory reporting began in the United States in the 1960s, following the influential work of paediatrician C. Henry Kempe on battered child syndrome, and was encouraged nationwide by the federal Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act of 1974. Debates continue over the balance between protecting children and the effects of over-reporting, which can overwhelm child protection systems and disproportionately affect poor and minority families.
