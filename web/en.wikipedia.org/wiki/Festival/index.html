Festival

A festival is an event ordinarily celebrated by a community and centering on some characteristic aspect or aspects of that community's religion or culture. Festivals often serve to fulfill specific communal purposes, marking the passage of time, honoring deities or ancestors, celebrating harvests, or reinforcing social bonds. The word derives from the Latin festum (feast) via Old French.

Types

  • Religious festivals — among the oldest: Christmas and Easter (Christianity), Ramadan and Eid (Islam), Diwali and Holi (Hinduism), Passover and Hanukkah (Judaism), Vesak (Buddhism), and many others, often tied to liturgical calendars.
  • Seasonal and harvest festivals — such as Thanksgiving, the Mid-Autumn Festival, and Oktoberfest (originating as a Bavarian royal celebration), marking agricultural cycles.
  • Carnivals — public celebrations with parades, street parties, and masquerade, notably in Rio de Janeiro, Venice, and New Orleans (Mardi Gras), traditionally preceding Lent.
  • Arts festivals — film festivals (Cannes, Sundance, Venice), music festivals (Glastonbury, Coachella, Bayreuth), theater festivals (Edinburgh Festival Fringe, the world's largest arts festival), and literary festivals.
  • Food and drink festivals — celebrating local produce, wine, beer, or cuisine.

History and significance

Festivals are documented from ancient Egypt, Greece (the Olympic Games began as a religious festival), and Rome (Saturnalia). Scholars such as Émile Durkheim analyzed festivals as moments of "collective effervescence" that renew solidarity; Mikhail Bakhtin's work on carnival emphasized temporary inversion of hierarchy. In the modern era, festivals are also major economic drivers, attracting tourism and supporting local industries, while some have grown into global commercial events.
