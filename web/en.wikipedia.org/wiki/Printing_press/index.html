Printing press

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A wooden Gutenberg-style screw printing press with a compositor setting metal type]

A printing press is a mechanical device for applying pressure to an inked surface resting on a print medium (such as paper or cloth), thereby transferring the ink. The movable-type printing press developed by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 in Mainz, Germany, transformed communication and is often called one of the most influential inventions of the second millennium.

Printing existed earlier in China and Korea — including movable type — but Gutenberg's system (a hand mould for mass-producing metal type, oil-based inks, and a screw press) enabled cheap mass production of books in Europe.

Impact

Within decades, presses operated across Europe and book production rose from thousands of hand-copied volumes to millions of printed ones. Cheap books and pamphlets fed the Renaissance, the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution and rising literacy.

Technology

Early presses could print a few hundred pages a day. Steam-powered presses (1814, The Times) and later rotary presses and offset printing industrialised the process.
