Rotor (electric)

The rotor is the rotating component of an electric motor, electric generator, or alternator, interacting with the stationary part, the stator, through the magnetic field across the air gap between them. In a motor, torque is applied to the rotor's shaft; in a generator, mechanical torque applied to the shaft induces electric current in the windings.

Construction

A rotor typically consists of a laminated steel core — laminated to reduce eddy-current losses — mounted on a shaft and carrying either windings or permanent magnets. In large generators the rotor may weigh many tonnes and carry the field winding, fed with direct current through slip rings and brushes or by a brushless exciter.

Types

Squirrel-cage rotor

The most common type in induction motors, the squirrel-cage rotor carries bars of copper or aluminium embedded in slots in the core and short-circuited by end rings, resembling a cage. Current is induced in the bars by the stator's rotating field; the rotor needs no external electrical connection, making it rugged and cheap.

Wound rotor

In a wound-rotor (slip-ring) motor the rotor has a true three-phase winding connected to slip rings, allowing external resistance to be inserted for starting torque and speed control.

Permanent-magnet and salient-pole rotors

Synchronous machines use either salient-pole rotors with projecting wound poles (typical of low-speed hydroelectric generators) or smooth cylindrical rotors (used in high-speed turbogenerators). Permanent-magnet rotors, increasingly common in servo motors, electric-vehicle traction motors, and wind turbines, embed rare-earth magnets in or on the core, eliminating excitation losses.

Principles

In induction machines the rotor turns slightly slower than the rotating field of the stator; the fractional difference is called slip, and it is this relative motion that induces the rotor currents. In synchronous machines the rotor locks to the field and rotates at exactly synchronous speed. Balancing the rotor is critical: even small asymmetries produce vibration at high rotational speeds.
