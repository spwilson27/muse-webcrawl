Siderophile

Siderophile elements (from Greek sideros, "iron", and phileo, "to love") are chemical elements that, in the Goldschmidt classification of the geochemical affinities of the elements, tend to bond with iron and to partition into a planet's metallic core during planetary differentiation. They are typically transition metals with high densities and a weak affinity for oxygen and sulfur.

During Earth's differentiation, siderophile elements largely sank into the core with iron.

Goldschmidt classification

Victor Goldschmidt divided the elements by their preferred phase in a differentiating planet: siderophile (iron-loving, entering the metal phase), chalcophile (sulfur-loving, entering sulfide melts), lithophile (rock-loving, entering silicate melts), and atmophile (entering the atmosphere). Iron, nickel, cobalt, and the platinum-group elements — ruthenium, rhodium, palladium, osmium, iridium, and platinum — are classic siderophile elements; gold and several others (such as molybdenum and tungsten) show strong siderophile behaviour under reducing conditions.

Geochemical significance

Because Earth formed hot and largely molten, most of its siderophile elements were carried down into the core, leaving the crust and mantle strongly depleted in them. The fact that highly siderophile elements (platinum-group metals and gold) are nonetheless present in the mantle at higher abundances than core formation alone would predict is a key argument for the "late veneer" hypothesis: after the core had formed, a late rain of chondritic material re-supplied the mantle with these elements. The iridium anomaly at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary — an enrichment of the siderophile element iridium in a global clay layer — provided early evidence for the impact origin of that mass extinction.
