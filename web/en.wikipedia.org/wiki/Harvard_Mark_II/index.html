The Harvard Mark II, also known as the Aiken Relay Calculator, was an electromechanical computer built at Harvard University under the direction of Howard H. Aiken and completed in 1947 (some sources give 1948 for full operation). It was built for the United States Navy for use at the Naval Proving Ground at Dahlgren, Virginia, where it computed ballistic firing tables.

Design

Unlike its predecessor, the Mark I, which used mechanical switches and countershafts, the Mark II was built entirely from electromagnetic relays — some 13,000 of them. It used floating-point arithmetic with numbers of 23 significant digits, and had hardware for the elementary functions such as reciprocal, logarithm and trigonometric functions. Addition took about 0.125 seconds and multiplication about 0.25 seconds, roughly twelve times faster than the Mark I.

The machine was in fact constructed as two identical units that could work on the same problem and check each other's results, or run as separate computers. Grace Hopper was among the programmers who worked on the Mark II at Harvard.

The first "bug"

The Mark II is famous in computing folklore: on 9 September 1947 its operators found a moth trapped in relay #70 of Panel F, taped it into the logbook, and noted that they had been "debugging" the machine — an anecdote often, though inaccurately, credited as the origin of the term "bug" for a computer fault.

Later life

The Mark II was delivered to Dahlgren in 1948 and served the Navy for many years computing ballistics tables. It was succeeded at Harvard by the electronic Mark III and Mark IV.
