Covered wagon

The covered wagon was a large, canvas-topped wagon used by pioneers and emigrants travelling westward across North America during the 18th and 19th centuries. Drawn by oxen, mules or horses, it carried a family's possessions on journeys along trails such as the Oregon Trail, the California Trail and the Santa Fe Trail. Because of their canvas tops, covered wagons were nicknamed "prairie schooners".

Design

The best-known type was derived from the Conestoga wagon of Pennsylvania, though the lighter "prairie schooner" farm wagon was more common on the trails. A typical emigrant wagon had a wooden bed about 4 feet by 10 feet, with high sides to keep goods from shifting, and ran on large, iron-tyred wooden wheels — the rear wheels up to five or six feet in diameter — which helped it cross ruts, mud and shallow streams. The wagon bed was made watertight for river crossings. Over hoops of hickory or metal rose the "bonnet", a cover of canvas or oilcloth that protected the contents from rain and dust and could be drawn closed at each end.

Use on the trails

Emigrants typically loaded 1,600 to 2,500 pounds of food, tools, clothing and household goods, and most travellers walked beside the wagon rather than ride, to spare the animals and reduce weight. Journeys of some 2,000 miles to Oregon or California took five to six months at 15–20 miles per day. Wagons travelled in companies for mutual assistance and protection, circling at night to form a corral for livestock.

Decline

The completion of the transcontinental railroad in 1869 greatly reduced long-distance wagon migration, though covered wagons remained in use on farms and for shorter journeys into the early 20th century. The covered wagon has since become an enduring symbol of the American pioneer era.
