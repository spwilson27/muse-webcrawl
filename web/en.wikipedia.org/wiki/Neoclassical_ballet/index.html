Neoclassical ballet is a 20th-century style of ballet that uses traditional classical technique and vocabulary but departs from the elaborate narratives, sets and costumes of 19th-century ballet. It is generally considered to have begun with George Balanchine's Apollo (1928), choreographed for Diaghilev's Ballets Russes to music by Igor Stravinsky. Neoclassical ballets are frequently plotless, presenting the movement itself, in close relation to the score, as the subject of the work.

Characteristics

Neoclassical choreography retains the turned-out positions, pointe work and formal steps of classical ballet, but stretches and distorts them: hips and shoulders tilt off-centre, arms break the classical line, and dancers move at unusual speeds with an athletic, off-balance energy. Settings are pared down — dancers often perform in simple leotards and tights on a bare stage — and the music, frequently by modernist composers such as Stravinsky, Prokofiev or Hindemith, is treated as a partner rather than an accompaniment.

Development

After Diaghilev's death in 1929, Balanchine eventually settled in the United States, where with the School of American Ballet and New York City Ballet he built the style's central repertoire: Serenade (1934), Concerto Barocco (1941), Symphony in C (1947), Agon (1957) and Jewels (1967). In Britain, Frederick Ashton developed a more lyrical neoclassicism in works such as Symphonic Variations (1946), while Kenneth MacMillan and John Cranko expanded the expressive range. Jerome Robbins, William Forsythe and Jiří Kylián carried aspects of the style into contemporary ballet.

Legacy

Neoclassicism remains the dominant idiom of many major companies, and the term is used broadly for modern works that remain rooted in classical technique, in contrast both to story ballets and to fully contemporary dance.
