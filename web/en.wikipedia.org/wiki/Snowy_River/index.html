The Snowy River is a major river of south-eastern Australia. It rises on the slopes of Mount Kosciuszko, Australia's highest mainland peak, in the Snowy Mountains of New South Wales, and flows generally south through the Snowy River National Park in Victoria before emptying into Bass Strait near the town of Marlo. At about 352 kilometres long, it is one of the longest rivers in Australia to flow entirely within the south-eastern highlands and coastal plain.

Geography

The river begins high on the eastern slopes of the Great Dividing Range, fed by snowmelt and alpine streams. It passes near Jindabyne, where it was dammed as part of the Snowy Mountains Scheme, and then descends through steep, rugged gorges in the Snowy River National Park. Its major tributaries include the Eucumbene River, the Delegate River, and the Buchan River. The lower reaches wind across the coastal plain of East Gippsland in Victoria.

Snowy Mountains Scheme

Between 1949 and 1974 the river's flow was dramatically reduced by the Snowy Mountains Hydro-electric Scheme, which diverted up to 99 per cent of its water at Jindabyne Dam westward through tunnels to the Murray and Murrumbidgee river systems for irrigation and hydro-electricity generation. The diversion left long stretches of the riverbed dry or stagnant and became a symbol of the environmental cost of large water engineering projects.

Environmental flows

Following decades of campaigning by farmers, environmentalists and local communities, an agreement between the New South Wales, Victorian and Commonwealth governments committed to restoring environmental flows to the river. From 2002, water releases from Jindabyne Dam progressively increased, with a long-term target of returning about 21 per cent of the river's original average annual flow. Monitoring has recorded improvements in river health, though the river remains far below its natural flow regime.

Cultural significance

The Snowy River is immortalised in A. B. "Banjo" Paterson's 1890 ballad "The Man from Snowy River", which recounts a daring ride down a steep mountainside to recover a prized colt. The poem, and the 1982 film of the same name, made the river a national icon of Australian bush mythology. The river also holds deep significance for the Aboriginal peoples of the region, including the Ngarigo and Bidawal peoples, whose connection to the country long predates European settlement.
