Computus

Computus (Latin for "computation") is the calculation used to determine the calendar date of Easter. At the First Council of Nicaea in AD 325, it was agreed that Easter should be observed by all churches on a common date, the Sunday following the first full moon on or after the vernal equinox — but churches computed the date by differing methods, and the computus became a major branch of medieval learning concerned with harmonizing the solar and lunar calendars.

History

Dionysius Exiguus devised influential Easter tables in AD 525, introducing the Anno Domini era in the process. The Venerable Bede's treatise De temporum ratione (725) became the standard textbook of computus in early medieval Europe. Because the Julian calendar drifts against the solar year, the computed date of Easter gradually shifted; the Gregorian calendar reform of 1582 introduced corrected lunar tables (epacts), and the calculation was refined further by astronomers such as Clavius. Gauss later gave a compact arithmetic algorithm for the Gregorian computus.

The calculation

Easter Sunday is the first Sunday after the Paschal full moon, the ecclesiastical full moon falling on or after 21 March. In the Gregorian computus, Easter can fall between 22 March and 25 April. Eastern Orthodox churches generally use the Julian computus, so Western and Eastern Easter often differ.
