Town hall

A town hall typically houses the offices of a town or city council and a hall for public meetings.

A town hall is the chief administrative building of a town or city, housing the offices of the local government — typically the town or city council, the mayor's office, and municipal departments — and usually a hall used for council meetings, public gatherings, ceremonies and other civic functions. In larger cities the equivalent building is often called a city hall; in some countries the terms vary (for example, Rathaus in German-speaking countries, hôtel de ville in French, and municipio in Italian and Spanish contexts).

History

The town hall developed in medieval Europe as towns gained charters and self-governing institutions. Market towns built halls that combined functions: a covered market or storage space on the ground floor, council chambers above, and often a bell tower or clock tower symbolizing civic pride and independence from feudal or ecclesiastical authority. Grand town halls of the Low Countries, Germany and England — often among the largest and most ornate secular buildings in their towns — expressed the wealth of merchant guilds and the growing power of the urban middle class.

Functions

A modern town hall typically contains:

  • The council chamber, where elected representatives meet
  • Offices for the mayor and municipal administration
  • Registry and records offices, including for births, marriages and deaths
  • Public meeting rooms, assembly halls and spaces for weddings and civic events

Town hall meetings

By extension, the term "town hall meeting" — especially in the United States — denotes a public meeting at which officials or candidates meet constituents, present information and answer questions. The format descends from the New England town meeting, a form of direct democratic local government still practised in parts of New England, in which registered voters assemble to legislate on town business.
