The Book of Ezra is a book of the Hebrew Bible which formerly included the Book of Nehemiah in a single book, commonly distinguished in scholarship as Ezra–Nehemiah. The two became separated with the first printed Rabbinic Bible of the early 16th century, following late medieval Latin Christian tradition. Ezra recounts the end of the Babylonian exile and the rebuilding of the Temple in Jerusalem.

Summary

The book opens with the decree of Cyrus the Great of Persia (538 BCE) permitting the Jews to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the Temple, destroyed by the Babylonians in 586 BCE. Under Zerubbabel the altar is restored and the Temple's foundations laid, but opposition from neighbouring peoples and royal indifference halt the work until the prophets Haggai and Zechariah stir a renewal; the Second Temple is completed in 515 BCE under Darius I. Chapters 7–10 shift to a later period: Ezra the priest and scribe arrives from Babylon (traditionally 458 BCE, under Artaxerxes I) armed with royal authority to teach and enforce the Law, and confronts the problem of intermarriage with foreign wives, requiring their dissolution.

Composition

Ezra–Nehemiah is usually dated to the 4th century BCE and attributed to the same editor (the "Chronicler") responsible for the Books of Chronicles. The book incorporates purported official documents — royal edicts, letters, and lists — some written in Aramaic (Ezra 4:8–6:18; 7:12–26) rather than Hebrew. Questions of Ezra's historical date and the relationship of his mission to Nehemiah's remain debated.
