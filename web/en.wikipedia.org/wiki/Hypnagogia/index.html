Hypnagogia

Hypnagogia is the transitional state from wakefulness to sleep, also known as the hypnagogic state. Mental phenomena that may occur during this phase include lucid dreaming, hallucinations, and sleep paralysis. The corresponding transition on awakening is called the hypnopompic state. The term "hypnagogia" was coined by Alfred Maury in the 19th century; the psychologist Andreas Mavromatis wrote an influential study of the phenomenon in 1987.

Phenomena

Hypnagogic experiences span the senses: geometric visual patterns and faces, voices calling one's name, music, the sensation of floating or falling, and the hypnic jerk — a sudden muscle contraction at sleep onset. Tetris effect imagery, in which the day's repetitive activities replay in the mind's eye, is a well-studied example. Phenomena range from fleeting imagery of which the subject remains aware to hallucinatory episodes accompanied by a sensed presence, sometimes interpreted culturally as supernatural visitation.

Creativity and research

Artists and inventors including Salvador Dalí and Thomas Edison deliberately cultivated the hypnagogic state, napping while holding an object that would drop and wake them as they drifted off, so they could capture its imagery. Modern sleep research links hypnagogia to stage N1 sleep, with characteristic EEG changes as alpha activity gives way to theta rhythms. A 2021 study found that even brief entry into this stage tripled the chance of insight into a hidden problem-solving rule.
