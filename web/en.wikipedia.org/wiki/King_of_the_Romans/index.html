King of the Romans (Latin: Rex Romanorum; German: Römisch-deutscher König) was the title used by the elected king of the German kingdom within the Holy Roman Empire, particularly before — or, in periods when coronation in Rome lapsed, instead of — his coronation as emperor. From the early modern period the title also denoted an emperor's designated heir elected during his lifetime.

Origins

Medieval German kings initially styled themselves rex Francorum ("king of the Franks") or simply rex. The designation rex Romanorum became established under Henry II and was used regularly from the 11th century onward, asserting the king's claim to the imperial dignity: once elected and crowned king at Aachen, the ruler was expected to travel to Rome to be crowned emperor by the pope. Because papal coronation was a separate and sometimes long-delayed event, "King of the Romans" was the working title of many effective imperial rulers.

Later usage

In 1508, Maximilian I, with papal consent, assumed the style "elected Roman Emperor" (erwählter römischer Kaiser) without a Roman coronation, and thereafter German kings bore the imperial title from their election and coronation in Germany. The title King of the Romans then increasingly served for the heir apparent: the prince-electors would elect a successor during the emperor's lifetime, who reigned as King of the Romans until the emperor's death — examples include Ferdinand I (elected 1531) and Joseph II (elected 1764).

Napoleonic use

After the Empire's dissolution in 1806, Napoleon revived the title in Italian form (Re di Roma, King of Rome) for his infant son Napoleon II in 1811; it carried no connection to the imperial electoral system.
