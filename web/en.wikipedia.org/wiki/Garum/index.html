Garum

RegionAncient Mediterranean (Greece, Rome, Carthage)

Garum is a fermented fish sauce that was used as a condiment in the cuisines of Phoenicia, ancient Greece, Rome, Carthage and later Byzantium. The liquid was produced by the fermentation of salted fish, especially the entrails of fishes such as mackerel, tuna and anchovies, left in the sun for weeks or months. The resulting amber liquid was rich in glutamates and provided a savoury, umami flavour similar to that of modern Asian fish sauces.

Production and trade

Garum factories (cetariae) lined the coasts of the Mediterranean, notably in Hispania — the celebrated garum of Baelo Claudia and Gades was shipped across the empire in labelled amphorae — and in North Africa and Lusitania. The finest grade, garum sociorum, made from mackerel, commanded high prices in Rome; the residue paste, allec, was a cheap food for the poor. Pompeii was a notable production centre, dominated by the firm of Aulus Umbricius Scaurus, whose mosaic-advertised products are preserved by the eruption of Vesuvius.

Use and decline

Mixed with wine (oenogarum), oil, vinegar or honey, garum seasoned most dishes in the Roman cookbook attributed to Apicius. Its use declined in the West with the collapse of the Roman salting industry and changing tastes in the early medieval period, though related sauces survived in Byzantium and in local traditions such as the colatura di alici of Campania. Modern chefs and food historians have revived garum production, recognising it as a Mediterranean ancestor of global fermented fish sauces.
