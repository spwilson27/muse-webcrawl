Endurance training

Endurance training is the act of exercising to increase endurance. The term endurance training generally refers to training the aerobic system as opposed to the anaerobic system. The need for endurance in sports is often predicated as the need of cardiovascular and simple muscular endurance, but endurance encompasses far more than this. Endurance training can decrease cardiovascular diseases and improve maximal oxygen uptake (VO2 max).

Physiological adaptations

Long-term endurance training induces many physiological adaptations, both centrally and peripherally mediated. Central cardiovascular adaptations include decreased heart rate, increased stroke volume of the heart, increased blood plasma volume, and increased cardiac output. Peripheral adaptations include increased capillarisation of trained muscle, increased mitochondrial density, and an improved ability to oxidise fat and spare muscle glycogen during exercise.

Training methods

Common endurance training methods include long slow distance training, tempo training, interval training, and high-intensity interval training (HIIT). Training programmes are typically structured around principles of progressive overload, specificity, and periodisation, balancing training load with rest and recovery to avoid overtraining syndrome.

Health effects

Regular endurance exercise is associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and all-cause mortality. Negative effects from a very high lifetime accumulation of endurance exercise have been proposed, including increased incidence of atrial fibrillation and coronary artery calcification in some veteran athletes, though the overall health benefits for most people are strongly positive.
