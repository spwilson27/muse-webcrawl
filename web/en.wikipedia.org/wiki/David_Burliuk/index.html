David Davidovich Burliuk (Ukrainian: Дави́д Дави́дович Бурлю́к; 21 July 1882 – 15 January 1967) was a Ukrainian-American artist, poet, illustrator and publicist associated with the Futurist and Neo-Primitivist movements. He is frequently described as the "father of Russian Futurism" for his central role in launching the movement in the years before the First World War.

Early life and education

Burliuk was born in the village of Semyrotivka near Lebedyn in the Kharkov Governorate of the Russian Empire, into a family of Cossack descent. His brothers Vladimir and Nikolai were also artists, and the family home became a gathering place for avant-garde writers and painters. He studied art in Kazan and Odessa, and later at the Royal Academy in Munich and in Paris, before returning to Russia, where he studied at the Moscow School of Painting, Sculpture and Architecture and was expelled, along with Vladimir Mayakovsky, for his provocative public activities.

Futurism

In 1910 Burliuk helped found the Hylaea group, the seedbed of Russian literary Futurism, whose members included Mayakovsky, Velimir Khlebnikov, Aleksei Kruchenykh and Benedikt Livshits. In December 1912 he co-signed the manifesto A Slap in the Face of Public Taste, which demanded that Pushkin, Dostoevsky and Tolstoy be thrown "overboard from the Ship of Modernity". The Burliuk brothers organised scandalous Futurist tours and exhibitions across Russia, appearing in yellow waistcoats with painted faces, and published almanacs that mixed poetry, manifestos and crude typography.

As a painter, Burliuk combined Fauvist colour, Cubist fragmentation and motifs drawn from Ukrainian folk art and Scythian antiquity — the so-called Neo-Primitivist manner he shared with Natalia Goncharova and Mikhail Larionov.

Emigration and later life

After the Russian Revolution, Burliuk travelled east across Siberia, spent two years in Japan, where he exhibited and helped stimulate a Japanese Futurist movement, and in 1922 settled in the United States. He became a US citizen and lived for many years at Hampton Bays, Long Island, editing the journal Color and Rhyme and writing his memoirs. He continued to paint landscapes, portraits and scenes of Ukrainian and American life until his death in 1967.

Legacy

Burliuk's organisational energy and polemical flair made him the indispensable impresario of the early Russian avant-garde. Although his own painting was later overshadowed by that of his protégés, retrospectives in Ukraine, Russia, Europe and the United States have restored his reputation as a pivotal figure linking Ukrainian folk traditions to international modernism.
