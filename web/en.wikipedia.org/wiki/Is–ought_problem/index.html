Is–ought problem

The is–ought problem, also known as Hume's law or the Humean guillotine, is the thesis that no normative conclusion (about what ought to be) can be deduced purely from descriptive premises (about what is). The problem was first articulated by the Scottish philosopher David Hume in Book III of A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40).

Hume's passage

Hume observed that writers on moral systems proceed from propositions joined by "is" and "is not" to conclusions joined by "ought" and "ought not", without explaining how this new relation is deduced from the others. Because the change is "imperceptible" but consequential, Hume demanded that a reason be given for what seemed "altogether inconceivable".

Significance and responses

The problem is central to meta-ethics and the fact–value distinction: it challenges theories of moral realism that claim moral facts are simply natural facts. It is closely related to G. E. Moore's naturalistic fallacy and open-question argument. Philosophers have responded in various ways: some accept Hume's law as a logical truth; others, such as John Searle, have attempted to derive "ought" from "is" (for example, from the institution of promising); and some treat the boundary between descriptive and normative language as less sharp than Hume assumed.
