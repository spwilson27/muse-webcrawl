Hoodoo (spirituality)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hoodoo is a set of spiritual practices, traditions and beliefs created by African Americans in the Southern United States from the blending of West and Central African spiritual traditions with Native American herbal knowledge and European folk magic. Also known as conjure or rootwork, hoodoo is a body of magical practice rather than a religion: it has no creed or clergy, and practitioners may be of any faith, historically most often Protestant Christianity — a distinction often drawn with Louisiana Voodoo, which is a religion with its own theology and rituals.

Origins and history

Enslaved Africans brought spiritual and healing traditions to North America, where they were adapted under the conditions of slavery and combined with the plant lore of Native Americans and the folk magic of European settlers. Hoodoo developed particularly in the Mississippi Delta, the Sea Islands (where it overlaps with Gullah culture) and the wider South. Practitioners — "root doctors", "conjure men" and "conjure women" — supplied charms, healing, protection from harm, and means of resisting or avenging the violence of slavery and, later, Jim Crow. From the early 20th century, mail-order companies sold hoodoo curios, oils, candles and powders nationwide, and figures such as the author and anthropologist Zora Neale Hurston documented the tradition in works including Mules and Men (1935).

Practices

Hoodoo work ("workings" or "tricks") uses herbs, roots, minerals, candles, oils and personal concerns (such as hair or nail clippings). Characteristic items include the mojo hand, a small flannel bag containing roots and curios carried for luck or protection; High John the Conqueror root, associated with the folk hero of that name; "goopher dust" (graveyard dirt); condition oils such as Van Van; and the use of Psalms and Bible verses as spoken charms. Goals include healing, luck in love and gambling, protection, "uncrossing" (removing curses) and, in the darker side of the tradition, jinxing enemies.

Cultural influence

Hoodoo is deeply woven into blues music — in songs about mojo hands, black cat bones and goopher dust — and has influenced American literature and popular culture, though it is also frequently caricatured or conflated with Voodoo.
