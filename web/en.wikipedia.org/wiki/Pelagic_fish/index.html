Pelagic fish

Pelagic fish live in the pelagic zone of ocean or lake waters — the water column neither close to the bottom nor near the shore — in contrast to demersal fish, which live on or near the bottom, and reef fish, which are associated with coral reefs. Pelagic fish can be categorised as coastal (epipelagic) fish, inhabiting sunlit surface waters down to about 200 metres, and oceanic fish, inhabiting deeper waters beyond the continental shelves.

Forage and predator fish

Small coastal pelagic fish such as herrings, sardines, anchovies and menhaden are forage fish: they feed on plankton and are preyed on by larger predators. They often travel in huge schools and account for a large fraction of the world's marine fish catch, much of it reduced to fishmeal and fish oil. Large pelagic predators include tunas, billfish, king mackerel, sharks and large squid. These are fast swimmers, often highly migratory, and many have special adaptations — such as regional endothermy in tunas — for life in the open ocean.

Deep water fish

Below the epipelagic zone lies the mesopelagic (200–1,000 m), where lanternfish and other small fish undertake vast daily vertical migrations to feed at the surface at night. Deepwater pelagic fish such as rattails and certain squaliform sharks live in the bathypelagic and abyssopelagic zones in darkness, relying on scavenging and marine snow.

Fisheries

Pelagic fisheries use purse seines, pelagic trawls, drift nets and longlines. Small pelagics are among the most abundant fish resources but are highly variable, collapsing periodically with oceanographic changes such as El Niño, as in the Peruvian anchoveta fishery. Large pelagic fisheries for tuna are managed by regional fisheries organisations because of the species' migrations and histories of overfishing. See also pelagic zone.
