Dysosmia

Dysosmia is a disorder described as any qualitative alteration or distortion of the perception of smell. Qualitative alterations differ from quantitative alterations, which include anosmia (complete loss of smell) and hyposmia (partial loss). Dysosmia can be classified as parosmia, phantosmia, or both.

Parosmia

Parosmia (also called troposmia) is a distortion of a smell in the presence of an actual odorant — a familiar odour is perceived as different, usually unpleasant. Common reports include coffee, roasted foods, or chocolate smelling burnt, chemical, or rotten. Parosmia frequently appears during recovery from post-viral olfactory loss, including after COVID-19, and is thought to result from mis-wiring of regenerating olfactory receptor neurons or incomplete odour coding in the olfactory bulb.

Phantosmia

Phantosmia is the perception of a smell when no odorant is present — an olfactory hallucination. The phantom smell is usually unpleasant (cacosmia), such as smoke, burning, or decay, and may be perceived in one or both nostrils. Causes include upper respiratory infection, head trauma, nasal polyps, temporal lobe seizures, brain tumours, migraine aura, and Parkinson's disease.

Causes

The causes of dysosmia are still poorly understood and span peripheral and central origins: viral upper respiratory infections (including influenza and SARS-CoV-2), chronic rhinosinusitis, head injury, exposure to toxins or certain medications, neurodegenerative disease, and ageing. In many cases no cause is identified.

Diagnosis and treatment

Assessment typically includes a clinical history, nasal endoscopy, standardized smell tests (e.g. UPSIT or Sniffin' Sticks), and sometimes MRI when a central cause is suspected. Treatment addresses the underlying cause where one is found; olfactory training — regular deliberate sniffing of a set of odours over months — has the best evidence for post-viral dysfunction. Many cases, particularly parosmia following viral infection, improve gradually over months to a few years.
