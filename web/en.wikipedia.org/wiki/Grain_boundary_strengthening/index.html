Grain boundary strengthening

FieldMaterials science, metallurgy
Key relationHall–Petch relation

Grain boundary strengthening (or grain size strengthening) is a method of strengthening materials by changing their average crystallite (grain) size. It is based on the observation that grain boundaries are insurmountable borders for dislocations and that the number of dislocations within a grain has an effect on how stress builds up in adjacent grains, which will eventually activate dislocation sources and thus allow deformation in the neighbouring grain.

Hall–Petch relation

The dependence of yield strength on grain size is described by the Hall–Petch equation: σy = σ0 + ky·d−1/2, where σy is the yield stress, σ0 is a materials constant (friction stress), ky is the strengthening coefficient, and d is the average grain diameter. By manipulating the grain size — through processing such as cold working and annealing — the strength of a polycrystalline material can be increased, usually at the cost of some ductility at very fine grain sizes.

Inverse Hall–Petch effect

Below a critical grain size, typically around 10–20 nanometres, yield strength may decrease with further grain refinement, a phenomenon known as the inverse Hall–Petch effect, attributed to grain-boundary sliding and other boundary-mediated deformation mechanisms in nanocrystalline materials.
