The Geissenklosterle (German: Geißenklösterle) is an archaeological site of the Upper Palaeolithic in the Swabian Jura of southern Germany, near Blaubeuren. Together with neighbouring caves in the Ach and Lone valleys, it preserves some of the earliest known figurative art and musical instruments in the world.

Excavations and finds

Systematic excavations from 1973, largely directed by Joachim Hahn and continued by Nicholas Conard's team at the University of Tübingen, revealed layers from the Middle Palaeolithic through the Aurignacian, Gravettian and Magdalenian.

The Aurignacian layers (about 43,000–35,000 years old) yielded small mammoth-ivory figurines, including a standing figure and animal carvings, personal ornaments, and — most famously — flutes made from swan bone and mammoth ivory. Radiocarbon dating published in 2012 placed the flutes at roughly 42,000–43,000 years old, among the oldest musical instruments known (see also the flutes of Hohle Fels).

Significance

The finds support the view that anatomically modern humans arriving in the Danube corridor during the Aurignacian produced a flourishing tradition of art and music almost immediately. In 2017 the cave was inscribed, with five other Swabian caves, on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of "Caves and Ice Age Art in the Swabian Jura".
