A chocolate brownie, or simply a brownie, is a chocolate baked confection. Brownies come in a variety of forms and may be either fudgy or cakey, depending on their density. Brownies often, but not always, have a glossy "skin" on their upper crust. They may also include ingredients such as nuts, frosting, chocolate chips, or other ingredients. A variation made with brown sugar and vanilla rather than chocolate in the batter is called a blondie, or blond brownie.

Brownies are typically eaten by hand, sometimes accompanied by milk, served warm with ice cream ("à la mode"), with whipped cream, or sprinkled with powdered sugar. In North America they are common homemade treats and lunchbox items, and they are also popular in restaurants and coffeehouses.

History

The brownie developed in the United States at the end of the 19th century. One famous legend concerns Bertha Palmer, the prominent Chicago socialite whose husband owned the Palmer House Hotel: in 1893 she asked the hotel's pastry chef for a dessert suitable for ladies attending the World's Columbian Exposition — smaller than a piece of cake, still cake-like, and easily eaten from boxed lunches. The resulting Palmer House brownie, made with chocolate and walnuts and an apricot glaze, is still served at the hotel. The first-known printed use of the word "brownie" for a confection appeared in the 1896 edition of Fannie Farmer's Boston Cooking-School Cook Book, though that recipe described a molasses cookie baked in individual tins — essentially the blondie. Recipes explicitly for chocolate brownies appeared in print from 1904–1906, including in Farmer's revised 1906 cookbook, and the formula quickly standardised around chocolate, butter, sugar, eggs and a small amount of flour.

Preparation and varieties

Brownie batter is a rich mixture of melted butter and chocolate (or cocoa), sugar, eggs, a pinch of salt and just enough flour to bind, often with vanilla and optional walnuts or pecans. Texture is governed by the ratios of fat, sugar and flour and by baking time: high fat and sugar with minimal flour and slight underbaking produce dense, fudgy brownies; more flour, leavening and longer baking produce cakey ones; an intermediate style is described as chewy. The crackly top is associated with dissolving sugar and whipping eggs into the batter. Brownies are usually baked in a square or rectangular pan and cut into squares or rectangles. Common variants include cream-cheese-swirled, peanut-butter, salted-caramel and mint brownies, frosted brownies, and the "brownie sundae".

In popular culture

Brownies are a staple of bake sales and school lunches in the United States and gave their name to the junior branch of the Girl Scouts (Brownies, after the helpful household spirits of British folklore — an earlier, unrelated use). Packaged brownie mixes, introduced in the mid-20th century, made the dessert a standard of home baking, and the "brownie point" idiom for credit earned has been linked, debatably, to the treat.
