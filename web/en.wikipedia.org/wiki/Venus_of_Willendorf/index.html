Venus of Willendorf
Datec. 28,000–25,000 BCE
MaterialOolitic limestone, tinted with red ochre
Height11.1 cm (4.4 in)

The Venus of Willendorf is a small Venus figurine carved from oolitic limestone, dated to roughly 28,000–25,000 years ago (Gravettian period). It was discovered in 1908 during excavations near Willendorf in Lower Austria, directed by Josef Szombathy, and is now displayed in the Naturhistorisches Museum in Vienna. At just 11.1 cm tall, it is one of the most famous works of Paleolithic art.

Description

The figurine depicts a nude woman with greatly exaggerated breasts, belly, hips and thighs. Her arms are thin and rest on her breasts; the head has no face, being covered by a pattern of horizontal bands that may represent braided hair or a woven cap. The figure was coloured with red ochre. The limestone is not local to the Willendorf area — analyses suggest the stone may have come from northern Italy or elsewhere, implying the figurine (or its raw material) travelled long distances, and its style links it to figurines found as far away as France and Siberia.

Interpretation

Like other Venus figurines, its meaning is uncertain. It has been read as a fertility symbol, an image of a mother goddess, a representation of health and abundance in an Ice Age environment, or a self-depiction by a woman of her own body. Whatever its purpose, its portability and the care lavished on it show it was a valued object for the hunter-gatherers who made it.
