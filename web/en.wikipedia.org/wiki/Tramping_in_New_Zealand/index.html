Tramping in New Zealand

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tramping, known in other countries as hiking, trekking or bushwalking, is a popular recreational activity in New Zealand. The country's extensive network of tracks and huts, maintained largely by the Department of Conservation (DOC), ranges from short walks to multi-day wilderness journeys through national parks, mountain ranges and native forest.

Great Walks

The best-known tramping routes are the ten Great Walks managed by DOC, including the Milford Track, Routeburn Track, Kepler Track, Abel Tasman Coast Track, Tongariro Northern Circuit, Heaphy Track, Rakiura Track, Lake Waikaremoana Track, Whanganui Journey (a river journey) and the Paparoa Track. The Milford Track, in Fiordland National Park, has been described as "the finest walk in the world" since 1908. The Great Walks operate under a booking system during the peak season, with huts and campsites that must be reserved in advance.

Hut system

New Zealand has a uniquely extensive backcountry hut network of more than 950 huts, ranging from large serviced Great Walk huts with gas cooking and flush toilets to small basic bivouacs. Most backcountry huts operate on an annual pass or per-night ticket system. Tramping clubs and alpine clubs also maintain their own huts.

History and culture

Tramping developed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as New Zealanders explored the Southern Alps and bush country. Tramping clubs were founded in major cities, such as the Tararua Tramping Club (1919), and fostered a distinctive culture of self-reliance. The term "tramping" itself distinguishes the activity from day walking, generally implying carrying a pack and staying overnight.

Safety

New Zealand's weather is notoriously changeable, and rivers can rise rapidly after rain, making river crossings one of the leading causes of tramping fatalities. The New Zealand Mountain Safety Council promotes the Outdoor Safety Code: plan your trip, tell someone your plans, be aware of the weather, know your limits, and take sufficient supplies. Many tracks cross avalanche terrain in winter and spring in alpine regions such as Fiordland and Aoraki / Mount Cook.
