A bank holiday is a national public holiday in the United Kingdom, the Republic of Ireland, and some other countries. The name originated in the 19th century, when the Bank of England observed about 33 saints' days and religious festivals as holidays; legislation, notably Sir John Lubbock's Bank Holidays Act 1871, formalised and reduced these, and the days off spread from bank employees to the general public.

United Kingdom

In England and Wales there are typically eight bank holidays a year: New Year's Day (1 January), Good Friday, the early May bank holiday, the spring bank holiday (last Monday in May), the summer bank holiday (last Monday in August), Christmas Day and Boxing Day. Scotland has additional holidays including 2 January and St Andrew's Day, while Northern Ireland observes St Patrick's Day and the Twelfth of July. When a fixed-date holiday falls on a weekend, a substitute weekday is usually designated. Additional one-off bank holidays are proclaimed for royal events such as jubilees, weddings and coronations. There is no automatic statutory right to time off or extra pay on bank holidays in the UK; entitlements depend on employment contracts, though most employees receive them as part of the statutory minimum annual leave.

History

The 1871 Act created separate lists for England and Wales, Scotland, and Ireland. The Banking and Financial Dealings Act 1971 is the current governing statute. The May Day and spring holidays were added in the 1970s, and campaigns periodically propose further holidays, such as the national saints' days.
