Wine tasting is the sensory examination and evaluation of wine. While the practice is as old as wine production itself, a formalized methodology has developed slowly from the 14th century onward. Modern professional wine tasters—sommeliers, critics, winemakers, and buyers—use a consistent systematic approach that involves the wine's appearance, aroma, taste, mouthfeel, and finish, and may record their impressions with standardized vocabulary and scores.

The tasting process

Appearance. The taster holds the glass against a white background and assesses clarity, colour intensity and hue. Colour gives clues about grape variety, age (white wines deepen and red wines fade and turn brick-toned with age), and condition.

Aroma. The glass is swirled to release volatile compounds, then smelled. Aromas are often grouped as primary (grape and fermentation-derived), secondary (winemaking, e.g. oak, lees, malolactic notes), and tertiary (from ageing, e.g. leather, tobacco, dried fruit). Because much of "taste" is actually retronasal smell, the nose is considered the most informative stage.

Palate and finish. A sip is drawn over the tongue, often with air ("slurping") to volatilize aromatics. The taster evaluates sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, flavour intensity and character, and balance among them. The finish is the persistence of flavour after spitting or swallowing; length is a traditional mark of quality. Professionals usually spit to remain sober through a flight.

Formats and bias

Tastings may be vertical (one wine across vintages), horizontal (one vintage across producers), or blind, where labels are concealed to prevent bias. Research shows tasters are influenced by price, labels, colour (white wine dyed red has famously been described with red-wine vocabulary by students), and context—findings that fuel debate about the reliability of expert judgement, scores, and wine competitions.

Scoring

Popular systems include the 20-point scale used in much European criticism and the 100-point scale popularized by Robert Parker, in which wines are banded from below 60 (unacceptable) to 96–100 (extraordinary). Scores strongly influence prices and consumer choices, particularly for fine wine.
