Seventh chord

A dominant seventh chord (G7) built on the fifth degree of C major.

A seventh chord is a chord consisting of a triad plus a note forming an interval of a seventh above the chord's root. When not otherwise specified, a "seventh chord" usually means a dominant seventh chord: a major triad together with a minor seventh. Seventh chords are a cornerstone of Western harmony, appearing in classical music, jazz, blues, and popular music.

Types

Combining major, minor, diminished, and augmented triads with major, minor, and diminished sevenths yields the principal types: the dominant seventh (major triad, minor seventh), the major seventh (major triad, major seventh), the minor seventh (minor triad, minor seventh), the half-diminished seventh (diminished triad, minor seventh), the diminished seventh (diminished triad, diminished seventh), and the minor-major seventh (minor triad, major seventh). In figured bass and jazz chord symbols these are written with suffixes such as 7, maj7, m7, ø7, and °7.

Function and history

The dominant seventh, built on the fifth scale degree, carries strong tension — its tritone between the third and seventh drives resolution to the tonic chord, making it central to tonal cadences. Seventh chords appeared sporadically in Renaissance music, became common in the Baroque era through composers such as Monteverdi and Corelli, and were systematized by Rameau in his 1722 Treatise on Harmony. In jazz and blues, seventh chords are default sonorities, extended further to ninth, eleventh, and thirteenth chords.

Inversions

A seventh chord has four positions: root position and three inversions, traditionally figured 7, 6/5, 4/3, and 4/2 (or 2). Voice-leading rules in common-practice harmony generally require the chordal seventh to resolve downward by step.
