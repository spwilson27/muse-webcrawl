Prodrug

A prodrug is a pharmacologically inactive (or less active) compound that is converted within the body, usually by enzymatic metabolism, into an active drug. Prodrugs are designed to improve how a medicine is absorbed, distributed, metabolised or tolerated — for example by increasing oral bioavailability, solubility, stability, or targeting a specific tissue.

Examples

Well-known prodrugs include codeine, converted by CYP2D6 to morphine; levodopa, which crosses the blood–brain barrier and is decarboxylated to dopamine; valaciclovir, the valine ester of aciclovir with much better oral absorption; clopidogrel, activated in the liver; oseltamivir, hydrolysed to its active carboxylate; and the ACE inhibitor enalapril, converted to enalaprilat. Aspirin and heroin were historically recognised examples of the concept, and the term was coined by Albert in 1958.

Classification

Prodrugs are often divided into Type I, activated inside cells (e.g., many antivirals and statins), and Type II, activated extracellularly, for instance in the gut or bloodstream (e.g., antibody-directed and gene-directed enzyme prodrug therapies in oncology). Activation may depend on individual enzyme levels, so genetic variation — such as in CYP2D6 — can make a prodrug ineffective or excessively potent in some patients.
