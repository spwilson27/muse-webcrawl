Hot cathode

Hot cathode
TypeElectrode
MechanismThermionic emission
Common materialsTungsten; oxide-coated nickel; thoriated tungsten
ApplicationsVacuum tubes, fluorescent lamps, CRTs, electron microscopes

A hot cathode is a cathode electrode that is heated so that it emits electrons by thermionic emission. Hot cathodes are used in vacuum tubes, gas-discharge lamps, cathode-ray tubes, and electron guns. The heat may be supplied by an electric filament, either directly (the filament itself is the cathode) or indirectly (a separate heater warms an insulated cathode sleeve).

Materials and coatings

Pure tungsten filaments must reach about 2,200 °C to emit usefully. Most modern hot cathodes use oxide coatings of barium and strontium oxides on nickel, which emit strongly at only about 800 °C, or thoriated tungsten in high-power tubes. Emission current density and lifetime depend on the work function of the surface and on avoiding contamination.

Richardson's law

The emitted current density follows Richardson's law, increasing steeply with temperature and decreasing exponentially with the material's work function. Cathodes eventually fail as the emissive coating is depleted or evaporated.
