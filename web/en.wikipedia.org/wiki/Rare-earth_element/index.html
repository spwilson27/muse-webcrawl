Rare-earth element

The rare-earth elements (REE), also called the rare-earth metals or simply rare earths, are a set of seventeen nearly indistinguishable lustrous silvery-white soft heavy metals. They comprise the fifteen lanthanides on the periodic table, together with scandium and yttrium, which tend to occur in the same ore deposits as the lanthanides and have similar chemical properties.

Rare-earth oxides, clockwise from top centre: praseodymium, cerium, lanthanum, neodymium, samarium, gadolinium

Despite their name, most rare-earth elements are relatively plentiful in the Earth's crust: cerium is the 25th most abundant element, more common than copper. They are termed "rare" because they are seldom found in economically exploitable concentrations, being dispersed through many minerals rather than concentrated in rich veins.

List

The seventeen rare-earth elements are scandium, yttrium, and the lanthanides: lanthanum, cerium, praseodymium, neodymium, promethium, samarium, europium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, holmium, erbium, thulium, ytterbium, and lutetium. They are often divided into light rare earths (lanthanum through samarium) and heavy rare earths (europium through lutetium, plus yttrium).

Properties

The lanthanides are chemically very similar to one another because their 4f electron shells fill progressively while their outer electron configurations remain nearly the same. The gradual decrease in ionic radius across the series is known as the lanthanide contraction. Their similarity makes separation from one another laborious, historically achieved by fractional crystallization and now by solvent extraction and ion exchange.

Sources and production

The principal ore minerals are bastnäsite, monazite, and lateritic ion-adsorption clays, which are the main source of the scarcer heavy rare earths. Major production has concentrated in China, including the giant Bayan Obo mine in Inner Mongolia; other producers include the United States (Mountain Pass), Australia (Mount Weld), and Myanmar.

Applications

  • Neodymium magnets (NdFeB) for motors, generators, headphones, and wind turbines.
  • Phosphors for displays and lighting (europium, terbium, yttrium).
  • Catalysts, including cerium compounds in catalytic converters and lanthanum in petroleum refining.
  • Polishing powders (cerium oxide), glass additives, lasers (neodymium-doped YAG), and nickel–metal hydride batteries (lanthanum-rich mischmetal).

Supply concerns

The concentration of mining and, especially, of separation and magnet manufacture in China has made rare earths a recurring subject of trade and industrial policy disputes. Recycling rates remain very low, and substitution is difficult for many applications, so the elements are classed as critical materials by several governments.
