Cenobitic monasticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cenobitic (or coenobitic) monasticism is a monastic tradition that stresses community life. Often in the West the community belongs to a religious order, and the life of the cenobitic monk is regulated by a rule (such as the Rule of St Basil or the Rule of St Benedict) and a superior, an abbot or prior. The term derives from the Greek words koinos (κοινός), meaning "common", and bios (βίος), meaning "life".

Cenobitic monasticism is contrasted with eremitic monasticism, in which monks live in solitude as hermits, and with the semi-eremitic lavra system, in which monks live largely alone but gather for common worship. In the cenobitic life the monks eat, work, pray and sleep together under one roof and one rule.

Origins

The cenobitic form of monastic life is generally credited to Saint Pachomius the Great, who in the 320s organised a community of monks at Tabennisi in Egypt. Pachomius had served as a soldier and, having become a Christian, first lived as a hermit before gathering disciples into an organised community — a koinonia — governed by a written rule, with monks assigned to houses, trades, and common prayer. By the time of his death in 346, the Pachomian federation comprised thousands of monks and nuns in multiple monasteries.

Development

In the Eastern church, the rules of Basil of Caesarea gave cenobitic life its characteristic form, emphasising obedience, common property, hospitality and care for the poor. In the West, the Rule of Saint Benedict (c. 530) became the dominant model, structuring the day around the Liturgy of the Hours (opus Dei), manual labour, and sacred reading (lectio divina). Nearly all later Western religious orders — Benedictines, Cistercians, and many others — adopted cenobitic organisation.

Characteristics

Key features of cenobitic life include a vow of stability (remaining in one community), common ownership of goods, obedience to a superior, a fixed daily horarium of prayer and work, common meals taken in silence while listening to readings in the refectory, and the chapter meeting at which faults are acknowledged and community business discussed.
