Producers-consumers problem

The producers-consumers problem — also known as the bounded-buffer problem — is a classic problem in concurrent programming. One or more producer threads generate data and place it into a shared buffer, while one or more consumer threads remove and process it. The challenge is to coordinate access so that producers do not add to a full buffer, consumers do not remove from an empty buffer, and the buffer is never accessed inconsistently.

Solution concepts

The problem is a standard vehicle for teaching synchronisation primitives. With an unbounded buffer, only consumers need to wait; with a bounded buffer, semaphores are typically used to count empty and filled slots, together with a mutex to protect buffer manipulation. Monitor-based solutions use condition variables: producers wait on a "not full" condition and signal "not empty" after adding an item, and vice versa for consumers. Incorrect solutions can suffer race conditions, deadlock, lost wakeups, or busy waiting.

History

Edsger W. Dijkstra described early forms of the problem in the 1960s in his work on cooperating sequential processes, alongside related classics such as the dining philosophers problem and the sleeping barber problem. The producer–consumer pattern underlies modern message queues, pipelines and stream-processing systems.
