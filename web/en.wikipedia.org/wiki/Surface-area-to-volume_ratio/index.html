Surface-area-to-volume ratio

The surface-area-to-volume ratio (also written SA:V) is the amount of surface area per unit volume of an object. For a given shape, as its size increases the volume grows faster than the surface area, because volume scales with the cube of a linear dimension while area scales with the square. The ratio therefore decreases with increasing size, a principle with wide consequences in physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering.

Key facts
Units: inverse length (e.g. m-1)
For a sphere: SA:V = 3/r
For a cube: SA:V = 6/a

Mathematics

For a sphere of radius r, the surface area is 4πr2 and the volume is (4/3)πr3, so the ratio is 3/r. For a cube of side a, the ratio is 6/a. Among all shapes of a given volume, the sphere has the smallest surface area, and hence the lowest SA:V ratio; elongated or flattened shapes have higher ratios.

Biological significance

Cells exchange materials with their environment across their surface membranes, while their metabolic needs scale with volume. This constrains cell size: as a cell grows, its SA:V falls, and diffusion alone can no longer supply the interior. Small cells, flattened cells, and cells with folded membranes (such as the microvilli of intestinal cells) all increase surface area relative to volume. The same principle explains why small mammals lose heat faster than large ones and must eat proportionally more: heat is lost through the surface but generated in the volume.

Physical and chemical applications

Reaction rates at surfaces, dissolution, cooling, and evaporation all depend on surface area, so finely divided materials react faster: a powdered fuel burns far more rapidly than the same mass in a single lump. In nanotechnology, the very high SA:V of nanoparticles gives them distinctive catalytic and optical properties. Engineers designing heat exchangers use fins to raise surface area and improve heat transfer.
