Windbreak

A windbreak (also called a shelterbelt when planted on a large scale) is a planting of one or more rows of trees or shrubs planted to provide shelter from the wind and to protect soil from erosion. Windbreaks are commonly planted around farms, fields, and rural homes to reduce wind speed for a distance of roughly ten times the height of the planting on its downwind side.

Benefits

Field windbreaks reduce wind erosion of topsoil, moderate temperature and moisture extremes for crops and livestock, and can measurably increase crop yields in the sheltered zone. Farmstead windbreaks lower heating costs in winter, provide privacy, and create habitat for birds and beneficial insects. Snow management is another important function: properly placed windbreaks control where snow accumulates, keeping roads and farmyards clearer.

History

Large-scale shelterbelt planting dates prominently to the 1930s in the United States, when the Prairie States Forestry Project — launched in response to the Dust Bowl — planted more than 200 million trees in a belt from North Dakota to Texas. Similar programs were undertaken in the Soviet Union, and China's "Green Wall" continues this tradition on a vast scale.

Design

Effective windbreaks are typically planted perpendicular to prevailing winds, with medium density (40–60% porosity) to maximize the sheltered distance. Conifers are often used for year-round protection, combined with deciduous species for structure and diversity.
