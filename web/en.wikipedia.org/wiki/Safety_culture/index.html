Safety culture

Safety culture
DefinitionShared values, attitudes and practices of an organisation concerning safety
Term popularisedIAEA report on the Chernobyl disaster (INSAG-1, 1986)
Related conceptsSafety climate, just culture, high-reliability organisations
Notable modelsReason's organisational accident model; Westrum's typology; Hudson's maturity ladder

Safety culture is the collection of beliefs, perceptions, attitudes and values that members of an organisation share in relation to safety — often summarised as "the way we do things around here" when it comes to risk. The concept rose to prominence after the International Atomic Energy Agency's 1986 report on the Chernobyl disaster attributed the accident in part to a poor safety culture, and it has since become a central idea in safety management across nuclear power, aviation, healthcare, rail, construction and process industries.

Components

Analyses commonly distinguish the psychological aspects of culture (how people feel about safety), the behavioural aspects (what people do) and the situational or organisational aspects (policies, procedures and structures). James Reason argued that an effective safety culture combines an informed culture with reporting, just, flexible and learning subcultures. Maturity models arrange organisations on a ladder from pathological ("who cares?") through reactive and calculative stages to proactive and generative cultures in which safety is an integral part of everything the organisation does.

Measurement and criticism

Safety culture is commonly assessed through workforce questionnaires measuring "safety climate", interviews, and audits of practices and artefacts. Critics note that the term is used loosely, that questionnaire scores may not predict accidents, and that emphasising culture can distract from engineering and organisational causes of disasters or become a way of blaming workers. Nevertheless, inquiries into accidents such as the Texas City refinery explosion (2005) and the Deepwater Horizon blowout (2010) have repeatedly identified weak safety culture at leadership level as a root cause, keeping the concept central to regulation and management practice.
