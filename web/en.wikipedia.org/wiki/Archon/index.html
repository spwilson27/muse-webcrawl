Archon (Greek: ἄρχων, romanized: árchōn, plural: ἄρχοντες, árchontes) was a title used in ancient Greece for a chief magistrate or ruler. In Athens, the archons were among the most important officials of the state, and the chief archon, the archon eponymos, gave his name to the year in official records.

Athens, where the archons were chief magistrates

Archons of Athens

According to tradition, Athens was originally ruled by kings, whose power was gradually limited. The office of archon was said to have been created in the 8th century BC, and by 683/682 BC the Athenians maintained an official list of annual archons. Originally the archon held office for life, later for ten years, and finally for a single year.

By the classical period there were nine archons: the archon eponymos (chief archon), the archon basileus (king archon), who retained religious functions of the old kingship; the polemarch (war archon), originally the military commander; and the six thesmothetai, who were concerned with the law. After 487 BC the archons were chosen by lot from a shortlist elected by the tribes, and the importance of the office declined as military and political leadership passed to the elected strategoi (generals).

Functions

The archon eponymos supervised festivals such as the City Dionysia, appointed choregoi, and had jurisdiction in family and inheritance cases. The archon basileus presided over religious rites and homicide trials. The polemarch handled cases involving metics (resident foreigners). The thesmothetai scheduled trials and presided over a range of legal proceedings.

Later use

The word continued in use in the Byzantine Empire for governors and officials, and in the Eastern Orthodox Church the title "Archon" is conferred as an honor on laymen by the Ecumenical Patriarchate. In modern Greek, archon can simply mean a ruler or lord.
