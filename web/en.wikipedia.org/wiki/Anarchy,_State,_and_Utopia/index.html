Anarchy, State, and Utopia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anarchy, State, and Utopia is a 1974 book by the American political philosopher Robert Nozick, written largely in response to John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971). It won the National Book Award in Philosophy and Religion and, with Rawls's work, defines the poles of contemporary Anglophone distributive debate: egalitarian liberalism versus rights-based libertarianism.

Argument

Part I argues that a minimal "night-watchman" state — limited to protection against force, theft and fraud, and to contract enforcement — could arise from a state of nature by a rights-respecting invisible-hand process (dominant protective associations), without violating the anarchist's objection to coercion. Part II argues no more extensive state is justified: justice is entitlement — holdings are just if justly acquired or justly transferred, with rectification for past injustice — and patterned redistribution ("from each as they choose, to each as they are chosen") requires continuous interference with liberty; taxation of earnings is, in Nozick's famous comparison, on a par with forced labour. The Wilt Chamberlain example presses the challenge to patterned theories. Part III sketches a meta-utopia: a framework within which diverse communities may realise their own utopias voluntarily. Critics answered on acquisition (what counts as just original appropriation), rectification (its implications for real histories of conquest and slavery), and the moral baseline itself.
