Taqi ad-Din Ahmad ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328), commonly known as Ibn Taymiyya, was a Sunni Muslim scholar, jurist and theologian of the Hanbali school who lived in Mamluk Syria. He is one of the most influential and controversial figures in Islamic intellectual history, known for his insistence on returning to the Qur'an and Sunna as understood by the early generations (the salaf) and for his polemics against practices and doctrines he considered innovations.

Life

Born in Harran, his family fled the Mongol advance to Damascus when he was a child. A prodigiously learned jurist, he taught and preached in Damascus and took part in campaigns against the Mongol invasions of Syria, issuing famous fatwas concerning the Mongols. His uncompromising views — including criticism of saint veneration, tomb visitation, Sufi practices and philosophical theology — repeatedly brought him into conflict with the authorities and rival scholars; he was imprisoned several times and died in the Citadel of Damascus in 1328.

Works and influence

His vast output, including the 37-volume Majmu' al-Fatawa, Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa an-Naql (a critique of Aristotelian logic and kalam) and Al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya, profoundly influenced his student Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya and, centuries later, revivalist movements from Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab to modern Salafism. Both quietist scholars and, controversially, militant groups have claimed his legacy, particularly his fatwas on jihad and on the Mongols.
