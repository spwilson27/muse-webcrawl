Orchiopexy

Orchiopexy

Other names: Orchidopexy

Specialty: Paediatric urology, urology

Indication: Cryptorchidism (undescended testicle)

Orchiopexy (or orchidopexy) is a surgical operation to move an undescended testicle (cryptorchidism) into the scrotum and permanently fix it there. It is one of the most common operations of paediatric surgery. The term derives from the Greek orchis (testicle) and pexis (fixation).

Rationale

The testes normally descend from the abdomen into the scrotum before birth. In roughly 3% of full-term and up to 30% of premature boys one or both testes fail to descend fully. A testicle retained in the abdomen or groin is exposed to higher temperatures, which impairs sperm production, and is at increased risk of torsion, associated inguinal hernia, trauma, and testicular cancer. Bringing the testicle into the scrotum improves fertility prospects, allows the organ to be examined for early signs of malignancy, and gives a normal appearance. Current guidelines recommend surgery between 6 and 18 months of age, after allowing time for spontaneous descent in the first months of life.

Technique

For a testicle that can be felt in the groin, the standard operation uses an inguinal incision: the testicle and cord are mobilised, the accompanying hernia sac is divided and ligated, and the testicle is placed in a pouch created in the scrotum (the dartos pouch) and fixed without tension. For a non-palpable testicle, laparoscopy is first used to locate it; high abdominal testes may require a staged Fowler–Stephens procedure in which the testicular vessels are divided and the testicle is later brought down on collateral blood supply. The operation is usually performed as day surgery under general anaesthesia.

Outcomes

Success rates exceed 90% for low undescended testes and are somewhat lower for abdominal testes. Complications include re-ascent of the testicle, atrophy from damage to its blood supply, and injury to the vas deferens. Even after successful orchiopexy, men with a history of cryptorchidism are advised to perform regular testicular self-examination, as their lifetime risk of testicular cancer remains somewhat elevated — though the operation makes such examination possible.
