History of Tibet

The history of Tibet covers the Tibetan Empire of the 7th–9th centuries, the spread of Buddhism, Mongol and Qing overlordship, a period of de facto independence (1912–1951), incorporation into the People's Republic of China, and the exile of the Dalai Lama after 1959.

Tibetan Empire

Songtsen Gampo (7th century) unified the plateau, founded Lhasa, and is credited with introducing Buddhism and the Tibetan script. Under Trisong Detsen, Buddhism was established as state religion and Samye, the first monastery, was founded. The empire reached its height under Ralpachen, signing a peace treaty with Tang China in 821; it collapsed in 842 after the assassination of Langdarma.

Fragmentation and the later diffusion

After a period of fragmentation, Buddhism revived in the "later diffusion" from the 10th century, producing the great schools — Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya and later Gelug. In the 13th century Tibet came under Mongol influence; Sakya lamas served as imperial preceptors to the Yuan court, and Phagmodrupa and other regimes followed.

The Ganden Phodrang and Qing period

The 5th Dalai Lama, Ngawang Lobzang Gyatso, with Mongol support, unified Tibet in 1642 and built the Potala Palace, founding the Ganden Phodrang government. From 1720 the Qing dynasty stationed ambans in Lhasa and exercised varying degrees of control. The Younghusband expedition of 1904 forced Tibet open to Britain.

1912–1951 and after

After the Qing fell, the 13th Dalai Lama declared Tibet's independence in 1913, and Tibet functioned as a de facto independent state. In 1950–51 the People's Liberation Army entered Tibet; the Seventeen Point Agreement was signed, and after the failed 1959 uprising the 14th Dalai Lama fled to India, where the Central Tibetan Administration sits in Dharamsala. The Tibet Autonomous Region was established in 1965. Cultural Revolution destruction, subsequent liberalisation, tourism and railway links to China have reshaped Tibetan society, while the political status of Tibet remains disputed internationally.
