Giffen good

Giffen good
FieldMicroeconomics
Named afterSir Robert Giffen
PropertyDemand rises as price rises
Related conceptsVeblen good, inferior good, law of demand

A Giffen good is a product whose demand increases as its price rises — and falls as its price falls — in apparent violation of the law of demand. Such behaviour can occur for a strongly inferior good that absorbs a large share of a poor consumer's budget and has no close substitutes: when the staple's price rises, the income effect of the price change (the consumer is effectively poorer) forces the consumer to cut back on costlier foods and buy more of the staple, outweighing the normal substitution effect.

History

The phenomenon is named for the Scottish economist and statistician Sir Robert Giffen, to whom Alfred Marshall attributed the observation in the third edition of his Principles of Economics (1895): as the price of bread rose, the poorest working families, already spending most of their income on bread, were driven to consume more of it and less meat. Whether Giffen himself documented such a case has been disputed, and the potato during the Irish famine of the 1840s — long cited as the classic example — is now generally doubted, since potato supply collapsed and consumption fell.

Conditions and evidence

For Giffen behaviour a good must (1) be inferior, so that demand rises as real income falls; (2) take up a large share of expenditure; and (3) lack readily available substitutes. Because these conditions are rarely all met, convincing real-world cases are scarce. The best-known evidence is a 2008 field study by Robert Jensen and Nolan Miller, who subsidised staple prices for poor households in China and found Giffen behaviour for rice in Hunan province (and weaker evidence for wheat in Gansu): households given a discount on rice bought less of it, diversifying into other foods.

Giffen vs. Veblen goods

Giffen goods are often confused with Veblen goods — luxuries whose demand rises with price because the high price itself confers status. The mechanisms are entirely different: Giffen behaviour is driven by poverty and the income effect on a necessity; Veblen behaviour by conspicuous consumption of luxuries.
