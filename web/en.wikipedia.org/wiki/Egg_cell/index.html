Egg cell

The egg cell, or ovum (plural ova), is the female reproductive cell (gamete) in most anisogamous organisms — those in which the reproductive cells of the two sexes differ markedly in size. In animals the egg is a large, typically non-motile cell carrying a haploid nucleus, a store of cytoplasm, yolk, and maternal messenger RNAs, and protective coverings such as the zona pellucida in mammals. Upon fertilisation by a sperm cell it becomes a diploid zygote, the first cell of a new organism.

In animals

In mammals, egg cells develop inside ovarian follicles through oogenesis, a process that begins before birth: primary oocytes arrest in meiosis during fetal life and resume development after puberty, usually with one egg ovulated per menstrual cycle in humans. The mature human oocyte, about 0.1 mm in diameter, is among the largest cells of the body. In egg-laying animals — birds, reptiles, most fish, amphibians, and invertebrates — eggs are provisioned with large amounts of yolk and are laid in shells or gelatinous coats; the cleidoic egg of birds and reptiles, with its amnion, chorion, and allantois, freed vertebrate reproduction from standing water.

In plants and other organisms

In flowering plants the egg cell sits inside the embryo sac within the ovule; double fertilisation joins one sperm nucleus to the egg to form the zygote and another to the central cell to form the nutritive endosperm. Mosses, ferns, and other plants with an alternation of generations produce eggs in archegonia. In oogamous algae and fungi the egg is retained in the oogonium where fertilisation occurs.

History of research

William Harvey's motto ex ovo omnia ("everything from an egg," 1651) anticipated the cell theory; Karl Ernst von Baer identified the mammalian ovum in 1827, and Oscar Hertwig showed in the 1870s that fertilisation consists of the fusion of egg and sperm nuclei. The egg's cytoplasm also carries mitochondria, so mitochondrial DNA is inherited almost exclusively from the mother in most animals.
