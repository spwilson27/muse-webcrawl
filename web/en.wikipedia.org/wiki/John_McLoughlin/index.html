John McLoughlin (October 19, 1784 – September 3, 1857) was a French-Canadian, later American, chief factor and superintendent of the Columbia District for the Hudson's Bay Company from 1824 to 1845. From Fort Vancouver on the Columbia River he exercised near-total authority over the Pacific Northwest fur trade, and his generous aid to arriving American settlers earned him the title "Father of Oregon".

Early life

McLoughlin was born at Rivière-du-Loup, Quebec, trained as a physician, and joined the North West Company in 1803. After the 1821 merger with the Hudson's Bay Company he was appointed chief factor of the Columbia District and moved its headquarters to the new Fort Vancouver in 1825.

Chief factor at Fort Vancouver

Tall, imposing and famed for both his temper and his humanity, McLoughlin built Fort Vancouver into a self-sufficient depot with farms, mills and a shipyard, dominating trade from California to Alaska. Although instructed to discourage American settlement, he extended credit, food and supplies to exhausted Oregon Trail emigrants, saving many lives and enabling the settlements he was meant to prevent.

Later life in Oregon

Ordered to relocate the depot north after the 1846 Oregon Treaty set the border at the 49th parallel, McLoughlin resigned and settled at Oregon City, where he had founded a milling claim. He became a U.S. citizen in 1849 and was elected mayor of Oregon City in 1851, though nativist rivals cheated him of much of his land claim under the Donation Land Act. He died there in 1857; the Oregon legislature formally cleared his name in 1957, a century later. His Oregon City house is now a National Historic Site.
