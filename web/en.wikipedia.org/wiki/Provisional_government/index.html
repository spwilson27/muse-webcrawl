Provisional government

A provisional government, also called an interim government, emergency government or transitional government, is a temporary government formed to manage a period of transition, usually following the collapse of a previous administration, a revolution, or the creation of a new state. Provisional governments are generally appointed rather than elected, and their legitimacy derives from the promise of organising elections or a constitutional settlement.

Characteristics

Provisional governments typically exercise power for a limited period, organise elections or a constituent assembly, and may be constrained from making irreversible policy decisions. In international law, a provisional government may seek recognition from other states in order to conduct foreign relations, sign treaties and control state assets abroad.

Examples

Famous examples include the Russian Provisional Government of 1917, which replaced the Tsar and was overthrown by the Bolsheviks; the Provisional Government of the French Republic (1944–1946) under Charles de Gaulle; the provisional governments formed across Eastern Europe in 1989–1990; the Coalition Provisional Authority in Iraq (2003–2004); and transitional councils formed after the Arab Spring, such as Libya's National Transitional Council in 2011.
