Stable marriage problem

In mathematics, economics, and computer science, the stable marriage problem (also called the stable matching problem) is the problem of finding a stable matching between two equally sized sets of elements, given an ordering of preferences for each element. A matching is stable if there do not exist two elements — a "blocking pair" — who would both prefer each other to their current partners.

Gale–Shapley algorithm

David Gale and Lloyd Shapley showed in 1962 that a stable matching always exists, and gave an algorithm to find one: the deferred acceptance algorithm. In the classic formulation, one side (say the men) proposes in order of preference; the other side tentatively accepts the best offer received so far and rejects the rest; rejected proposers move on to their next choice. The process ends with a stable matching that is optimal for the proposing side among all stable matchings. The algorithm runs in O(n2) time.

Applications

The theory of stable matching underpins real matching markets. Alvin Roth applied it to the design of the National Resident Matching Program, which assigns medical graduates to hospital residencies in the United States, and to school-choice systems in cities such as New York and Boston, as well as kidney-exchange programmes. Gale and Shapley's work and its later development were recognised by the 2012 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, awarded to Shapley and Roth.

Related problems

Variants include the stable roommates problem (one set, where stable matchings need not exist), the hospitals/residents problem with capacities, and matching with incomplete lists or ties. Stable matchings form a distributive lattice, and the set of achievable partners for each participant is characterised by the "rural hospitals theorem".
