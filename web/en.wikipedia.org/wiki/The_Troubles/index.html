The Troubles

PeriodLate 1960s – 1998 (Good Friday Agreement)
LocationNorthern Ireland; spillover to Ireland and Britain
DeathsMore than 3,500

The Troubles (Irish: Na Trioblóidí) was an ethno-nationalist conflict in Northern Ireland lasting roughly thirty years, from the late 1960s to the Good Friday Agreement of 1998. It centred on the constitutional status of Northern Ireland: unionists, predominantly Protestant, wished it to remain in the United Kingdom; Irish nationalists and republicans, predominantly Catholic, sought a united Ireland.

Origins and course

The conflict grew out of discrimination against the nationalist minority in housing, employment and voting. A civil rights movement in the late 1960s met a loyalist and police backlash; rioting in Derry and Belfast in 1969 led to the deployment of British troops. Republican and loyalist paramilitary organisations — including the Provisional IRA, the UVF and the UDA — began campaigns of bombings and shootings. Key episodes included Bloody Sunday (1972), when British paratroopers killed fourteen unarmed civil rights marchers in Derry; the 1981 hunger strikes in which ten republican prisoners, including Bobby Sands MP, died; and atrocities such as Enniskillen (1987) and Omagh (1998).

Resolution

More than 3,500 people were killed, over half of them civilians. Ceasefires in 1994 and talks involving the British and Irish governments and Northern Irish parties produced the Good Friday Agreement of April 1998, establishing a power-sharing assembly, cross-border bodies and the principle of consent. Decommissioning of weapons and a devolved administration followed, though sporadic dissident violence continued.
