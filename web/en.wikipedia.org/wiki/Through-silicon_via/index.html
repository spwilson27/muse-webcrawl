Through-silicon via

In electronic engineering, a through-silicon via (TSV) is a vertical electrical connection (via) that passes completely through a silicon wafer or die. TSV technology is a key enabler of three-dimensional integrated circuits (3D ICs), allowing multiple chips to be stacked and interconnected with far shorter, denser connections than conventional wire bonding.

Advantages

Stacking dies with TSVs shortens interconnect lengths dramatically compared with routing signals around the edges of stacked packages. The result is higher bandwidth, lower latency and power consumption, and a smaller footprint — important for high-performance computing, image sensors, and memory devices.

Applications

TSVs first reached high-volume production in CMOS image sensors. Their best-known application is in High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), in which DRAM dies are stacked on a base die and connected by thousands of TSVs to deliver very wide memory interfaces close to processors and GPUs. TSVs are also used in silicon interposers (sometimes called 2.5D integration), where chips sit side by side on an interposer containing the vias.

Manufacturing

TSVs are typically formed by deep reactive-ion etching of holes into the silicon, lining them with an insulating layer and barrier, and filling them with copper by electroplating; the wafer is then thinned to expose the vias. Processes are categorised as via-first, via-middle, or via-last depending on when the vias are made relative to transistor fabrication. Challenges include thermal and mechanical stress from copper's differing expansion, wafer thinning and handling, yield, and test of stacked dies.
