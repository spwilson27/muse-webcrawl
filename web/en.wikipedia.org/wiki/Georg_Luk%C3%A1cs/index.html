Georg Lukács

Georg Lukács
Born: 13 April 1885, Budapest, Austria-Hungary
Died: 4 June 1971, Budapest, Hungary
Occupation: Philosopher, literary critic

Georg Lukács (13 April 1885 – 4 June 1971) was a Hungarian Marxist philosopher, literary historian, and critic. He is widely considered one of the founders of Western Marxism, an interpretive tradition that departed from Soviet orthodoxy, and an influential theorist of the novel and literary realism.

Life

Born György Bernát Löwinger to a wealthy Jewish banking family in Budapest, Lukács studied in Budapest, Berlin, and Heidelberg, where he associated with Max Weber. He joined the Hungarian Communist Party in 1918 and served as People's Commissar for Education in the short-lived Hungarian Soviet Republic of 1919. After its fall he fled to Vienna, later living in Berlin and Moscow before returning to Hungary in 1945. He served briefly as Minister of Culture during the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 and was deported to Romania before being allowed back to Budapest.

Works

His early The Theory of the Novel (1920) analyzed the novel as the form of a "transcendental homelessness". History and Class Consciousness (1923), containing the famous essay on reification, became a foundational text of Western Marxism. Later works defended 19th-century realism — Balzac, Scott, Tolstoy, Thomas Mann — against modernism, in studies such as The Historical Novel.

Influence

Lukács influenced the Frankfurt School, the Budapest School (including Ágnes Heller), and generations of literary theorists; concepts such as reification and class consciousness remain central in critical theory.
