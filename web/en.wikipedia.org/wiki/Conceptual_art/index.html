Conceptual art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: One and Three Chairs (1965) by Joseph Kosuth: a wooden chair, a photograph of the chair, and a dictionary definition of 'chair' mounted side by side]

Conceptual art is art in which the concept or idea behind the work takes precedence over traditional aesthetic and material concerns. The term came into wide use in the late 1960s for work by Sol LeWitt — who wrote that 'the idea becomes a machine that makes the art' — Joseph Kosuth, Lawrence Weiner, and the British group Art & Language, though Marcel Duchamp's readymades are its acknowledged foundation.

Conceptual works include Kosuth's One and Three Chairs (1965), Weiner's text statements, On Kawara's date paintings, and instruction-based pieces executed by others or not at all: the artwork as definition, proposition or documentation rather than precious object.

Dematerialisation

Critics Lucy Lippard and John Chandler dubbed the trend 'the dematerialization of the art object' (1968): art that might exist as a typed statement, a map, a photograph of an action, or a set of instructions — resistant, in theory, to commodification, though the market soon learned to sell the certificates. The movement overlapped with Minimalism, Fluxus, land art and performance art, and shifted art education and curating for good.

Legacy

Conceptual art's insistence that art is whatever the artist and discourse frame as art defined the terms of contemporary practice; its textual, archival and systems-based methods remain the default grammar of biennial art worldwide.
