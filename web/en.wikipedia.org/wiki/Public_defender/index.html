Public defender

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A public defender is a lawyer employed by the state to represent defendants in criminal cases who are unable to afford private counsel. The institution implements the right to counsel — in the United States, the Sixth Amendment right as extended to the states and to all felony (and imprisonable misdemeanour) prosecutions by Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), one of the most famous Supreme Court decisions; comparable guarantees exist in most democratic legal systems through legal aid, duty solicitor schemes and assigned counsel.

Operation and strains

Public defender offices (at county, state and federal levels in the US) handle the majority of criminal defendants in many jurisdictions. Chronic underfunding, excessive caseloads and low pay are documented problems — defenders have litigated and struck over caseloads far above professional standards — with consequences for plea quality, wrongful convictions and equality of arms against better-resourced prosecutors. Alternatives include assigned-counsel panels and mixed models, and reform literature compares salaried defender offices favourably with flat-fee appointment systems on quality per dollar.
