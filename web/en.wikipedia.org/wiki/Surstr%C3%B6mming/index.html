Surströmming

Surströmming (Swedish for "soured herring") is a lightly salted, fermented Baltic Sea herring traditionally eaten in northern Sweden. The herring is fermented in barrels for several months and then canned, where fermentation continues — causing the cans to bulge — making surströmming notorious for one of the most pungent odours of any food in the world.

Preparation and consumption

Small Baltic herring are caught in spring, salted, and fermented. The canning process dates to the 19th century. Because of the powerful smell — a mixture of propionic, butyric, and acetic acids and hydrogen sulfide — the cans are traditionally opened outdoors or under water. The fish is usually eaten in late summer at special surströmmingsskiva parties, served on tunnbröd flatbread with boiled potatoes, diced onion, and sour cream, often accompanied by snaps.

Regulations

The European Union exempts surströmming from rules limiting dioxins in fish. Several airlines classify the cans as potentially explosive hazards and have banned them from flights.
