The Pueblo peoples are Native American peoples of the Southwestern United States who share common agricultural, material and religious traditions and whose historic and modern communities are characterised by compact, multi-storey dwellings built of adobe, stone and timber — the villages the Spanish called pueblos ("towns"). Today there are 19 federally recognised Pueblo tribes in New Mexico, plus the Hopi in Arizona and Ysleta del Sur Pueblo in Texas. Despite their shared way of life, the Pueblo peoples speak languages of several unrelated families and maintain distinct tribal identities and governments.

Origins

The Pueblo peoples descend principally from the Ancestral Puebloans, whose great centres included Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde. From around the 12th to 14th centuries, large Ancestral Pueblo settlements in the Four Corners region were abandoned — under pressure from drought and social change — and populations consolidated in the Rio Grande valley and in the high deserts of western New Mexico and Arizona, where their descendants live today.

History

Spanish contact and the Pueblo Revolt

Spanish expeditions reached the Pueblos in 1540 with Francisco Vázquez de Coronado. Colonisation from 1598 brought missions, tribute demands and religious suppression. In 1680 the Pueblos united in the Pueblo Revolt, coordinated by the religious leader Po'pay of San Juan Pueblo, driving the Spanish from New Mexico for twelve years — the most successful Indigenous uprising against a European colonial power in North America. Spanish rule was restored from 1692, after which a pragmatic accommodation left Pueblos substantial autonomy and confirmed their land grants.

American period

After the Mexican–American War (1848) the Pueblos came under United States rule. Their status as sovereign tribal nations with communal land grants was litigated for decades; the Pueblo Lands Act of 1924 addressed land-title disputes, and an influential 1913 Supreme Court decision (United States v. Sandoval) confirmed federal guardianship. In the 20th century the Pueblos defended water rights, religious freedom — including access to sacred sites and eagle feathers — and tribal self-government through the All Pueblo Council, one of the oldest Indigenous intergovernmental bodies in the US.

Culture

Pueblo societies have historically been organised around clan systems (matrilineal among the western Keres, Zuni and Hopi), ceremonial societies and rituals held in round ceremonial chambers called kivas. Agriculture centred on maize, beans and squash, irrigated in the Rio Grande valley and dry-farmed among the western Pueblos. Pueblo pottery — notably the black-on-black ware popularised by Maria Martinez of San Ildefonso — jewellery, weaving and feast-day dances remain vital cultural expressions. Religious practice is guarded: several Pueblos restrict photography and outside attendance at ceremonies.

Languages

Pueblo languages belong to several unrelated families: Keresan (a language isolate), the Tanoan languages Tiwa, Tewa and Towa (Kiowa-Tanoan family), Zuni (isolate) and Hopi (Uto-Aztecan). Language revitalisation programmes operate in many communities.
