Oak

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An oak is a tree or shrub of the genus Quercus in the beech family, with roughly 500 species across the Northern Hemisphere. Oaks are famed for their strong, durable timber, their fruit — the acorn — and their longevity: many live for centuries, and they support more wildlife than almost any other tree in their range.

Characteristics

Oaks divide broadly into white oaks (rounded leaf lobes, sweeter acorns maturing in one season) and red oaks (bristle-tipped lobes, bitter acorns taking two seasons). They are wind-pollinated, producing catkins in spring; acorns are dispersed and cached by jays, squirrels and other animals, which plant many future trees. A single mature oak can host hundreds of insect species and the birds and bats that feed on them.

Uses and culture

Oak timber builds ships, houses, furniture and wine and whisky barrels (whose flavours partly come from the wood); cork comes from the bark of the cork oak. Oaks symbolise strength and endurance in many cultures — they are national trees of several countries including England, Germany and the United States — and ancient oaks feature in myth, law and landmark place names. Threats include sudden oak death and acute oak decline diseases.
