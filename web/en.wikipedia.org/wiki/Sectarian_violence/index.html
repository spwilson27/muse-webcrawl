Sectarian violence

Sectarian violence and/or sectarian strife refer to violence that is inspired, influenced, or targeted based on religious or ideological identification with a group, often between groups of differing religions or political factions. It is a form of communal violence and is commonly associated with disputes over territory, political power, and communal identity.

Definition

Sectarian violence differs from other forms of violence in that the victim is targeted not for who they are as an individual but for their membership in a particular sect or group. Scholars distinguish it from ethnic conflict, though in practice sectarian and ethnic identities frequently overlap, and violence may be perpetrated by state actors, paramilitary organisations, or communal mobs.

Examples

Historical and modern examples include Sunni–Shia violence in the Middle East and South Asia, Protestant–Catholic conflict during the Troubles in Northern Ireland, Hindu–Muslim communal riots in India, and violence between Buddhist and Muslim communities in Myanmar and Sri Lanka. Sectarian violence intensified in Iraq following the 2003 invasion, particularly after the 2006 bombing of the al-Askari Mosque in Samarra, and in Syria during its civil war.

Causes

Analysts attribute sectarian violence to a combination of political manipulation by elites, competition for resources and representation, historical grievances, and security dilemmas in which each community arms itself out of fear of the other. Once embedded, segregated housing, education, and commemorative practices can reproduce sectarian identities across generations.
