Paul Baran

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Paul Baran
Born29 April 1926, Grodno, Poland (now Belarus)
Died26 March 2011 (aged 84), Palo Alto, California
Known forInventing distributed packet switching (1960–1964)
[Image: Portrait photograph of Paul Baran in the 1960s at RAND]

Paul Baran (29 April 1926 – 26 March 2011) was a Polish-American engineer who was a pioneer in the development of computer networks. At the RAND Corporation in the early 1960s, working on Cold War communications survivability, he developed the concept of distributed, redundant packet networks — the intellectual foundation of Packet switching.

Baran's problem was stark: how could command-and-control communications survive a nuclear first strike? His answer, set out in the eleven-volume 1964 study On Distributed Communications, was a network with no centre: messages chopped into standardised 'message blocks', routed adaptively ('hot-potato routing') over many redundant links, reassembling at their destination. Each node was equal; the network could lose many and still deliver. AT&T, then America's telephone monopoly, famously dismissed the idea as unworkable.

When the ARPANET was designed, Baran's work — alongside Donald Davies's independent British invention of packets — informed its architecture; the Internet that resulted embodies Baran's principles at world scale. He later co-founded the Institute for the Future and several networking companies, and received the National Medal of Technology and Innovation in 2007.
