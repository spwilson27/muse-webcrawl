Angel investor

An angel investor (also known as a business angel, informal investor, angel funder, private investor, or seed investor) is an individual who provides capital for a business or businesses to start up, usually in exchange for convertible debt or ownership equity. Angel investors usually give support to start-ups at the earliest stages, when the risk of failure is highest and institutional funding is unavailable.

Origin of the term

The term "angel" came from Broadway theatre, where it described wealthy individuals who provided money for productions that would otherwise have been shut down. In 1978, University of New Hampshire professor William Wetzel Jr. applied the term to individuals who invested in entrepreneurial ventures, based on his study of how entrepreneurs raised seed capital in New England.

Investment profile

Angel investors are typically affluent individuals — in the United States they generally must meet the Securities and Exchange Commission's definition of accredited investors, with a net worth of at least $1 million or annual income above $200,000. Typical investments range from tens of thousands to a few million dollars, made in pre-seed and seed rounds ahead of venture capital financing. Angels often invest in syndicates or through angel groups and networks, which pool due diligence and capital. Because most startups fail, professional angels build portfolios, expecting a few successes to pay for many losses.

Role in the funding ecosystem

Angel investment fills the gap between "friends and family" money and venture capital. Besides money, angels — frequently successful former entrepreneurs — provide mentoring, contacts and credibility. The growth of angel activity since the 1990s, and the rise of platforms and crowdfunding, has made early-stage capital more widely available, while regulations governing solicitation and accreditation continue to shape the market.

Notable examples

Famous angel investments include Jeff Bezos's early funding of Google, Peter Thiel's $500,000 investment in Facebook (its first outside investment), and Ron Conway's broad portfolio of Silicon Valley startups. Business angels funded early stages of companies such as Apple, Amazon and Uber before institutional rounds.
