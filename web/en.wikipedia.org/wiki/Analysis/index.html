Mathematical analysis

[Image: a blackboard covered with epsilon-delta limit definitions and integral signs in chalk]

Mathematical analysis is the branch of mathematics dealing with limits and related theories, such as differentiation, integration, measure, infinite sequences and series, and analytic functions. These theories are usually studied in the context of real and complex numbers and functions, and analysis evolved from calculus, which involves functions, sequences and series.

Major subfields include real analysis, complex analysis, functional analysis, harmonic analysis, differential equations and numerical analysis — the toolkit underlying most of modern science and engineering.

History

Ideas of analysis appear in ancient Greek mathematics — Eudoxus's method of exhaustion and Archimedes' calculations of areas and volumes anticipated integration. Calculus was created independently in the seventeenth century by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, but rested on vague notions of infinitesimals that drew criticism, famously from Bishop Berkeley.

In the nineteenth century, Cauchy, Weierstrass, Riemann, Dedekind and others rebuilt analysis on rigorous foundations: the epsilon–delta definition of limit, precise definitions of continuity, the construction of the real numbers, and Lebesgue's measure theory (1902) which transformed integration.

Main ideas

Analysis begins with limits and continuity, proceeds through derivatives and integrals, and extends to infinite series, sequences of functions, and functions of several variables. Functional analysis generalises these ideas to infinite-dimensional vector spaces, providing the mathematical language of quantum mechanics.

Rigorous analysis distinguishes mathematics from mere calculation: it supplies proofs that the methods of calculus work, and precise statements of when they fail.
