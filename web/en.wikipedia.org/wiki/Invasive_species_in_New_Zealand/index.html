Invasive species in New Zealand

A number of introduced species, some of them invasive species, have caused severe damage to New Zealand's native flora and fauna. Because New Zealand was isolated for roughly 80 million years before human settlement, its ecosystems evolved in the absence of land mammals (other than bats), and many endemic species — including flightless birds such as the kiwi, the kākāpō and the moa — lacked defences against mammalian predators and browsers. The arrival of humans, first Māori and then Europeans, brought a succession of introductions that transformed the environment.

Introduced animals

Māori brought the Pacific rat (kiore) and the kurī dog. European settlement added ship and Norway rats, mice, cats, stoats, ferrets and weasels — the mustelids deliberately released in the 1880s to control rabbits, themselves introduced for food and fur and a major pasture pest. The Australian common brushtail possum, introduced for a fur trade, became one of the most destructive invaders, browsing native forests and preying on eggs and chicks. Deer, goats, pigs, tahr and chamois were liberated for hunting and now damage native vegetation, while introduced wasps, Argentine ants and freshwater fish such as trout and koi harm native invertebrates and aquatic ecosystems.

Introduced plants

New Zealand has more introduced plant species naturalised in the wild than native species. Problem weeds include gorse, old man's beard, wilding conifers (self-sown pines invading tussock grasslands), wandering willy and many garden escapes.

Control and eradication

The Department of Conservation and regional councils run extensive pest control using trapping, fencing, and toxins such as 1080 poison. Introduced predators have been eradicated from many offshore islands, which now serve as sanctuaries for endangered species, and fenced mainland sanctuaries such as Zealandia recreate predator-free habitat. In 2016 the government announced Predator Free 2050, an ambitious programme aiming to eradicate rats, stoats and possums from the entire country by 2050. Border biosecurity, administered by the Ministry for Primary Industries, is correspondingly strict to prevent new invasions.
