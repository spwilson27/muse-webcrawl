Periodontitis, also called gum disease, is a chronic inflammatory disease of the tissues surrounding and supporting the teeth (the periodontium). It is caused by microorganisms in dental plaque and the host's inflammatory response to them, and it progressively destroys the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, forming periodontal pockets. Left untreated, it leads to tooth mobility and tooth loss; it is the leading cause of tooth loss in adults.

Signs and symptoms

Early periodontitis develops from untreated gingivitis. Gums become red, swollen and bleed easily, and may recede, exposing root surfaces. Deep pockets form between tooth and gum, trapping plaque and calculus below the gumline. Later features include persistent bad breath, pus, drifting or loosening of teeth, and pain on biting. Much of the damage is painless, so the disease is often advanced when detected.

Causes and risk factors

The disease is initiated by a dysbiotic dental biofilm, with anaerobic Gram-negative bacteria such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia and Treponema denticola prominent in deep pockets. Tissue destruction is driven largely by the host immune response. Smoking is the strongest modifiable risk factor; diabetes (especially if poorly controlled), genetic susceptibility, stress, poor nutrition and inadequate oral hygiene also increase risk and severity.

Diagnosis and classification

Diagnosis is by periodontal probing (pocket depths and clinical attachment loss), assessment of bleeding on probing, and dental radiographs showing alveolar bone loss. The 2017 World Workshop classification stages periodontitis by severity and complexity (Stages I–IV) and grades it by rate of progression (Grades A–C).

Treatment

Treatment aims to control the biofilm and inflammation: meticulous oral hygiene, professional scaling and root planing (deep cleaning), risk-factor control (smoking cessation, diabetes management), and, where indicated, antibiotics or periodontal surgery including flap surgery, bone grafts and guided tissue regeneration. Lifelong supportive periodontal maintenance is essential because the condition is chronic and can recur.

Systemic links

Periodontitis is associated epidemiologically with cardiovascular disease, adverse pregnancy outcomes, rheumatoid arthritis and poorer glycaemic control in diabetes, though the causal nature of most links remains under study.
