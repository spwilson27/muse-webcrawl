James Smithson

Bornc. 1765, Paris, France
Died27 June 1829 (aged about 64), Genoa, Italy
EducationPembroke College, Oxford
Known forFounding bequest of the Smithsonian Institution
FieldsChemistry, mineralogy

James Smithson (c. 1765 – 27 June 1829) was an English chemist and mineralogist. He published numerous scientific papers for the Royal Society during the late 1700s as well as assisting in the development of calamine, which would eventually be renamed after him as "smithsonite". He was the founding donor of the Smithsonian Institution, which also bears his name — despite the fact that he never visited the United States.

Early life

Smithson was born in Paris, the illegitimate son of Hugh Percy (born Hugh Smithson), who became the 1st Duke of Northumberland, and Elizabeth Hungerford Keate Macie, a widow descended from Henry VII. He was educated in England, attending Pembroke College, Oxford, where he graduated in 1786 with a degree in chemistry and mineralogy. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1787, at about the age of 22.

Scientific work

Smithson travelled widely in Europe studying geology and chemistry, visiting mines, volcanoes and universities. His research included the chemical analysis of minerals; he showed that the ores known as calamine were in fact two distinct minerals, zinc carbonate and zinc silicate — the carbonate was later named smithsonite in his honour. He published some 27 papers on topics including mineral analysis, the chemistry of teardrops, and improved methods for making coffee.

The bequest

Smithson's will left his fortune to his nephew, Henry James Hungerford; but it contained a famous contingency clause: if Hungerford died without children, the estate would pass "to the United States of America, to found at Washington, under the name of the Smithsonian Institution, an Establishment for the increase and diffusion of knowledge among men." Hungerford died childless in 1835, and after litigation in the Chancery courts the United States received the bequest — over 100,000 gold sovereigns, worth more than $500,000 — in 1838. After a decade of debate over what form the institution should take, Congress established the Smithsonian Institution in 1846.

Death and remains

Smithson died in Genoa in 1829 and was buried in the Protestant cemetery there. In 1904, when the cemetery was to be moved, Alexander Graham Bell, a Smithsonian regent, arranged for Smithson's remains to be brought to Washington; they now rest in a crypt in the Smithsonian Institution Building ("the Castle"). Smithson's reasons for the bequest remain unknown — he is reported to have remarked that his name would live on through it long after the dukes of Northumberland were forgotten.
