Tennis Court Oath

The Tennis Court Oath (French: Serment du Jeu de Paume) was a pivotal event during the first days of the French Revolution. On 20 June 1789, deputies of the Third Estate — who had declared themselves the National Assembly three days earlier — found their usual meeting hall in Versailles locked and guarded. Fearing a royal dissolution, they repaired to a nearby indoor tennis (jeu de paume) court and swore not to separate until France had a constitution.

The oath

The text resolved "never to separate, and to assemble wherever circumstances require, until the constitution of the kingdom is established and consolidated upon solid foundations." Only one deputy, Martin d'Auch, refused to sign. The oath defied the royal order to disperse by estate; three days later, at the royal session, the deputies refused the king's command to deliberate separately — prompting Mirabeau's famous reply that they were there by the will of the people and would leave only at bayonet point. Within days most clergy and many nobles joined the Assembly, and Louis XVI capitulated, ordering the remaining deputies to join.

Legacy

The oath is remembered as the founding act of national sovereignty in France — the moment representatives of the nation claimed constituent power. Jacques-Louis David's monumental painting of the scene was left unfinished amid the Revolution's shifting politics; the court itself survives in Versailles as a museum. The event is commemorated as a landmark in the development of modern representative democracy.
