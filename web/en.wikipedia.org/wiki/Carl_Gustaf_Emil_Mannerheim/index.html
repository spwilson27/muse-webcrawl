Baron Carl Gustaf Emil Mannerheim (4 June 1867 – 27 January 1951) was a Finnish military commander, statesman and explorer. A member of a Swedish-speaking Finnish family of Dutch origin, Mannerheim made a career in the Imperial Russian Army, rising to lieutenant general and travelling overland across Asia on an intelligence and ethnographic expedition from Turkestan to Beijing (1906–1908). He became Finland's commander-in-chief during the Finnish Civil War of 1918, served as Regent (head of state) in 1918–1919, and led Finland's armed forces in the Winter War (1939–1940) and Continuation War (1941–1944) against the Soviet Union. He served as the sixth President of Finland from 1944 to 1946, steering the country through its exit from the Second World War.

Service in Russia and the Asian expedition

Educated at the Nikolayev Cavalry School in St Petersburg, Mannerheim served in the Russo-Japanese War and the First World War, commanding divisions and an army corps on the Eastern Front. Between 1906 and 1908, disguised partly as an ethnographic collector, he rode some 14,000 km across Central Asia to China for Russian military intelligence, meeting the 13th Dalai Lama along the way and producing maps, photographs and scholarly collections still held in Helsinki.

Independent Finland

After Finland declared independence in December 1917, Mannerheim was appointed commander of the government's "White" forces and won the Civil War by May 1918. As Regent he oversaw Finland's transition toward a republic, losing the presidential election of 1919 to K. J. Ståhlberg. In the 1930s he chaired the Defence Council and oversaw the construction of the defensive line across the Karelian Isthmus that came to bear his name — the Mannerheim Line.

Second World War and presidency

Mannerheim commanded Finnish forces through the Winter War, the Continuation War alongside Germany, and the Lapland War against Germany. In August 1944 parliament appointed him president by special law; he negotiated the Moscow Armistice with the USSR, preserved Finnish independence, and resigned in March 1946 on grounds of ill health. He died in Lausanne in 1951 and received a state funeral in Helsinki. In a 2004 Finnish television poll he was voted the greatest Finn of all time.
