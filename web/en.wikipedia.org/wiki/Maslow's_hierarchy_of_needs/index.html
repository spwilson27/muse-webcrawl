Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Maslow's hierarchy of needs is a psychological theory proposed by Abraham Maslow in his 1943 paper "A Theory of Human Motivation". The theory describes human needs as a five-tier hierarchy, conventionally depicted as a pyramid: physiological needs, safety needs, love and belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. According to the theory, needs lower in the hierarchy must be largely satisfied before individuals are strongly motivated to pursue higher-level needs.

The five levels

Physiological needs — air, water, food, sleep, shelter, and other biological requirements for survival.

Safety needs — personal security, health, financial security, and protection from harm.

Love and belonging — friendship, intimacy, family, and a sense of connection with groups.

Esteem — respect, status, recognition, competence, and freedom.

Self-actualization — the realisation of one's full potential; in Maslow's words, "what a man can be, he must be".

Expansion

Maslow later expanded the model, adding cognitive needs and aesthetic needs below esteem, and transcendence needs above self-actualization, yielding an eight-stage model. He also emphasised that the hierarchy is not rigid: the order can vary by individual and circumstance, and behaviour is typically motivated by several needs at once.

Criticism

The theory has been widely taught in psychology, management, and education, but researchers have criticised its weak empirical support: studies have generally failed to confirm a strict five-level ordering of needs, and critics note the model reflects individualistic cultural assumptions — for example, ethnographic work by Maslow himself among the Blackfoot (Siksika) people suggested a different ordering in which community needs were foundational. Despite these critiques, the hierarchy remains one of the most popular frameworks in introductory psychology and in business and nursing education.
