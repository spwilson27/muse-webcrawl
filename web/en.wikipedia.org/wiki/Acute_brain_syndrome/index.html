Acute brain syndrome

Acute brain syndrome is a historical term for an acute, transient disorder of brain function marked by impaired consciousness, attention, perception, and cognition. In modern terminology it corresponds largely to delirium (also called acute confusional state), as distinct from chronic brain syndromes such as dementia.

Terminology

Mid-twentieth-century psychiatric classifications, including the first edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-I, 1952), grouped these conditions as "acute brain syndromes," emphasizing sudden onset and potential reversibility. Later classifications abandoned the term in favor of delirium, defined by a disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over a short period, fluctuates during the day, and is caused by a medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal, or multiple etiologies.

Symptoms

Characteristic features include disorientation in time and place, inattention, disorganized thinking, illusions and hallucinations (often visual), memory impairment, and altered level of consciousness ranging from hypervigilance and agitation to drowsiness. Symptoms characteristically fluctuate and worsen at night.

Causes

Underlying causes include infections (such as urinary tract infection or pneumonia), metabolic disturbances, electrolyte imbalance, hypoxia, stroke, head injury, drug effects — especially anticholinergic and sedative medications — alcohol or sedative withdrawal, and the postoperative state. Older adults and patients with pre-existing dementia are at highest risk, and delirium is associated with increased mortality and long-term cognitive decline.

Management

Treatment centers on identifying and correcting the underlying cause, ensuring hydration and orientation, mobilization, sleep hygiene, and avoiding restraints and deliriogenic drugs. Antipsychotic medication may be used cautiously for severe agitation.
