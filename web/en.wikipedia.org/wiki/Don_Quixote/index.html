Don Quixote (in full, The Ingenious Gentleman Don Quixote of La Mancha) is a Spanish novel by Miguel de Cervantes, published in two parts in 1605 and 1615. It is widely regarded as the first modern novel and one of the greatest works of literature ever written, and has been translated into more languages than almost any other book after the Bible.

Plot

Alonso Quixano, a minor nobleman from La Mancha who has read too many romances of chivalry, loses his reason and sets out as a knight-errant under the name Don Quixote, recruiting the earthy peasant Sancho Panza as his squire with promises of an island to govern. Astride his thin horse Rocinante and armed with rusty weapons, Quixote mistakes inns for castles, wineskins for giants and — in the novel's most famous episode — windmills for monsters, charging them and being knocked from his horse ("tilting at windmills" entered the language). The second part, published a decade later after a spurious sequel by another author, is more melancholic: the characters have read the first part, and Quixote's illusions are systematically dismantled until, dying sane, he renounces chivalry.

Influence

Don Quixote founded a new kind of fiction: ironic, self-aware, mixing high and low speech, and built on the dialogue between idealist and realist. Its influence runs through the whole history of the novel, from Fielding and Sterne to Dostoevsky and Flaubert, and the adjective "quixotic" — for extravagantly idealistic pursuits — remains in daily use. The critic Harold Bloom called Cervantes, with Shakespeare, the central figure of the Western canon.
