Wilma Rudolph

Wilma Glodean Rudolph (June 23, 1940 – November 12, 1994) was an American sprinter who became a world-record-holding Olympic champion and an international sports icon in track and field following her successes in the 1956 and 1960 Olympic Games. Rudolph competed in the 100 and 200 meter sprints and won three gold medals at the 1960 Summer Olympics in Rome, making her the first American woman to win three gold medals in track and field during a single Olympic Games match thus, earning on the track.

Early life

Rudolph was born in Saint Bethlehem, Tennessee, the twentieth of 22 children of Ed Rudolph, a railroad porter, and Blanche Rudolph, a maid. As a young child she contracted several illnesses, including scarlet fever, whooping cough, measles, and double pneumonia. At age five she was diagnosed with polio, which left her left leg paralyzed; she wore a leg brace until she was twelve and required orthopedic shoes for years afterward. Through physical therapy at Meharry Medical College and the determination of her family, she gradually regained full use of the leg.

Olympic career

Rudolph first competed in the 1956 Summer Olympics in Melbourne at age sixteen, winning a bronze medal in the 4 × 100 m relay. At the 1960 Rome Olympics she won gold medals in the 100 m, 200 m, and 4 × 100 m relay, tying or setting world records in the process. Her victory in the 100 m final, run into a headwind, earned her the nickname "La Gazzella Nera" ("The Black Gazelle") from Italian journalists and "La Chattanooga Choo-Choo" at home.

Later life and legacy

Rudolph retired from competition in 1962 and went on to work as a teacher, coach, and sports commentator. She founded the Wilma Rudolph Foundation to support amateur athletics. She died of a brain tumor in Nashville, Tennessee, in 1994 at age 54. She is remembered as one of the greatest sprinters of all time and a pioneering figure for African-American women in sport.
