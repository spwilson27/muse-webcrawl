Planetesimal

Planetesimals are solid objects that form in protoplanetary disks by the accretion of dust and pebbles, and are the building blocks of planets. Typically from about a kilometre to a few hundred kilometres across, they grow by colliding and merging under gravity into planetary embryos and eventually terrestrial planets, or into the cores of giant planets. Surviving planetesimals persist today as asteroids, comets and Kuiper Belt objects.

Planetesimals accreting in a protoplanetary disk

Formation

In the standard model, dust grains in the disk around a young star settle and collide to form pebbles and boulders. Concentrating these solids enough for gravity to take over was long a puzzle (the "metre-size barrier"), but the streaming instability — in which drag between gas and solids clumps pebbles into dense filaments — can collapse pebble clouds directly into planetesimals of around 100 km. The planetesimal hypothesis was developed by Viktor Safronov in the 1960s and is a foundation of the core-accretion theory of planet formation.

Growth and fate

Once formed, planetesimals grow through runaway and then oligarchic accretion, sweeping up neighbouring material; the largest become planetary embryos of Moon-to-Mars size. In the outer Solar System, planetesimal cores of 5–15 Earth masses accreted gas to become the giant planets. Collisions among leftover planetesimals ground much of the population to dust, but many survived: the visited Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth, a pristine contact binary of two gently merged lobes, is considered a largely unaltered planetesimal, and asteroids such as Vesta are differentiated survivors.
