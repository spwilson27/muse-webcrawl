Stimson Doctrine

The Stimson Doctrine is a policy of the United States government, enunciated in a note of January 7, 1932, to Japan and China, of nonrecognition of international territorial changes that were executed by force. The doctrine was an application of the principle of ex injuria jus non oritur (legal rights cannot arise from wrongful acts).

The policy was named after Henry L. Stimson, the U.S. Secretary of State under President Herbert Hoover. It was issued in response to the Japanese invasion of Manchuria following the Mukden Incident of 1931 and the establishment of the Japanese puppet state of Manchukuo.

History and application

The United States, followed by the League of Nations, refused to recognize Manchukuo. The doctrine proved ineffective at reversing the Japanese conquest but established a lasting principle of international law. It was later invoked in other cases of territorial change by force, most notably the United States' nonrecognition of the Soviet annexation of the Baltic states (affirmed in the Welles Declaration of 1940), which continued until the restoration of Baltic independence in 1991. The principle of nonrecognition of situations created by the illegal use of force is reflected in the Charter of the United Nations and subsequent international legal instruments.
