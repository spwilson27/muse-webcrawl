Blind wine tasting (or simply blind tasting) is a method of wine evaluation in which the tasters do not know the identity of the wines they are judging. Bottles are concealed — decanted, bagged, or served in black glasses — so that assessments cannot be influenced by reputation, region, or price. In a "single blind" tasting the tasters know the general theme (for example, red Burgundies) but not the individual wines; in a "double blind" tasting they know nothing in advance.

Uses

Blind tasting is standard in wine competitions, professional examinations such as those of the Master of Wine and Master Sommelier programmes, and serious criticism, because expectation strongly shapes perception. Skilled tasters use blind tasting in reverse — reasoning from structure, fruit, and aroma to identify a wine's grape, origin, and vintage — as a demonstration of expertise.

Famous results

The most famous blind tasting is the Judgment of Paris of 1976, at which French judges ranked California wines above celebrated French bottles, transforming the world wine industry. Research using blind formats has repeatedly found weak correlation between price and enjoyment for non-expert drinkers, and studies in which the same wine is presented at different stated prices have shown measurable expectation effects.
