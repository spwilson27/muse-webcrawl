Single-nucleotide polymorphism

A single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP; pronounced "snip") is a genomic variant at a single base position in DNA. Scientists typically consider a variant a SNP if it appears in at least 1% of the population, though the term is often used more loosely for any single-base difference. SNPs are the most common type of genetic variation among people: roughly 4 to 5 million SNPs occur in a person's genome, and more than 600 million have been catalogued in the human population worldwide.

Most SNPs are located in the DNA between genes and have no effect on health or development. Some SNPs, however, occur within genes or in regulatory regions near genes and can influence traits such as height, eye colour, and disease risk. Because SNPs are inherited and change slowly over generations, they are valuable markers for tracing ancestry, mapping disease-associated genes, and studying population history.

Types

SNPs are classified by their location and effect. Coding SNPs lie within protein-coding regions of genes. A synonymous (or silent) SNP does not change the amino-acid sequence of the protein, whereas a nonsynonymous SNP does. Nonsynonymous SNPs may be missense, changing one amino acid, or nonsense, introducing a premature stop codon. Non-coding SNPs, which are far more numerous, can still affect gene splicing, transcription factor binding, messenger RNA degradation, and the expression level of nearby genes.

Nomenclature

SNPs are commonly identified by an "rs" (reference SNP) number assigned by dbSNP, such as rs6311. Older conventions describe a variant by the nucleotide change, for example C>T, or by its effect on a protein, such as Arg38His.

Applications

SNPs underpin genome-wide association studies (GWAS), in which hundreds of thousands of markers are compared between people with and without a disease to find associated variants. They are also used in pharmacogenomics to predict drug response, in forensic identification, in direct-to-consumer ancestry testing, and in agriculture, where they assist marker-assisted selection in crops and livestock. SNP arrays ("SNP chips") allow the simultaneous genotyping of millions of positions at low cost.

SNPs and disease

Variants identified through GWAS have been associated with conditions including type 2 diabetes, Crohn's disease, cardiovascular disease, and several cancers. Individual common SNPs usually confer only a small increase in risk, but combined into polygenic risk scores they can stratify individuals meaningfully. Well-known examples include the APOE variants associated with Alzheimer's disease risk and variants in the FTO gene associated with body mass.
