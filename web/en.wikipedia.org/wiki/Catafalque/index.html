Catafalque

A catafalque is a raised box, platform, or similarly structured stand, often movable, that is used to support the coffin, casket, or body of a deceased person during a Christian funeral or memorial service. In the modern era, it is most familiar from state funerals and lyings in state, where the coffin lies draped with a flag or cloth on the catafalque while mourners file past.

Etymology and origins

The word derives from the Italian catafalco, probably related to the Vulgar Latin catafalicum and ultimately to words meaning a scaffold or stage. Elaborate catafalques were a feature of grand Renaissance and Baroque funerals in Italy and France, where temporary structures of wood, canvas, and plaster were designed by leading artists and architects, adorned with candles, heraldry, allegorical sculpture, and inscriptions, and then dismantled after the ceremony.

Famous catafalques

The Lincoln catafalque, built hastily of pine boards and covered in black cloth for the lying in state of President Abraham Lincoln in 1865, has since supported the coffins of every American president and many other distinguished figures who have lain in state in the United States Capitol rotunda. In Britain, the catafalque in Westminster Hall is used for lyings in state of monarchs and other national figures. Similar platforms are used in state ceremonies in many countries.

Religious use

In the traditional Roman Catholic Requiem Mass, an empty catafalque draped with a black pall and surrounded by candles could represent the deceased during the absolution when the body itself was not present; the practice survives in some traditional communities.
