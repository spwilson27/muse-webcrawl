Ellen Henrietta Swallow Richards (December 3, 1842 – March 30, 1911) was an American industrial and safety engineer, environmental chemist, and university instructor. A pioneer in the fields of sanitary engineering and experimental research in domestic science, she founded the home economics movement in the United States and was the first woman admitted to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT).

Early life and education

Richards was born in Dunstable, Massachusetts, the only child of Fanny Gould Taylor and Peter Swallow. Educated largely at home, she entered Vassar College in 1868 and graduated in 1870. When she sought graduate training in chemistry, Boston University could not accommodate her, but MIT admitted her in 1870 as a "special student" — the institute's first woman student — and she earned a Bachelor of Science in 1873. She also received a master's degree from Vassar for a thesis on the chemical analysis of iron ore.

Career

In 1876 Richards helped establish the Women's Laboratory at MIT, where women could study chemistry and other sciences. She became an instructor in sanitary chemistry and, with her husband, mining engineer Robert Hallowell Richards, whom she married in 1875, promoted scientific approaches to everyday life. She tested water supplies for the Massachusetts State Board of Health, analyzing some 40,000 samples of local waters between 1887 and 1890, work that produced the "Normal Chlorine Map" and Massachusetts' first water-quality standards — a landmark in American sanitary engineering.

Home economics

Richards coined the term "euthenics" and organized the Lake Placid Conferences (beginning 1899) that defined the field of home economics. In 1908 she became the first president of the American Home Economics Association. Her books include The Chemistry of Cooking and Cleaning (1882) and Euthenics: The Science of Controllable Environment (1910).

Legacy

Richards died in Boston in 1911. She is remembered as a founder of home economics and as an early advocate of applying science to public health, nutrition, and the environment.
