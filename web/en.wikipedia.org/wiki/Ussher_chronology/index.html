Ussher chronology

The Ussher chronology is a 17th-century chronology of the history of the world formulated from a literal reading of the Old Testament by James Ussher, the Anglican Archbishop of Armagh and Primate of All Ireland. The chronology is sometimes associated with the young Earth creationist view that the Earth was created around 6,000 years ago.

The work

Ussher published his chronology in Annales Veteris Testamenti, a prima mundi origine deducti ("Annals of the Old Testament, deduced from the first origins of the world", 1650), with a continuation on the New Testament in 1654. Working backward from datable events in secular history and summing the ages given in the genealogies of Genesis, Ussher concluded that the world was created at nightfall preceding Sunday, 23 October 4004 BC in the proleptic Julian calendar.

Method and context

Ussher's work was a serious scholarly undertaking of its time, comparable to the chronologies of Johannes Kepler (who calculated 3992 BC) and Isaac Newton (c. 4000 BC). Earlier chronologers, including Bede (3952 BC) and John Lightfoot (3929 BC), reached similar results. Ussher fixed the birth of Jesus in 4 BC, correcting the error of Dionysius Exiguus.

Later influence

From 1701, editions of the King James Bible printed Ussher's dates in the margin, giving them wide authority in the English-speaking world. In the 19th century, geology and later radiometric dating established that the Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, and the chronology came to be cited as a symbol of biblical literalism, though it remains used by young Earth creationists.
