William Henry Harrison (February 9, 1773 – April 4, 1841) was an American military officer and politician who served as the ninth president of the United States in 1841. He died of either typhoid, pneumonia, or paratyphoid fever just 31 days after taking office, becoming the first president to die in office and serving the shortest tenure in U.S. presidential history. He was also the last president born a British subject in the Thirteen Colonies and, at age 68, the oldest president elected until Ronald Reagan in 1980.

Harrison was born into the Harrison family of Virginia at Berkeley Plantation. His father, Benjamin Harrison V, was a signer of the Declaration of Independence. After studies at Hampden–Sydney College and the University of Pennsylvania, Harrison entered the army and fought in the Northwest Indian War, resigning in 1798 to become Secretary of the Northwest Territory.

Indiana Territory and the War of 1812

In 1801 Harrison became the first governor of the newly created Indiana Territory, a post he held for twelve years. He negotiated numerous treaties with Native American nations, acquiring large areas of land for settlement, including the Treaty of Fort Wayne (1809). The resulting resentment helped bring about Tecumseh's confederacy and the Battle of Tippecanoe in November 1811, in which Harrison's forces repelled an attack led by Tecumseh's brother Tenskwatawa ("the Prophet"). The battle made Harrison a national hero and supplied the famous campaign slogan "Tippecanoe and Tyler Too."

During the War of 1812 Harrison commanded American forces in the Northwest. After the American naval victory on Lake Erie, he defeated a combined British and Native force at the Battle of the Thames in October 1813, where Tecumseh was killed.

Political career and the election of 1840

Harrison later served in the U.S. House of Representatives and Senate from Ohio, as minister to Gran Colombia, and lost the 1836 presidential election as one of several Whig candidates. In 1840 the Whigs united behind Harrison, running on his military record and a campaign of log cabins and hard cider that portrayed him—despite his aristocratic Virginia birth—as a man of the people. He defeated the incumbent Martin Van Buren.

Presidency and death

Harrison delivered the longest inaugural address in American history—8,445 words—on March 4, 1841, a bitterly cold day, speaking for nearly two hours without a coat or hat. He caught a cold that developed into a fatal illness, and he died on April 4, 1841. His vice president, John Tyler, succeeded him, establishing the precedent of vice-presidential succession. His grandson, Benjamin Harrison, later became the 23rd president.
