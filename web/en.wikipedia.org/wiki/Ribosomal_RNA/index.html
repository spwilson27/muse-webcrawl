Ribosomal RNA

TypeNon-coding RNA
FunctionStructural and catalytic core of the ribosome
LocationRibosomes of all cells; synthesized in the nucleolus (eukaryotes)

Ribosomal ribonucleic acid (rRNA) is a type of non-coding RNA which is the primary component of ribosomes, essential to all cells for protein synthesis. rRNA is a ribozyme which carries out protein synthesis in ribosomes: the ribosome's peptidyl transferase activity, which links amino acids together, resides in the rRNA of the large subunit, while the small subunit rRNA decodes messenger RNA.

Structure

Ribosomes consist of two subunits, each built around one or more rRNA molecules plus dozens of ribosomal proteins. In bacteria, the small (30S) subunit contains 16S rRNA (about 1,500 nucleotides), and the large (50S) subunit contains 23S (about 2,900 nucleotides) and 5S rRNA. In eukaryotes, the small (40S) subunit contains 18S rRNA, and the large (60S) subunit contains 28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNAs. rRNA folds into extensive double-helical secondary structures held together by base pairing, and accounts for roughly 60–65% of the mass of the ribosome.

Synthesis

In eukaryotes, the 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs are transcribed by RNA polymerase I as a single long precursor in the nucleolus, then chemically modified and cleaved into mature forms; the 5S rRNA is transcribed separately by RNA polymerase III. Hundreds of copies of the ribosomal DNA genes are arranged in tandem repeats, reflecting the enormous quantity of rRNA a growing cell requires — rRNA can make up 80–90% of a cell's total RNA.

Function in translation

During translation, the small subunit rRNA binds the mRNA and monitors the pairing between codons and the anticodons of transfer RNAs; mismatches disturb the rRNA's shape and cause the tRNA to be rejected. In the large subunit, the 23S (or 28S) rRNA catalyses peptide bond formation. Because this catalysis is performed by RNA itself, the ribosome is regarded as the largest and most important ribozyme, and is central evidence for the RNA world hypothesis — the idea that early life used RNA as both genetic material and catalyst.

Use in phylogenetics and medicine

The gene for small-subunit rRNA (16S in prokaryotes, 18S in eukaryotes) evolves slowly and is present in all organisms, making it a standard molecular clock and barcode for identifying organisms and reconstructing evolutionary relationships; Carl Woese's use of 16S rRNA sequences led to the recognition of the Archaea as a third domain of life. Many antibiotics — including aminoglycosides, macrolides and tetracyclines — work by binding bacterial rRNA and disrupting translation, exploiting structural differences between bacterial and human ribosomes.
