Factor analysis

Factor analysis is a statistical method used to describe variability among observed, correlated variables in terms of a potentially lower number of unobserved variables called factors. It is commonly used in psychometrics, personality psychology, biology, marketing and social sciences to uncover latent structure in data.

History

Charles Spearman originated factor analysis in 1904 in his work on general intelligence, proposing a single g factor. Louis Thurstone later developed multiple-factor models and methods such as principal axis factoring and rotation.

Models

In the basic model each observed variable is expressed as a linear combination of common factors plus a unique factor. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) searches for structure without a prior hypothesis, while confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) tests a specified structure, often within structural equation modelling.

Methods and interpretation

Analysts choose the number of factors using eigenvalues, scree plots and parallel analysis, extract factors by principal components, principal axis or maximum likelihood methods, and rotate them — orthogonally (e.g. varimax) or obliquely (e.g. promax) — to aid interpretation. Factor loadings indicate the strength of association between variables and factors.

Applications and criticism

Applications include developing psychological tests, identifying dimensions of personality such as the Big Five, and reducing large data sets. Criticisms concern subjectivity in choosing factors and rotations, and the reification of factors as real entities.
