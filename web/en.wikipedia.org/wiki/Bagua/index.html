Bagua

The bagua (Chinese: 八卦; pinyin: bāguà; lit. "eight trigrams") is a set of eight symbols originating in ancient Chinese philosophy and cosmology. Each symbol, called a trigram, consists of three lines — solid (yang) or broken (yin) — representing the fundamental principles of reality. The eight trigrams are commonly arranged in an octagonal diagram, often surrounding a taijitu (yin-yang) symbol, and are central to the I Ching (Book of Changes), Taoist cosmology, feng shui, and Chinese martial arts.

The eight trigrams

  • Qián (乾) ☰ — Heaven, three solid lines
  • Kūn (坤) ☷ — Earth, three broken lines
  • Zhèn (震) ☳ — Thunder
  • Xùn (巽) ☴ — Wind
  • Kǎn (坎) ☵ — Water
  • Lí (離) ☲ — Fire
  • Gèn (艮) ☶ — Mountain
  • Duì (兌) ☱ — Lake

Pairs of trigrams combine to form the 64 hexagrams of the I Ching, each associated with an interpretive text used in divination.

Arrangements

Two principal arrangements of the trigrams are recognised. The Primordial (Earlier Heaven) arrangement, attributed to the legendary sage Fu Xi, places opposite trigrams in perfect balance. The Manifested (Later Heaven) arrangement, attributed to King Wen of Zhou, follows the cyclical order of the seasons and directions and is the arrangement used in feng shui practice.

Uses

In feng shui, a bagua map divides a home or plot into eight (or nine, including the centre) sectors associated with aspects of life such as wealth, health, and relationships. Bagua mirrors — octagonal mirrors bearing the trigrams — are hung outside buildings to deflect harmful influences. In martial arts, the internal style of baguazhang takes its name from the trigrams and is characterised by continuous circular walking and coiling movements.
