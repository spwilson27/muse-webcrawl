Shimazaki Tōson

Shimazaki Tōson (島崎 藤村, 25 March 1872 – 22 August 1943) was the pen name of Shimazaki Haruki, a Japanese author, active in the Meiji, Taishō, and early Shōwa periods of Japan. He began his career as a Romantic poet, but went on to establish himself as a major proponent and pioneer of naturalism in Japanese literature, and is regarded as one of the founders of the modern Japanese novel.

Early life and poetry

Tōson was born in Magome, a post town on the Nakasendō highway in what is now Nakatsugawa, Gifu, the seventh son of a village headman and honjin keeper. He studied English and Christianity at Meiji Gakuin University in Tokyo, where he met the critic Kitamura Tōkoku, and taught at a girls' school in Sendai. His first collection of poems, Wakanashū ("Collection of Young Herbs", 1897), written at age 25, made him a leading voice of Romantic poetry in Japan, followed by Rakubaishū ("Fallen Plum Blossoms", 1901) and other verse collections.

Novels

Turning to prose, Tōson published The Broken Commandment (Hakai, 1906), considered the first Japanese naturalist novel, about a schoolteacher from the outcaste burakumin community who breaks his father's injunction to conceal his origins. Spring (Haru, 1908) was an autobiographical account of his youthful literary circle, and The Family (Ie, 1910) traced the decline of two provincial families. After three of his children died in infancy and a scandalous affair with his niece, he went into self-imposed exile in France from 1913 to 1916; the experience informed the confessional novel A New Life (Shinsei, 1918–19).

His masterpiece is the vast historical novel Before the Dawn (Yoakemae, 1929–1935), modelled on his own father, which depicts the fall of the Tokugawa shogunate and the disillusionment of provincial intellectuals during the Meiji Restoration. He served as the first chairman of the Japan PEN Club from 1935. His birthplace and grave in Magome are preserved as museums, and the town's setting inspired much of his work.
