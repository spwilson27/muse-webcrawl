Vasospasm

Vasospasm is a condition in which the smooth muscle of a blood vessel contracts strongly and persistently, narrowing the vessel lumen and reducing blood flow to the tissue it supplies. Unlike normal vasoconstriction, which is a regulated physiological response, vasospasm is prolonged and pathological, and can cause ischaemia or infarction of downstream organs.

Cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid haemorrhage, seen on angiography.

Clinical contexts

Cerebral vasospasm is the most feared form: it develops in a large proportion of patients several days after aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage, as blood breakdown products around the basal arteries provoke sustained contraction. It typically begins around day 3, peaks at days 6–8, and resolves over two to three weeks. It can cause delayed cerebral ischaemia and stroke. The calcium channel blocker nimodipine improves outcomes, and severe cases are treated with induced hypertension or endovascular therapy (balloon angioplasty or intra-arterial vasodilators).

Coronary artery spasm (Prinzmetal or variant angina) causes episodes of chest pain at rest, often at night, with transient ST-segment elevation on the ECG; calcium channel blockers and nitrates are the main treatments. Raynaud's phenomenon is vasospasm of the small digital arteries triggered by cold or stress, producing the characteristic white-blue-red colour changes of the fingers. Vasospasm can also affect the nipple during breastfeeding, the retinal vessels, and transplanted organs, and can be provoked by drugs such as ergots, triptans and cocaine.

Mechanisms

Vasospasm reflects an imbalance between vasoconstrictor influences (endothelin-1, free haemoglobin scavenging nitric oxide, inflammatory mediators) and vasodilator mechanisms, together with increased sensitivity of vascular smooth muscle calcium-handling machinery, including Rho-kinase-mediated calcium sensitisation. Endothelial injury is a common initiating factor.
