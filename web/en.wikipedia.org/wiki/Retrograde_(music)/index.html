Retrograde (music)

In music, retrograde is a technique in which a melody or series of notes is played backwards. A melodic line that is the reverse of a previously or simultaneously stated line is said to be its retrograde or cancrizans ("walking backward", like a crab).

History

Retrograde was a favorite device of contrapuntal composers from the Middle Ages onward. Composers of the Franco-Flemish school, such as Guillaume de Machaut and Johannes Ockeghem, wrote canons in which one voice sings the retrograde of another; Machaut's rondeau Ma fin est mon commencement ("My end is my beginning") is constructed so that its second half mirrors the first. Johann Sebastian Bach included a crab canon (canon cancrizans) in The Musical Offering.

In twentieth-century serialism, retrograde became one of the four basic transformations of a tone row, alongside the prime form, inversion, and retrograde inversion. Anton Webern and Arnold Schoenberg made extensive use of these transformations, and composers such as Olivier Messiaen composed "non-retrogradable rhythms" that read the same forwards and backwards.

Related concepts

Retrograde differs from inversion, which flips a melody's intervals in pitch space rather than reversing its order in time. Combining both operations gives retrograde inversion. Palindromic structures in works by composers from Haydn to Berg exploit similar symmetry principles.
