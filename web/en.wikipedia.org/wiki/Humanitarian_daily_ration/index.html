Humanitarian daily ration

Humanitarian daily rations (HDRs) are food rations manufactured in the United States to be used in humanitarian response to crises in other countries. Each provides a full day's sustenance to a person, with roughly 2,200 calories. They are designed to be culturally neutral — containing no animal products for religious acceptability — and are dropped from aircraft or distributed on the ground in emergencies.

History

HDRs were developed following food relief operations in northern Iraq (1991) and were first widely used during the Bosnian War. They were famously air-dropped over Afghanistan beginning in October 2001, and later used in Iraq, Haiti after the 2010 earthquake, and other disaster responses.

Contents

A typical ration contains two main entrées drawn from menus such as bean salad, barley stew, lentil stew, rice pilaf and beans and rice, plus peanut butter, jam, crackers or bread, fruit bars, and an accessory packet with salt, pepper, sugar, matches and a spoon. Instructions are printed in multiple languages with pictograms.

Packaging controversy

In Afghanistan, the bright yellow HDR packets were criticised because yellow BLU-97 cluster bomblets posed a risk of confusion; the packaging was changed to a salmon/pink colour to distinguish rations from munitions.
