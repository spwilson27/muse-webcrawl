The Mosque–Cathedral of Córdoba (Spanish: Mezquita-Catedral de Córdoba), officially known by its ecclesiastical name, the Cathedral of Our Lady of the Assumption, is a cathedral of the Roman Catholic Church in Córdoba, Spain. It was originally built as a mosque beginning in 785–786 CE under Abd al-Rahman I, founder of the Umayyad Emirate of Córdoba, and was expanded over the following two centuries into one of the largest mosques in the Islamic world. Following the Christian conquest of Córdoba in 1236, it was converted into a cathedral, and in the 16th century a Renaissance cathedral nave was inserted into the centre of the vast prayer hall. The building is one of the most celebrated monuments of Islamic architecture and a symbol of the layered history of al-Andalus.

History

The site was previously occupied by a Visigothic church dedicated to Saint Vincent of Saragossa, which, according to traditional accounts, was shared by Christians and Muslims after the Islamic conquest before Abd al-Rahman I purchased the Christian portion and demolished the church to build the Great Mosque. His successors — Abd al-Rahman II, al-Hakam II, and the chamberlain al-Mansur — successively enlarged the prayer hall, the caliph al-Hakam II adding the richly decorated mihrab and maqsurah in the 960s. The final expansion under al-Mansur (987–988) brought the mosque to its present dimensions, among the largest in the world at the time.

Architecture

The mosque's most famous feature is its hypostyle prayer hall, a vast "forest" of more than 850 columns of jasper, onyx, marble, and granite supporting double-tiered horseshoe arches of alternating red brick and white stone voussoirs. The arches derive from Roman and Visigothic precedents, and many columns were reused from Roman buildings. The 10th-century mihrab, a small octagonal chamber rather than a simple niche, is sheathed in Byzantine-style gold mosaics sent, according to tradition, by the Byzantine emperor. The complex also includes the Courtyard of the Orange Trees (Patio de los Naranjos) and a minaret, built under Abd al-Rahman III, later encased in the present Renaissance bell tower after earthquake damage.

Cathedral

After Ferdinand III captured Córdoba in 1236, the mosque was consecrated as a cathedral. Chapels were built around its edges over the centuries. In 1523, despite opposition from the city council, the cathedral chapter began building a full Renaissance church — designed by the Hernán Ruiz family of architects — in the middle of the prayer hall, demolishing a portion of the columns. The Emperor Charles V, who had authorized the work, is said to have later lamented: "You have built here what you or anyone might have built anywhere else, but you have destroyed what was unique in the world."

Present day

The building functions as Córdoba's cathedral and is among Spain's most visited monuments; it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1984 as part of the historic centre of Córdoba. Its official status and name have been subjects of public debate in Spain: the Catholic Church registered ownership of the building in 2006, Muslim prayer inside is prohibited by the Church authorities, and campaigns by some citizens' groups have sought public ownership and recognition of its mosque heritage.
