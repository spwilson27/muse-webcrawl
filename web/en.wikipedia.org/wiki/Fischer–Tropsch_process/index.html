The Fischer–Tropsch process is a collection of chemical reactions that converts a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, known as synthesis gas or syngas, into liquid hydrocarbons. The process is a key component of gas-to-liquids (GTL) and coal-to-liquids technology, producing synthetic fuels and lubricants from coal, natural gas or biomass. It was invented by Franz Fischer and Hans Tropsch at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Coal Research in Mülheim an der Ruhr, Germany, in the 1920s.

Chemistry

The overall reaction converts syngas into alkanes according to the general equation (2n + 1) H2 + n CO → CnH(2n+2) + n H2O. The products follow the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, so a range of chain lengths from methane to heavy waxes is always obtained. Commercial processes operate over iron or cobalt catalysts: cobalt is preferred for natural-gas feedstocks, while iron, which also catalyses the water–gas shift reaction, suits hydrogen-poor syngas from coal.

History

Fischer and Tropsch filed their first patents in 1925–26. Germany built Fischer–Tropsch plants during the Second World War to convert coal into fuel, producing several hundred thousand tonnes per year by 1944. After the war the process was developed at scale in South Africa by Sasol, which used the country's coal reserves to produce fuels during the apartheid-era sanctions. Large gas-to-liquids plants have since been built in Qatar (Shell's Pearl GTL and Sasol's Oryx GTL) and elsewhere.

Applications and issues

Fischer–Tropsch fuels are of high quality: the diesel fraction has a high cetane number and is essentially free of sulfur and aromatics, and the process also produces valuable waxes and base oils. However, the plants are capital-intensive, and fuels made from coal have a large carbon footprint unless carbon capture is used. Interest has revived in biomass-to-liquids and power-to-liquids variants for sustainable aviation fuel.
