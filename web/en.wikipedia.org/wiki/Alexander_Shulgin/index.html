Alexander Theodore Shulgin (17 June 1925 – 2 June 2014), nicknamed "Sasha", was an American medicinal chemist, biochemist, organic chemist and pharmacologist. He is best known for synthesising and systematically documenting hundreds of psychedelic compounds, for reintroducing MDMA to the psychotherapeutic community in the 1970s, and for the books PiHKAL and TiHKAL, co-written with his wife Ann Shulgin.

Early life and Dow Chemical

Shulgin studied organic chemistry at Harvard University and earned his PhD in biochemistry from UC Berkeley in 1954. At Dow Chemical he developed profitable products, including early work related to the biodegradable insecticide Zectran, and the company granted him freedom to pursue his own research. His self-experiment with mescaline around 1960 set the direction of his career: he resolved to map the relationship between chemical structure and psychedelic effect.

Independent research

Leaving Dow in 1965, Shulgin worked from a small home laboratory in Lafayette, California, holding a DEA licence that for many years permitted him to possess scheduled compounds for analysis, and he served as an analytical consultant to law enforcement. His method was systematic: modify a known scaffold, synthesise the new compound, and assay it through cautious self-experimentation with a small circle of trained volunteers, recording qualitative effects on the "Shulgin Rating Scale".

MDMA and the books

In 1976 Shulgin synthesised MDMA — first made by Merck in 1912 — and introduced it to psychotherapists, including Leo Zeff. Nicknamed "Adam" in therapeutic circles, it was investigated as an adjunct to psychotherapy before its recreational spread as "ecstasy" led to emergency scheduling in 1985. With Ann Shulgin he published PiHKAL: A Chemical Love Story (1991), covering 179 phenethylamines, and TiHKAL: The Continuation (1997), covering 55 tryptamines; each combined autobiography with synthesis procedures and bioassay reports. After a 1994 DEA raid his licence was surrendered, though he was never charged, and he continued non-scheduled research.

Legacy

Shulgin created or characterised more new psychoactive compounds — over 230 — than any other individual chemist. His documentation underpins modern psychedelic chemistry, and compounds he first described, such as 2C-B and the DOx series, remain reference points in pharmacology and drug policy debates. He died of liver cancer in 2014.
