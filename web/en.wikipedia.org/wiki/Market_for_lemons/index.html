The Market for Lemons

"The Market for 'Lemons'"
AuthorGeorge A. Akerlof
PublishedQuarterly Journal of Economics, August 1970
SubjectInformation asymmetry; adverse selection

"The Market for 'Lemons': Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism" is a 1970 paper by economist George Akerlof that is a foundational work in the theory of asymmetric information. The paper explains how differences in information between buyers and sellers about the quality of a good can cause markets to unravel, driving good products out and leaving only poor-quality products — "lemons". Akerlof shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz for analyses of markets with asymmetric information.

The model

Akerlof illustrates the problem with the market for used cars. Sellers know whether their car is a good car (a "plum") or a defective one (a "lemon"), but buyers cannot distinguish between them before purchase. A rational buyer will therefore pay only a price reflecting the average quality of cars offered. At that price, owners of good cars — whose cars are worth more — withdraw them from the market, lowering the average quality of the remaining pool, which lowers the price buyers will pay, which drives out still more good cars. In the extreme, this process of adverse selection can cause the market to collapse entirely, with only lemons traded.

Applications in the paper

Akerlof extended the argument beyond used cars to insurance markets (where those most likely to claim are most eager to buy), employment of minority groups, credit markets in developing countries, and the costs of dishonesty. He emphasised that the costs of asymmetric information "drive honest (and good-quality) trade out of the market".

Remedies

The paper discusses institutions that counteract quality uncertainty: guarantees and warranties, brand names and reputation, licensing and certification, and chains and franchising. Later literature developed related responses including signalling (Spence) and screening (Stiglitz), dealer reputation, independent inspection, and "lemon laws" requiring sellers to remedy or refund defective vehicles.

Reception

The paper was famously rejected by several leading journals as trivial before being published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics. It has since become one of the most cited papers in economics and a standard teaching example of market failure.
