Malolactic fermentation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Malolactic conversion (also known as malolactic fermentation or MLF) is a process in winemaking in which tart-tasting malic acid, naturally present in grape must, is converted to softer-tasting lactic acid. Malolactic fermentation is most often performed as a secondary fermentation shortly after the end of the primary fermentation, but can sometimes run concurrently.

Process

The conversion is carried out by lactic acid bacteria, principally Oenococcus oeni. The bacteria decarboxylate L-malate to L-lactate, releasing carbon dioxide. Because lactic acid is a weaker acid than malic acid, total acidity decreases and the pH rises slightly, making the wine taste softer and rounder.

Sensory effects

Beyond deacidification, MLF can add buttery aromas: diacetyl, a by-product, gives a buttery or nutty note characteristic of many Chardonnays. It also increases microbiological stability by removing malic acid that spoilage organisms could otherwise metabolise.

Usage

MLF is standard for most red wines and for fuller-bodied whites such as barrel-fermented Chardonnay, while crisp aromatic whites such as Riesling usually avoid it to preserve acidity and freshness. Winemakers may inoculate selected bacterial cultures or allow indigenous bacteria to act.
