Gauss sum

In algebraic number theory, a Gauss sum or Gaussian sum is a particular kind of finite sum of roots of unity. In modern notation, a Gauss sum is given by

G(χ) := Σ χ(a) · e^(2πia/N)

where χ is a Dirichlet character modulo N and the sum runs over residues a mod N coprime to N, with e(x) = e^(2πix).

History

The classical case with χ the Legendre symbol modulo an odd prime p was studied by Carl Friedrich Gauss in the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801). Gauss determined that the quadratic Gauss sum equals √p if p ≡ 1 (mod 4) and i√p if p ≡ 3 (mod 4), a result he described as requiring several years of effort to prove the sign.

Properties

  • For a primitive Dirichlet character χ modulo N, |G(χ)| = √N.
  • The quotient G(χ)/√N is a root of unity; determining its exact argument is the heart of the theory.
  • Gauss sums appear in the functional equations of Dirichlet L-functions.
  • Products of Gauss sums relate to Jacobi sums, which in turn connect to counting points on curves over finite fields.

Quadratic Gauss sums

The simplest Gauss sums are the quadratic ones, g = Σ (a/p) ζ_p^a, where ζ_p is a p-th root of unity and (a/p) is the Legendre symbol. They evaluate to √p or i√p depending on p mod 4.

Generalizations

Gauss sums generalize to finite fields, where they are sums of a multiplicative character times an additive character, and to higher settings such as Gauss sums attached to Galois representations. They play a central role in analytic number theory, the theory of L-functions, and exponential sum estimates.
