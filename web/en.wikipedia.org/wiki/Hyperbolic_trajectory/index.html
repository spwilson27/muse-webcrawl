In astrodynamics or celestial mechanics, a hyperbolic trajectory or hyperbolic orbit is the trajectory of any object around a central body with more than enough speed to escape the central object's gravitational pull. The name derives from the fact that, under Newton's law of universal gravitation, such an orbit has the shape of a hyperbola, one of the conic sections.

Characteristics

A hyperbolic orbit has a semi-major axis that is conventionally negative, an eccentricity greater than 1, and positive specific orbital energy: the object arrives from infinity with a hyperbolic excess velocity v∞, is deflected by the central body, and departs to infinity again, its path bent through a turning angle determined by the impact parameter and excess speed. The excess velocity is related to the launch energy by C3 = v∞2, the characteristic energy used in mission design.

Applications

Hyperbolic trajectories occur naturally for interstellar visitors such as the asteroids 1I/ʻOumuamua (eccentricity about 1.2) and 2I/Borisov, whose paths through the Solar System are unbound. In astronautics, spacecraft departing a planet follow hyperbolic escape trajectories relative to that planet, and gravity-assist flybys are hyperbolic encounters: a spacecraft passing behind a planet gains heliocentric energy, as exploited by the Voyager probes, while one passing in front loses energy, as used by Mariner 10 and MESSENGER to reach Mercury. Within a planet's sphere of influence, interplanetary cruise appears as a hyperbola in the patched-conic approximation.

Radial hyperbolic trajectory

A radial hyperbolic trajectory is the degenerate case in which the orbit is a straight line: the object moves directly toward or away from the central body, corresponding to collision or purely radial escape with speed above the escape velocity.
