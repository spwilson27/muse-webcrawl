Amor fati

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Amor fati (Latin for "love of fate" or "love of one's fate") is a phrase describing an attitude in which one sees everything that happens in one's life — including suffering and loss — as good, or at the very least necessary. It is closely associated with the philosophy of Friedrich Nietzsche, though it has antecedents in Stoicism, particularly in Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius.

Nietzsche

Nietzsche introduced the phrase in The Gay Science (1882), in an aphorism written as a New Year's resolution:

"I want to learn more and more to see as beautiful what is necessary in things; then I shall be one of those who make things beautiful. Amor fati: let that be my love henceforth!"

He returned to the concept in Ecce Homo, calling it his "formula for greatness in a human being": to want nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity — not merely to bear what is necessary, but to love it. The idea is linked to his thought experiment of the eternal recurrence: the affirmation of one's life so completely that one would will it to recur exactly, forever.

Stoic antecedents

Similar attitudes appear in Stoic philosophy: Epictetus taught that one should not wish events to happen as one wants, but want them as they happen; Marcus Aurelius wrote of accepting the workings of the whole and likening the wise person to a fire that makes flame and brightness out of whatever is thrown into it. Nietzsche's version differs in emphasising joyful affirmation rather than resignation.
