Symon Vasylyovych Petliura (Ukrainian: Симон Васильович Петлюра; 22 May 1879 – 25 May 1926) was a Ukrainian politician and journalist who led Ukraine's struggle for independence following the Russian Revolution of 1917. As head of the Directorate and commander-in-chief (Chief Otaman) of the army of the Ukrainian People's Republic, he fought simultaneously against the Bolsheviks, the White movement, and Poland at different stages of the war.

Early life and career

Born in Poltava to a family of Cossack descent, Petliura studied at a theological seminary, from which he was expelled for his involvement in the Ukrainian national movement. He became a journalist and a founding member of the Ukrainian Social Democratic Labour Party, editing socialist and national publications in Kyiv, Lviv, St Petersburg and Moscow. During the First World War he worked in the Union of Zemstvos aiding the war effort.

Revolution and leadership

In 1917 Petliura organised Ukrainian military units and became secretary for military affairs in the Central Rada. After the Skoropadskyi Hetmanate was overthrown in late 1918, he emerged as the dominant figure in the restored Ukrainian People's Republic. His forces briefly retook Kyiv several times amid chaotic multi-sided fighting, concluded the Treaty of Warsaw with Poland in 1920, and were finally defeated by the Red Army; Petliura went into exile, eventually settling in Paris.

Pogroms controversy

Petliura's legacy is fiercely contested because of the pogroms committed in 1918–1920 by forces nominally under his command, in which tens of thousands of Jews were killed. Petliura issued orders condemning pogroms and punished some perpetrators, but historians debate how much control he exercised and whether he did enough to stop the violence.

Assassination

On 25 May 1926 Petliura was shot in Paris by Sholem Schwarzbard, a Jewish anarchist whose family had perished in the pogroms. Schwarzbard was acquitted by a French jury. Petliura is buried in Montparnasse Cemetery; streets and institutions across Ukraine are named for him, and his reputation there as a national hero coexists with continuing scholarly controversy.
