Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot (1 June 1796 – 24 August 1832) was a French military engineer and physicist, often described as the "father of thermodynamics". In his 1824 work Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire, Carnot gave the first successful theoretical account of heat engines, introducing the idealised Carnot cycle and proving that the efficiency of any heat engine depends only on the temperatures between which it operates.

Life

Carnot was the son of Lazare Carnot, a leading mathematician and statesman of the French Revolution. He studied at the École Polytechnique and served as an officer in the engineering corps. Working largely in obscurity, he published his Reflections at his own expense in 1824; it sold few copies and was almost forgotten until Clapeyron revived it a decade later.

Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire

Carnot asked how much work could be extracted from heat and whether steam engines could be improved without limit. Analysing an ideal reversible cycle of isothermal and adiabatic steps, he showed that no engine operating between two reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible one — Carnot's theorem — laying conceptual groundwork later formalised by Clausius and Kelvin as the second law of thermodynamics and the concept of entropy. Although Carnot worked within the (incorrect) caloric theory, his central result survived the transition to the modern energy-based theory.

Death and legacy

Carnot died of cholera in Paris in 1832, aged 36; many of his papers were burned as a precaution against infection. His nephew, also named Sadi Carnot, became President of the French Republic. Kelvin later wrote that Carnot's work marked an epoch in the history of physics, and the absolute temperature scale and the Carnot efficiency limit remain core undergraduate physics.
