Virgo Supercluster

The Virgo Supercluster (Virgo SC) or the Local Supercluster (LSC or LS) is a mass concentration of galaxies containing the Virgo Cluster and the Local Group, which itself contains the Milky Way and Andromeda galaxies. At least 100 galaxy groups and clusters are located within its diameter of 33 megaparsecs (110 million light-years).

History

Beginning with the first large sample of nebulae published by William and John Herschel, it was noticed that there was an excess of nebulae in a band running through the constellation Virgo. In the 1950s the French-American astronomer Gérard de Vaucouleurs argued that this excess represented a large-scale galaxy-like structure, coining the term "Local Supergalaxy" in 1953, which he changed to "Local Supercluster" in 1958.

Structure

The Virgo Supercluster is a flattened, disk-like structure. Its dominant member is the Virgo Cluster, a rich cluster of roughly 1,300–2,000 galaxies about 53 million light-years away, whose gravitational influence produces a measurable infall of neighbouring groups — including the Local Group — toward it, a phenomenon associated with the "Virgocentric flow". The supercluster contains groups such as the Leo, Ursa Major, Virgo II and Fornax groups strung along its plane.

Larger context

In 2014 it was announced that the Virgo Supercluster is only a lobe of a much larger structure, the Laniakea Supercluster, centred on the Great Attractor. Laniakea in turn is part of the Pisces–Cetus Supercluster Complex, a galaxy filament.
