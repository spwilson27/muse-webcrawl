The music of the Dominican Republic is dominated worldwide by two genres: merengue, the fast-paced national dance music, and bachata, a guitar-based music of love and heartbreak. Dominican music blends Spanish, African and Taíno influences and is central to the country's cultural identity at home and in its large diaspora.

Merengue

Merengue emerged in the 19th century and is played in a brisk 2/4 rhythm. Its rural form, merengue típico or perico ripiao, is built around the button accordion (introduced by German traders), the metal scraper called the güira, and the two-headed tambora drum. In the 1930s dictator Rafael Trujillo promoted merengue as a national symbol and big-band orchestras took it to the cities. Later stars such as Johnny Ventura modernized the sound, and Juan Luis Guerra brought it international acclaim in the 1980s–90s. UNESCO added merengue to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016.

Bachata

Bachata arose in the early 20th century in the countryside and poor urban barrios, played on acoustic guitars with lyrics of romance and loss. Long stigmatized as vulgar, it was popularized in the 1980s–90s by artists such as Luis Segura and Antony Santos, and Juan Luis Guerra's Bachata Rosa (1990) won a Grammy Award. The New York group Aventura and its singer Romeo Santos made bachata a global pop phenomenon in the 2000s. UNESCO inscribed bachata in 2019.

Other traditions

Afro-Dominican traditions include the palos (long-drum) music of religious brotherhoods, the salve a cappella singing of the southwest, and gagá processions during Holy Week. Contemporary Dominican artists also work in dembow — the Dominican offshoot of reggaeton associated with artists like El Alfa — salsa, rock and jazz.
