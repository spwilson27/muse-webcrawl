Social psychology

Social psychology is the scientific study of how the thoughts, feelings, and behaviours of individuals are influenced by the actual, imagined, or implied presence of others. It bridges psychology and sociology, examining topics such as attitudes, persuasion, social perception, group dynamics, interpersonal attraction, and intergroup relations.

History

Social psychology emerged as a distinct discipline around the beginning of the 20th century. Floyd Allport's experimental work in the 1920s established the individual as the unit of analysis in the American tradition. Kurt Lewin, often called the founder of modern social psychology, emphasised that behaviour is a function of the person and their environment, summarised in his formula B = f(P, E). The Second World War spurred research on propaganda, persuasion, and group morale.

The post-war decades produced many of the field's classic experiments: Muzafer Sherif's studies of norm formation, Solomon Asch's conformity experiments (1951), Leon Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance (1957), Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments (1963), and Philip Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment (1971). The 1970s saw a "crisis" debate about the field's methods and ethics, followed by a turn toward social cognition.

Core topics

Social perception and cognition

Researchers study how people form impressions of others, attribute causes to behaviour (see attribution theory and the fundamental attribution error), and use schemas and heuristics in social judgment. Stereotyping and implicit bias fall in this area.

Attitudes and persuasion

Attitudes are evaluations of people, objects, and ideas. Social psychologists study how attitudes form, when they predict behaviour, and how they change through persuasion — for example through the central and peripheral routes described by the elaboration likelihood model.

Social influence

Conformity, compliance, and obedience describe how individuals yield to group pressure or authority. Classic findings include Asch's line judgment studies and Milgram's finding that a majority of participants would administer what they believed were dangerous electric shocks when instructed by an authority figure.

Group processes

This area covers group decision-making, groupthink, social facilitation, social loafing, leadership, and intergroup conflict and cooperation, including social identity theory and realistic conflict theory.

Interpersonal relations

Topics include attraction, love, prosocial behaviour and helping, aggression, and prejudice and discrimination.

Methods

Social psychology relies heavily on controlled laboratory experiments, supplemented by field experiments, observational studies, and survey research. Modern concerns include replication — several famous findings have been re-examined in large-scale replication projects — research ethics, and the generalisability of samples drawn largely from Western, educated populations.
