Receiver operating characteristic

A receiver operating characteristic curve, or ROC curve, is a graphical plot that illustrates the performance of a binary classifier as its discrimination threshold is varied. The curve is created by plotting the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1 − specificity) at various threshold settings. ROC analysis originated in signal detection theory and radar engineering during the Second World War — hence "receiver operating" — and is now widely used in medicine, radiology, psychology, and machine learning.

ROC curves: the closer a curve approaches the top-left corner, the better the classifier.

Basic concepts

A binary classifier's outcomes can be tabulated in a confusion matrix of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives. The true positive rate (TPR) equals TP / (TP + FN); the false positive rate (FPR) equals FP / (FP + TN). As the decision threshold is swept from strict to lax, the (FPR, TPR) pair traces the ROC curve from (0, 0) to (1, 1). The diagonal line represents a classifier with no discriminative ability.

Area under the curve

The area under the ROC curve (AUC, also called AUROC) summarises performance in a single number: 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is chance-level. The AUC equals the probability that the classifier ranks a randomly chosen positive instance higher than a randomly chosen negative one, and it is closely related to the Mann–Whitney U statistic. The Gini coefficient is sometimes reported as 2·AUC − 1.

Uses and limitations

ROC curves are used to select operating thresholds and to compare diagnostic tests and machine-learning models independently of class prevalence. Criticisms include that AUC can be misleading with highly imbalanced datasets, where precision–recall curves may be more informative, and that AUC averages over threshold regions that may be irrelevant in practice; partial AUC measures address the latter concern.
