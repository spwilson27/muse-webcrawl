Catherine of Braganza (Portuguese: Catarina de Bragança; 1638–1705) was Queen of England, Scotland and Ireland as the wife of King Charles II, whom she married in 1662. The daughter of King John IV of Portugal, she brought an enormous dowry that included the ports of Tangier and Bombay (Mumbai), the latter becoming a cornerstone of the English East India Company's position in India.

Marriage and court

Catherine's Catholicism made her unpopular in Protestant England, and during the Popish Plot of 1678 false accusations were levelled against her, though Charles staunchly refused to divorce her. The marriage produced no surviving children. She is popularly credited with introducing or popularising tea drinking at the English court, along with customs such as the use of forks and the taste for oranges; the borough of Queens in New York City is often said to be named in her honour.

Later life

After Charles's death in 1685 Catherine remained in England through the reign of James II and the Glorious Revolution, returning to Portugal in 1692. There she acted as regent for her brother, Peter II, during his illness in 1701 and again in 1704–05, dying in Lisbon at the end of 1705.
