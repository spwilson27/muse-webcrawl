Patch test

A patch test is a diagnostic method used to determine which specific substances cause allergic inflammation of a patient's skin, known as allergic contact dermatitis. It is considered the gold standard for diagnosing delayed-type (Type IV) hypersensitivity reactions of the skin.

Procedure

Small amounts of suspected allergens, diluted in petrolatum or aqueous solution, are applied to chambers taped to the patient's back, usually for 48 hours. The patches are then removed and the skin examined; a further reading is typically made at 72–96 hours, since allergic reactions develop slowly. A positive reaction shows redness, swelling, and sometimes blistering at the test site.

Allergens tested

Standard screening series — such as the European baseline series or the TRUE test — include common contact allergens: metals such as nickel and cobalt, fragrances, preservatives (including formaldehyde releasers), rubber chemicals, adhesives such as epoxy resin, and components of cosmetics and hair dyes (for example p-phenylenediamine). Additional series can be tailored to a patient's occupation or exposures.

Interpretation

Interpreting patch tests requires distinguishing allergic reactions from irritant reactions, which can look similar. Positive results must also be assessed for clinical relevance — whether the identified allergen actually explains the patient's dermatitis. Patch testing differs from the skin prick tests used for immediate (Type I) allergies such as hay fever and food allergy.
