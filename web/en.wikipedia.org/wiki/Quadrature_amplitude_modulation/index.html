Quadrature amplitude modulation

Quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) is the name of a family of digital and analogue modulation schemes that convey data by changing (modulating) the amplitude of two carrier waves. These two waves, usually sinusoids, are out of phase with each other by 90 degrees and are called the in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) components. QAM is widely used in digital telecommunications, including Wi-Fi, cable television, DSL and 4G/5G mobile networks.

Digital QAM

In digital QAM, symbols are points on a two-dimensional constellation diagram. An M-ary scheme encodes log2 M bits per symbol: 16-QAM carries 4 bits per symbol, 64-QAM 6 bits, 256-QAM 8 bits, and standards such as Wi-Fi 6 reach 1024-QAM (10 bits). Higher orders pack more data into the same bandwidth but require a higher signal-to-noise ratio, because the constellation points lie closer together and are more easily confused by noise.

Demodulation

The receiver multiplies the incoming signal by the two quadrature carriers and low-pass filters the results to recover the I and Q amplitudes, then maps them to the nearest constellation point. Coherent demodulation requires accurate carrier phase recovery, typically with a phase-locked loop or pilot symbols.

Applications

  • Digital cable television (DVB-C, DOCSIS)
  • DSL and cable modems
  • Wi-Fi (802.11), LTE and 5G NR
  • Digital terrestrial television and microwave links
