Luminosity function (astronomy)

In astronomy, a luminosity function gives the number of stars or galaxies per unit volume as a function of their luminosity (or absolute magnitude). It is a fundamental statistical description of stellar populations and galaxy populations, used to estimate total light, stellar mass and the frequency of objects such as bright galaxies or white dwarfs.

Galaxy luminosity function

The galaxy luminosity function is usually described by the Schechter function (1976), which combines a power law at faint luminosities with an exponential cut-off above a characteristic luminosity L*:

Φ(L) dL = Φ* (L/L*)α exp(−L/L*) d(L/L*).

Typical values of the faint-end slope α are around −1 to −1.3 for field galaxies. The shape varies with galaxy type and environment and evolves with redshift, tracing the growth of structure and star formation over cosmic time.

Stellar luminosity function

For stars, the luminosity function counts stars per cubic parsec in each absolute-magnitude bin. In the solar neighbourhood it rises toward faint red dwarfs down to about MV ≈ +12 and then declines into the brown-dwarf regime; combined with a mass–luminosity relation and an assumed star-formation history, it is used to derive the initial mass function (IMF). Separate luminosity functions are measured for globular clusters, open clusters, white dwarfs and other populations.
