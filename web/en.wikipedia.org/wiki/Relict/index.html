Relict

A relict, in biology and geology, is a population, species, or feature that was once widespread or characteristic of a period but now survives only in a restricted area or in altered conditions. The word derives from the Latin relictus, "left behind".

Biology

In biogeography and ecology, a relict species is one that was formerly widespread but is now confined to a small area, often having survived environmental change in a refugium. Examples include the coelacanth and the ginkgo tree — sometimes called "living fossils" — and glacial relicts such as arctic-alpine plants stranded on mountain tops after the retreat of the ice sheets. Relict populations often hold distinctive genetic diversity and are of high conservation value.

Geology and geomorphology

In geology, a relict is a structure, sediment or landform that remains from an earlier time or environment: for example, relict sediments on the continental shelf deposited under past sea levels, or a relict landscape partly preserved beneath younger deposits.

Other uses

In linguistics, a relict area is a region where older language features survive. Historically, "relict" was also a legal term for a widow or widower — the surviving spouse.
