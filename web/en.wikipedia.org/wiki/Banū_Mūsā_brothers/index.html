Banū Mūsā brothers

A device from the Banū Mūsā's Book of Ingenious Devices (c. 850)

The Banū Mūsā brothers were three 9th-century Persian scholars of Baghdad — Abū Jaʿfar Muḥammad ibn Mūsā ibn Shākir, Abū al-Qāsim Aḥmad ibn Mūsā ibn Shākir, and Al-Ḥasan ibn Mūsā ibn Shākir — sons of Mūsā ibn Shākir, an astrologer (and, by some accounts, reformed highwayman) in the service of the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun. Educated in the caliph's circle, they became leading patrons and practitioners of science in the Islamic Golden Age.

Work

The brothers sponsored and participated in the Graeco-Arabic translation movement, financing expeditions to obtain Greek manuscripts and employing translators such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq and Thabit ibn Qurra. Their best-known work, the Book of Ingenious Devices (Kitāb al-Ḥiyal, c. 850), describes about one hundred automata, trick vessels, fountains, oil lamps and mechanical devices, including automatic controls and the crankshaft in an early form. In mathematics, their Book on the Measurement of Plane and Spherical Figures developed Archimedean geometry, and they wrote on astronomy, the astrolabe and mechanics, as well as making astronomical observations from Baghdad. They were also major figures at court — wealthy, politically active and famously rivals of the philosopher al-Kindi.
