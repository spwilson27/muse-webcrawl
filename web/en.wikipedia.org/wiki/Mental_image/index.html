Mental image

Mental imagery: seeing with the mind's eye.

A mental image is an experience that, on most occasions, significantly resembles the experience of perceiving some object, event, or scene, but occurs when the relevant object, event, or scene is not actually present to the senses. Mental imagery is a central topic in psychology, neuroscience and philosophy, and figures in memory, dreaming, hallucination, and creative thought.

Types and modalities

Imagery occurs in all sensory modalities: visual (the "mind's eye"), auditory (imagined sounds or music — "earworms" being an involuntary example), olfactory, gustatory, tactile and motor (or kinaesthetic) imagery, the last being widely used in sports psychology and mental rehearsal. Individuals differ strikingly in imagery vividness, measured since Francis Galton by questionnaires such as the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire. At one extreme is aphantasia, the inability to form voluntary mental images, described by Adam Zeman and colleagues; at the other, hyperphantasia, imagery as vivid as perception.

The imagery debate

In the 1970s and 1980s cognitive scientists debated the format of imagery. Stephen Kosslyn argued that images are depictive — picture-like representations that preserve spatial structure — citing experiments in which the time to scan a mental image increases with distance. Zenon Pylyshyn countered that imagery is underwritten by propositional, language-like representations, and that experimental results reflect subjects' tacit knowledge and experimenter demands. The debate helped shape computational theories of mind and has been partly reframed by neuroscience evidence.

Neuroscience

Visual imagery recruits much of the same cortical machinery as perception, including, in many studies, early visual cortex (V1). Imagery and perception differ in the direction of information flow: perception is driven bottom-up from the retina, imagery top-down from frontal and parietal regions. Deficits in imagery can dissociate from deficits in perception, indicating partially separate processes.

Uses

Imagery is exploited deliberately in memory techniques such as the method of loci, in guided imagery therapies, in exposure treatment for post-traumatic stress, and in athletic training.
