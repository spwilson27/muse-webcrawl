Scientific visualization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scientific visualization (also spelled scientific visualisation) is an interdisciplinary branch of computer graphics concerned with the visual representation of scientific data — typically three-dimensional phenomena from simulations, experiments and medical imaging — so that researchers can see, explore and understand the structures within their results. It is distinguished from information visualization, which usually deals with abstract data without an inherent spatial layout.

History

Scientists have always made pictures of data — from hand-drawn weather maps to the photographic plates of astronomy — but the field crystallised with computer graphics in the 1980s. A landmark 1987 report to the U.S. National Science Foundation, Visualization in Scientific Computing, coined much of the field's vocabulary and warned that computing power was outstripping researchers' ability to interpret its output. Supercomputing centres, medical scanners and satellite instruments were producing data at volumes that only interactive 3D graphics could make intelligible.

Techniques

  • Volume rendering: displaying 3D scalar fields (from CT, MRI or simulation grids) directly, using transfer functions that map values to colour and opacity.
  • Isosurfaces: extracting surfaces of constant value, classically with the marching cubes algorithm (1987).
  • Flow visualization: streamlines, particle traces, glyphs and line integral convolution for vector fields such as wind and blood flow.
  • Colour mapping and shading: encoding quantities in colour ramps and lighting models to reveal shape and magnitude.
  • Interactive exploration: clipping planes, time animation, virtual-reality immersion and in-situ visualisation performed alongside a running simulation.

Applications

Scientific visualization is routine in medicine (surgical planning from scan data), meteorology and climate science, computational fluid dynamics and engineering, astrophysics, molecular biology (rendering proteins and electron densities), and geophysics. Software ecosystems include open-source tools such as ParaView and VisIt, built on the VTK library, and domain packages in astronomy, chemistry and medicine. Large facilities render some visualisations at very high resolution on tiled display walls or in planetarium-style domes for public outreach.

Relationship to other fields

The field overlaps with computer graphics, human–computer interaction, data science and perceptual psychology: effective visualisations must respect how the visual system perceives colour, depth and motion. Modern practice increasingly merges scientific and information visualisation under the umbrella of visual analytics, and adds statistical graphics and machine-learning-based summarisation.
