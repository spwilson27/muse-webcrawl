Twenty-six Martyrs of Japan

The Twenty-six Martyrs of Japan were a group of Christians — Franciscan missionaries, Jesuits, and Japanese lay converts, including three young boys — crucified by order of Toyotomi Hideyoshi at Nagasaki on 5 February 1597. They were the first martyrs of the Catholic Church in the Far East to be canonized, which Pope Pius IX did in 1862. Their feast day is 5 February.

Background

Christianity had spread in Japan after St Francis Xavier's arrival in 1549, but Hideyoshi, suspicious of foreign influence, issued an edict expelling missionaries in 1587. Tensions rose when the Spanish galleon San Felipe wrecked off Shikoku in 1596 and its pilot reportedly boasted that missionaries were the vanguard of Spanish conquest. Hideyoshi ordered the arrest of Franciscans and their Japanese associates in Kyoto and Osaka.

Execution

The prisoners had part of an ear cut off, were paraded through cities, and were marched in winter from Kyoto to Nagasaki. There, at Nishizaka Hill, they were fastened to crosses and dispatched with lances. Among them were the Mexican-born Franciscan Felipe de Jesús, later patron saint of Mexico City, and the Japanese Jesuit Paul Miki, who preached from his cross.

Legacy

The Twenty-six Martyrs Museum and Monument in Nagasaki commemorates them. The executions began the era of persecution that produced Japan's "Hidden Christians" (Kakure Kirishitan). In 2020, the story of the martyrs remains central to Japanese Catholic identity.
