Neoplatonism is a school of Hellenistic philosophy that emerged in the 3rd century CE, founded by Plotinus and developed by his successors Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus and others. It taught that all reality emanates from a single ineffable source, the One, through the divine Intellect (Nous) and the World Soul, down to the material world; the goal of philosophy was the soul's return and mystical union with the One. Neoplatonism became the dominant philosophy of late antiquity and profoundly influenced Christian, Islamic and Jewish theology, medieval scholasticism, and the Renaissance.

Origins and doctrine

Plotinus, who studied in Alexandria under Ammonius Saccas and taught in Rome, presented his system as an interpretation of Plato, fusing Platonic metaphysics with elements of Aristotle, Stoicism and Pythagoreanism. In the Enneads — 54 treatises arranged by his student Porphyry into six sets of nine — reality unfolds by emanation, as light radiates from the sun: the One, beyond being and thought, overflows into Intellect, which contains the Platonic Forms; Intellect generates Soul, which animates the cosmos and individual souls; matter, at the furthest remove, borders on non-being and is the source of evil as privation. Human souls, fallen into embodiment, can ascend by virtue, contemplation and philosophy back through the levels to union (henosis) with the One — an experience Plotinus claimed, in Porphyry's account, four times in his life.

Later development

Porphyry organised and defended the school against Christianity; Iamblichus, a generation later, emphasised theurgy — ritual practice to invoke the gods — as necessary for salvation, since the soul's descent is complete. The Athenian school under Proclus (412–485) produced vast systematic commentaries and the Elements of Theology; the Alexandrian school, where Hypatia taught until her murder in 415, leaned toward mathematics and reconciliation with Christianity. The emperor Justinian's closure of the pagan schools of Athens in 529 scattered the last philosophers, some briefly to Persia. The Christian writer Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500) recast Neoplatonic hierarchy and negative theology in Christian form, transmitting them to the Middle Ages.

Influence

Through Augustine, who read Plotinus in Latin translation, Neoplatonism entered the foundations of Western Christian thought; through Arabic translations and adaptations (the so-called Theology of Aristotle) it shaped Islamic philosophy — al-Farabi, Avicenna — and Jewish thinkers such as Solomon ibn Gabirol. Revived in Renaissance Florence by Marsilio Ficino's translations of Plotinus and the founding of the Platonic Academy, it influenced art, literature and philosophy into the modern period.
