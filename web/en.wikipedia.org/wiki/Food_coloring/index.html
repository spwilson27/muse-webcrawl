Food coloring

Food coloring, or color additive, is any dye, pigment, or substance that imparts color when it is added to food or drink. Research has suggested that consumers form expectations about flavor based on the color of food, and colorings are used to offset color loss due to storage and processing, correct natural variations in color, enhance naturally occurring colors, and provide color to otherwise colorless foods.

History

The addition of colorants to foods is thought to have occurred in Egyptian cities as early as 1500 BC, when candy makers added natural extracts and wine to improve the products' appearance. During the Middle Ages, butter was colored with marigold or carrot. The first synthetic food dye, mauveine, was discovered in 1856 by William Henry Perkin; by 1900, many artificially colored foods were sold in the United States, often without disclosure.

Regulation

Food colorings are tested for safety by various bodies around the world. In the United States, color additives are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act; certified synthetic colors are identified by FD&C numbers, while in the European Union, permitted colorings are assigned E numbers (for example, E102 for tartrazine). Many consumers and manufacturers have shifted toward natural colorings derived from sources such as annatto, beetroot, turmeric, spirulina, and cochineal.
