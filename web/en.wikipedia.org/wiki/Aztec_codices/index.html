Aztec codices

Aztec codices are pictorial and glyphic manuscripts produced in central Mexico before and after the Spanish conquest, recording history, genealogy, tribute, ritual calendars, divination and religious practice. They were painted by trained scribes (tlacuiloque) on long strips or screens of deerskin or of amatl — bark paper — folded accordion-style, or on European paper after the conquest.

Survival and destruction

Very few pre-conquest codices survive: Spanish missionaries and officials destroyed large numbers as idolatrous, most famously in the campaigns associated with Bishop Juan de Zumárraga in Texcoco. The certain pre-Hispanic survivors from the Aztec sphere are few; many manuscripts in indigenous style date from the early colonial period, when native scribes continued working, often under friars' direction and increasingly with alphabetic glosses in Nahuatl or Spanish.

Major codices

The Codex Mendoza (c. 1541), made for Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza, records Aztec rulers' conquests, the tribute paid by subject provinces, and scenes of daily life; intended for Charles V, it was captured by French pirates and is now in the Bodleian Library, Oxford. The Florentine Codex, Bernardino de Sahagún's vast bilingual encyclopedia of Nahua culture, represents the richest single source on Aztec life. Other notable manuscripts include the ritual-divinatory Codex Borbonicus, the Codex Borgia group of religious screenfolds, the tribute list known as the Matrícula de Tributos, and the historical Codex Boturini, which narrates the Mexica migration from Aztlan.

Writing system

Aztec writing combined pictorial scenes, logographic signs and phonetic (rebus) elements, particularly for place and personal names. It recorded what a trained reader, who supplied much of the narrative orally, needed — meaning the codices are as much mnemonic prompts as self-sufficient texts. Modern scholarship, including decipherment work in recent decades, has shown the phonetic component to be more extensive than once thought.
