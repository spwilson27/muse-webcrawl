Civil liberties

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Civil liberties are guarantees and freedoms that governments agree not to abridge, either by constitution, legislation or judicial interpretation. They include freedom of speech, press, religion, assembly and association; the right to privacy; due process and fair trial; freedom from unreasonable search and from cruel and unusual punishment; and the right to vote and to equal treatment under law. The term is often used interchangeably with civil rights, though a common distinction makes civil liberties freedoms from government action and civil rights claims to government protection against discrimination.

In the United States the principal guarantees are the United States Bill of Rights and the Fourteenth Amendment, applied against the states by incorporation; in Europe, the European Convention on Human Rights and national constitutions, supervised by constitutional and international courts. Civil-liberties protection is tested in emergencies — wartime internment, post-9/11 surveillance and detention, pandemic restrictions — when courts and legislatures weigh security against liberty, often deferring in the moment and regretting in retrospect. Organisations such as the ACLU (founded 1920) and Liberty (1934) exist to litigate and campaign for these protections.
