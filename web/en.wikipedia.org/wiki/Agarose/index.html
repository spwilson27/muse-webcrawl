Agarose

Agarose is a polysaccharide, generally extracted from certain red seaweed. It is a linear polymer made up of the repeating unit of agarobiose, which is a disaccharide made up of D-galactose and 3,6-anhydro-L-galactopyranose. Agarose is one of the two principal components of agar, the other being agaropectin, and is purified during the production of agar.

Properties

Agarose is available as a white powder which dissolves in near-boiling water and forms a gel when it cools. Most commercially available agarose has a gelling temperature between 34 and 38 °C and a melting temperature between 90 and 95 °C; this pronounced thermal hysteresis is a consequence of the extensive hydrogen bonding within the gel network. When dissolved, the polymer chains form random coils; on cooling they assemble into double helices that aggregate into a three-dimensional mesh with pores whose size depends on the agarose concentration.

Applications

The best-known application of agarose is as the matrix in gel electrophoresis for the separation of macromolecules such as DNA, RNA and proteins by size. Agarose gels are also used in immunodiffusion, gel filtration chromatography (in cross-linked bead form, e.g. Sepharose), and as a support for cell culture and tissue engineering.
