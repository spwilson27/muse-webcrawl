House of Worth

The House of Worth was a French fashion house founded in 1858 by the English designer Charles Frederick Worth, widely regarded as the originator of haute couture. From its salon at 7 rue de la Paix in Paris, Worth dressed royalty and aristocracy across Europe, including Empress Eugénie of France and Empress Elisabeth of Austria, and revolutionised dressmaking by presenting seasonal collections on live models and signing his garments with a woven label.

Charles Frederick Worth

Charles Frederick Worth (1825–1895) trained in London before moving to Paris, where he opened his own house with partner Otto Bobergh. Worth reversed the traditional relationship between dressmaker and client: rather than executing the customer's instructions, he designed the garments and dictated taste, becoming the first couturier as artist. He popularised the crinoline and then the bustle, and his ateliers employed more than a thousand workers at their height.

Successors and perfume

After Worth's death his sons Gaston-Lucien and Jean-Philippe ran the house; Jean-Philippe's designs included the gown for Sarah Bernhardt and costumes for the stage. In the 20th century the house diversified into perfume — the 1932 fragrance Je Reviens is its best-known scent — under perfumer Maurice Blanchet. The couture operation declined after the Second World War and closed in the mid-1950s, while the perfume business continued under new ownership.

Legacy

The House of Worth is credited with inventing the modern fashion system: the designer label, seasonal collections, fashion shows with live models, and the licensing of patterns. Worth gowns are held by major museum collections, including the Victoria and Albert Museum and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.
