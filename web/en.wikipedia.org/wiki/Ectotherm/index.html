Ectotherm

An ectotherm (from the Greek ektós, "outside", and thérmē, "heat") is an organism in which internal physiological sources of heat are of relatively small or negligible importance in controlling body temperature. Such organisms rely on environmental heat sources such as sunlight. Ectotherms are often loosely called "cold-blooded," although their blood temperature can in fact be quite high when they are active in warm environments. Most fishes, amphibians, reptiles and nearly all invertebrates are ectotherms.

Thermoregulation

Ectotherms regulate body temperature largely through behaviour: basking in the sun, seeking shade, changing body orientation, moving between warm and cool microclimates, or burrowing. Many lizards maintain activity temperatures within a narrow, preferred range this way and can rival endotherms in the precision of regulation while active. Some ectotherms use physiological mechanisms as well — for example, certain fish retain metabolic heat in swimming muscles (regional heterothermy), and large sea turtles benefit from thermal inertia ("gigantothermy").

Advantages and disadvantages

Because they do not burn energy to maintain a constant body temperature, ectotherms need far less food than endotherms of similar size — a lizard may require only a tenth of the energy of a similar-sized mammal. This permits survival in habitats with sparse or seasonal food supplies, and allows more energy to be allocated to growth and reproduction. The trade-offs include dependence on ambient temperatures, reduced capacity for sustained activity in the cold, and geographic distributions generally limited by climate.

Seasonal strategies

In cold climates, ectotherms overwinter in states of dormancy such as brumation in reptiles. Some frogs, notably the wood frog, tolerate partial freezing of their body tissues. Many ectotherms estivate to escape heat and drought.
