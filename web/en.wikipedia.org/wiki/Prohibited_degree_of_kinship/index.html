Prohibited degree of kinship

The prohibited degrees of kinship refer to the degree of consanguinity (blood relation) or affinity (relation by marriage) between two persons that makes their sexual relations or marriage illegal under the law of a jurisdiction. The rules vary widely between legal systems and religions, but nearly all prohibit unions between parents and children and between siblings.

Terminology

Consanguinity measures blood relationship: in the civil-law (Roman) method of computation, the number of steps (generations) between two persons through their common ancestor is counted; parent and child are related in the first degree, siblings in the second, uncle/aunt and niece/nephew in the third, and first cousins in the fourth. Affinity arises through marriage: a person is related by affinity to their spouse's blood relatives in the same degree as the spouse.

Religious law

The biblical book of Leviticus (chapter 18) lists prohibited unions that underlie much Western law. Roman Catholic canon law historically prohibited marriage to the fourth degree of collateral consanguinity (third cousins) before the Council of Trent and later reforms reduced the restriction; the 1983 Code of Canon Law prohibits marriage in the direct line and to the fourth degree of the collateral line (first cousins), and affinity in the direct line. The Church of England's Table of Kindred and Affinity, printed in the Book of Common Prayer, set out the prohibited degrees used in English law.

Civil law

Modern jurisdictions differ considerably:

  • Almost all ban parent–child and sibling marriages, and many ban uncle/aunt–niece/nephew unions.
  • Cousin marriage: first-cousin marriage is legal in most of Europe and in Canada, but prohibited in roughly half of US states; it is common and preferred in parts of the Middle East and South Asia.
  • Affinity prohibitions have been relaxed in many systems: England, for example, once barred marriage to a deceased wife's sister — a restriction repealed by the Deceased Wife's Sister's Marriage Act 1907 after decades of parliamentary debate.
