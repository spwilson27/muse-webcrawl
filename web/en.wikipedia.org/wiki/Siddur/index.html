Siddur

Type: Jewish prayer book
Language: Hebrew (often with vernacular translation)
First complete versions: 9th–10th century CE (Babylonia)

A siddur (Hebrew: סִדּוּר, romanized: siddūr, plural siddurim; literally "order" or "arrangement") is a Jewish prayer book containing a set order of daily prayers. The word derives from the Hebrew root ס־ד־ר, meaning "order", the same root as seder. A siddur contains the texts of the three daily prayer services — Shacharit (morning), Mincha (afternoon), and Maariv (evening) — together with blessings, Sabbath and festival liturgy (festival prayers are often printed in a separate volume, the machzor), and prayers for home rituals and life-cycle events.

History

The structure of Jewish prayer was largely fixed by the rabbis of the Talmudic era, but the earliest complete prayer books survive from the Babylonian geonic period: the Seder Rav Amram (9th century), sent by Rav Amram Gaon to the community of Barcelona, and the siddur of Saadia Gaon (10th century), which included the author's Arabic commentary and rubrics. Medieval manuscripts multiplied local rites — Ashkenazic, Sephardic, Italian, Yemenite, Romaniote, and others — distinguished by the order and wording of poems (piyyutim) and some prayers. Printing, beginning in the 15th and 16th centuries, standardized the major rites.

Contents

A complete daily siddur typically includes morning blessings and the Pesukei dezimra psalms, the Shema and its blessings, the Amidah (the central standing prayer), Torah readings for Mondays and Thursdays, concluding prayers, the Grace after Meals, blessings for various occasions, the bedtime Shema, and the counting of the Omer. Sabbath editions add Friday-night prayers, the zemirot (table hymns), and the Sabbath services. Modern siddurim commonly print a vernacular translation, commentary, and instructions; notable 20th-century editions include the Birnbaum siddur, the ArtScroll series used widely in Orthodox synagogues, and denomination-specific siddurim of the Reform, Conservative, and Reconstructionist movements, such as Mishkan T'filah.
