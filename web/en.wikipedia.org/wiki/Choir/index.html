Choir

A choir (also known as a chorale or chorus) is a musical ensemble of singers. Choral music, in turn, is the music written specifically for such an ensemble to perform. Choirs may perform music from the classical music repertoire, which spans from the medieval era to the present, or popular music repertoire.

Structure

Most choirs are led by a conductor or choirmaster, who guides the ensemble with hand gestures and facial expressions. A mixed choir is usually composed of four sections corresponding to the four voice types: soprano, alto, tenor, and bass (SATB). Choirs can also be organised as male, female, or children's choirs, or by size — from small chamber choirs of a dozen singers to symphonic choruses of over a hundred.

History

Group singing is among the oldest musical traditions. In the Western tradition, choral singing was formalised in the chant of the medieval church; polyphonic choir music flourished in the Renaissance with composers such as Josquin des Prez and Palestrina. The Baroque and Classical eras added large-scale works for choir and orchestra, including Handel's Messiah and Beethoven's Ninth Symphony.

Accompaniment

Choirs perform with or without instrumental accompaniment. Choral music performed without accompaniment is designated a cappella, a term derived from the practice of the Sistine Chapel choir.
