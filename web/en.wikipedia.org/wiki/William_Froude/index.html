William Froude

Born28 November 1810, Dartington, Devon, England
Died4 May 1879 (aged 68), Simonstown, South Africa

William Froude (28 November 1810 – 4 May 1879) was an English engineer and naval architect who founded the scientific study of ship hydrodynamics. He devised the first practical method for testing scale models of ships in a towing tank and extrapolating the results to full-size vessels, a technique still fundamental to ship design.

Career

Froude studied at Oriel College, Oxford, and worked as a railway engineer under Isambard Kingdom Brunel. His interest in ship resistance began with studies for Brunel's steamship projects. In 1870 the Admiralty funded his experimental tank at Torquay — the first ship model testing tank in the world. There Froude towed models of HMS Greyhound and compared their measured resistance with trials of the full-size ship, confirming the accuracy of his scaling method.

Froude number and scaling laws

Froude showed that the wave-making resistance of geometrically similar hulls scales predictably when the speeds are proportional to the square roots of their lengths — the principle embodied in the dimensionless Froude number (Fr = v / √(gL)), used throughout fluid mechanics for ships, open channels and spillways. He also developed the dynamometer and the hydraulic brake (the Froude brake) for measuring the power of large engines.

Legacy

The Torquay tank's successor facilities at Haslar carried British naval model testing into the twentieth century. Froude was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1870. Model basins around the world still operate on Froude's principles, and the Froude number remains a standard parameter of naval architecture.
