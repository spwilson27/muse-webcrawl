Old-school hip hop

Cultural origins1970s, the Bronx, New York City
Typical instrumentsTurntables, drum machine, vocals

Old-school hip hop is the earliest commercially recorded hip hop music, generally considered to span from the genre's emergence in the 1970s until approximately the mid-1980s. It is principally associated with artists from the Bronx and other parts of New York City, and is characterized by block parties, disco- and funk-derived beats, party-oriented lyrics, and the foundational techniques of DJing and MCing.

History

Hip hop culture is widely dated to 11 August 1973, when DJ Kool Herc (Clive Campbell) hosted a back-to-school party at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue in the Bronx. Herc pioneered the use of two turntables to isolate and loop the "breaks" — the percussive sections of funk and soul records — while dancers he dubbed break-boys and break-girls (b-boys and b-girls) performed. His friend Coke La Rock is often credited as the first MC, hyping the crowd over the beats.

Other foundational figures included Afrika Bambaataa, who founded the Universal Zulu Nation and broadened the culture's philosophy, and Grandmaster Flash, whose technical innovations — backspinning, punch phrasing, and cutting — transformed turntablism. Grandmaster Flash and the Furious Five's "The Message" (1982) introduced socially conscious lyricism to the genre.

Commercial breakthrough

The first hip hop record to reach the Top 40 of the Billboard Hot 100 was the Sugarhill Gang's "Rapper's Delight" (1979), released on Sugar Hill Records. Other notable early recordings include Kurtis Blow's "The Breaks" (1980) and Afrika Bambaataa and the Soulsonic Force's "Planet Rock" (1982), which drew on Kraftwerk and helped spawn electro. The era is generally considered to end around 1983–1984 with the rise of Run-D.M.C., whose harder, drum-machine-driven sound ushered in the new school.

Characteristics

Old-school hip hop relied heavily on disco and funk samples and loops, relatively simple rhyme schemes often focused on party chants and call-and-response, and a strong emphasis on the DJ as the centerpiece of performance. Fashion of the era included tracksuits, Kangol hats, and Cazal glasses; later old-school acts such as Run-D.M.C. popularized unlaced Adidas sneakers.
