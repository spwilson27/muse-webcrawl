Founded: 1942, Chicago
Abbreviation: CORE

Congress of Racial Equality

The Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) is an African-American civil rights organization founded in Chicago in 1942. It was one of the "Big Four" civil rights organizations in the United States, along with the NAACP, the Urban League, and the SCLC.

Founding and philosophy

CORE was founded by James Farmer, George Houser, James R. Robinson, Samuel E. Riley, Bernice Fisher, and Homer Jack, drawing on the nonviolent methods of Mahatma Gandhi. Its early actions used sit-ins to desegregate public facilities in Chicago, and in 1947 it organized the Journey of Reconciliation, an early test of desegregation in interstate travel.

Freedom Rides and voter registration

CORE gained national prominence with the Freedom Rides of 1961, in which integrated groups rode interstate buses into the segregated South. Under James Farmer's leadership it also participated in the Freedom Summer voter-registration campaign in Mississippi in 1964, during which CORE workers James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner were murdered.

Later years

In 1966 Floyd McKissick succeeded Farmer and steered CORE toward Black Power; Roy Innis became national director in 1968 and moved the organization in a more conservative direction. CORE remains in existence, though much reduced in influence.
