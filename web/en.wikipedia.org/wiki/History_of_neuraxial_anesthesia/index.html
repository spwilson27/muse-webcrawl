History of neuraxial anesthesia

The history of neuraxial anesthesia traces the development of spinal and epidural anesthesia — techniques that inject local anesthetic near the spinal cord to block pain in a region of the body while the patient remains conscious. Neuraxial techniques are now central to surgery, obstetrics and pain medicine.

Early experiments

In 1885 the American neurologist James Leonard Corning injected cocaine between the spinous processes of a dog and then of a human volunteer, producing lower-body numbness; whether his injection was truly epidural or subarachnoid remains debated. In 1891 Heinrich Quincke described lumbar puncture for diagnostic purposes, providing the anatomical access that made spinal anesthesia possible.

First spinal anesthetics

The German surgeon August Bier performed the first deliberate spinal anesthetic with cocaine on 16 August 1898, operating on a patient with a tuberculous ankle; he and his assistant then experimented on themselves, famously documenting severe post-dural-puncture headaches. Bier also pioneered intravenous regional anesthesia. The French surgeon Théodore Tuffier championed spinal anesthesia in Paris, and the technique spread rapidly, though toxicity of cocaine and unpredictable spread limited it.

Epidural anesthesia

In 1901 Fernand Cathelin and Jean Sicard independently described caudal epidural injection. The Spanish military surgeon Fidel Pagés described lumbar epidural anesthesia in 1921, and the Italian Achille Dogliotti popularised the loss-of-resistance technique in the 1930s. Alberto Gutiérrez described the hanging drop sign. The Tuohy needle and catheter techniques developed by Edward Tuohy in the 1940s enabled continuous epidural analgesia.

Safer drugs and wider use

The synthesis of procaine (1905) and later lidocaine (1943) and bupivacaine (1957) made neuraxial blocks safer and longer-lasting. John Bonica championed epidural analgesia in obstetrics and chronic pain in the mid-20th century. Spinal anesthesia became standard for caesarean section and lower-limb surgery because it avoids the airway risks of general anesthesia in pregnancy.

Modern practice

Modern neuraxial anesthesia uses fine pencil-point needles that reduce headache rates, ultrasound guidance for difficult placements, and combined spinal-epidural techniques. Low-dose mixtures of local anesthetic and opioids provide labour analgesia to millions of women each year, and neuraxial blocks form part of enhanced-recovery protocols in major surgery.
