Antitoxin

Antitoxin
TypeAntibody preparation
SourceTypically equine (horse) serum
UseNeutralising bacterial exotoxins

An antitoxin is an antibody with the ability to neutralise a specific toxin. Antitoxins are produced by certain animals, plants and bacteria in response to toxin exposure. When injected into a patient, an antitoxin preparation — usually purified serum from an immunised horse — binds circulating toxin molecules before they can damage tissue, providing immediate passive immunity.

History

In 1890 Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato showed that serum from animals immunised against diphtheria and tetanus toxins could cure infected animals, opening the era of serum therapy. Diphtheria antitoxin dramatically reduced deaths from the disease and was famously carried by dog sled to Nome, Alaska, during the 1925 diphtheria outbreak — the event commemorated by the Iditarod.

Clinical uses

Antitoxins remain essential treatments for diphtheria, tetanus, botulism and certain snake and scorpion envenomations (in the form of antivenom). Because they are foreign proteins, they can cause allergic reactions, including serum sickness, so patients are often tested or premedicated before administration.
