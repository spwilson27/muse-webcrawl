Lèse-majesté in Thailand

Lèse-majesté in Thailand
Legal basis: Section 112, Thai Criminal Code
Penalty: 3 to 15 years' imprisonment per count
Protected: The King, Queen, Heir-apparent, Regent

Lèse-majesté in Thailand refers to the crime of defaming, insulting, or threatening the Thai monarch. It is defined by Section 112 of the Thai Criminal Code, which provides: "Whoever defames, insults or threatens the King, the Queen, the Heir-apparent or the Regent, shall be punished with imprisonment of three to fifteen years." Thailand's law is among the strictest lèse-majesté laws in the world.

History

Modern Thai lèse-majesté dates to the early 20th century; Section 112 in its current form stems from the 1956 criminal code, and penalties were increased in 1976. Because charges may be filed by anyone and police are obliged to investigate, the law has frequently been used amid political conflicts — after the 2006 and 2014 coups, prosecutions rose sharply, and cases were for a period tried in military courts.

Notable cases and criticism

Sentences accumulate per count, producing very long terms: in 2015 a man received 30 years (halved from 60 on a guilty plea) for Facebook posts, and in 2021 a former civil servant was sentenced to 87 years, reduced to 43 years and six months — the longest known sentence. Human rights organizations, the UN, and foreign governments have criticized the law's breadth and its chilling effect on public discussion. During the 2020–21 Thai protests, reform or repeal of Section 112 became a prominent demand, and prosecutions of protest leaders followed. The Constitutional Court ruled in 2024 that campaigning to amend the law in a particular way could amount to an attempt to overthrow the monarchy, leading to the dissolution of the Move Forward Party.
