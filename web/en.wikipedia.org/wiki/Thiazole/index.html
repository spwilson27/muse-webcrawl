Thiazole

Thiazole
Formula: C3H3NS
Molar mass: 97.16 g/mol
Appearance: pale yellow liquid
Boiling point: 116–118 °C

Thiazole, or 1,3-thiazole, is a heterocyclic compound that contains both sulfur and nitrogen; thiazole also refers to a large family of derivatives. Thiazole itself is a pale yellow liquid with a pyridine-like odour and the molecular formula C3H3NS. The thiazole ring is notable as a component of the vitamin thiamine (B1).

Structure and properties

Thiazoles are members of the class of azoles, five-membered heterocycles including imidazoles and oxazoles. The thiazole ring is planar and aromatic. It has a greater pi-electron delocalization than the corresponding oxazoles and is therefore more aromatic. The nitrogen is basic and reactive toward electrophiles, while the C-2 proton is relatively acidic and readily removed by strong bases, a property exploited in synthesis.

Occurrence and synthesis

Thiazole rings occur in many natural products and pharmaceuticals. They are a core structure in drugs such as the antibiotic sulfathiazole, the antiretroviral ritonavir, and various antifungal and anticancer agents. Thiazolium salts, such as those found in thiamine, play important roles in biochemistry as coenzymes. A classic laboratory preparation is the Hantzsch thiazole synthesis, in which a thioamide reacts with an α-haloketone.

Reactions

Thiazoles undergo electrophilic substitution preferentially at C-5, and nucleophilic substitution at C-2. Deprotonation at C-2 gives a nucleophilic carbene-like species (the basis of thiamine catalysis in the benzoin condensation and Stetter reaction). Oxidation of the ring sulfur and alkylation of the nitrogen to form thiazolium salts are also characteristic reactions.
