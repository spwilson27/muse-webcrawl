The Algeciras Conference of 1906 was an international conference held in Algeciras, Spain, to resolve the First Moroccan Crisis, a diplomatic confrontation between France and Germany over the status of Morocco.

Background

In 1904 France and Britain concluded the Entente Cordiale, under which Britain recognised France's special position in Morocco. Germany, excluded from the arrangement, challenged it dramatically when Kaiser Wilhelm II landed at Tangier in March 1905 and declared support for Moroccan independence. France's foreign minister Théophile Delcassé was forced to resign, and the parties agreed to settle the question at a conference.

Outcome

At Algeciras, Germany found itself isolated: only Austria-Hungary supported its position, while Britain, Russia, Italy, Spain and the United States backed France. The resulting Act of Algeciras (7 April 1906) formally preserved Moroccan sovereignty and the "open door" for trade, but gave France and Spain control of the Moroccan police and France a dominant role in the new State Bank of Morocco. The conference thus strengthened the Entente and deepened Germany's sense of encirclement, a milestone on the road to the First World War.
