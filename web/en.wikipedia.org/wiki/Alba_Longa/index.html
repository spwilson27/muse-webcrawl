Alba Longa was an ancient Latin city in central Italy, in the Alban Hills southeast of Rome, traditionally founded by Ascanius, son of the Trojan hero Aeneas, about thirty years after the founding of Lavinium. It was the leading city of the Latin League and, in Roman tradition, the mother city of Rome itself: its royal line produced Rhea Silvia, mother of Romulus and Remus.

Legendary history

According to Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Ascanius (also called Iulus) founded Alba Longa ("long white [city]", supposedly named for a white sow Aeneas had seen, or for its elongated plan on a ridge). A dynasty of Silvian kings, descended from Aeneas's son Silvius, ruled for some three centuries. The last legitimate king, Numitor, was deposed by his brother Amulius, whose attempt to extinguish the line by forcing Numitor's daughter Rhea Silvia into Vestal virginity failed when she bore the twins Romulus and Remus.

Destruction

Roman tradition held that Alba Longa was destroyed by the Roman king Tullus Hostilius in the 7th century BC, after the famous combat between the Horatii and Curiatii — triplet brothers representing Rome and Alba respectively. The Alban dictator Mettius Fufetius was executed for treachery, the city razed, and its population transferred to Rome, where leading Alban families (including the Julii, ancestors of Julius Caesar) entered the Roman nobility. Alba's religious role survived: the Alban Mount remained the site of the Latin Festival (Feriae Latinae) shared by Rome and the Latin cities.

Archaeology

The exact site of Alba Longa has never been securely identified; it is usually placed along the eastern shore of Lake Albano near Castel Gandolfo, where Iron Age remains and the great Roman-era villas (later including the papal summer residence area) attest long occupation of the ridge.
