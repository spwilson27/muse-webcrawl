The AGM-114 Hellfire is an American air-to-surface missile system developed primarily for anti-armour use. Its name derives from its original concept, Helicopter-Launched Fire-and-Forget missile. First deployed in 1984, the Hellfire has become one of the most widely used precision-guided munitions in the world, fired from attack helicopters such as the AH-64 Apache and, since the 2000s, extensively from unmanned aircraft such as the MQ-1 Predator and MQ-9 Reaper.

Development

Development began in the 1970s as the US Army sought a helicopter-launched anti-tank missile able to destroy modern Soviet armour. Rockwell International designed the missile around semi-active laser homing: the target is illuminated ("painted") by a laser designator, either on the launching aircraft, another aircraft or troops on the ground, and the missile's seeker homes on the reflected energy. Production passed to Martin Marietta (later Lockheed Martin), and the missile entered service with the Apache in the mid-1980s.

Variants

The basic AGM-114A carried a shaped-charge anti-tank warhead. Later variants include the AGM-114K with a tandem warhead to defeat explosive reactive armour; the AGM-114M blast-fragmentation model for softer targets; the AGM-114N with a thermobaric (metal-augmented charge) warhead; and the AGM-114L Longbow Hellfire, which uses a millimetre-wave radar seeker in the AH-64D Longbow system for true fire-and-forget operation in poor visibility. The AGM-114R "Romeo" (Hellfire II) introduced a multi-purpose warhead selectable in flight. The AGM-114R-9X, a variant with deployable blades instead of explosives, was developed for strikes intended to minimise collateral damage. The Hellfire is progressively being succeeded in US service by the AGM-179 JAGM.

Operational history

Hellfires were first used in combat during the 1989 invasion of Panama and fired in large numbers during the 1991 Gulf War, where Apaches opened the air campaign by destroying Iraqi radar sites. The missile has since been used in the Balkans, Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, Yemen, Somalia and Syria, by the United States and numerous allied operators including the United Kingdom, France, Israel, Australia and others. Its adoption as the standard armament of armed drones made it central to post-2001 counterterrorism campaigns, and also to public controversy over targeted killings.
