Simvastatin, sold under the brand name Zocor among others, is a statin medication used to lower cholesterol and reduce the risk of heart disease and stroke in people at high risk. It is taken by mouth, usually once daily in the evening, and is recommended together with diet, exercise, and weight management. Simvastatin was one of the best-selling drugs in the world before its patent expired in 2006, and it remains widely prescribed as a generic.

The chemical structure of simvastatin, a semi-synthetic derivative of lovastatin.

Medical use

Simvastatin lowers low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol by roughly 30–50% depending on dose, modestly raises high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and lowers triglycerides. Large outcome trials, notably the Scandinavian Simvastatin Survival Study (4S, 1994) and the Heart Protection Study (2002), demonstrated that simvastatin reduces mortality and major vascular events in patients with or at risk of cardiovascular disease, establishing the statin class as a cornerstone of preventive cardiology.

Mechanism

Simvastatin is administered as an inactive lactone prodrug that is hydrolysed in the liver to its active β-hydroxy acid form. It competitively inhibits HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis; the liver responds by increasing expression of LDL receptors, clearing more LDL from the blood. The drug is metabolised principally by the enzyme CYP3A4, so strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (such as certain antifungals, macrolide antibiotics, and grapefruit juice) can raise simvastatin levels dangerously.

Side effects

Common side effects include headache, constipation, and muscle pain. Uncommon but serious risks include myopathy and rhabdomyolysis, which led regulators in 2011 to restrict the 80 mg dose, and liver enzyme elevations. Simvastatin was developed by Merck from the earlier statin lovastatin, patented in 1980, and first marketed in the late 1980s and early 1990s.
