Constitution of the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the United States of America. Drafted at the Philadelphia Convention in 1787, ratified by the states in 1788 and in effect from 4 March 1789, it succeeded the Articles of Confederation, creating a federal union with a stronger national government structured by the separation of powers and checks and balances. It is the world's longest-surviving written national constitution still in force, and has been amended 27 times — the first ten amendments (1791) forming the Bill of Rights.

Structure

The text opens with the Preamble ("We the People of the United States...") and seven articles: Congress (I — enumerating legislative powers, including commerce, taxing and war), the presidency (II), the federal judiciary (III), states' relations and citizenship (IV), the amendment process (V), supremacy of federal law (VI) and ratification (VII). The amendment procedure — proposal by two-thirds of Congress or a convention, ratification by three-quarters of states — has been completed 27 times, most recently in 1992 (congressional pay, proposed in 1789).

Interpretation

The Constitution's brevity has made judicial interpretation central to American public life: implied powers (McCulloch v. Maryland, 1819), judicial review (Marbury, 1803), the Reconstruction Amendments' transformation of federalism and rights (1865–70), the New Deal settlement, and the Warren Court's rights revolution anchor its history. Interpretive schools — originalism, living constitutionalism and their variants — contend in confirmation politics and scholarship, and the document's global influence is visible in constitutional borrowing from Latin America to post-1945 Europe and Asia.
