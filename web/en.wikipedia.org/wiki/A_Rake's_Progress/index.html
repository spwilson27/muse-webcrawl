A Rake's Progress

ArtistWilliam Hogarth
Year1733–1735 (engravings 1735)
MediumOil on canvas; engravings
LocationSir John Soane's Museum, London

A Rake's Progress is a series of eight paintings and engravings by the English artist William Hogarth, produced in 1732–1734 and published as engravings in 1735. The series depicts the decline and fall of Tom Rakewell, the spendthrift heir of a rich merchant, who wastes his fortune on luxurious living, prostitutes, and gambling, and ends in debtors' prison and finally the Bethlem Royal Hospital madhouse.

The scenes

The plates trace Tom's story: (1) The Heir, in which he inherits and rejects his pregnant fiancée Sarah Young; (2) The Levée, morning reception amid sycophants; (3) The Tavern Scene, drunken debauchery; (4) The Arrest, saved from bailiffs by Sarah; (5) The Marriage, a mercenary wedding to an old maid; (6) The Gaming House, ruin at the tables; (7) The Prison, Fleet debtors' prison; and (8) The Madhouse, Bedlam, where the faithful Sarah still attends him.

Legacy

The series exemplifies Hogarth's "modern moral subjects" and helped popularize sequential narrative art. It inspired the 1951 opera The Rake's Progress by Igor Stravinsky with a libretto by W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman, as well as later reinterpretations including a 1975 series by David Hockney. Its popularity was such that piracy of the engravings helped prompt the Copyright Act of 1735, known as "Hogarth's Act."
