Ceramic glaze

Ceramic glaze, or simply glaze, is a glassy coating fused onto the surface of a ceramic body through firing. Glazes make pottery impermeable to liquids, protect and strengthen the ware, and provide colour and texture ranging from glossy and transparent to matte and crystalline.

Composition

Glazes consist mainly of silica (the glass former), alumina (to stiffen the melt and prevent running) and fluxes such as sodium, potassium, calcium or lead compounds that lower the melting point. Colourants include metal oxides: cobalt (blue), copper (green or, in reduction, red), iron (browns and celadon greens) and manganese.

Types

Traditional glazes include ash glazes (from wood or plant ash, among the earliest, in East Asia), salt glaze (produced by throwing salt into a hot kiln), lead glazes of the ancient world and Islamic world, tin-glazed ware such as maiolica and Delftware, and the high-fired feldspathic glazes of porcelain.

Application and firing

Glaze is usually applied by dipping, pouring, spraying or brushing a suspension of the powdered materials onto bisque-fired ware, then fired to temperatures from about 900 °C (low-fire earthenware) to 1,400 °C (porcelain), in oxidising or reducing kiln atmospheres that strongly affect colour.
