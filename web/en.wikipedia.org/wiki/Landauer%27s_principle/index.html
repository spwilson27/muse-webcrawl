Landauer's principle

Landauer's principle is a physical principle in the thermodynamics of computation, proposed by Rolf Landauer in 1961, stating that any logically irreversible manipulation of information — such as the erasure of a bit — must be accompanied by a corresponding entropy increase in the system's environment, dissipating at least kBT ln 2 of heat per bit erased.

Statement

At temperature T, erasing one bit of information releases at least E = kB T ln 2 joules of heat, where kB is Boltzmann's constant. At room temperature this is about 2.9 × 10−21 joules — far below the energy used by current computers, but a fundamental lower bound.

Implications

The principle links information theory to the second law of thermodynamics and was used by Charles Bennett to resolve the Maxwell's demon paradox: the demon must eventually erase its memory, and the erasure cost pays for the entropy reduction it achieves. Logically reversible operations, by contrast, can in principle be performed without dissipation, motivating research into reversible computing.

Experimental tests

Experiments with colloidal particles in optical traps (2012) and with nanomagnetic bits have measured heat dissipation close to the Landauer bound, supporting the principle, though its precise status and generality remain subjects of theoretical debate.
