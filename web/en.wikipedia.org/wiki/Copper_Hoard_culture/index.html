Copper Hoard culture

The Copper Hoard culture is the name given to a set of copper artefact hoards found in northern India and dated approximately to the 2nd millennium BCE, roughly 2000–1500 BCE. The hoards, concentrated in the Ganges–Yamuna doab and extending from Haryana and Rajasthan to Bihar and Odisha, were first brought to scholarly attention in the 19th century, when implements were recovered near Bareilly and elsewhere in Uttar Pradesh.

Artefacts

Characteristic objects include:

  • Anthropomorphic figures — flat, human-shaped copper plates with curved arms and legs, of debated function
  • Antennae swords — swords whose hilts end in two prongs resembling antennae
  • Harpoons with barbed edges
  • Celts (axes), rings, and hooked spears

The metal is generally quite pure copper, smelted presumably from ores of the Aravalli region or the Singhbhum belt. Many objects show little use-wear, prompting speculation that some were ritual or votive items, though others are plainly weapons and tools.

Associations

Copper Hoards are frequently found in the same regions as Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP), leading many archaeologists to associate the two, and debates continue over whether the makers were late Harappans, distinct local cultures of the doab, or early Indo-Aryan groups. The hoards' dating and identity remain unresolved questions of South Asian archaeology.
