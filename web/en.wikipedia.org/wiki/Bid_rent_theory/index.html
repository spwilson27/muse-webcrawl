Bid rent theory

Bid rent theory is a geographical economic theory that explains how the price and demand for real estate change as distance from the central business district (CBD) increases. It states that different land users will compete with one another for land close to the city centre, because accessibility is greatest there; the amount each user is willing and able to pay is called its bid rent.

The model

The theory rests on the idea that the closer a location is to the CBD, the greater the advantage it offers in terms of customers, workforce and transport connections, and therefore the higher the rent users will bid for it. Retailers, who depend most heavily on passing trade, have the steepest bid rent curve and outbid others in the centre. Offices follow, then industry, and finally residential users, whose curve is flattest because households can trade space against commuting cost.

The resulting pattern resembles the concentric zones described by Ernest Burgess: a commercial core surrounded by industry, then progressively lower-density housing, with land values declining outward along each curve.

Origins and development

The theory descends from the agricultural land-rent model of Johann Heinrich von Thünen (1826), adapted to the city by urban economists and geographers in the twentieth century, notably William Alonso, whose 1964 work Location and Land Use formalised the bid rent function for households, firms and farmers within a single framework.

Limitations

Real cities depart from the model's assumptions: transport networks create corridors of accessibility, planning rules and zoning constrain competition, multiple suburban centres break the single-centre assumption, and amenities such as parks, coastline and school quality distort rent gradients.
