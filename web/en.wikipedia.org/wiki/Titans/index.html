Titans

The Titans
ParentsUranus and Gaia
AbodeMount Othrys; later Tartarus

The Titans were a race of powerful gods in Greek mythology, the generation that preceded the Olympians. According to Hesiod's Theogony, they were the twelve children of the primordial parents Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth): Oceanus, Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, Iapetus, and Cronus; and Theia, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, Phoebe, and Tethys. From their mountain seat on Othrys they ruled during the Golden Age, with Cronus as their king.

Mythology

Cronus, urged by Gaia, overthrew his father Uranus with a sickle. Warned that his own child would depose him, Cronus swallowed each of his children by Rhea at birth, until Rhea hid the infant Zeus and substituted a stone. Grown in secret, Zeus forced Cronus to disgorge his siblings and led the Olympians and their allies — the Cyclopes and Hecatoncheires freed from Tartarus — in the ten-year Titanomachy. Victorious, Zeus divided the cosmos with his brothers Poseidon and Hades, cast the defeated Titans into Tartarus, and set Atlas to bear the heavens.

Individual Titans

Not all Titans fought or were imprisoned: Oceanus remained neutral, Themis and Mnemosyne (mother of the Muses) joined the Olympian order, and Epimetheus and Prometheus — sons of Iapetus — figure in the myths of humankind's creation, the theft of fire, and Pandora. Later writers extended the name "Titan" to many of the first generation's descendants, including Helios, Selene, and Leto.

Legacy

The word "titan" has entered general usage for anything of colossal size or power, giving us "titanic" and the element titanium. The Titans' fall has been retold from Hesiod and the Orphic poems through Renaissance painting to modern novels, films, and games.
