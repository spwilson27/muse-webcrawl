Glycosylation

Glycosylation
TypePost-translational modification
Major formsN-linked, O-linked, O-GlcNAc, C-linked, glypiation
LocationER, Golgi, cytosol/nucleus

Glycosylation is the reaction in which a carbohydrate (a glycosyl donor) is attached to a hydroxyl or other functional group of another molecule (a glycosyl acceptor), usually a protein or lipid. In biology, glycosylation is an enzyme-directed process of adding glycans to proteins and other organic molecules, and is one of the most common and complex forms of post-translational modification: a majority of proteins synthesised in the endoplasmic reticulum undergo it.

N-linked glycosylation

N-linked glycosylation begins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) with the transfer of a 14-sugar precursor (Glc3Man9GlcNAc2) from dolichol phosphate to an asparagine in the sequon Asn–X–Ser/Thr. Glucoses and mannoses are trimmed during protein-folding quality control (the calnexin cycle), and in the Golgi the glycans are remodelled into high-mannose, complex, or hybrid types, often capped with sialic acid.

O-linked glycosylation

O-linked glycosylation adds sugars sequentially to serine or threonine residues, chiefly in the Golgi; mucin-type glycosylation begins with GalNAc. The cytosolic and nuclear modification O-GlcNAc adds a single sugar dynamically, analogous to phosphorylation, and responds to nutrient state.

Functions and significance

Glycosylation affects protein folding, stability, trafficking, half-life and immunogenicity, and creates recognition epitopes (blood-group antigens, selectin ligands). Errors cause congenital disorders of glycosylation, a group of multisystem diseases. In biotechnology, controlling glycosylation is essential for therapeutic proteins such as erythropoietin and monoclonal antibodies.
