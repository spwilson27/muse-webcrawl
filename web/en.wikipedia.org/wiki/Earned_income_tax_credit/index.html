Earned income tax credit

The earned income tax credit (EITC or EIC) is a refundable tax credit in the United States for low- to moderate-income working individuals and couples, particularly those with children. The amount of EITC benefit depends on a recipient's income and number of children. For a person or couple to claim one or more persons as their qualifying children, requirements such as relationship, age, and shared residency must be met. In the absence of children, the amount of the credit is much smaller.


The EITC phases in with earnings, plateaus, then phases out

Enacted in 1975, the initially modest EITC has been expanded by tax legislation on a number of occasions, including the widely publicised Tax Reform Act of 1986, and was further expanded in 1990, 1993, 2001, and 2009. It is one of the largest anti-poverty tools in the United States: in recent years roughly 25 million eligible workers and families have received billions of dollars in credits annually, and research credits the program with lifting millions of people — about half of them children — above the poverty line each year.

Structure

The credit has a distinctive trapezoidal shape: it phases in at a fixed rate as earnings rise from zero, reaches a maximum plateau, and then phases out as income continues to increase. Because it is refundable, filers can receive the credit as a refund even when it exceeds their tax liability. The credit is calculated on earned income only, and claimants must meet residency and filing-status rules; investment income above a modest threshold disqualifies the claimant. Many states and some localities offer their own supplementary EITCs calculated as a percentage of the federal credit.

Effects and criticism

Studies generally find the EITC increases labour-force participation, particularly among single mothers, and reduces poverty more per dollar than many transfer programs. Criticisms include its complexity, which contributes to a significant rate of improper payments, and the small benefit available to childless workers. The temporary 2021 expansion under the American Rescue Plan substantially increased the credit for workers without children for one year.
