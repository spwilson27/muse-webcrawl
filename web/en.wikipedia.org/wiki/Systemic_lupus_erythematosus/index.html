Systemic lupus erythematosus

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), commonly known simply as lupus, is an autoimmune disease in which the body's immune system mistakenly attacks healthy tissue in many parts of the body. Symptoms vary between people and may be mild to severe. Common symptoms include painful and swollen joints, fever, chest pain, hair loss, mouth ulcers, swollen lymph nodes, feeling tired, and a red rash which is most commonly on the face. Often there are periods of illness, called flares, and periods of remission during which there are few symptoms.

The cause of SLE is not clear. It is thought to involve genetics together with environmental factors. Among identical twins, if one is affected there is a 24% chance the other one will also be affected. Female sex hormones, sunlight, smoking, vitamin D deficiency, and certain infections are also believed to increase the risk. The mechanism involves an immune response by autoantibodies against a person's own tissues, most characteristically antinuclear antibodies (ANA), which result in inflammation.

Signs and symptoms

SLE can affect any organ system. The classic malar ("butterfly") rash appears across the cheeks and the bridge of the nose while sparing the nasolabial folds. Other manifestations include photosensitivity, discoid skin lesions, arthritis, serositis (pleuritis or pericarditis), kidney involvement (lupus nephritis), seizures or psychosis, and blood abnormalities such as anemia, leukopenia and thrombocytopenia. Kidney disease is among the most serious complications and a major determinant of prognosis.

Diagnosis

There is no single test for SLE. Diagnosis is based on a combination of clinical findings and laboratory tests, including a positive antinuclear antibody test, anti-double-stranded DNA and anti-Smith antibodies, low complement levels, and urinalysis and blood counts. Classification criteria published by the American College of Rheumatology and, later, the EULAR/ACR 2019 criteria, are widely used for research and to support diagnosis.

Treatment

There is no cure for SLE, and treatment aims to control flares and prevent organ damage. Hydroxychloroquine is recommended for nearly all patients. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids such as prednisone, and immunosuppressants including methotrexate, azathioprine and mycophenolate mofetil are used according to severity. Biologic agents such as belimumab and anifrolumab have been approved for certain patients. Sun protection and smoking cessation are also advised.

Epidemiology and history

SLE affects women roughly nine times as often as men, most frequently during the reproductive years. It is more common and often more severe in people of African, Hispanic and Asian ancestry. The name "lupus" (Latin for wolf) dates to medieval descriptions of the disease's erosive skin lesions. The modern systemic understanding of the disease developed in the late 19th and 20th centuries, with the discovery of the LE cell in 1948 and of antinuclear antibodies in the 1950s.
