The Whig Interpretation of History

AuthorHerbert Butterfield
Published1931
SubjectHistoriography

The Whig Interpretation of History is a short book by the British historian Herbert Butterfield, published in 1931. It gave a name — "the whig interpretation" — to a style of history writing that Butterfield condemned, and the term has since become a standard piece of historiographical vocabulary.

Argument

Butterfield attacked histories that read the past as an inevitable march of progress toward the (liberal, Protestant, parliamentary) present: dividing historical figures into the friends and enemies of progress, abridging the complexity of the past into a story that ratifies the present. Such writing, he argued, produces "a story which is the ratification if not the glorification of the present". Against it he urged historians to understand past agents in their own terms, recovering the strangeness and contingency of events rather than judging them by their outcomes.

Legacy

"Whig history" was soon extended beyond Butterfield's original target — notably to the history of science, where Thomas Kuhn and others criticised textbook accounts that portray science as steady progress toward current truth. In American usage, "Whig history" is sometimes used neutrally or even approvingly for progress narratives; Butterfield himself later softened some claims, and historians have debated whether his alternative is attainable, but the book remains required reading on historical method.
