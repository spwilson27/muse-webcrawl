River Tywi

River Tywi
Welsh name: Afon Tywi
Location: Carmarthenshire, Wales
Length: approx. 120 km (75 miles)
Source: Cambrian Mountains
Mouth: Carmarthen Bay

The River Tywi (Welsh: Afon Tywi) is the longest river flowing entirely within Wales. It rises in the Cambrian Mountains of mid Wales, just south of the Teifi Pools, and flows generally south-west through Carmarthenshire, passing the towns of Llandovery, Llandeilo, and Carmarthen before entering Carmarthen Bay at Pendine Sands, between the Three Rivers estuary shared with the River Taf and the River Gwendraeth.

Course

The river rises at an altitude of roughly 490 metres in the Cambrian Mountains, in the area of the Llyn Brianne reservoir, which was constructed in the late 1960s to regulate flow and supply water. From there the Tywi flows south through a deeply incised valley past Llandovery, then turns west past the market town of Llandeilo and the village of Dryslwyn, famous for its ruined castle overlooking the floodplain. Near Carmarthen the river becomes tidal, and at Carmarthen it is crossed by the Towy Bridge. Below Carmarthen the river widens into an estuary that meets Carmarthen Bay near Laugharne, the town associated with Dylan Thomas.

Ecology and fishing

The Tywi is renowned as one of the finest sea trout (sewin) rivers in the United Kingdom, and also supports salmon fishing. Its tributaries include the Afon Dulais, Afon Cothi, and Afon Bran. The river and its floodplain support otters, and the estuary is important for wading birds. Much of the catchment is designated for conservation purposes.

History

The Tywi valley has been settled since prehistoric times. During the medieval period, castles were built along the river at Dryslwyn, Dinefwr, and Carmarthen to control the route through south-west Wales. The Romans established a fort at Moridunum, near modern Carmarthen. In more recent centuries the river was used for coracle fishing, a tradition maintained by licensed coracle fishermen working in pairs on the lower river.
