Mordant

A mordant or dye fixative is a substance used to set (i.e. bind) dyes on fabrics. It does this by forming a coordination complex with the dye, which then attaches to the tissue or fabric. It may be used for dyeing fabrics or for intensifying stains in cell or tissue preparations. Although mordants are still used, especially by small batch dyers, they have been largely displaced in industry by substantives (direct dyes).

The term mordant comes from the Latin mordere, "to bite". In the past, it was thought that a mordant helped the dye "bite" onto the fibre so that it would hold fast during washing.

Common mordants

Metal salts are the classic mordants, including alum (potassium aluminium sulfate), chrome alum, copper sulfate ("blue vitriol"), ferrous sulfate ("copperas"), and tin salts. Sodium chloride (common salt) and tannic acid are also used. The choice of mordant strongly affects the final colour: iron tends to "sadden" (darken) colours, tin brightens them, and alum preserves clear bright shades.

Methods

The mordant may be applied before dyeing (pre-mordanting), together with the dye (meta-mordanting), or after dyeing (post-mordanting / after-mordanting), with different effects on shade and fastness. The dye–mordant–fibre complex formed is typically insoluble, which improves wash and light fastness.

Other uses

In histology and microscopy, mordants such as those used in Gram staining and haematoxylin formulations fix dyes to tissues. Mordants are also used in metallurgy and in the etching of metals, and the word is applied metaphorically to biting, caustic wit or criticism.
