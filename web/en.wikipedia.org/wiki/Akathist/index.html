Akathist

An akathist (Greek: ἀκάθιστος ὕμνος, "unseated hymn") is a type of hymn in Eastern Orthodox and Byzantine Catholic devotion, traditionally sung while standing, as a sign of reverence. The term derives from the Greek a- (not) and kathizein (to sit).

The Akathist to the Theotokos

The most famous akathist, and the model for all later ones, is the Akathist to the Theotokos (Mother of God), attributed by tradition to the deliverance of Constantinople from siege — often associated with the Avar-Persian siege of 626, when the city's defenders carried the icon of the Theotokos along the walls and the siege collapsed. The hymn consists of 24 stanzas (oikoi and kontakia) alternating in an alphabetical acrostic, each long stanza ending with the refrain "Rejoice, O Bride unwedded!" It is sung in parts during Great Lent and in full on the Saturday of the Akathist, the fifth Saturday of Lent.

Later akathists

Hundreds of later akathists have been composed to Christ, the saints, and particular icons or feasts, especially in the Russian Orthodox tradition from the 18th century onward. They are commonly served in parishes as a devotional service on weekday evenings, often before an icon of the saint commemorated.
