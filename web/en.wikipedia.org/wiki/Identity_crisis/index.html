Identity crisis

An identity crisis involves questioning one's sense of self and place in the world.

In psychology, an identity crisis is a developmental event that involves a person questioning their sense of self or place in the world. The concept originates in the theory of developmental psychologist Erik Erikson, who believed that the formation of identity was one of the most important conflicts people face. According to Erikson, an identity crisis is a time of intensive analysis and exploration of different ways of looking at oneself.

Erikson's stages

Erikson proposed eight stages of psychosocial development spanning the lifespan. The fifth stage, typically occurring during adolescence, pits identity versus role confusion: teenagers experiment with roles, values and beliefs to forge a stable sense of self, and failure to do so leaves role confusion. Erikson considered resolution of the identity crisis a prerequisite for later stages, such as intimacy versus isolation in young adulthood. He also applied the concept to his own life and to historical figures in his "psychohistory" studies of Luther and Gandhi.

Marcia's identity statuses

Building on Erikson, James Marcia described four identity statuses based on the presence or absence of exploration (crisis) and commitment: identity diffusion (neither), foreclosure (commitment without exploration), moratorium (exploration without commitment — the crisis itself), and identity achievement (exploration followed by commitment).

Beyond adolescence

Although Erikson located the crisis in adolescence, the term is now used loosely for periods of self-questioning at any age — after bereavement, career change, migration, illness or retirement. The related popular notion of the "midlife crisis" reflects a similar reassessment in middle age.
