Organ trade

The organ trade is the trade in human organs for the purpose of transplantation. Because the number of patients awaiting organs far exceeds the supply of legally donated organs — most of which come from deceased donors — a black market has developed in many countries in which kidneys, and less commonly parts of the liver and other tissues, are bought from living sellers, often poor people in developing countries, and transplanted into wealthier recipients, sometimes through "transplant tourism".

Scale and practice

The World Health Organization has estimated that a substantial minority of kidney transplants worldwide involve commercial transactions. Brokers recruit sellers, arrange travel and surgery, and take a large share of the payment; sellers frequently report receiving far less than promised, along with poor aftercare and lasting health problems, while recipients face risks from unregulated surgery and inadequate screening of organs.

Law and policy

Most countries prohibit the sale of organs, following principles such as the US National Organ Transplant Act (1984) and the WHO Guiding Principles on Human Cell, Tissue and Organ Transplantation. The Declaration of Istanbul (2008, revised 2018) defines and calls for the elimination of organ trafficking and transplant tourism. Iran is the notable exception, operating a regulated system of compensated living kidney donation, while debates continue elsewhere about whether regulated incentives could expand supply without exploitation.
