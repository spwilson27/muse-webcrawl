Geisha

OccupationTraditional Japanese performing artist and hostess
RegionsKyoto (Gion), Tokyo, Kanazawa, and other hanamachi
TermsGeiko (Kyoto), maiko (apprentice)

Geisha (芸者), also known as geiko (芸子) in Kyoto dialect, are female Japanese performing artists and traditional hostesses whose skills include classical music, dance, games, and conversation. Geisha emerged in the 18th century from earlier female performers and courtesan cultures, and became established as artists distinct from the oiran courtesans of the pleasure quarters. Their role is to entertain guests at banquets (ozashiki) in teahouses (ochaya) and traditional inns.

Training

A young woman typically begins as an apprentice (shikomi, then maiko in Kyoto) living in a geisha house (okiya) under an "older sister" mentor. Training in shamisen, singing, classical dance, tea ceremony, and the arts of conversation can take around five years before debut (misedashi) as a full geisha. Apprentices are recognised by their elaborate hairstyles with ornaments (kanzashi), long dangling obi sashes, and tall wooden clogs (okobo).

Appearance

Geisha are known for formal kimono, white face makeup (oshiroi), and traditional coiffures. Fully formal white makeup and the most elaborate dress are now worn mainly by apprentices and for special performances; senior geisha dress more simply, often without white makeup for everyday engagements.

Modern status

Numbers have declined greatly since the profession's peak in the early 20th century, when thousands worked across Japan; today the active geisha communities number perhaps a thousand or fewer, concentrated in Kyoto's Gion district and the geisha districts (hanamachi) of Tokyo and other cities. The profession has adapted with public dance performances such as Kyoto's Miyako Odori, which make the art accessible to general audiences.
