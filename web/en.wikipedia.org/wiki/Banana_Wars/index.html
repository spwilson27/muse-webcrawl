Banana Wars

Date1898–1934
LocationCentral America and the Caribbean
ParticipantsUnited States; governments and insurgents in Cuba, Honduras, Nicaragua, Haiti, the Dominican Republic, Mexico and Panama

The Banana Wars were a series of conflicts and military interventions carried out by the United States in Central America and the Caribbean between the end of the Spanish–American War in 1898 and the adoption of the Good Neighbor policy in 1934. The interventions were driven by a mixture of strategic concerns — especially protecting the approaches to the Panama Canal — and commercial interests, notably those of American fruit companies such as the United Fruit Company and the Standard Fruit Company, whose banana and sugar operations gave the conflicts their nickname.

Interventions

United States forces intervened repeatedly in Honduras (1903, 1907, 1911, 1912, 1919, 1920, 1924 and 1925) and occupied Nicaragua from 1912 to 1933, fighting the insurgency of Augusto César Sandino. The U.S. occupied Haiti from 1915 to 1934 and the Dominican Republic from 1916 to 1924, and it intervened militarily in Cuba and Mexico, including the occupation of Veracruz in 1914 and the Punitive Expedition of 1916–17.

Consequences

The occupations reshaped local institutions: American-trained national guards in Nicaragua, Haiti and the Dominican Republic later became the foundations of dictatorial regimes. The Marine Corps codified its experience in the Small Wars Manual (1940). Growing resentment in Latin America, and criticism at home by veterans such as Major General Smedley Butler, contributed to President Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy, which renounced armed intervention in the Americas.
