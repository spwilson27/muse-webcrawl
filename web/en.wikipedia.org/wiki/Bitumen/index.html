Bitumen is an immensely viscous, black, sticky, fully soluble in carbon disulfide, liquid or semi-solid form of petroleum. In North American usage the material is commonly called asphalt, while "asphalt" elsewhere more often refers to the mixture of bitumen with aggregate used for paving. Bitumen may be found in natural deposits or obtained as the residue of petroleum distillation.

History

Bitumen has been used since prehistory as an adhesive, sealant and waterproofing agent. Ancient Mesopotamian peoples used it to waterproof boats and buildings and as mortar; it occurs naturally at seeps such as those around the Dead Sea (called Slimepits in the Book of Genesis) and in the famous Pitch Lake of Trinidad. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, refined bitumen became the standard binder for road construction.

Composition and production

Chemically, bitumen is a complex mixture of high-molecular-weight hydrocarbons, conventionally fractionated into saturates, aromatics, resins and asphaltenes. Most commercial bitumen is produced in oil refineries as the heaviest fraction of crude oil, remaining after lighter products such as petrol and diesel are distilled. Natural bitumen impregnates the Athabasca oil sands of Canada, the world's largest known deposit.

Uses

The overwhelming majority of bitumen is used in road construction, where it binds aggregate in asphalt concrete, and in roofing felts and waterproofing membranes. Smaller amounts are used in paints, coatings and soundproofing. Aged bitumen can be recycled by reprocessing old road surfaces.
