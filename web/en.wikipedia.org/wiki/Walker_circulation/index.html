Walker circulation

The Walker circulation, also known as the Walker cell, is a conceptual model of the air flow in the troposphere over the tropical Pacific Ocean. It is named after physicist Sir Gilbert Walker, who discovered in the early 20th century that atmospheric pressure at sea level oscillates between the eastern and western tropical Pacific — the phenomenon known as the Southern Oscillation.

Schematic cross-section of the Walker circulation

Mechanism

Under normal conditions, the western Pacific near Indonesia is covered by warm surface water, low pressure, convection, cloudiness, and heavy rainfall, while the eastern Pacific near South America has cooler water, high pressure, and dry, stable conditions. Air rises in the west, flows eastward high in the troposphere, sinks over the eastern Pacific, and returns westward at the surface as the trade winds. This overturning loop is closed by the ocean: the trade winds drive westward surface currents and upwelling of cold water in the east, maintaining the temperature contrast. Jacob Bjerknes proposed in 1969 that the atmosphere and ocean reinforce each other in this coupled system.

El Niño and La Niña

During an El Niño event, the trade winds weaken, the Walker circulation slows and shifts eastward, warm water spreads across the central and eastern Pacific, and convection and rainfall move with it, bringing floods to coastal Peru and drought to Indonesia and Australia. During La Niña, the circulation intensifies beyond normal. Together these phases make up the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), the dominant source of interannual climate variability worldwide.

Other oceans

Similar but weaker zonal overturning cells exist over the tropical Atlantic and Indian Oceans, sometimes called Walker-type circulations, and they interact with the Pacific cell through the global atmosphere.
