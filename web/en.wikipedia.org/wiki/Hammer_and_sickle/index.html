Hammer and sickle

The hammer and sickle (☭) is a symbol of proletarian solidarity that was first adopted during the Russian Revolution of 1917 and has since become the most widely recognised emblem of communism. The hammer represents industrial workers and the sickle represents the peasantry; together they symbolise the alliance of workers and farmers under which communist movements sought to build a classless society.

Origin

Crossed tools as emblems of labour predate the revolution, but the specific combination of hammer and sickle was developed in revolutionary Russia in 1917–1918. A design by the artist Yevgeny Kamzolkin, featuring a hammer and sickle with a sword, won the competition for the seal of the new Soviet state in 1918, though Vladimir Lenin removed the sword, and the emblem without it was adopted. The symbol appeared on the flag and coat of arms of the Soviet Union: the state emblem, adopted in 1923, showed the hammer and sickle over a globe framed by ears of wheat, while the national flag bore the gold hammer and sickle beneath a star in the canton of a red field.

Use

Communist parties and states around the world adopted the hammer and sickle in their flags, emblems, and iconography, including the Chinese Communist Party, whose flag still bears it. It appears, often modified, in the symbols of parties in Europe, Asia, Africa, and Latin America, and is encoded in Unicode as U+262D (☭).

Legal status

Since the fall of the Soviet Union, display of the hammer and sickle has been restricted or banned in several countries, including Ukraine, Lithuania, Latvia, Poland, Georgia, and Moldova at various times, and Indonesia bans communist symbols generally; such laws typically group it with Nazi symbols as an emblem of a totalitarian regime. In much of the rest of the world it remains a lawful symbol of legal communist and labour movements.
