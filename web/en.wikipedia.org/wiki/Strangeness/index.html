In particle physics, strangeness (denoted S) is a property of particles, expressed as a quantum number, introduced to explain the "strangely" long lifetimes of certain hadrons — such as kaons and hyperons — that were produced copiously in high-energy collisions but decayed far more slowly than expected for particles of their mass.

Definition

Strangeness is defined in terms of the numbers of strange quarks (ns) and strange antiquarks (ns̄) in a particle:

S = −( ns − ns̄ )

Thus a particle composed of a single strange quark has strangeness −1. The convention is that the flavour quantum numbers of a particle carry the same sign as its electric charge pattern established by Murray Gell-Mann and Kazuhiko Nishijima in the Gell-Mann–Nishijima formula, Q = Iz + (B + S)/2, which relates charge, isospin, baryon number, and strangeness.

Conservation

Strangeness is conserved in strong and electromagnetic interactions but not in weak interactions. Consequently, the lightest particle carrying strangeness (the kaon) cannot decay by the strong or electromagnetic force and must instead decay weakly, which proceeds slowly — explaining the anomalously long lifetimes that puzzled physicists in the 1940s and 1950s. In strong interactions, strangeness production always occurs in pairs (for example, a kaon K⁺ with S = +1 produced together with a hyperon Λ with S = −1), a phenomenon historically called "associated production".

History

The concept was introduced by Murray Gell-Mann and Abraham Pais in the early 1950s to account for the behaviour of the newly discovered "strange particles" (the V particles seen in cloud chambers, later identified as kaons and Lambda baryons). With the quark model of 1964, strangeness was understood as a count of strange quarks, and the concept was later generalised to other flavour quantum numbers such as charm and bottomness.
