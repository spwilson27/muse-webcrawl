California condor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scientific name: Gymnogyps californianus
Wingspan: up to 3.0 m (9.8 ft)
Conservation status: Critically Endangered

The California condor (Gymnogyps californianus) is a New World vulture and the largest North American land bird. This condor became extinct in the wild in 1987 when all remaining wild individuals were captured, but trials and conservation efforts have since reintroduced populations in California, Arizona, Utah and Baja California, Mexico.

Description

The California condor has black plumage with striking white patches under the wings and a bald head whose skin colour varies from yellowish to bright red depending on the bird's emotional state—an adaptation for feeding inside large carcasses. With a wingspan of up to 3 metres and weight of up to 12 kg, it soars on rising air currents for hours with hardly a wingbeat, travelling up to 250 km in a day in search of carrion.

Decline and recovery

Once ranging across North America, the species declined drastically from poisoning (chiefly lead fragments in carcasses), poaching, habitat destruction and DDT. By 1987 only 27 individuals remained, all brought into captive breeding at the San Diego and Los Angeles zoos. Reintroduction began in 1992; the first wild-hatched chick of the recovery fledged in 2003. The population passed 500 birds (wild and captive combined) in 2019, though lead poisoning remains the principal obstacle to a self-sustaining population.

Behaviour

Condors are scavengers, feeding mainly on the carcasses of large mammals. They are long-lived—up to 60 years—and slow to reproduce: pairs raise a single chick roughly every other year, with young dependent on their parents for many months. The species holds an important place in the traditions of several Native American peoples of the West.
