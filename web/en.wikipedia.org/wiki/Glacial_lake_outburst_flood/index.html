A glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) is a sudden, catastrophic release of water from a lake dammed by a glacier or by its moraine. In Iceland such floods are called jökulhlaups. GLOFs can discharge millions of cubic metres of water and sediment within hours, devastating valleys tens of kilometres downstream.

Mechanisms

Outbursts occur when a dam fails or is overtopped: triggers include the collapse or piping of an ice-cored moraine dam, a rock or ice avalanche plunging into the lake and generating a displacement wave, heavy melt or rainfall raising the lake level, or subglacial volcanic eruptions melting an ice cap. Lakes that form beneath or within a glacier may drain periodically through subglacial channels.

Risk and examples

Retreating glaciers under climate change are forming and enlarging thousands of glacial lakes, particularly in the Himalayas and the Andes, where outburst floods have repeatedly destroyed villages, bridges and hydropower plants — for example the 1941 Huaraz flood in Peru and the 2023 South Lhonak Lake flood in Sikkim, India. Past megafloods from ice-age lakes, such as the Missoula floods, reshaped whole landscapes. Monitoring, early-warning systems, and artificial lowering of dangerous lakes are the principal mitigation measures.
