Lothar Rendulic (23 November 1887 – 18 January 1971) was an Austrian-born German general during the Second World War, who rose to the rank of Generaloberst and commanded army groups on the Eastern Front and in the Balkans. He was tried after the war in the Hostages Trial at Nuremberg, and his case gave rise to the so-called "Rendulic rule" in the law of armed conflict concerning military necessity judged from the commander's perspective.

Career

Rendulic, of Croatian descent, was commissioned in the Austro-Hungarian Army, served in the First World War, and afterwards studied law, earning a doctorate. He served in the Austrian Bundesheer and joined the German Wehrmacht after the Anschluss of 1938.

In the Second World War he commanded divisions and corps in Yugoslavia and on the Eastern Front, took command of the 2nd Panzer Army in the Balkans (1943–44), and then of the 20th Mountain Army in Lapland, where he directed the German withdrawal from Finland — a retreat accompanied by scorched-earth destruction of much of Finnish Lapland, including the burning of Rovaniemi. In 1945 he successively commanded Army Group Courland, the renamed Army Group South, and Army Group Ostmark in the war's final weeks.

Hostages Trial and the Rendulic rule

Rendulic was a defendant in the Hostages Trial (1947–48), one of the Subsequent Nuremberg trials, facing charges over reprisal killings of hostages in Yugoslavia and the devastation of Finnmark and Lapland in the retreat from Norway and Finland. He was acquitted on the scorched-earth charge: the tribunal held that a commander must be judged on the conditions and information as they appeared to him at the time, and that Rendulic could honestly have believed military necessity required the destruction. This standard — that a commander's assessment is judged on the facts reasonably available to him, not on hindsight — is known in international humanitarian law as the Rendulic rule. He was nevertheless convicted on other counts and sentenced to 20 years, later reduced; he was released in 1951.

Later life

After release Rendulic wrote on military and political questions and died in Austria in 1971.
