The Muhajirun (Arabic: المهاجرون, "the Emigrants") were the early followers of the Islamic prophet Muhammad who emigrated with him from Mecca to Medina — the event known as the Hijrah (622 CE), which marks the beginning of the Islamic calendar. In Medina they were paired in brotherhood with the local converts, the Ansar.

The emigrations

Before the great migration to Medina, persecuted Meccan Muslims had already made two emigrations to the Christian kingdom of Abyssinia (c. 613–615), where the Negus granted them protection; those exiles are also often counted among the Muhajirun. Emigration to Medina began in earnest after the pledges of al-Aqaba, when Muslims left Mecca in small groups during 622, culminating in the departure of Muhammad himself and Abu Bakr.

Status in Islam

The Qur'an praises the Muhajirun together with the Ansar as the foremost believers: "The first to embrace Islam of the Muhajirun and the Ansar and those who followed them in goodness — God is pleased with them" (9:100). They left homes and property behind, and Muhammad instituted formal bonds of brotherhood (mu'akhah) between individual emigrants and Medinan helpers, who shared wealth and housing with them. The Muhajirun formed the core of the early caliphate's leadership: the first four caliphs — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali — were all Meccan emigrants, as were most of the "ten promised Paradise."

Later usage

The term has been reused in later history for other Muslim emigrant communities — notably the Muhajirs of Pakistan, Urdu-speaking Muslims who migrated from India after Partition in 1947 — and occurs in the names of some modern movements.
