Hemangioblastoma

Hemangioblastomas are benign (WHO grade 1) vascular tumours of the central nervous system that most often arise in the cerebellum, brainstem and spinal cord. They account for roughly 1–2.5% of all intracranial tumours. About three quarters occur sporadically, typically in middle-aged adults, while up to about a quarter are associated with the inherited disorder von Hippel–Lindau disease (VHL), in which patients may develop multiple tumours.

Presentation

Symptoms depend on location and include headache, nausea, dizziness, gait disturbance (ataxia) and, for spinal lesions, sensory or motor deficits. A classic radiological appearance is a cyst with an enhancing mural nodule ("cyst with a nodule"). Because the tumours can secrete erythropoietin, some patients develop secondary polycythemia with elevated red cell counts.

Pathology

Under the microscope, hemangioblastomas consist of abundant thin-walled capillaries interspersed with large vacuolated "stromal" cells; the stromal cells are the neoplastic component and in VHL-associated cases show loss of function of the VHL tumour suppressor on chromosome 3p, stabilising hypoxia-inducible factors and driving expression of VEGF. Similar tumours occur in the retina (retinal capillary hemangioblastomas, historically called angiomas) in VHL.

Treatment

Complete surgical resection is usually curative for sporadic tumours, although their rich vascularity can make surgery demanding; preoperative embolisation is sometimes used. Stereotactic radiosurgery is an option for small, deep or recurrent lesions. Patients with VHL require lifelong surveillance imaging and multidisciplinary care.
