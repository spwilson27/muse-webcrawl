Stirling Moss

Sir Stirling Craufurd Moss OBE (17 September 1929 – 12 April 2020) was a British Formula One racing driver. An inductee into the International Motorsports Hall of Fame, he won 212 of the 529 races he entered across several categories of competition and has been described as "the greatest driver never to win the World Championship". In a seven-year span between 1955 and 1961, Moss finished the championship runner-up four times and third the other three.

Early career

Moss was born in West Kensington, London, the son of Alfred Moss, a dentist and amateur racing driver who had competed in the Indianapolis 500. Stirling began racing in 1948 in a Cooper 500, funded partly by his father's support and his own work as a waiter. He quickly demonstrated exceptional versatility, winning in Formula Three, rallies, hill climbs, and sports cars.

Formula One

Moss made his Formula One debut in 1951 and first came to prominence with Maserati in 1954. In 1955 he joined Mercedes-Benz alongside Juan Manuel Fangio, winning his first Grand Prix at Aintree — the first Briton to win the British Grand Prix. When Mercedes withdrew, Moss drove for Maserati and then Vanwall. In 1958 he lost the championship to Mike Hawthorn by a single point, having famously defended Hawthorn against a penalty that would have handed Moss the title — a landmark act of sportsmanship. Later drives for Cooper, Lotus, and Ferrari-powered privateer entries included a celebrated victory at Monaco in 1961, holding off far faster Ferraris in an underpowered Lotus.

Sports cars and the Mille Miglia

Moss's most famous single drive came in the 1955 Mille Miglia, which he won with navigator Denis Jenkinson in a Mercedes-Benz 300 SLR, covering nearly 1,000 miles of Italian public roads at an average speed of 97.96 mph — a record never broken before the race was discontinued. He also won the RAC Tourist Trophy multiple times, the Targa Florio (1955), and the 12 Hours of Sebring.

Accident and later life

On Easter Monday 1962, Moss crashed heavily at Goodwood, suffering injuries that left him in a coma for a month. Though he recovered, a test session in 1963 convinced him his instincts had dulled, and he retired from top-level racing. He remained a fixture of historic racing and motorsport commentary for the rest of his life, was knighted in 2000, and retired from even historic racing at age 81 in 2011. He died in London on 12 April 2020, aged 90.
