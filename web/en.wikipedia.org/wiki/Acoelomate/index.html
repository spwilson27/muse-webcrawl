Acoelomate

An acoelomate is an animal that lacks a coelom — a fluid-filled body cavity lying between the gut and the body wall and lined with mesoderm. In acoelomates, the space between the digestive tract and the outer body wall is filled with mesenchyme (parenchyma), a tissue of loosely packed cells. Because they have no body cavity, acoelomates have no space for the development of large, suspended internal organs, and most are small and flattened.

Examples

The classic acoelomate group is the phylum Platyhelminthes (flatworms), including free-living planarians and parasitic tapeworms and flukes. Other acoelomate or near-acoelomate animals include the acoels and nemertodermatids (now grouped in Xenacoelomorpha), gnathostomulids, and gastrotrichs in the traditional textbook scheme.

Consequences of the body plan

Without a coelom to act as a hydrostatic skeleton or a circulatory space, acoelomates rely on diffusion and on the movements of the gut and cilia to distribute materials, which constrains body thickness — flatworms are famously thin, and none of their cells is far from either the gut or the surface. Acoelomates also lack an anus in many groups (the gut is a blind sac in most flatworms), and they have no separate circulatory or (usually) respiratory organs.

Classification history

Textbooks traditionally divided bilaterians into acoelomates, pseudocoelomates, and coelomates, implying an evolutionary progression. Modern phylogenetics shows that body-cavity types have been gained and lost repeatedly, and the acoel condition is not a single clade; xenacoelomorphs are now thought to be among the earliest-branching bilaterians, while flatworms belong to the Spiralia (Lophotrochozoa).
