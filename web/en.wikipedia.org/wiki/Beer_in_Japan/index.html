Beer in Japan accounts for the majority of alcohol consumed in the country by volume. Beer was introduced by Dutch traders during the Edo period and domestic brewing began in the Meiji era; today the market is dominated by four large brewers — Asahi, Kirin, Sapporo and Suntory — whose crisp pale lagers, above all Asahi Super Dry, define the Japanese style.

History

Dutch merchants at Dejima, Nagasaki, served beer to traders from the 17th century. In 1869 the American-trained William Copeland founded the Spring Valley Brewery in Yokohama, a forerunner of Kirin. Sapporo Breweries traces its origin to the Kaitakushi Brewery founded in Hokkaido in 1876 with German-trained brewer Seibei Nakagawa. Beer became a mass drink in the 20th century, overtaking sake consumption in the post-war period.

Market and taxation categories

Japanese liquor tax law long taxed beer according to malt content, producing two lower-tax categories: happoshu ("sparkling alcohol", under 67% malt or using adjuncts) and "third beer" (shin janru), beer-like beverages made from pea or corn protein or blends with spirits. Tax reforms in 2020 began phasing these categories toward a unified beer tax by 2026. Draft beer in bars is served meticulously with a creamy foam head, and canned beer is sold in distinctive seasonal packaging.

Craft beer

Deregulation in 1994 lowered the minimum brewing licence volume, sparking the ji-bīru (local beer) boom. After an initial wave of sweet, novelty brews, a mature craft sector emerged with breweries such as Yo-Ho Brewing (Yona Yona Ale) and Kiuchi Brewery (Hitachino Nest), winning international awards and exporting widely.

Culture

Beer is the default first drink at social gatherings (nomikai), ordered "toriaezu bīru" — "beer for now" — and poured for others from shared bottles or pitchers. Summer beer gardens on department-store rooftops, vending machines, and train-station kiosks make beer omnipresent, though alcohol vending machines have been phased out.
