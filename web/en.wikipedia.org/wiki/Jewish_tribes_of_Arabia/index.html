The Jewish tribes of Arabia were Jewish communities organised along tribal lines that lived in the Arabian Peninsula from antiquity until the rise of Islam in the 7th century CE. They were prominent in the oases of the Hejaz—especially Yathrib (later Medina), Khaybar and Tayma—and in Yemen, where the Himyarite kingdom adopted a form of Judaism in the 4th–6th centuries.

History

Jewish settlement in Arabia may date to the destruction of the First or Second Temple and Roman-era migrations, though the origins of many tribes are obscure and some may have been Arab converts or proselytising communities. The Arabian Jewish tribes spoke Arabic, farmed date palms, worked as goldsmiths and merchants, and were known for their fortresses in the lava fields around Medina.

Major tribes

In Medina the three principal Jewish tribes were the Banu Qaynuqa (goldsmiths and market traders), the Banu Nadir and the Banu Qurayza (date farmers and landowners). Other significant communities lived at Khaybar, Fadak and Tayma in the north, and in Yemen under the Jewish-influenced Himyarite kings, most famously Dhu Nuwas.

Relations with Muhammad

After Muhammad's migration (Hijra) to Medina in 622 CE, the Constitution of Medina initially included the Jewish tribes in the city's confederation. Relations deteriorated amid the wars with Mecca: the Qaynuqa were expelled in 624, the Nadir in 625, and the Qurayza were destroyed after the Battle of the Trench in 627, events recorded in Islamic tradition and debated by historians. Khaybar surrendered in 628 on terms allowing its Jews to remain as tenant farmers; they were expelled from the Hejaz under Caliph Umar.

Legacy

Jewish communities persisted in Yemen for many centuries, forming the distinctive Teimani (Yemeni Jewish) tradition, most of whose members emigrated to Israel in 1949–50. The Arabian Jewish tribes are a significant subject in the study of late antique religion and the origins of Islam.
