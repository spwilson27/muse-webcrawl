Anesthetic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An anesthetic (anaesthetic in British English) is a drug that causes anaesthesia — a reversible loss of sensation. General anaesthetics act on the brain to produce unconsciousness; local anaesthetics block nerve conduction in a limited area so the patient feels no pain there while remaining conscious.

General anaesthetics

Inhaled agents include the modern fluorinated ethers sevoflurane, desflurane and isoflurane, and the gas nitrous oxide (in use since the 1840s and still common in dentistry and childbirth). Intravenous agents include propofol (the most widely used induction drug), etomidate, ketamine and barbiturates such as thiopental. Exactly how these chemically diverse drugs produce unconsciousness — largely by enhancing inhibitory GABA signalling and blocking excitatory pathways — was clarified only in recent decades.

Local anaesthetics

Local anaesthetics such as lidocaine, bupivacaine and the original cocaine block voltage-gated sodium channels in nerves, stopping pain signals. They are injected around nerves, into the spinal or epidural space, or applied to skin and mucous membranes; adrenaline is often added to prolong the block. Overdose can cause seizures and heart rhythm problems, so doses are carefully limited.
