Soviet–Afghan War

The Soviet–Afghan War was a protracted armed conflict fought in the Democratic Republic of Afghanistan from 1979 to 1989 between the Soviet Union and various forces, principally the Afghan mujahideen. The mujahideen were variously backed by the United States, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, China, and others as a Cold War proxy conflict. The Soviets deployed forces to prop up the communist government in Kabul, which faced widespread rebellion following the 1978 Saur Revolution. Unable to defeat the insurgency, the USSR withdrew under the 1988 Geneva Accords, completing its pullout in February 1989.

Background

In April 1978 the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan seized power and launched radical land and social reforms that provoked armed revolt across the countryside. Hafizullah Amin's purges destabilized the regime, and in December 1979 the USSR invaded, killing Amin and installing Babrak Karmal. The intervention triggered international condemnation, a boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics, and a massive covert aid program.

The war

Soviet forces, peaking at over 100,000 troops, controlled the cities and main roads but never the countryside. Mujahideen fighters exploited the mountainous terrain; Pakistan's ISI funneled weapons supplied under the CIA's Operation Cyclone, and from 1986 shoulder-fired Stinger anti-aircraft missiles blunted Soviet air superiority. Commanders such as Ahmad Shah Massoud in the Panjshir Valley became renowned resistance leaders.

Withdrawal and aftermath

Mikhail Gorbachev sought an exit, calling the war a "bleeding wound." Under the Geneva Accords of April 1988, the last Soviet troops crossed the Amu Darya on 15 February 1989. The Soviet-backed regime of Mohammad Najibullah survived until 1992, after which Afghanistan descended into civil war and eventually Taliban rule. The war contributed to the dissolution of the Soviet Union, displaced millions of refugees, and left a generation of battle-hardened Islamist fighters whose networks later formed al-Qaeda.
