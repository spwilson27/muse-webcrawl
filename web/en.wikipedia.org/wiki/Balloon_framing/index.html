Balloon framing

A balloon-framed house under construction

Balloon framing is a method of wood construction, also known as "Chicago construction", that emerged in the United States in the 1830s. It uses long, continuous vertical framing members (studs) that run the full height of the building, from the foundation sill to the roof plate, with floor joists hung from a ribbon board let into the studs.

History and characteristics

The technique replaced heavy timber framing with lighter, standardised sawn lumber joined by inexpensive machine-made nails, made possible by industrial sawmills and nail manufacture. It allowed rapid, relatively unskilled construction and drove the fast growth of cities such as Chicago. Detractors nicknamed the light frames "balloons" because they seemed flimsy, but the structures proved durable. The principal drawback is fire safety: the continuous wall cavities act as chimneys, spreading fire between floors, so fire blocking is required. From the mid-20th century, balloon framing was largely superseded by platform framing, in which each storey is built on its own floor platform, providing inherent fire stops and using shorter, more readily available studs.
