Cant (road and rail)

The cant of a railway track or road curve is the rate of change in elevation (height) between the two rails or edges of the roadway. Known as superelevation in railway engineering and as cross slope or banking on roads, cant allows vehicles to negotiate curves at speed by tilting them so that part of the centripetal force is provided by the normal reaction of the surface rather than by friction alone.

Principles

For a given curve radius and speed there is an equilibrium cant at which the resultant force is perpendicular to the running surface. Trains running faster than the equilibrium speed exhibit cant deficiency — partly compensated on tilting trains — while slower trains exhibit cant excess. Maximum cant is limited (typically around 150–180 mm on standard-gauge lines) by stability for slow or stationary freight and by passenger comfort.

Roads

On highways the equivalent superelevation is expressed as a cross-slope rate, commonly up to about 8–12 percent on sharp curves in cold climates less, to prevent slow vehicles sliding inward on ice. Transition curves and superelevation runoff sections introduce cant gradually to avoid abrupt steering or load shifts.
