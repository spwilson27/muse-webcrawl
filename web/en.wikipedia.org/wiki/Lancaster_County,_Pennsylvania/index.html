Lancaster County, sometimes known as the "Garden Spot of America," is a county in the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, United States. Its county seat is the city of Lancaster. The county is known for its fertile farmland, its Pennsylvania Dutch heritage, and one of the largest Amish populations in the United States.

History

Lancaster County was created on May 10, 1729, from part of Chester County; it was the fourth county established in Pennsylvania. It was named by settler John Wright after his home city of Lancaster in England. Several counties were later carved out of its original territory. During the American Revolutionary War, Lancaster city briefly served as the capital of the United States for one day on September 27, 1777, when the Continental Congress fled Philadelphia, and it was Pennsylvania's capital from 1799 to 1812.

Pennsylvania Dutch Country

The county is the heart of Pennsylvania Dutch Country. German-speaking immigrants — including Mennonites and the Amish, who first settled here in the 1720s — shaped its farming culture. Lancaster County's Amish community, numbering tens of thousands, is among the largest in the world, and its horse-drawn buggies, covered bridges, and quilt and farmers-market traditions attract millions of tourists annually. The Central Market in Lancaster city, founded in the 1730s, is among the oldest continuously operating farmers markets in the United States.

Geography and economy

The county borders the Susquehanna River to the west. Its rich limestone soils make it one of the most productive non-irrigated agricultural counties in the United States, leading the nation in production of eggs and other farm goods; it was also the first county in the nation to adopt a farmland preservation program (through the Lancaster Farmland Trust and county easements). Alongside agriculture, tourism, manufacturing, and health care are major employers.
