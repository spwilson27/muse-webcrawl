Delayed gratification
Key studyStanford marshmallow experiment (1972)
Key researcherWalter Mischel

Delayed gratification, or deferred gratification, is the ability to resist the temptation of an immediate reward in favour of a larger or more enduring reward later. It is studied as a component of self-control and emotional intelligence, and is related to the economic concept of time preference.

The marshmallow experiment

In studies conducted at Stanford University around 1970, psychologist Walter Mischel offered children a choice between one small reward (such as a marshmallow) immediately, or two rewards if they waited alone in the room for about fifteen minutes. Follow-up studies reported that children who waited longer tended to have better later-life outcomes, including higher educational attainment and healthier body-mass indices.

Reassessment

Later replications, notably a 2018 study by Watts, Duncan, and Quan, found the predictive effect much reduced once family background and socioeconomic environment were controlled, suggesting that the ability to delay reflects a child's circumstances and trust that the promised reward will arrive, as much as an innate trait. Experiments show that children who have experienced unreliable adults rationally choose the immediate reward.

Mechanisms

Mischel proposed a "hot/cool" model: impulsive "hot" processing focuses on the reward's attractive features, while "cool" cognitive strategies — distraction, mentally transforming the reward — enable waiting. The capacity develops through childhood alongside the prefrontal cortex.
