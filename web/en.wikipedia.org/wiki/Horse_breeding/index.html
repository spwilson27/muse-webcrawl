Horse breeding

Horse breeding is reproduction in horses, and particularly the human-directed process of selective breeding of animals, particularly purebred horses of a given breed. It involves knowledge of genetics, animal husbandry, and veterinary reproduction. Planned matings can be used to produce specifically desired characteristics in domesticated horses.

Reproduction

Mares are seasonal breeders, coming into estrus ("heat") roughly every 21 days during spring and summer, with each heat lasting about 5–7 days. Gestation lasts approximately 11 months (320–360 days), usually resulting in a single foal. Breeding may be by natural cover — live cover in hand or pasture breeding — or by artificial insemination with fresh, cooled, or frozen semen, though some registries, notably for Thoroughbreds, require live cover.

Breeding systems

Breeders use several mating strategies:

  • Inbreeding and linebreeding: mating related individuals to concentrate desirable traits, at the risk of exposing harmful recessive genes.
  • Outcrossing: mating unrelated individuals to increase genetic diversity and vigor.
  • Crossbreeding: mating horses of different breeds to combine traits, producing types such as warmblood sport horses.

The stud farm

A stud farm is an establishment for selective breeding of livestock, in this context horses, centered on one or more stallions standing at stud for a fee. Bloodstock agents, pedigree analysts, and auction houses at sales such as Keeneland and Tattersalls form a substantial industry, especially in Thoroughbred racing, where horses such as Galileo and Frankel commanded enormous covering fees.

History

Horses were domesticated around 3500 BC on the Eurasian steppe. Systematic stud books began with works such as the General Stud Book (1791) for Thoroughbreds. Arabian, Thoroughbred, and various warmblood registries maintain closed or open studbooks that define breed membership today.
