House of Welf

The House of Welf (also Guelf or Guelph) is a European dynasty that has included many German and British monarchs from the 11th to 20th centuries, together with a number of Italian noble families. One of the oldest noble houses in Europe, it traces its origins to the Swabian count Welf I in the 9th century.

Rise in Germany

The Elder House of Welf produced dukes of Bavaria and the kings of Burgundy; its heiress brought the family estates to the House of Este in Italy. The Younger House, descending through the Este line, rose to prominence in Saxony: Welf I became Duke of Bavaria in 1070, and Henry the Lion (1129–1195), Duke of Saxony and Bavaria, was the most powerful prince of the Empire before his fall from favour with Emperor Frederick Barbarossa in 1180. Henry's son Otto IV became Holy Roman Emperor, the only Welf to hold the imperial crown. The family's rivalry with the Hohenstaufen gave the Italian factions their names: Guelphs (papal party) and Ghibellines (imperial party).

Hanover and Britain

The Welfs retained Brunswick-Lüneburg in northern Germany. Ernest Augustus, Duke of Brunswick-Lüneburg, became Elector of Hanover in 1692; his son ascended the British throne in 1714 as George I, because his mother Sophia, Electress of Hanover, was heiress presumptive under the Act of Settlement. The personal union of Britain and Hanover lasted until 1837, when Hanover's Salic law separated the crowns at the accession of Queen Victoria. Hanover was annexed by Prussia in 1866, and the dispossessed Welfs remained in exile; the last reigning Welf duke, Ernest Augustus of Brunswick, abdicated in 1918. The head of the house today is Ernst August, Prince of Hanover.
