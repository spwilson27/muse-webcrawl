Electromagnetic induction is the production of an electromotive force (voltage) across an electrical conductor in a changing magnetic field. It was discovered independently by Michael Faraday in 1831 and Joseph Henry in 1832. Faraday was the first to publish the results of his experiments, in which he found that moving a magnet through a coil of wire, or changing the current in a neighbouring coil, induced a current in the wire.

Faraday's law

Faraday's law states that the induced electromotive force in a closed circuit is equal in magnitude to the rate of change of the magnetic flux through the circuit, and — as expressed by Lenz's law — in a direction such that the current it drives opposes the change in flux that produced it. For a coil of N turns, ε = −N dΦ/dt. This law, together with Maxwell's equations, underlies much of electrical engineering.

Applications

Electromagnetic induction is the operating principle of electric generators, which convert mechanical rotation into alternating current by spinning coils within magnetic fields, and of transformers, which transfer energy between circuits through a shared changing flux and thereby step voltages up or down. It is also used in induction motors, induction cooktops, wireless charging, magnetic braking, guitar pickups, and metal detectors.

Eddy currents

Changing magnetic fields also induce circulating currents — eddy currents — within solid conductors. These are exploited for induction heating and non-destructive testing, but in transformer cores they waste energy as heat, which is why cores are laminated into thin insulated sheets.

History and theory

Faraday's qualitative account was put into mathematical form by Franz Ernst Neumann and later incorporated by James Clerk Maxwell into his theory of electromagnetism; Faraday's law appears as one of Maxwell's equations. Albert Einstein noted that the induction effect depends only on the relative motion of magnet and conductor, a symmetry that helped lead him to special relativity.
