Hypercapnia

Hypercapnia (from the Greek hyper = "above" or "too much" and kapnos = "smoke"), also known as hypercarbia, is a condition of abnormally elevated carbon dioxide (CO2) levels in the blood. Carbon dioxide is a gaseous product of the body's metabolism and is normally expelled through the lungs. Hypercapnia can result from diseases that impair ventilation, from reduced respiratory drive, or from breathing air with an abnormally high CO2 content, as in rebreather diving accidents and enclosed environments.

Hypercapnia arises when alveolar ventilation fails to clear metabolic CO2.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on the rate of rise and the absolute level of CO2. Mild hypercapnia may cause headache, confusion, lethargy, flushed skin, muscle twitching and a bounding pulse. As levels rise further, drowsiness progresses to stupor and coma (CO2 narcosis), and respiratory acidosis develops. Chronic hypercapnia, as seen in advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, is often partly compensated by renal retention of bicarbonate.

Causes

Causes include hypoventilation from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, severe asthma, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, neuromuscular diseases, chest wall deformities, central nervous system depression from opioids or sedatives, and increased CO2 production combined with a fixed minute ventilation. In diving, skip breathing, faulty rebreather scrubbers and excessive dead space can cause dangerous CO2 retention.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause and at improving alveolar ventilation, escalating from supplemental oxygen (titrated carefully in chronic retainers) to non-invasive ventilation and, when necessary, intubation and mechanical ventilation.
