Milton Ernest "Robert" Rauschenberg (October 22, 1925 – May 12, 2008) was an American painter and graphic artist whose early works anticipated the pop art movement. Rauschenberg is well known for his Combines (1954–64), a group of artworks which incorporated everyday objects as art materials and which blurred the distinctions between painting and sculpture.

Early life and education

Rauschenberg was born in Port Arthur, Texas, to fundamentalist Christian parents. He studied pharmacology at the University of Texas at Austin before being drafted into the United States Navy in 1944, where he worked as a neuropsychiatric technician. While stationed in California he visited the Huntington Library and saw his first original paintings, Thomas Gainsborough's The Blue Boy and Sir Thomas Lawrence's Pinkie, which inspired him to pursue art.

After the war he studied at the Kansas City Art Institute and in Paris at the Académie Julian, then enrolled at Black Mountain College in North Carolina, where he studied under Josef Albers and met composer John Cage, dancer Merce Cunningham, and artist Cy Twombly. He also studied at the Art Students League of New York.

Early work

Rauschenberg's early 1950s works include the White Paintings (1951), the Black Paintings, and Erased de Kooning Drawing (1953), in which he erased a drawing by Willem de Kooning — with the older artist's permission — and exhibited the faintly marked sheet. In 1953 he and Cage created the Automobile Tire Print, made by driving a Model A Ford over sheets of paper.

Combines

From 1954 Rauschenberg developed his "Combines", hybrid works neither painting nor sculpture, incorporating found objects, photographs, fabric, newspaper and even stuffed animals. Famous examples include Bed (1955), made from his own quilt and pillow splashed with paint; Monogram (1955–59), featuring a stuffed Angora goat encircled by a tyre; and Canyon (1959), which includes a stuffed bald eagle. Together with his friend Jasper Johns, Rauschenberg was labelled a Neo-Dadaist for bringing everyday imagery back into art in reaction against abstract expressionism.

Later career

In 1962 Rauschenberg turned to silkscreen painting, transferring photographic images onto canvas; he won the Grand Prize for painting at the Venice Biennale in 1964, the first American artist to do so. He collaborated frequently with Cage and Cunningham, designing sets and costumes for the Merce Cunningham Dance Company, and in 1966 co-founded Experiments in Art and Technology (E.A.T.). His ROCI (Rauschenberg Overseas Culture Interchange) project of the 1980s took exhibitions and collaborations to ten countries. Late in life he worked mainly from his studio on Captiva Island, Florida.

Death and legacy

Rauschenberg died of heart failure on May 12, 2008, on Captiva Island. The Robert Rauschenberg Foundation, established in 1990, continues to promote his work and philanthropy. His openness to found materials, photography and chance profoundly influenced pop art, conceptual art and later generations of artists.
