Helmuth von Moltke the Elder

Helmuth Karl Bernhard Graf von Moltke (26 October 1800 – 24 April 1891), known as Moltke the Elder to distinguish him from his nephew Helmuth Johann Ludwig von Moltke, was a Prussian field marshal and chief of staff of the Prussian Army for thirty years (1857–1888). He is regarded as one of the great military strategists of the 19th century, the architect of Prussia's victories over Denmark, Austria and France that led to German unification, and the creator of a new, more modern method of directing armies in the field.

Career

Moltke was born in Parchim in Mecklenburg and began his service in the Danish army before transferring to Prussia in 1822. After training at the Kriegsakademie in Berlin, he served as an adviser to the Ottoman army in the 1830s, witnessing the Egyptian–Ottoman war, and joined the Prussian General Staff, becoming its chief in 1857 under Prince Regent (later King and Emperor) Wilhelm I. Working with Chancellor Otto von Bismarck and War Minister Albrecht von Roon, he reformed mobilisation and planning around railways, the telegraph and breech-loading arms.

Wars of unification

Moltke's campaigns executed Prussia's wars of unification: the defeat of Denmark in 1864, the seven-week victory over Austria sealed at Königgrätz (Sadowa) in 1866, and the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, in which French armies were encircled at Metz and Sedan, Napoleon III captured, and Paris besieged. On 18 January 1871 the German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles. Moltke was made a count and field marshal and remained chief of staff until 1888, when he retired at the age of 87.

Military thought

Moltke emphasised detailed peacetime planning combined with decentralised execution — subordinates were given missions and intent rather than minute orders, an approach later called Auftragstaktik. His dictum that "no plan of operations extends with certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main strength" (often shortened to "no plan survives first contact with the enemy") captures his view of war's friction and uncertainty. His writings and the General Staff system influenced armies worldwide.
