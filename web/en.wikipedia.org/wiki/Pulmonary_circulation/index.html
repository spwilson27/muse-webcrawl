Pulmonary circulation

The pulmonary circulation is the portion of the circulatory system which carries deoxygenated blood away from the right ventricle of the heart, to the lungs, and returns oxygenated blood to the left atrium and ventricle of the heart. The term pulmonary circulation is readily paired with the systemic circulation — the circuit that supplies the rest of the body. A separate system, the bronchial circulation, supplies blood to the tissue of the larger airways of the lung.

Course

Deoxygenated blood leaves the right ventricle through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary trunk, which divides into right and left pulmonary arteries — the only arteries in the adult body that carry deoxygenated blood. Within the lungs the arteries branch alongside the airways down to dense capillary networks surrounding the alveoli, where gas exchange occurs: carbon dioxide is released and oxygen absorbed. Oxygenated blood returns through four pulmonary veins (the only veins carrying oxygenated blood in the adult) to the left atrium.

Physiology

The pulmonary circuit receives the entire cardiac output but operates at much lower pressures and resistance than the systemic circuit. Uniquely, hypoxia causes pulmonary vasoconstriction (rather than dilation), diverting blood away from poorly ventilated regions of lung to better-ventilated ones — ventilation–perfusion matching. At birth, the first breaths dramatically reduce pulmonary vascular resistance and close the foetal shunts (foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus), converting the circulation to its adult pattern.

History

The pulmonary circulation was first described by the Arab physician Ibn al-Nafis in 1242, who rejected the Galenic idea of pores in the interventricular septum. In Europe, Michael Servetus (1553) and Realdo Colombo (1559) described the circuit independently, and William Harvey's De motu cordis (1628) established the circulation of the blood as a whole.

Disorders

Conditions affecting the pulmonary circulation include pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension, and the congenital heart defects that allow right-to-left shunting, as well as high-altitude pulmonary oedema.
