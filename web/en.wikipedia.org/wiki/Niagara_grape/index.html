Niagara grape

SpeciesVitis labrusca hybrid
OriginNiagara County, New York, 1868
ParentageConcord × Cassady
ColourPale green to white

The Niagara grape is a variety of the North American grape species Vitis labrusca, and is the leading green grape grown in the United States, as well as the principal source of most American white grape juice. It was created in Niagara County, New York, in 1868 by the grape breeders Claudius L. Hoag and Benjamin W. Clark, who crossed the Concord grape with the white Cassady grape. It was first sold commercially in 1882.

Characteristics

Niagara vines are vigorous and hardy, suited to the cool climates of the Great Lakes region, and are grown extensively in New York, Pennsylvania, Michigan, Ohio and Ontario, as well as in Brazil. The berries are large, round and pale green to golden white, borne in compact bunches. Like other labrusca varieties it has a "foxy" aroma — a musky, sweet flavour associated with wild American grapes — and is a slip-skin variety, meaning the skin separates easily from the pulp.

Uses

The grape is used mainly for white grape juice (it is the grape behind most American white grape juice, including that of Welch's), and also for inexpensive sweet and semi-sweet white wines, jellies and fresh table consumption. Wines made from Niagara are intensely aromatic and are typically finished sweet to balance the grape's pronounced flavour.
