Digital image processing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digital image processing is the use of digital computers to process digital images through algorithms. As a subcategory of digital signal processing, it allows a much wider range of operations than analogue processing and avoids problems such as the build-up of noise and distortion during processing. Tasks range from basic enhancement — contrast adjustment, noise reduction, sharpening — to restoration, segmentation, compression, and the preparation of images for analysis by computer vision systems and machine learning models.

History

The field began in the 1960s at institutions such as the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, MIT, and Bell Labs, driven by applications in satellite imagery, medical imaging, and character recognition. An early triumph was the computer enhancement of Moon photographs from the Ranger probes. The invention of the charge-coupled device (1969) and the fast development of cheaper computing in the 1970s and 1980s spread the techniques into industry, while the discrete cosine transform (1974) later became the basis of JPEG image compression. From the 2010s, deep learning — especially convolutional neural networks — transformed high-level tasks such as denoising, super-resolution, and segmentation.

Fundamental operations

  • Point operations: brightness/contrast adjustment, thresholding, gamma correction, histogram equalisation.
  • Spatial filtering: convolution with kernels for smoothing (Gaussian blur, median filtering), sharpening, and edge detection (Sobel, Prewitt, Laplacian).
  • Frequency-domain processing: filtering via the two-dimensional Fourier transform.
  • Morphological operations: erosion, dilation, opening, and closing for shape analysis.
  • Colour processing: conversion between colour spaces (RGB, HSV, CIE L*a*b*) and white balancing.
  • Geometric transformations: scaling, rotation, warping, and registration of images.
  • Compression: lossy (JPEG, WebP, AVIF) and lossless (PNG) encoding.

Applications

Digital image processing is foundational to medical imaging (CT, MRI, microscopy), remote sensing and earth observation, industrial inspection, astronomy, forensics, document scanning and OCR, facial recognition, and consumer photography — every smartphone photo is the output of an extensive processing pipeline. Its techniques overlap heavily with computer vision, which focuses on extracting information and decisions from images rather than producing enhanced images for human viewing.
