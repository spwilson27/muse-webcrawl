The Fischer indole synthesis is a chemical reaction that produces the aromatic heterocycle indole from a (substituted) phenylhydrazine and an aldehyde or ketone under acidic conditions. The reaction was discovered in 1883 by Emil Fischer, who first carried it out while working with phenylhydrazine and pyruvic acid. It remains one of the oldest and most widely used methods for preparing indoles, a class of compounds of great importance in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and dye chemistry.

Reaction mechanism

The reaction proceeds through the initial formation of a phenylhydrazone from the phenylhydrazine and the carbonyl compound. Under acid catalysis, the hydrazone tautomerizes to an enamine-like intermediate (an “ene-hydrazine”), which then undergoes a [3,3]-sigmatropic rearrangement analogous to the Cope rearrangement. The resulting di-imine cyclizes onto the aromatic ring, and loss of ammonia (through an aminal intermediate) furnishes the aromatic indole ring system.

Regioselectivity can be a concern when unsymmetrical ketones are used, since either of the two enolizable positions may participate, giving mixtures of 2- and 3-substituted indoles. With meta-substituted phenylhydrazines, cyclization may occur at either ortho position, again potentially giving isomeric products.

Variations and modifications

Numerous variants of the classical conditions have been developed. Lewis acids such as zinc chloride, boron trifluoride, and polyphosphoric acid can replace Brønsted acids, and the reaction can be performed under microwave irradiation or in ionic liquids. The Buchwald modification employs palladium catalysis to prepare the hydrazone intermediate from aryl halides and hydrazones, widening the scope of available starting materials. The related Japp–Klingemann reaction provides access to the hydrazones from β-keto esters and aryldiazonium salts. The Fischer synthesis has also been adapted to solid-phase and one-pot protocols, and enantioselective variants using chiral phosphoric acids have been reported.

Applications

The Fischer indole synthesis has been used in the industrial and laboratory preparation of numerous indole-containing drugs, including the antimigraine triptans such as sumatriptan, and in the total synthesis of natural products such as strychnine-related alkaloids and the antitumour agent vindoline precursors. It features prominently in the synthesis of indole alkaloids by the group of Fukuyama and others, demonstrating that a reaction discovered in the nineteenth century remains a cornerstone of heterocyclic chemistry.
