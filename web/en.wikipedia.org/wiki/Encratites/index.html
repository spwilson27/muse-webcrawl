Encratites

The Encratites ("self-controlled") were an ascetic second-century Christian sect — or rather a tendency found across several early Christian groups — characterised by severe abstinence: they forbade marriage, the eating of meat, and the drinking of wine. The movement is usually traced to the influence of Tatian, the Syrian author of the Diatessaron, who after the death of his teacher Justin Martyr (c. 165) adopted increasingly rigorist teachings in Mesopotamia.

Teachings and practice

Early church writers including Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Hippolytus describe the Encratites as rejecting marriage as corruption and abstaining from animal food and wine; some substituted water for wine even in the Eucharist and were accordingly nicknamed Aquarii or Hydroparastatae. Their outlook overlapped with strands of Gnosticism and with the apocryphal Acts of the Apostles, in which apostles preach continence. The orthodox writers treated Encratism as a heresy because it condemned created things — marriage, meat, and wine — as evil in themselves, though moderate asceticism remained mainstream in early Christianity.

Legacy

Encratite ideas persisted in Syria and Mesopotamia and influenced later movements, including elements of early monasticism and, arguably, Manichaean elect practice. The term is used by historians more broadly for the strong continent tendency within second- and third-century Christianity.
