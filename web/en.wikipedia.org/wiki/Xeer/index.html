Xeer

Xeer (pronounced [ħeːɾ]), also spelled heer, is the traditional legal system of Somalia and the wider Horn of Africa region. Under the system, elders (odayaal) serve as mediators and judges, applying customary law developed over centuries within and between Somali clans.

Principles

Xeer is polycentric: there is no single codified text, and its rules are maintained through precedent and the collective memory of elders. Key features include:

  • Collective responsibility: the clan or sub-clan (through the diya-paying group) is collectively liable for the actions of its members.
  • Compensation (diya or mag): wrongs are remedied by negotiated payments — classically in camels — rather than by state punishment.
  • Contracts and agreements between groups are themselves xeer, binding the parties that negotiated them.

Practice

Disputes — over grazing, water, injury, homicide, marriage and property — are heard at assemblies (shir) under a tree, where elders argue cases by appeal to precedent. Xeer coexists with Islamic law (sharia) and, where a functioning state exists, with statutory law. In Somaliland and other Somali territories, xeer remains an important mechanism of governance and conflict resolution, and is formally recognised in some judicial contexts.

Significance

Scholars such as I. M. Lewis documented xeer as central to the "pastoral democracy" of Somali society. During the stateless period after 1991, xeer provided one of the principal frameworks for law and order in much of Somalia.
