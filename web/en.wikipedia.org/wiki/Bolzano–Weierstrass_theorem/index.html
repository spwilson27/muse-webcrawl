Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem

In mathematics, specifically in real analysis, the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, named after Bernard Bolzano and Karl Weierstrass, states that every bounded sequence in ℝn has a convergent subsequence. Equivalently, a subset of ℝn is sequentially compact if and only if it is closed and bounded, and the theorem also has an equivalent formulation in terms of accumulation points: every infinite bounded set of real numbers has an accumulation point. It is a cornerstone result in the rigorous foundations of calculus, used in proving the extreme value theorem and the Heine–Borel theorem's relatives.

History

Bolzano first proved the result for sequences of real numbers in his 1817 work Rein analytischer Beweis, as a lemma on the way to proving the intermediate value theorem, but the work went largely unnoticed. Weierstrass proved the theorem again in his Berlin lectures decades later, and it entered the mainstream of analysis through his students. The one-dimensional statement can be proved by repeated interval bisection: bisect a bounded interval containing infinitely many terms of the sequence, keep a half still containing infinitely many terms, and iterate; the nested intervals shrink to a single point, which is the limit of a suitable subsequence.

Higher dimensions and applications

For sequences in ℝn, convergent subsequences are extracted coordinate by coordinate. Together with the Heine–Borel theorem, the result characterises compact subsets of Euclidean space as exactly the closed and bounded sets. Applications include proofs that continuous functions on closed bounded intervals attain maxima and minima, existence results in optimisation, and foundations of functional analysis through sequential compactness arguments. The statement fails in infinite-dimensional spaces, where bounded sets need not be sequentially compact in norm.
