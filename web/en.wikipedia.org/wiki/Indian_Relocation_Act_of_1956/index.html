Indian Relocation Act of 1956

The Indian Relocation Act of 1956 (Public Law 959, 70 Stat. 986), also known as the Adult Vocational Training Act, was a United States federal law intended to encourage Native Americans to leave Indian reservations and assimilate into the general population. A central element of the federal Indian termination policy of the mid-20th century, it funded vocational training and relocation assistance for Native people who moved to designated cities.

Background

During the termination era (roughly 1945–1968), the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA), under Commissioner Dillon S. Myer, had already begun a voluntary relocation programme in 1952, modeled in part on Myer's earlier experience directing the War Relocation Authority. The 1956 Act formalised and expanded the programme, authorising vocational training of up to 24 months and assistance with transportation, housing and subsistence for relocatees.

Operation and effects

Relocation offices were opened in cities including Chicago, Denver, Los Angeles, San Francisco, Cleveland, Dallas, Minneapolis–Saint Paul, St. Louis and others. Promotional literature promised jobs and housing, but many relocatees found low wages, discrimination and isolation; the BIA was criticised for "dumping" families in poor urban neighbourhoods with minimal support. Between the 1950s and 1970s, well over 100,000 Native Americans moved to cities under federal encouragement, and the urban share of the Native population rose from under 10% in 1950 to a majority by 2000.

Legacy

Relocation inadvertently created large urban Indian communities and intertribal networks that fed the activism of the 1960s and 1970s, including the American Indian Movement (founded in Minneapolis in 1968). The policy is now generally regarded as part of the assimilationist termination era, repudiated by the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975.
