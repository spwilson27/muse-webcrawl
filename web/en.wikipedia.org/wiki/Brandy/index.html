Brandy gains its amber colour and much of its flavour from ageing in oak casks; it is traditionally served in a short-stemmed snifter.

Brandy is a spirit produced by distilling wine; the term usually refers to grape wine spirits, though brandies are also made from other fruits — apples, pears, cherries, plums — and from pomace, the skins and pips left after pressing. The name comes from the Dutch brandewijn, "burnt wine," referring to the application of heat in distillation. Most brandies are aged in wooden casks and contain 35–60% alcohol by volume. The best-known regional types are Cognac and Armagnac from France, protected designations distilled exclusively from specified grapes in delimited regions.

History

Distillation of wine was practised in the medieval Mediterranean world by the 12th–13th centuries, initially for medicinal purposes ("aqua vitae"). By the 16th century, Dutch merchants were concentrating wine by distillation to reduce shipping volume and taxes, intending to reconstitute it with water at the destination; the concentrated spirit proved to have improved in wooden casks during the voyage, and "burnt wine" became a product in its own right. The Charente region around Cognac perfected double distillation in copper pot stills, while Armagnac, in Gascony, is traditionally distilled once in a continuous column still — it is often cited as France's oldest brandy, with production documented from the early 15th century.

Production

Brandy begins with a low-alcohol, acidic base wine (or fruit wine). Grape brandies of the Cognac type are double-distilled in pot stills, retaining the heart of the second distillation; other styles use column stills for a lighter spirit. The raw spirit (eau-de-vie) is then aged in oak — traditionally Limousin or Tronçais oak for Cognac — where it extracts colour, tannins, vanillin, and lactones, loses harshness through oxidation and evaporation (the "angels' share"), and is blended by a master blender from casks of many vintages to a house style before bottling at typically 40% ABV. Pomace brandies such as Italian grappa and French marc are often bottled unaged or only briefly rested, and taste fiercely of their raw material.

Varieties

  • Cognac — from the Cognac region of western France; made mainly from Ugni blanc grapes, double pot-distilled, oak-aged.
  • Armagnac — from Gascony; single continuous distillation, often vintage-dated, with a more rustic profile.
  • Spanish brandy — Brandy de Jerez is aged in a solera system of American oak casks formerly used for sherry.
  • Pisco — grape brandy of Peru and Chile, distilled to proof and not (by Peruvian rules) aged in wood.
  • Calvados — apple (and pear) brandy from Normandy.
  • Fruit brandies (eaux-de-vie) — clear, unaged spirits such as kirsch (cherry), slivovica (plum), and Williams pear brandy.
  • American brandy — chiefly from California, where producers such as E. & J. Gallo dominate volume production.

Age designations

The traditional grades used for Cognac and adopted elsewhere are based on the age of the youngest spirit in the blend: V.S. ("Very Special," at least 2 years in cask), V.S.O.P. ("Very Superior Old Pale," at least 4 years), and X.O. ("Extra Old," at least 10 years under rules tightened in 2018). Older or special blends may be labelled Napoléon, Extra, or Hors d'âge, though only the regulated terms carry legal force within the appellation.

Serving and uses

Brandy is traditionally served neat in a tulip glass or snifter, whose shape concentrates the aroma; warming the glass by hand was once customary but is now often discouraged as it drives off delicate aromatics. Brandy is also a classic cocktail ingredient (the Sidecar, Brandy Alexander, and the original Sazerac), a cooking ingredient (flambéing, sauces, Christmas puddings and cakes), and historically was carried as a medicinal "restorative" — an image perpetuated by the small barrel on the collar of St. Bernard rescue dogs, a myth popularised by 19th-century art.
