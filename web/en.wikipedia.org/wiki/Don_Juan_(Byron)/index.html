Don Juan (Byron)

Don Juan is a satiric narrative poem by Lord Byron, first published in 1819–1824. Based on the legend of Don Juan, Byron inverted the traditional character: instead of a womaniser, his Juan is an innocent young man seduced by women, whose adventures take him across Europe.

Structure and publication

The poem, written in ottava rima, runs to over 16,000 lines in sixteen complete cantos (a seventeenth was left unfinished at Byron's death in 1824). Cantos I and II were published anonymously in 1819 and were condemned by many reviewers as immoral, though the poem sold widely. Byron called it an "epic satire" on contemporary society, manners, war and politics.

Plot summary

Juan, born in Seville, is sent abroad after an affair with Donna Julia. He is shipwrecked and washed up on a Greek island, where he loves the pirate's daughter Haidée; captured and sold into slavery in Constantinople; joins the Russian army at the siege of Ismail; becomes a favourite at the court of Catherine the Great; and is sent on a mission to England, where the poem breaks off amid satire of English society.

Reception

Don Juan is widely considered Byron's masterpiece and one of the greatest long poems in English. Percy Bysshe Shelley praised its originality, and later critics have celebrated its digressive, conversational voice and its blend of comedy and seriousness.
