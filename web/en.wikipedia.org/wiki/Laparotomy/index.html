Laparotomy

A laparotomy is a surgical procedure involving an incision through the abdominal wall to gain access to the abdominal cavity. It is also known as celiotomy. Laparotomy may be exploratory, performed to diagnose a condition that cannot be confirmed by other means, or therapeutic, as the means of carrying out an operation on the abdominal organs.

Procedure

The most common incision is the midline incision, made along the linea alba; it is quick, relatively bloodless and can be extended the full length of the abdomen, and is favoured in trauma and emergency surgery. Other incisions include the paramedian, transverse, Pfannenstiel (low transverse, used in pelvic and caesarean surgery), Kocher subcostal (for gallbladder and biliary surgery) and rooftop (chevron) incisions. After the peritoneum is opened, the surgeon explores the organs systematically, performs the required procedure, and closes the abdominal wall in layers, typically with mass closure of the fascia.

Indications and risks

Indications include abdominal trauma, peritonitis, bowel obstruction or perforation, gastrointestinal bleeding, staging of malignancy and gynaecological operations. Since the late 20th century, diagnostic laparoscopy has replaced exploratory laparotomy in many elective settings. Complications of laparotomy include wound infection, dehiscence, incisional hernia, postoperative ileus and adhesions, which can cause later bowel obstruction.
