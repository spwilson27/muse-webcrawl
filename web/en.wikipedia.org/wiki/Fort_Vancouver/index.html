Fort Vancouver was a 19th-century fur trading post on the north bank of the Columbia River, in present-day Vancouver, Washington. From 1825 it served as the headquarters of the Hudson's Bay Company's Columbia Department and the commercial and administrative centre of the Pacific Northwest.

History

The fort was established in 1824–25 under Chief Factor John McLoughlin, who moved the post from Fort George (Astoria) to a more fertile and defensible site upstream. Within a high wooden stockade stood the chief factor's house, stores, a school, workshops and a chapel; outside lay a large farm, orchards, mills and the multilingual village known as Kanaka Village, home to the company's diverse workforce of French-Canadian, Métis, Hawaiian and Indigenous employees. Fort Vancouver supplied trading posts across the region, exported salmon, timber and produce to Hawaii and California, and offered crucial assistance to American emigrants arriving over the Oregon Trail.

Transfer and preservation

The Oregon Treaty of 1846 placed the fort in United States territory. The Hudson's Bay Company withdrew in 1860, and the US Army's adjacent Vancouver Barracks continued in use into the modern era. The fort burned in 1866, but archaeology and reconstruction by the National Park Service have restored much of the stockade and several buildings as Fort Vancouver National Historic Site.
