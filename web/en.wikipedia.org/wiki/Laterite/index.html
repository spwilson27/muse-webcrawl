Laterite

Laterite is a soil type rich in iron and aluminium formed in hot and wet tropical areas. Nearly all laterites are of rusty-red coloration because of their high iron oxide content. They develop by intensive and prolonged weathering of the underlying parent rock, leaching away silica and bases and concentrating iron and aluminium oxides.

Formation and properties

Under high rainfall and temperature, silicate minerals break down; soluble elements are washed out while iron and aluminium accumulate as hematite, goethite and gibbsite. Soft when freshly cut, laterite hardens on exposure to air, making it a traditional building material.

Uses

Laterite blocks were used for temples at Angkor and for buildings and roads across India, Southeast Asia, Africa and Brazil. The name derives from Latin later, brick; Francis Buchanan-Hamilton coined the term in 1807 after observing its use in Kerala. Economically, some laterites are ores: bauxite is an aluminium laterite, and nickel laterites are mined in New Caledonia, Indonesia and the Philippines. Lateritic soils are nutrient-poor for agriculture once forest cover is removed, because nutrients are rapidly leached.
