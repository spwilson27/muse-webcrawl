A crystal detector is an electronic component used in early radio receivers, consisting of a piece of crystalline mineral — usually galena (lead sulfide) — contacted by a fine metal wire, the "cat's whisker". The contact forms a point-contact semiconductor diode that rectifies the radio signal, allowing the audio modulation to be recovered. It was the first practical semiconductor device.

History

The rectifying property of mineral crystals was noticed by Ferdinand Braun in 1874. In 1906 G. W. Pickard patented a silicon crystal detector and coined much of the early technology; Jagadish Chandra Bose patented a galena detector in 1901. Crystal detectors powered the simple crystal radios of the 1910s and 1920s, millions of which were built by hobbyists because they needed no battery or mains power — the headphones were driven by the received signal itself.

Operation

The user moved the springy cat's-whisker wire across the crystal surface hunting for a sensitive "hot spot" where rectification occurred. The device is nonlinear: it passes current preferentially in one direction, so the envelope of an amplitude-modulated carrier becomes an audio signal. Vacuum-tube receivers displaced crystal sets in the 1920s–30s, but crystal detectors returned in radar during the Second World War, and foxhole radios built by soldiers used improvised detectors of a razor blade and a pencil lead.
