The dot product relates vector lengths and the angle between vectors.

In mathematics, the dot product or scalar product is an algebraic operation that takes two equal-length sequences of numbers (usually coordinate vectors), and returns a single number. In Euclidean geometry, the dot product of the Cartesian coordinates of two vectors is widely used. It is often called the inner product (or rarely projection product) in the context of Euclidean space.

Definition

Algebraically, the dot product is the sum of the products of the corresponding entries of the two sequences of numbers:

a · b = Σi=1n aibi = a1b1 + a2b2 + … + anbn

Geometrically, it is the product of the Euclidean magnitudes of the two vectors and the cosine of the angle between them:

a · b = ||a|| ||b|| cos θ

These definitions are equivalent when using Cartesian coordinates. From the geometric definition it follows that the dot product is zero exactly when the vectors are orthogonal, positive when the angle between them is acute, and negative when it is obtuse.

Properties

The dot product is commutative (a · b = b · a), distributive over vector addition, and bilinear, scaling linearly in each argument. The dot product of a vector with itself is the square of its length: a · a = ||a||2. It underlies the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the definition of projection: the scalar projection of a onto b is (a · b)/||b||.

Applications

In physics, work is the dot product of force and displacement, and power is the dot product of force and velocity. In computer graphics, dot products compute lighting by measuring the angle between surfaces and light sources. In machine learning and information retrieval, normalized dot products (cosine similarity) quantify the similarity between vectors, including word and document embeddings.

Generalizations

The dot product generalizes to the inner product on arbitrary inner product spaces, including complex vector spaces (where one argument is conjugated) and function spaces, for example in Fourier analysis and quantum mechanics, where the probability amplitude between two states is an inner product.
