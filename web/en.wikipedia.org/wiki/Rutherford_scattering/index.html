Rutherford scattering

In particle physics, Rutherford scattering is the elastic scattering of charged particles by the Coulomb interaction. It is a physical phenomenon explained by Ernest Rutherford in 1911 that led to the development of the planetary Rutherford model of the atom and eventually the Bohr model. Rutherford scattering was first referred to as Coulomb scattering because it relies only upon the (static) electric potential, and the minimum distance between particles is set entirely by this potential. The classical Rutherford scattering process of alpha particles against gold nuclei is an example of "elastic scattering" because neither the particles' internal states nor their total kinetic energy change in the collision.

History

The phenomenon was first observed between 1908 and 1910 by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden in the gold foil experiment performed at the University of Manchester, under Rutherford's direction. Most alpha particles passed through thin gold foil with little deflection, as expected in J. J. Thomson's plum pudding model, but a small fraction — about 1 in 8,000 — were deflected through large angles, some nearly straight back. Rutherford famously compared this to firing a shell at tissue paper and having it rebound. In 1911 he deduced that the atom's positive charge and most of its mass must be concentrated in a tiny nucleus.

Differential cross section

The Rutherford differential scattering cross section, derived by Rutherford in 1911, gives the probability per unit solid angle that a particle is scattered through an angle θ:

(dσ/dΩ) = (Z₁Z₂e² / 16πε₀mv²)² · 1/sin⁴(θ/2)

where Z₁ and Z₂ are the atomic numbers of the projectile and target, e is the elementary charge, m is the projectile mass, and v its speed. The formula predicts that the number of particles scattered falls off rapidly with angle, in agreement with the Geiger–Marsden observations, and diverges at small angles where screening by atomic electrons becomes important.

Applications

Rutherford backscattering spectrometry (RBS) is an analytical technique in materials science that uses the scattering of high-energy ions to determine the composition and depth profile of thin films. Deviations from Rutherford scattering at high energies, where particles approach the nucleus closely enough for the strong nuclear force to act, provide information about nuclear sizes and structure.
