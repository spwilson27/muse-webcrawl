Medium-density fibreboard

Medium-density fibreboard (MDF) is an engineered wood product made by breaking down hardwood or softwood residuals into wood fibres, often in a defibrator, combining them with wax and a resin binder, and forming them into panels by applying high temperature and pressure. MDF is generally denser than plywood and is stronger and denser than particle board.

The material used in the manufacturing of MDF can be derived from a wide variety of sources, including recycled wood products. MDF is made up of separated fibres but can be used as a building material similar in application to plywood. It has a smooth surface, uniform density, and takes paint, veneer, and laminate well, which makes it a popular material for furniture, cabinetry, and interior mouldings.

History

The first MDF was developed in the United States in the 1960s, with large-scale production beginning in the 1980s. It drew on the technology of Masonite, a type of hardboard invented by William H. Mason in 1924. During the 1990s and 2000s, MDF became one of the most widely used panel products in the world, particularly in flat-pack furniture manufacturing.

Manufacturing

Production typically involves chipping logs, washing and refining the chips into fibres, mixing the fibres with resin (commonly urea-formaldehyde), drying, forming a mat, and hot-pressing the mat into boards of the desired thickness. The boards are then cooled, trimmed, and sanded. Typical densities range from about 600 to 800 kg/m³.

Properties and uses

MDF has a consistent structure throughout its thickness, so machined edges are smooth and can be routed into decorative profiles. It does not have a natural grain and does not split when screwed near the edge as readily as some other boards, although it is heavier than plywood and absorbs water readily if unsealed. Common uses include:

  • Furniture and flat-pack (ready-to-assemble) products
  • Kitchen and bathroom cabinets (often moisture-resistant grades)
  • Interior doors, mouldings, and architraves
  • Laminate flooring substrates
  • Speaker enclosures

Health and environmental concerns

Because most MDF historically used urea-formaldehyde resin, there were concerns about formaldehyde emissions, particularly in poorly ventilated indoor spaces. Regulations such as California's CARB standards and the U.S. TSCA Title VI have set strict emission limits, and low- or no-added-formaldehyde (NAF) products are now widely available. Sawdust generated when cutting MDF should be controlled with extraction and respiratory protection.
