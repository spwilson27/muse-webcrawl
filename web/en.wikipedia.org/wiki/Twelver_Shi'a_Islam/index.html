Twelver Shi'ism

Twelver Shi'ism (Arabic: اثنا عشرية, Ithnā ʿAshariyya), also known as Imami Shi'ism, is the largest branch of Shia Islam, comprising roughly 85–90% of all Shia Muslims. Twelvers believe in a line of twelve divinely appointed Imams, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatima and son-in-law Ali, the first Imam. The twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is believed to have entered occultation in the ninth century and to return as the Mahdi at the end of time.

The Twelve Imams

The line runs from Ali ibn Abi Talib (d. 661) through his sons Hasan and Husayn — whose martyrdom at Karbala in 680 is commemorated at Ashura — and nine further descendants, including Ja'far al-Sadiq, namesake of Ja'fari law. The Twelfth Imam disappeared as a child in 874 CE; Twelvers distinguish a Lesser Occultation, during which he communicated through four deputies, and the Greater Occultation, continuing to the present, during which religious authority devolves on qualified jurists.

Beliefs and practices

Twelver theology rests on five principles: divine unity (tawhid), divine justice (adl), prophethood (nubuwwa), leadership (imama) and resurrection (ma'ad). Distinctive practices include the mourning rituals of Muharram, pilgrimage (ziyara) to the shrines of the Imams in Karbala, Najaf, Mashhad and elsewhere, and the institution of the marja' — a grand ayatollah emulated by lay believers in matters of law. In the Islamic Republic of Iran, the doctrine of velayat-e faqih (guardianship of the jurist) places a senior cleric at the head of the state.
