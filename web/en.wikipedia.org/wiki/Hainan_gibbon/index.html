The Hainan black crested gibbon or Hainan gibbon (Nomascus hainanus) is a critically endangered species of gibbon endemic to Hainan Island, China. It is one of the rarest primates — and one of the rarest mammals — in the world: the entire population, confined to the Bawangling National Nature Reserve in the island's western mountains, numbered only a few dozen individuals in the 2020s.

Description

Like other crested gibbons, the species is sexually dichromatic: adult males are entirely black with a distinctive crest of upstanding hair, while females are golden-buff with a black patch on the crown. Gibbons are tailless lesser apes that move by brachiation through the canopy and live in small family groups, typically a pair with offspring, which advertise their territories with loud, elaborate duet songs at dawn. Hainan gibbons feed mainly on ripe fruit, supplemented with leaves and small animals.

Decline and conservation

Historical accounts suggest thousands of gibbons ranged across Hainan in the 1950s, but hunting and the conversion of lowland rainforest to rubber and other plantations reduced the species drastically; by the late 1970s only a handful survived at Bawangling. Protection began with the reserve's establishment in 1980, and dedicated monitoring teams of local rangers — some of them former hunters — have tracked each family group since 2003 under a conservation programme involving Chinese authorities and international partners such as Kadoorie Farm and Botanic Garden and the Zoological Society of London.

Numbers have slowly recovered from around 13 individuals at the lowest point to more than 30 — five family groups — by the early 2020s, including the formation of a new group in 2019, the first in years. The population nevertheless remains acutely vulnerable to a single typhoon, disease event or bout of inbreeding, and conservation work focuses on habitat restoration, canopy bridges of rope and replanted trees to reconnect forest fragments, and expansion into neighbouring reserves.
