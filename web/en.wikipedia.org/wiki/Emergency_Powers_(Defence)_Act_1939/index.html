Emergency Powers (Defence) Act 1939

Defence Regulations made under the Act governed everyday life in wartime Britain

The Emergency Powers (Defence) Act 1939 (2 & 3 Geo. 6. c. 62) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom passed in the days immediately before the outbreak of the Second World War. It granted the British government sweeping powers to rule by Order in Council for the defence of the realm, and was the legal basis for the wartime Defence Regulations that controlled almost every aspect of national life.

Provisions

The Act authorised His Majesty in Council to make Defence Regulations "for securing the public safety, the defence of the realm, the maintenance of public order and the efficient prosecution of war", and for maintaining supplies and services essential to the life of the community. Regulations could provide for the detention of persons, the requisitioning of property, control of industry and transport, rationing, censorship and blackout requirements. A second Act in May 1940 extended the powers still further, permitting regulations requiring persons to place themselves, their services and their property at the disposal of the state.

Defence Regulation 18B

The most notorious of the regulations was Defence Regulation 18B, under which the Home Secretary could order the detention without trial of persons suspected of hostile associations. It was used to intern British fascists including Oswald Mosley, and a number of enemy aliens. The sweeping delegation of legislative power was controversial even at the time, most famously criticised in the Lord Chief Justice Lord Hewart's tradition of concern over bureaucratic power.

Repeal

The Act and most of its regulations expired after the war, though transitional controls persisted for some years; the last Defence Regulations lapsed in the 1950s, and the Act itself was formally repealed by the Statute Law (Repeals) Act 1973.
