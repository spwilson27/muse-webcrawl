Nose piercing is a form of body piercing through the skin or cartilage of the nose, worn for cultural, religious, aesthetic or self-expressive reasons. The most common varieties are the nostril piercing, the septum piercing (through the tissue at the front of the nasal septum), and the bridge piercing across the top of the nose.

History and culture

Nose jewellery is ancient: a gold nose ring is mentioned in the Book of Genesis (24:22), and the practice spread through the Middle East and into India by the Mughal period. In India the nostril piercing (usually the left nostril, connected in Ayurvedic tradition with fertility and childbirth) and ornaments such as the nath nose ring — often chained to the ear at weddings — remain widespread among Hindu and Muslim communities, and the septum ring (bulak) is traditional in parts of India and Nepal. Nomadic Berber and Beja peoples, Bedouin, and many Indigenous peoples of the Americas and Australia also practised nose piercing; the Nez Perce (Nimiipuu) of the Pacific Northwest are named for it by French traders (nez percé, "pierced nose").

In the West, nostril piercing was popularised from the 1970s by travellers returning from India, then by punk and alternative subcultures, entering mainstream fashion in the 1990s; septum piercings followed in the 2010s.

Types

  • Nostril: through the flare of either nostril; worn with studs or hoops. Double and high-nostril variants exist.
  • Septum: through the thin strip of tissue ("sweet spot") in front of the septal cartilage; usually a circular barbell or clicker.
  • Bridge (Erl): a surface piercing across the bridge between the eyes, usually a straight or curved barbell, with a higher rejection risk.
  • Less common: nasallang (through both nostrils and septum), septril, rhino (vertical tip) piercings.

Procedure and care

Professional piercings are performed with a single-use hollow needle rather than a piercing gun. Initial jewellery is typically implant-grade titanium, niobium or 14–18k gold. Nostril piercings commonly take four to six months or longer to heal fully; aftercare generally involves sterile saline soaks and avoiding touching. Risks include infection, hypertrophic scarring ("piercing bumps", often irritation granulomas), migration and rejection (especially bridge piercings), and, rarely, septal haematoma.
