Sugar industry

The sugar industry comprises the production, processing, refining and marketing of sugars, principally sucrose from sugarcane and sugar beet. World production is roughly 180 million tonnes per year, about 80% from sugarcane grown in tropical and subtropical regions and 20% from sugar beet in temperate zones. Brazil is the largest producer and exporter, followed by India, the European Union, Thailand and China.

Production

Sugarcane is harvested, crushed to extract juice, and the juice is clarified, evaporated and crystallised to yield raw sugar and molasses, usually in mills adjacent to the fields since cane deteriorates quickly after cutting. Sugar beet is sliced and the sugar extracted by diffusion in hot water, then purified with lime and carbonatation. Raw sugar is shipped to refineries, often near consuming markets, where it is melted, decolourised — with bone char, activated carbon or ion-exchange resins — and recrystallised into white refined sugar, liquid sugar and specialty products. By-products include bagasse (burned for power, making many mills energy self-sufficient), molasses (for rum and animal feed) and beet pulp.

History

Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and spread through Asia; crystallised sugar was produced in India over two thousand years ago. From the 16th century, European colonial powers built a plantation system in the Caribbean and the Americas that depended on the transatlantic slave trade, making sugar one of the most profitable and brutal commodities of the early modern world. Beet sugar, developed in the 19th century, broke cane's monopoly in Europe. The 20th century saw international sugar agreements, quotas and subsidies that persist in various forms, notably the EU sugar regime and US import quotas.

Trade and markets

Sugar is traded globally on futures exchanges, notably the "Sugar No. 11" raw sugar contract in New York and the white sugar contract in London. Prices are volatile, driven by weather in Brazil and India, oil prices (because cane can be diverted to ethanol), and trade policy. Rising health concerns about sugar consumption and the growth of sugar taxes and alternative sweeteners are reshaping demand in developed markets.
