Wayne Clayson Booth (February 22, 1921 – October 10, 2005) was an American literary critic and rhetorician. He was the George M. Pullman Distinguished Service Professor Emeritus in English Language and Literature and the College at the University of Chicago. He is best known for his 1961 book The Rhetoric of Fiction, one of the most influential works of narrative theory of the twentieth century.

Life

Booth was born in American Fork, Utah, and raised in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. After army service in the Second World War he took his doctorate at the University of Chicago in 1950, under the influence of the Chicago School of criticism associated with Ronald Crane. He taught at Haverford College and Earlham College before returning to Chicago in 1962, where he remained until his retirement. With Sheldon Sacks and others he founded the journal Critical Inquiry in 1974.

Ideas

In The Rhetoric of Fiction Booth coined lasting critical concepts: the implied author (the guiding sensibility a text projects, distinct from both narrator and flesh-and-blood author), the unreliable narrator, and the critique of "showing versus telling" dogmas. His later work broadened rhetoric into a general theory of communication and judgement: Modern Dogma and the Rhetoric of Assent (1974) attacked irrationalism and proposed "systematic assent"; A Rhetoric of Irony (1974) analysed stable and unstable irony; and The Company We Keep: An Ethics of Fiction (1988) argued that stories, like friends, shape our character, and can be ethically evaluated. His memoir My Many Selves was published posthumously. Booth's notion of "coductive" criticism and his pluralism made him a central figure linking neo-Aristotelian criticism to later narratology and rhetorical theory.
