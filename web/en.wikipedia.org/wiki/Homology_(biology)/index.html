Homology (biology)

In biology, homology is similarity due to shared ancestry between a pair of structures or genes in different taxa. A common example is the forelimbs of vertebrates, where the wings of bats and birds, the arms of primates, the front flippers of whales, and the forelegs of four-legged animals such as horses all evolved from the same ancestral tetrapod structure, even though they now serve very different functions.

Definition and contrast with analogy

Homologous structures need not perform the same function; what defines them is common descent. Structures that perform similar functions but evolved independently—such as the wings of birds and the wings of insects—are called analogous, and their similarity is attributed to convergent evolution rather than common ancestry. The anatomist Richard Owen formalized the distinction in the 1840s, defining a homologue as "the same organ in different animals under every variety of form and function." After Darwin, homology came to be interpreted as evidence of evolution from a common ancestor.

Examples

The pentadactyl (five-digit) limb of tetrapods is the classic case: the humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, and digits can be traced across amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals despite radical modification. In plants, leaves, spines, tendrils, and the floral organs (petals, sepals, stamens, carpels) are homologous modifications of a basic leaf-like structure. At the molecular level, homologous DNA or protein sequences are those descended from a common ancestral sequence; these are subdivided into orthologs (separated by speciation) and paralogs (separated by gene duplication).

Developmental and deep homology

Evolutionary developmental biology has shown that even structures that are not homologous in adults—such as the eyes of vertebrates and cephalopods—may be built using homologous genetic toolkits, a phenomenon termed deep homology. For example, the Pax6 gene helps direct eye development across bilaterian animals. Serial homology describes repeated structures within one organism, such as vertebrae or the segments of an arthropod.
