British blues is a form of music derived from American blues that originated in the late 1950s, reached its peak of popularity in the 1960s, and developed into a distinct and influential style. British musicians took the electric Chicago blues of Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf and others — records that were often hard to obtain and marginalised in the United States — and performed it with increasing volume, distortion and rock energy.

Origins

American blues reached Britain through imported records, Lonnie Donegan's skiffle craze, and touring jazz musicians. Cyril Davies and Alexis Korner, founders of Blues Incorporated (1961), opened London's Ealing Blues Club in 1962, the nursery of the scene: members and guests included Charlie Watts, Mick Jagger, Keith Richards, Brian Jones, Eric Burdon and Jack Bruce.

The blues boom

From Blues Incorporated and John Mayall's Bluesbreakers — whose 1966 album with Eric Clapton became a landmark — sprang the bands that led the "British Invasion": the Rolling Stones, the Yardbirds, the Animals, Led Zeppelin, Cream and the original Peter Green-era Fleetwood Mac. Their success re-exported blues to white American audiences and revived interest in its Black originators. By the late 1960s many British acts moved toward heavier blues rock and progressive or hard rock, while a purist blues scene persisted in pubs and clubs.

Legacy

British blues shaped rock history: it launched generations of guitar heroes (Clapton, Jeff Beck, Jimmy Page, Peter Green, Rory Gallagher — from Ireland), influenced heavy metal, and continues through a live circuit of clubs and festivals. Later generations, from Gary Moore to Joanne Shaw Taylor, have kept the style current.
