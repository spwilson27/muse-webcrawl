Ex falso quodlibet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ex falso quodlibet (Latin for "from falsehood, anything [follows]"), also known as the principle of explosion (Latin: principium explosionis), is a rule of classical and intuitionistic logic stating that from a contradiction, any statement whatsoever can be proven. In symbolic terms, from P ∧ ¬P one may derive any Q: {P, ¬P} ⊢ Q. A system that accepts this principle becomes useless once inconsistent, because every statement — and its negation — becomes a theorem; such a theory is called trivial.

Justification

A standard proof runs as follows. Assume P ∧ ¬P. From P, disjunction introduction gives P ∨ Q for any Q. Then, since ¬P holds, disjunctive syllogism eliminates P and leaves Q. Intuitively defenders of the principle argue that a contradiction asserts something impossible, and an impossible premise can support any conclusion; semantically, there is no model in which P ∧ ¬P is true, so every statement is vacuously true "in all models" of the premises.

History

The principle was recognised by medieval logicians — a version appears in work associated with the 12th-century Parisian school and was stated explicitly by figures such as William of Soissons in some accounts. Bertrand Russell gave a famous informal demonstration, "proving" from 2 + 2 = 5 that he was the Pope: if 2 + 2 = 5, then subtracting 2 gives 2 = 3, subtracting 1 gives 1 = 2; the Pope and Russell are two, hence one, hence Russell is the Pope.

Criticism

Paraconsistent logics reject the principle of explosion so that inconsistent information — in databases, legal codes, or scientific theories in transition — can be reasoned about without triviality. Such systems typically restrict disjunctive syllogism or the rules that let a contradiction infect unrelated conclusions. Relevance logics similarly require that premises be relevant to conclusions. The dispute reflects a deeper question: whether logical consequence should track truth preservation in impossible situations or the preservation of information and relevance in actual ones.
