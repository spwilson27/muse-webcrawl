Free-air gravity anomaly

In geodesy and geophysics, the free-air gravity anomaly is the difference between the observed acceleration of gravity at a point and the theoretical gravity predicted for that point's latitude on a reference ellipsoid, after applying the free-air correction to account for the point's elevation above (or below) the reference surface.

Definition

The free-air correction adjusts the measured gravity for the change in gravity with height in free air, neglecting the gravitational attraction of any material between the measurement point and sea level. The vertical gradient of gravity near Earth's surface is about 0.3086 milligals per metre, so gravity measured on a mountain is reduced by this amount per metre of elevation before comparison with theoretical sea-level gravity.

The anomaly is conventionally written ΔgFA = gobs − γ + 0.3086 h, where h is the elevation in metres and γ is theoretical (normal) gravity.

Applications

Free-air anomalies are widely used in marine geophysics: satellite altimetry measurements of sea-surface height are converted into free-air anomaly maps of the oceans, revealing seafloor topography such as mid-ocean ridges, fracture zones, and seamounts. On land, free-air anomalies correlate strongly with topography in regions of isostatic compensation, in contrast to Bouguer anomalies, which apply an additional correction for the attraction of the intervening rock mass.
