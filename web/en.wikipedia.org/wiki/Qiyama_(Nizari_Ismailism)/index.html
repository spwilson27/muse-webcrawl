In Nizari Ismailism, the Qiyama (Arabic: قيامة, "resurrection") was a doctrinal proclamation made by the Imam Hasan II ibn Muhammad on 17 Ramadan 559 AH (8 August 1164) at the fortress of Alamut. It announced the spiritual resurrection: the unveiling of the esoteric truth (haqiqa) and the abrogation, for the initiated, of the external observances of the Sharia. This event marked a major phase of Nizari doctrine during the Alamut period.

Background

The Nizari Ismailis, centred on the mountain fortress of Alamut in Persia from 1090, developed an esoteric theology distinguishing outward religious law (zahir) from inner truth (batin). Under the early Alamut rulers, Ismailis observed the Sharia publicly while their leaders guarded the esoteric doctrine. Hasan II, upon succeeding his father Muhammad ibn Buzurg-Ummid, took the unprecedented step of proclaiming the Qiyama to the assembled community at Alamut.

Proclamation and meaning

According to Nizari tradition, Hasan II declared the Qiyama in a sermon in which he unveiled his status as the Imam and the Qa'im (the eschatological "He who arises"). The faithful, having entered the spiritual resurrection, were freed from the ritual prescriptions of the law—fasting, prayer in its outward form—and were to worship in the contemplation of the Imam. The Ismailis thereafter celebrated the Qiyama annually, turning toward Alamut in prayer.

The doctrine was systematised by Hasan II's son and successor, Nur al-Din Muhammad II, whose rule (1166–1210) was a high point of Nizari intellectual life, producing the theological works of Nasir al-Din al-Tusi's circle. The Qiyama was reversed by Hasan III (the caliph-imam Jalal al-Din Hasan) in 1211, when he reimposed Sunni observance.
