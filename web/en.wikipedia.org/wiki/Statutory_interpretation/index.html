Statutory interpretation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Statutory interpretation is the process by which courts determine the meaning of legislation and apply it to particular cases — necessary because general words meet unforeseen facts, language is ambiguous, and statutes outlive their circumstances. Common-law courts developed canons and approaches: the literal rule, the golden rule (avoiding absurdity), the mischief rule of Heydon's Case (1584, asking what defect the statute was meant to cure), and modern purposive approaches that read words in their statutory context — in the UK increasingly influenced by European method and by the Human Rights Act's interpretive duty (s.3); in the US framed by the modern contest between textualism (Scalia) and purposivism, fought over canons from ejusdem generis to constitutional avoidance.

Courts also use extrinsic aids: in the UK, since Pepper v Hart (1993), ministerial statements in Hansard may be consulted where legislation is ambiguous; in the US, legislative history is voluminous and contested. Interpretation is where much of law's real politics resides: rules of construction allocate power between legislature and judiciary, and presumptions (against retrospectivity, against ousting the courts, in favour of legality) encode constitutional values even in systems of parliamentary sovereignty.
