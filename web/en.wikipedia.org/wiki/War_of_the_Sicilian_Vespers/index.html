LocationSicily, southern Italy, western Mediterranean

The War of the Sicilian Vespers (Italian: Guerra del Vespro) was a conflict that began with the insurrection known as the Sicilian Vespers in 1282 and ended with the Peace of Caltabellotta in 1302. It was fought between the Angevin kings of Naples — backed by the Kingdom of France and the Papacy — and the Crown of Aragon for control of Sicily and the southern Mediterranean.

The Vespers uprising

Charles I of Anjou had conquered the Kingdom of Sicily from the Hohenstaufen in 1266, ruling both the island and the southern Italian mainland with a heavy hand. On Easter Monday, 30 March 1282, at the hour of vespers outside a church in Palermo, an incident between a French soldier and a Sicilian woman sparked a general massacre of the French on the island. The rebels offered the crown to Peter III of Aragon, who had a claim through his wife Constance, daughter of Manfred of Sicily; Peter landed in Sicily in August 1282.

Course of the war

Aragonese admiral Roger of Lauria repeatedly defeated Angevin–French fleets, notably at Malta (1283), the Bay of Naples (1284, capturing Charles of Salerno) and Les Formigues (1285). Pope Martin IV declared a crusade against Aragon and supported a French invasion of Catalonia in 1285, which failed disastrously. The war spread through the western Mediterranean for two decades, involving James II of Aragon, his brother Frederick, who was crowned king of Sicily in 1296, and Charles II of Naples.

Peace and consequences

The Peace of Caltabellotta (1302) recognised Frederick as king of the island — styled king of "Trinacria" — while the Angevins kept the mainland kingdom, henceforth called the Kingdom of Naples. The settlement formally divided the old Kingdom of Sicily in two, a partition that lasted, in changing dynastic forms, until Italian unification. The war also ended Angevin ambitions in the eastern Mediterranean and confirmed Aragon as a major naval power.
