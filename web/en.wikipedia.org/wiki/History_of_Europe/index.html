History of Europe

The history of Europe describes the passage of time from the earliest human habitation of the European continent to the present. Anatomically modern humans reached Europe by about 45,000 years ago, and the continent subsequently gave rise to Greek and Roman civilisation, feudal kingdoms, global empires and, in the twentieth century, the project of European integration.

Prehistory and antiquity

Neolithic farming spread from the Near East after about 7000 BCE, followed by Bronze and Iron Age cultures. Classical Greece developed the polis, philosophy and democracy, and the Roman Republic and Empire eventually unified the Mediterranean world under a single law and language of administration. Roman rule brought roads, cities and Christianity, which became the empire's official religion in the 4th century CE.

Middle Ages

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century, Europe fragmented into barbarian kingdoms, while the Byzantine Empire endured in the east. The Carolingian Empire briefly reunited much of the west around 800. Feudalism, monasticism and the Roman Catholic Church structured medieval society; the era also saw the Crusades, the growth of towns and universities, the Mongol invasions and the Black Death (1347–1351), which killed perhaps a third of the population.

Early modern Europe

The Renaissance, the printing press, and the Protestant Reformation transformed European culture and religion from the 15th century. European states built overseas empires in the Americas, Africa and Asia, while the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment reshaped thought. The French Revolution (1789) and the Napoleonic Wars spread ideals of legal equality and nationalism across the continent.

The long nineteenth century

The Industrial Revolution, beginning in Britain, remade economies and cities. National unification created Italy and Germany; imperial rivalry and alliances culminated in the First World War (1914–1918), which destroyed four empires and redrew the map.

Twentieth century and after

The interwar crises produced fascism and communism in power; the Second World War (1939–1945) devastated Europe and included the Holocaust. The continent then divided between the capitalist West and the communist East during the Cold War. Western states founded the European Economic Community (1957), which became the European Union in 1993; the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 and the dissolution of the USSR in 1991 ended the division, and the EU enlarged across central and eastern Europe.
