Central Asia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Central Asia is a region of Asia consisting of the five former Soviet republics of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan — sometimes defined more broadly to include Afghanistan, Mongolia and parts of neighbouring states. It is a land of steppe, desert and some of the world's highest mountains (the Pamirs, the "Roof of the World", and the Tien Shan), largely landlocked and historically defined by the routes of the Silk Road.

Geography and peoples

The region stretches from the Caspian Sea in the west to China in the east. Its great rivers, the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, once fed the Aral Sea, whose catastrophic shrinkage since the 1960s — caused by Soviet irrigation diversions — is one of the world's worst environmental disasters. Most inhabitants speak Turkic languages (Kazakh, Uzbek, Kyrgyz, Turkmen) except Tajikistan, which is Persian-speaking (Tajik), and Islam is the predominant religion. Great oasis cities — Samarkand, Bukhara, Khiva — glittered with Timurid architecture after Tamerlane made Samarkand his capital in the 14th century.

History in brief

Central Asia was a crossroads of empires: Persian, Macedonian (Alexander), Arab, Mongol (Genghis Khan's conquests devastated and then unified it under the Pax Mongolica), and Timurid. In the 19th century Russia and Britain competed for influence in the "Great Game"; the region was absorbed into the Russian Empire and then the USSR, whose 1920s border-drawing created the five republics that became independent in 1991. Today the region balances among Russian, Chinese (Belt and Road investment along the old Silk Road), and Western interests, and its economies rest on oil and gas (Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan), gold, cotton and remittances.
