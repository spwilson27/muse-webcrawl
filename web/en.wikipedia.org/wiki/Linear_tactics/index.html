Linear tactics is the military doctrine under which infantry fought deployed in long lines, typically two to four ranks deep, delivering controlled volleys of musket fire. It dominated European warfare from the late seventeenth century, when the socket bayonet and flintlock musket displaced the pike, until rifled weapons made close-order lines untenable in the mid-nineteenth century.

Rationale

The smoothbore musket was inaccurate and slow to load, so firepower depended on massed, disciplined volleys rather than individual aim. Deploying in line maximised the number of muskets that could fire at once and kept units controllable by voice and drum. Fire was delivered by platoon or by rank in sequence, and after softening the enemy with volleys, the line could advance to decide the fight with the bayonet. Elaborate drill — the hallmark of armies such as Frederick the Great's Prussians — was needed to wheel, dress ranks and change formation under fire without disorder.

Strengths and limits

A line was powerful frontally but thin, vulnerable to cavalry if caught in the open without forming square, and inflexible over broken ground. Attack columns, light infantry skirmishers and, in the French Revolutionary wars, mixed order (ordre mixte) offered alternatives. The system reached its climax in the Napoleonic Wars and declined as rifled muskets, and then breech-loaders, greatly increased the range and lethality of defensive fire, as the American Civil War and the Franco-Prussian War demonstrated.
