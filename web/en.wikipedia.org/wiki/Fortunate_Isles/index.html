Fortunate Isles

The Fortunate Isles or Isles of the Blessed (Ancient Greek: μακάρων νῆσοι, makárōn nêsoi; Latin: Insulae Fortunatae) were a legendary group of islands in Greek and Celtic mythology, situated in the Atlantic Ocean beyond the Pillars of Hercules. They were conceived as a paradisiacal land where the earth bore fruit three times a year without labour, the climate was eternally mild, and the souls of heroes and the virtuous dead dwelt in bliss — in later Greek thought functioning as an alternative destination to, or part of, the underworld's Elysian Fields.

In classical sources

Hesiod's Works and Days places the heroes of the fourth age on the Isles of the Blessed beside the deep-eddying Ocean, where "the grain-giving earth bears honey-sweet fruit flourishing thrice a year". Pindar's second Olympian ode describes the isle of the blessed, lit by ocean breezes and golden flowers, where the righteous dwell with heroes such as Peleus, Cadmus and Achilles under the judgement of Rhadamanthus. Homer locates the related Elysian Plain at the ends of the earth. Plutarch, in his life of Sertorius, reports sailors' tales of two Atlantic islands matching the description, and Pliny the Elder and Ptolemy used "Fortunate Islands" as an actual geographic name for the Canary Islands — Ptolemy even fixed his prime meridian through them (the Ferro meridian tradition).

Later identifications and influence

As Atlantic exploration advanced, the Fortunate Isles were identified variously with the Canaries, Madeira, the Azores and even the Americas. The Canary Islands retained the association: their name in Spanish usage, Islas Afortunadas, survives, and Macaronesia — the modern biogeographic grouping of the Canaries, Madeira, Azores, Cape Verde and Savage Islands — takes its name directly from the Greek makárōn nêsoi. The myth also fed later utopian and paradisiacal island traditions in European literature.
