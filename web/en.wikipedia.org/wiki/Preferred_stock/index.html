Preferred stock

Preferred stock (also called preference shares or preferred shares) is a class of stock that has features of both equity and debt. Preferred shareholders generally have a higher claim on a company's dividends and assets than common shareholders, but usually do not have voting rights.

Features

Preferred stock typically pays a fixed dividend that takes priority over dividends to common shareholders. In the event of liquidation, preferred shareholders are paid before common shareholders but after bondholders. Varieties include cumulative preferred stock (unpaid dividends accumulate), participating preferred stock (sharing in additional profits), convertible preferred stock (convertible into common shares), and callable or redeemable preferred stock (which the issuer may repurchase).

Issuers and investors

Preferred stock is commonly issued by banks, utilities, and other financial institutions, in part because regulators may treat certain preferred issues as capital. Investors are attracted by the relatively stable dividend income, though the shares usually appreciate less than common stock and remain subordinate to all debt.

Comparison with bonds and common stock

Like bonds, preferred stock tends to pay a fixed rate and trade around a par value, but dividends are paid from after-tax profits and can usually be deferred without triggering default, unlike bond interest. Unlike common stock, preferred shares generally lack voting rights and growth potential.
