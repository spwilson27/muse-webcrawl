Torr

The torr (symbol: Torr) is a unit of pressure based on an absolute scale, defined historically as exactly 1/760 of a standard atmosphere (atm). It is thus approximately equal to the pressure exerted by a column of mercury one millimetre high, and one torr is almost exactly equal to one millimetre of mercury (mmHg): 1 Torr = 0.999999857533699… mmHg.

In SI terms, one torr is exactly 101325/760 pascals, approximately 133.322 Pa. The unit is named after Evangelista Torricelli, the Italian physicist and mathematician who discovered the principle of the barometer in 1643.

Although the torr has largely been replaced by the pascal in scientific usage and by the millibar/hectopascal in meteorology, it remains common in vacuum technology, medicine (e.g. intraocular and blood pressure in some contexts, usually as mmHg), and engineering in the United States.

History

In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli, a student of Galileo, filled a glass tube about a metre long with mercury and inverted it into a dish of mercury, creating the first barometer. The column stood about 760 mm high, leaving the "Torricellian vacuum" above it — the first sustained vacuum produced in a laboratory. The unit commemorating him was later defined as one seven-hundred-and-sixtieth of the standard atmosphere, the pressure supporting 760 mm of mercury at 0 °C under standard gravity.

Definition and conversions

The torr was originally intended to be identical to the millimetre of mercury, but subsequent redefinitions of both units made them differ very slightly. The millimetre of mercury is defined via the density of mercury and standard gravity, whereas the torr is exactly 1/760 of the standard atmosphere (101325 Pa). The relative difference is about 1.4 × 10−7, negligible for nearly all practical purposes.

In vacuum practice, pressures are often given in millitorr (mTorr, 10−3 Torr). Vacuum ranges are conventionally described as rough vacuum (760–25 Torr), medium vacuum (25–10−3 Torr), high vacuum (10−3–10−9 Torr), and ultra-high vacuum (below 10−9 Torr).

Usage

The torr remains in routine use in vacuum technology (especially in the United States), in thin-film deposition and semiconductor processing, and in physiology and medicine alongside the mmHg — for example, normal intraocular pressure is about 10–21 mmHg, and arterial blood gas tensions are reported in torr or mmHg. European Union directives define the torr for pressure measurement in medicine exactly as 101325/760 Pa.

Note that "torr" is the unit name and "Torr" is its symbol; the symbol is capitalised because it derives from a proper name, while the spelled-out unit is lowercase.
