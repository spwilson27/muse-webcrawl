Induced seismicity

Induced seismicity is seismic activity — minor earthquakes and tremors — that results from human activity altering the stresses and strains in the Earth's crust. Most induced earthquakes are small, but some have been large enough to cause damage. Typical causes include the filling of large reservoirs, extraction of oil, gas and groundwater, geothermal energy production, mining, and the injection of fluids underground, including wastewater disposal and hydraulic fracturing.

Mechanisms

Human activities induce earthquakes mainly by changing pore-fluid pressure in faults or by changing the load on the crust. Injecting fluid raises pore pressure, which reduces the effective normal stress clamping a fault and can allow it to slip; removing mass or fluid can have comparable effects by unloading or compaction. Where faults are already close to failure, even small changes can trigger slip.

Examples and response

Well documented cases include reservoir-induced earthquakes behind large dams, seismicity associated with wastewater injection in Oklahoma and other oil- and gas-producing regions of the United States, and earthquakes near geothermal fields such as The Geysers in California. Regulators in several jurisdictions now use "traffic light" monitoring systems that reduce or halt operations when seismicity exceeds set thresholds.
