Anomie

Anomie (from Ancient Greek anomia, "lawlessness") is a sociological concept describing a state of normlessness — a breakdown or absence of shared social norms and values that guide behaviour. It is associated especially with the work of Émile Durkheim, who developed it in The Division of Labour in Society (1893) and Suicide (1897).

Durkheim

For Durkheim, anomie arises when rapid social or economic change outruns society's capacity to regulate individuals' expectations and desires. In Suicide he identified "anomic suicide", which rises in times of both economic collapse and sudden prosperity, because norms that previously limited aspirations cease to bind. More generally, anomie in the division of labour occurs when the parts of a differentiated society are insufficiently integrated or regulated.

Merton's strain theory

Robert K. Merton reformulated anomie in his influential 1938 essay "Social Structure and Anomie". Merton argued that deviance results from a strain between culturally approved goals (such as material success) and the institutionalized means available to achieve them. He distinguished five modes of adaptation: conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism, and rebellion. This "strain theory" became foundational in criminology.

Later usage

The concept has been developed by later theorists, including institutional anomie theory, which links crime to the dominance of economic institutions over family, education, and polity. In popular usage, "anomie" often denotes a personal feeling of alienation, purposelessness, or disconnection from society, loosely derived from the sociological sense.
