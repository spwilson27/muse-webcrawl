Chinese architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chinese architecture is the embodiment of an architectural style that developed over millennia in China and influenced building throughout East Asia. Its structural principles have remained largely unchanged since the Tang dynasty: an emphasis on horizontal lines and axial symmetry, timber-frame construction with interlocking bracket sets (dougong), buildings raised on podiums, walls treated as non-load-bearing screens, and enclosed courtyards organizing space.

Characteristics

  • Axial planning: complexes are laid out along a north–south axis, with the most important buildings facing south.
  • Timber framing: columns carry the roof; walls fill in between. Bracket sets (dougong) distribute weight and allow the characteristic upturned eaves.
  • Courtyards: the siheyuan courtyard house is the fundamental residential unit.
  • Feng shui: geomantic principles govern orientation and siting.
  • Symbolism and colour: yellow tiles signalled imperial use; numbers, animals and ornaments carried cosmic meaning.

History

Neolithic cultures raised timber and rammed-earth structures; by the Han dynasty multi-storey towers and sophisticated joinery were established. The oldest surviving timber buildings, such as the main hall of Nanchan Temple (782) and Foguang Temple (857), date to the Tang dynasty. The Song dynasty official treatise Yingzao Fashi (1103), compiled by Li Jie, codified modular building standards. Ming and Qing architecture reached its grandest expression in Beijing's Forbidden City and the Temple of Heaven, while regional styles produced Fujian tulou earth buildings, Jiangnan gardens, and cave dwellings (yaodong) of the loess plateau.

Modern period

The 20th century brought Western styles, socialist monumentalism, and — after 1978 — a construction boom that made China the world's largest building market. Contemporary Chinese architects such as Wang Shu, recipient of the 2012 Pritzker Prize, have sought to reconcile modern construction with traditional materials and forms, even as rapid urbanization has destroyed much historic fabric.
