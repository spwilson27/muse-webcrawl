Islamic taxation refers to the system of levies prescribed or sanctioned by Islamic law and applied historically in Muslim-ruled states, with revenues collected into the public treasury (bayt al-mal). Its main components were religious and land-based taxes that differed according to the taxpayer's religion, the nature of the land and the origin of the revenue.

Principal levies

Zakat, one of the Five Pillars of Islam, is an obligatory alms levy on qualifying wealth of Muslims — classically 2.5% of savings and assets above the nisab threshold, with separate rates for crops and livestock — distributed to categories of recipients named in the Qur'an, chiefly the poor. Jizya was a per-capita tax on adult male non-Muslim subjects (dhimmis) in return for state protection and exemption from military service and from zakat. Kharaj was a tax on agricultural land, originally on conquered land retained by its cultivators, assessed either as a share of produce or a fixed sum. Ushr ("one-tenth") was a tithe on produce from Muslim-held land, and khums ("one-fifth") applied to certain categories such as spoils and mineral wealth.

History and modern practice

The system was systematised under the Rashidun and Umayyad caliphates and elaborated by jurists such as Abu Yusuf in his Kitab al-Kharaj. Over time rulers added customs dues and market taxes of contested legality. The Ottoman Empire retained jizya until the 19th-century Tanzimat reforms replaced it with a military-exemption tax and then abolished it. Today zakat remains a religious duty performed privately in most countries, though several states — including Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Malaysia — collect or regulate it officially; the other historical levies survive only in the jurisprudence literature and in proposals by some Islamist movements.
