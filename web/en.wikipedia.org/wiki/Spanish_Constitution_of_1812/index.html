Spanish Constitution of 1812

The Spanish Constitution of 1812, also known as the Constitution of Cádiz and nicknamed La Pepa, was promulgated on 19 March 1812 by the Cortes of Cádiz, the Spanish parliament assembled in the port of Cádiz while most of Spain was occupied by Napoleon's armies during the Peninsular War. It was the first constitution in Spain and one of the earliest and most liberal constitutions in Europe, and it influenced constitutional movements across Europe and Latin America.

Provisions

The constitution established national sovereignty, a separation of powers, freedom of the press, free trade and universal male suffrage — remarkably broad for its time, though it made Roman Catholicism the sole permitted religion. It limited the king to a constitutional role, abolished the Inquisition, seigneurial rights and internal customs barriers, and provided for elected provincial and municipal governments in Spain and its overseas territories, whose deputies had sat in the Cortes.

Fate and legacy

Ferdinand VII abolished the constitution on his restoration in 1814 and restored absolutism. It was briefly revived during the Liberal Triennium of 1820–1823, after Rafael del Riego's military uprising forced the king to swear to it, and again in 1836–1837. The Cádiz Constitution served as the model for Portugal's 1822 constitution, influenced the revolutions of 1820 in Italy, and shaped early constitutionalism in the new republics of Spanish America. Its promulgation on the feast of St Joseph earned it the popular name La Pepa, and "¡Viva la Pepa!" became a liberal rallying cry.
