Bessel A. van der Kolk (born 1943) is a Dutch psychiatrist, author, researcher, and educator known for his work on the effects of trauma on the body and mind and for advocating body-oriented approaches to the treatment of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). His 2014 book The Body Keeps the Score: Brain, Mind, and Body in the Healing of Trauma became a long-running bestseller and brought his ideas to a wide popular audience.

Early life and education

Van der Kolk was born in The Hague in the Netherlands during the Second World War. He emigrated to the United States and studied at the University of Chicago, where he earned an undergraduate degree in 1965 and his medical degree in 1970. He completed his psychiatric residency at the Massachusetts Mental Health Center and was on the faculty of Harvard Medical School.

Career

Van der Kolk worked with Vietnam veterans at a Boston Veterans Administration clinic in the late 1970s, early in the development of PTSD as a formal diagnosis. In 1982 he founded the Trauma Center in Brookline, Massachusetts, a clinical and training institution for the treatment of traumatized children and adults (later part of the Justice Resource Institute). He has served as president of the International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies and has been a major researcher on developmental trauma, neurofeedback, EMDR, and yoga-based therapies.

Van der Kolk has argued that trauma is stored in the body as much as in the mind, affecting the brain's stress-response systems, and that effective treatment often requires bottom-up, body-based methods — such as yoga, theater, and neurofeedback — in addition to talk therapy. He campaigned unsuccessfully for the recognition of "developmental trauma disorder" as a diagnosis in the DSM-5.

The Body Keeps the Score

Published by Viking in 2014, The Body Keeps the Score synthesizes decades of research on how traumatic experiences reshape both body and brain, producing symptoms such as hypervigilance, emotional numbing, and dissociation. The book spent years on The New York Times bestseller list, particularly after a resurgence of interest in 2021. Some of van der Kolk's claims — especially concerning memory and recovered memories of trauma — have drawn criticism from other researchers, and the book's popular reception has outpaced scientific consensus on some of its proposals.
