Legendre's three-square theorem

In mathematics, Legendre's three-square theorem states that a natural number can be represented as the sum of three squares of integers if and only if it is not of the form

n = 4a(8b + 7)

for non-negative integers a and b. The first numbers that cannot be expressed as a sum of three squares are 7, 15, 23, 28, 31, 39, 47, 55, 60, 63, 71 and so on.

History

The theorem was stated by Adrien-Marie Legendre in 1797–98 in his Essai sur la théorie des nombres, but his proof contained a gap: it relied on a claim about quadratic residues that he could not establish (a precursor of the quadratic reciprocity and Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions). Carl Friedrich Gauss supplied a complete proof in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801), where the three-square theorem appears as a corollary of his theory of ternary quadratic forms.

Relation to other theorems

The three-square theorem sits between Lagrange's four-square theorem (1770), which states that every natural number is a sum of four squares, and Fermat's two-square theorem, which characterises numbers that are sums of two squares. Together with results on sums of three triangular numbers and Cauchy's polygonal number theorem, these form part of the classical theory of additive number theory. Legendre's three-square theorem is also closely related to the local–global principle and can be deduced from the Hasse–Minkowski theorem applied to the form x² + y² + z².
