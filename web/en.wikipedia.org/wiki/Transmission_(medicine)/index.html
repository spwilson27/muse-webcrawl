Transmission (medicine)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In medicine, public health, and biology, transmission is the passing of a pathogen causing an infectious disease from an infected host individual or group to a particular individual or group, regardless of whether the other individual was previously infected. The term strictly refers to the transmission of microorganisms directly from one individual to another by one or more of the following means:

  • airborne transmission — very small dry and wet particles that stay in the air for long periods of time, allowing airborne contamination even after the departure of the host; particle size is generally under 5 μm;
  • droplet transmission — small and usually wet particles that stay in the air for a short period of time; contamination usually occurs in the presence of the host, and particle sizes are above 5 μm;
  • direct physical contact — touching an infected individual, including sexual contact;
  • indirect physical contact — usually by touching a contaminated surface (a fomite);
  • fecal–oral transmission — usually from unwashed hands, contaminated food or water sources, because of lack of sanitation and hygiene, an important transmission route in pediatrics, veterinary medicine and developing countries.

Transmission can also be indirect, via another organism, either a vector (e.g. a mosquito or fly) or an intermediate host (e.g. tapeworm in pigs can be transmitted to humans who ingest improperly cooked pork). Indirect transmission could involve zoonoses or, more typically, larger pathogens like macroparasites with more complex life cycles.

Routes

Airborne and droplet

Respiratory pathogens such as influenza viruses, measles virus, and SARS-CoV-2 spread through particles expelled by coughing, sneezing, talking, and breathing. The historical distinction between large "droplets" that fall quickly and small "aerosols" that remain suspended is now understood as a continuum; ventilation, filtration, distance, and masking all reduce risk.

Contact

Direct contact spreads diseases such as herpes simplex and many sexually transmitted infections. Indirect contact via fomites — contaminated objects such as doorknobs, towels, or medical equipment — contributes to the spread of norovirus, rhinoviruses, and healthcare-associated pathogens such as MRSA.

Fecal–oral

Pathogens shed in feces reach another person's mouth through contaminated water, food, hands, or surfaces. Cholera, hepatitis A and E, poliomyelitis, rotavirus, and typhoid fever use this route; safe water, sanitation, and hand hygiene are the primary controls.

Vector-borne

Arthropod vectors transmit malaria (mosquitoes), dengue, Lyme disease and plague. Control focuses on the vector — bed nets, insecticides, habitat reduction — as well as on vaccination or chemoprophylaxis where available.

Vertical transmission

Transmission from parent to offspring — across the placenta, during birth, or through breastfeeding — is called vertical transmission. Examples include HIV, syphilis, rubella, and Zika virus. It contrasts with horizontal transmission between members of the same generation.

Quantifying transmission

Epidemiologists describe transmissibility with the basic reproduction number, R0 — the average number of secondary cases generated by one case in a fully susceptible population — and with measures such as the incubation period, serial interval, and attack rate. Interventions from isolation and quarantine to immunization aim to push the effective reproduction number below one, ending sustained transmission.
