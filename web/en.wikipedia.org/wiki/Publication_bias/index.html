Publication bias

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Publication bias is the tendency of researchers, editors, and pharmaceutical companies to publish experimental results only if they report a significant or favourable finding, leaving studies with null or negative results unpublished — the so-called file drawer problem, named by psychologist Robert Rosenthal in 1979 for the file drawers full of studies that were never submitted or accepted.

Causes and evidence

Publication bias arises at several stages: researchers may not write up null results (or may engage in outcome switching or selective reporting within a study), reviewers and editors may favour positive findings, and sponsors may suppress unfavourable trials. Empirical comparisons of registered trials with the published literature — notably studies of FDA-registered antidepressant trials, in which nearly all positive trials were published while most negative trials were unpublished or presented as positive — show that the published record can substantially exaggerate treatment effects.

Detection and correction

Meta-analysts use funnel plots — scatter plots of effect size against study precision — to look for asymmetry indicating missing small null studies. Statistical approaches include Egger's regression test, Begg's test, and "trim and fill" adjustment. However, funnel-plot asymmetry can also reflect genuine heterogeneity between studies.

Mitigation

Remedies include prospective trial registration (mandatory for ICMJE journals since 2005 and required for U.S. trials by the FDA Amendments Act of 2007, with results reporting to ClinicalTrials.gov), pre-registration and registered reports formats in which journals accept studies before results are known, open data and preprint servers, and journals devoted to null results. Publication bias is considered a major contributor to the replication crisis in psychology and biomedicine.
