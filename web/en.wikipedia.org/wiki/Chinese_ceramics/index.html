Chinese ceramics

Chinese ceramics encompasses pottery and porcelain produced in China from prehistoric times to the present. China developed ceramic traditions of unmatched longevity and sophistication, inventing stoneware, celadon glazes and true porcelain centuries before they appeared elsewhere, and Chinese wares profoundly influenced ceramic production across Asia, the Islamic world and Europe.

Early wares

Neolithic cultures such as Yangshao produced painted pottery, and by the Han dynasty (206 BCE – 220 CE) lead-glazed wares and early celadons were being made. Tang dynasty (618–907) potters produced the famous sancai ("three-colour") glazed tomb figures and vessels, alongside white wares from northern kilns and celadons from Yue kilns in the south.

Song dynasty refinement

The Song dynasty (960–1279) is often considered the classical age of Chinese ceramics, prized for restrained monochrome glazes: the "five great kilns" — Ru, Guan, Ge, Jun and Ding — produced wares of legendary subtlety, including Ru ware sky-blue crackled celadon made briefly for the imperial court. Longquan celadons were exported in vast quantities across Asia and as far as East Africa.

Blue-and-white and the imperial kilns

Under the Yuan dynasty (1271–1368), potters at Jingdezhen perfected underglaze blue-and-white porcelain, using cobalt imported from Persia; it became dominant under the Ming dynasty (1368–1644) and a global commodity. The imperial kilns at Jingdezhen produced court wares bearing reign marks, while Ming and Qing export porcelain — including kraak ware and armorial services — flooded European markets and inspired imitations at Delft and Meissen. Qing potters added brilliant enamel palettes such as famille rose and famille verte.
