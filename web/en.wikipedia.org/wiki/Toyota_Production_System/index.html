The Toyota Production System (TPS) is an integrated socio-technical system developed by Toyota that comprises its management philosophy and production practices. Built around the elimination of waste (muda), it became the model for lean manufacturing worldwide.

Origins

After World War II, Toyota faced a small domestic market, scarce capital, and a wide variety of vehicle demands. Kiichiro Toyoda and production engineer Taiichi Ohno developed an alternative to American mass production: instead of building to stock, the factory would produce only what the next process needed, when it needed it. Ohno drew on the pull-system used in American supermarkets as the inspiration for kanban.

The two pillars

Just-in-time (JIT): parts and materials are produced and delivered only as required, minimizing inventory and exposing problems immediately. Autonomation (jidoka): machines and workers automatically stop production when a defect is detected, so problems are fixed at the source rather than passed downstream — famously implemented through the andon cord that any worker may pull.

Concepts

  • Kaizen — continuous improvement by all employees.
  • Kanban — visual signals controlling the flow of parts.
  • Muda, mura, muri — waste, unevenness, and overburden as the three enemies of production.
  • Genchi genbutsu — "go and see" the actual place and thing.
  • Five whys — root-cause analysis by asking why repeatedly.

Influence

TPS was documented for Western audiences in Womack, Jones, and Roos's The Machine That Changed the World (1990), which coined the term "lean production". Its principles have been applied well beyond manufacturing, to healthcare, logistics, and software development (notably in the Toyota Kata tradition and lean startup thinking).
