Max Bruch (6 January 1838 – 2 October 1920) was a German Romantic composer, teacher, and conductor who wrote more than 200 works, including three violin concertos, the first of which — the Violin Concerto No. 1 in G minor, Op. 26 — has become a staple of the violin repertoire.

Life

Bruch was born in Cologne, the son of Auguste and Wilhelm Bruch. He received his earliest musical training from his mother, a soprano, and showed precocious talent, composing a symphony at the age of 14. He studied in Cologne with Ferdinand Hiller and Carl Reinecke, and in 1858 won a scholarship that allowed him to live and work in Frankfurt, Mannheim and other German cities.

He held conducting posts in Koblenz, Sondershausen, Berlin and Liverpool, where he was principal conductor of the Liverpool Philharmonic Society from 1880 to 1883. From 1890 until his retirement in 1910 he taught composition at the Berlin Hochschule für Musik, where his pupils included Ralph Vaughan Williams and Ottorino Respighi.

Music

Bruch's style is firmly rooted in the Romantic tradition of Mendelssohn and Schumann; he regarded Brahms as a kindred spirit and was openly hostile to the modernism of Wagner, Liszt and their successors, which partly explains the eclipse of much of his output after his death. His best-known works are for solo instruments and orchestra:

  • Violin Concerto No. 1 in G minor, Op. 26 (1866, revised 1868) — among the most popular of all violin concertos.
  • Scottish Fantasy, Op. 46 (1880), for violin and orchestra, based on Scottish folk melodies.
  • Kol Nidrei, Op. 47 (1880), for cello and orchestra, based on Hebrew melodies, including the Yom Kippur chant from which it takes its name.
  • Violin Concertos No. 2 in D minor, Op. 44, and No. 3 in D minor, Op. 58.
  • The oratorio Odysseus, Op. 41 (1872), a major success in his lifetime.
  • Three symphonies, chamber music including the Eight Pieces for clarinet, viola and piano, Op. 83, and numerous choral works.

Because of the Jewish themes of Kol Nidrei, Bruch's music was banned in Nazi Germany under the mistaken belief that he was Jewish; he was in fact a Protestant.
