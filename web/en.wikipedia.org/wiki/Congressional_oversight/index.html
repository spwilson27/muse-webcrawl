Congressional oversight

Congressional oversight is oversight by the United States Congress over the executive branch, including federal agencies, programs, and policy implementation. It is an implied power arising from Congress's legislative, appropriations, and impeachment powers, and it is exercised mainly through committee hearings, investigations, subpoenas, and the work of support agencies such as the Government Accountability Office (GAO).

Legal basis

The Supreme Court upheld Congress's power of inquiry in cases such as McGrain v. Daugherty (1927), while recognizing limits in Watkins v. United States (1957). Committees may compel testimony and documents by subpoena, and witnesses who refuse can be held in contempt of Congress. Executive privilege may be asserted against disclosure, producing recurring inter-branch litigation.

Practice

Oversight tools include authorization hearings, appropriations conditions, inspectors general, GAO audits, the Congressional Budget Office, and special investigative committees. Landmark investigations include the Truman Committee on defense waste, the Watergate hearings of 1973–74, and the Church Committee (1975–76), which investigated intelligence abuses and led to the creation of permanent intelligence committees and FISA.

Scholarship

Political scientists contrast "police patrol" oversight—routine, proactive monitoring—with "fire alarm" oversight, in which Congress reacts to scandals and complaints (McCubbins and Schwartz, 1984). Critics argue divided government produces more aggressive oversight, while unified government may soften it.
