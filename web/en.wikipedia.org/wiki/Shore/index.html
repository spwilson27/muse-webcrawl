Shore

A shore or a shoreline is the fringe of land at the edge of a large body of water, such as an ocean, sea, or lake. In physical oceanography, a shore is the wider fringe that is geologically modified by the action of the body of water past and present, while the beach is at the edge of the shore, representing the intertidal zone where there is one. In contrast to a coast, a shore can border any body of water, while the coast borders only the sea.

The shores of lakes and rivers can also be called banks, a term also used for the raised edges of rivers and streams. Shores may be made of many materials: sand, gravel, shingle, pebbles, cobbles, or bedrock. The sediment composition determines the shape and ecology of the shore.

Formation

Shores are shaped by the action of waves and currents, which erode, transport, and deposit sediment. Tides carry material up and down the shore, creating a gently sloping surface. Longshore drift moves sediment along the coast. Shores can be classified by their geology (rocky shores, sandy shores, muddy shores) and by whether they are advancing (depositional) or retreating (erosional).

Ecology

The intertidal zone of a shore is a challenging environment: organisms there must survive alternating immersion in water and exposure to air, temperature swings, and pounding waves. Rocky shores are often zoned, with distinct bands of algae, barnacles, mussels, and predatory snails at different heights. Sandy shores support burrowing animals such as clams and sand crabs. Shore ecosystems also provide feeding and nesting habitat for seabirds and shorebirds.

Human use

Shores have long attracted human settlement for fishing, trade, transport, and recreation. Ports and harbours are built on shores, and beaches are major tourist destinations. Human activity can destabilize shores through construction, dredging, and pollution, and coastal engineering works such as seawalls, groynes, and beach nourishment are used to protect shorefront property from erosion.
