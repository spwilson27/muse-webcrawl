Hereditary angioedema
Specialty: Immunology, haematology
Cause: C1 inhibitor deficiency or dysfunction (types I/II), or other mutations
Inheritance: Autosomal dominant
Treatment: C1 inhibitor concentrate, icatibant, ecallantide; prophylaxis with lanadelumab, berotralstat

Hereditary angioedema

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare genetic disorder that causes recurrent episodes of severe swelling (angioedema) in various parts of the body, including the hands, feet, face, gastrointestinal tract, and airways. It results most often from deficiency or malfunction of the C1 inhibitor protein, which leads to excess production of the peptide bradykinin, a potent vasodilator.

Symptoms

Attacks develop over hours and typically resolve in two to five days. Swelling of the bowel wall causes severe abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, while laryngeal swelling can obstruct the airway and is life-threatening. Triggers include trauma, dental work, infection, stress, and certain medications such as ACE inhibitors and oestrogens. Antihistamines, corticosteroids, and adrenaline — effective in allergic angioedema — generally do not work in HAE because the swelling is bradykinin- rather than histamine-mediated.

Types and treatment

Type I HAE involves low levels of C1 inhibitor, type II a dysfunctional protein; a third group has normal C1 inhibitor with mutations in other genes such as factor XII. Acute attacks are treated with plasma-derived or recombinant C1 inhibitor, the bradykinin B2 receptor blocker icatibant, or the kallikrein inhibitor ecallantide. Long-term prophylaxis uses the monoclonal antibody lanadelumab or the oral kallikrein inhibitor berotralstat.
