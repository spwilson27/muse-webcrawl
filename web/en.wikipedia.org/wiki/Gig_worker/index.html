Gig workers are independent contractors, online platform workers, contract firm workers, on-call workers, and temporary workers. Gig workers enter into formal agreements with on-demand companies to provide services to the company's clients, typically through digital platforms such as mobile apps. They form a central part of the gig economy.

Background

The term "gig" originates from the music industry, where musicians are hired for single performances. The modern gig economy expanded rapidly in the 2010s with platforms such as Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Deliveroo, and TaskRabbit, which match workers with short-term tasks or rides.

Characteristics

Gig work is characterised by flexibility in hours and location, payment per task rather than salary, and the use of algorithmic management by platforms to allocate work, set prices, and rate performance. Gig workers are usually classified as independent contractors rather than employees, which typically excludes them from minimum wage protections, unemployment insurance, and employer-provided benefits in many jurisdictions.

Legal status and debate

The classification of gig workers has been contested in courts and legislatures worldwide. In the United Kingdom, the Supreme Court ruled in 2021 that Uber drivers were "workers" entitled to minimum wage and holiday pay. In California, Assembly Bill 5 (2019) sought to reclassify many gig workers as employees, while Proposition 22 (2020) exempted app-based drivers while granting some benefits.
