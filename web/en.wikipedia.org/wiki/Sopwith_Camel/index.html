Sopwith Camel

Role: Biplane fighter aircraft

Manufacturer: Sopwith Aviation Company

First flight: 22 December 1916

Introduction: June 1917

Status: Retired

Number built: 5,490

The Sopwith Camel was a British First World War single-seat biplane fighter aircraft introduced on the Western Front in 1917. Developed from the earlier Sopwith Pup, it was powered by a rotary engine and armed with twin synchronized Vickers machine guns firing through the propeller arc. The fairing covering the gun breeches formed a distinctive "hump" that gave the aircraft its name.

Design and development

Designed by Herbert Smith and the Sopwith team, the Camel concentrated its engine, armament, fuel and pilot within a very short length of the fuselage. Combined with the gyroscopic torque of its rotary engine, this gave the Camel extreme manoeuvrability — it could turn right very quickly, as torque aided the turn — but also made it unforgiving for novices. It was often said that a pilot's choices in a Camel were "a Victoria Cross, a wooden cross, or a Red Cross."

Operational history

The Camel entered service with the Royal Flying Corps in mid-1917. Despite being difficult to fly and increasingly outpaced by newer types, it was highly effective in skilled hands: Camel pilots were credited with downing 1,294 enemy aircraft, more than any other Allied fighter type. The type also served in ground-attack roles and, in a two-seat night-fighter variant (the "Comic"), defended Britain against German bomber raids. Canadian ace William George Barker and others flew the type with distinction; the fictional Snoopy of Peanuts famously imagined his doghouse to be a Sopwith Camel.
