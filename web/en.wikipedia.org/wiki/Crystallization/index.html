Crystallization

Crystallization is the process by which a solid forms whose atoms or molecules are highly organized into a structure known as a crystal. Crystals can form by precipitating from a solution, freezing, or (more rarely) deposition directly from a gas. Crystallization is also a chemical solid–liquid separation technique, in which mass transfer of a solute from the liquid solution to a pure solid crystalline phase occurs.

Process

The crystallization process consists of two major events: nucleation, the appearance of a crystalline phase from a supercooled liquid or supersaturated solvent, and crystal growth, the subsequent increase in size of the particles. Solution crystallization can be driven by cooling, evaporation of solvent, adding an anti-solvent, or chemical reaction. In an industrial crystallizer, careful control of supersaturation determines crystal size distribution and purity.

Applications

Crystallization is fundamental to the production of sugar and salt, to the purification of pharmaceuticals (where it also controls polymorphism of drug substances), to semiconductor crystal growth (e.g. the Czochralski process for silicon), and to natural processes such as the formation of snowflakes, minerals and gemstones.

Recrystallization

In chemistry, recrystallization is a common purification technique: a solid is dissolved in a hot solvent and allowed to crystallize as the solution cools, leaving impurities behind in solution. In geology, the term also describes the metamorphic rearrangement of a mineral's crystal structure under heat and pressure.
