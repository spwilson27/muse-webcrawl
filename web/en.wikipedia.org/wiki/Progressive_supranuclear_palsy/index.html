Progressive supranuclear palsy

Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), also called Steele–Richardson–Olszewski syndrome, is a rare neurodegenerative disease involving the gradual deterioration and death of specific areas of the brain. It is a tauopathy, characterized by abnormal accumulation of the tau protein.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin in the sixth or seventh decade and include loss of balance with early falls, slowed movement, stiffness, changes in personality and cognition, and a distinctive difficulty moving the eyes—especially looking down—called supranuclear vertical gaze palsy. Speech and swallowing problems develop as the disease progresses.

Cause and diagnosis

The cause is unknown; most cases are sporadic. PSP is one of the "Parkinson-plus" syndromes and is frequently misdiagnosed as Parkinson's disease, from which it differs by early falls, gaze palsy, and poor response to levodopa. MRI may show midbrain atrophy, sometimes described as the "hummingbird sign".

Treatment

There is no cure and no treatment proven to slow progression; care is supportive, addressing mobility, swallowing, and eye symptoms. Median survival after onset is roughly seven years.
