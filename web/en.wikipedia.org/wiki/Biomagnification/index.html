Biomagnification

Concentrations of persistent pollutants increase at successive trophic levels.

Biomagnification, also known as bioamplification or biological magnification, is the increase in concentration of a substance — often a pollutant such as a pesticide or heavy metal — in the tissues of organisms at successively higher levels in a food chain. It occurs when a substance is absorbed faster than it is lost by catabolism and excretion.

Process

Biomagnification requires a pollutant that is long-lived, mobile, soluble in fats, and biologically active. Because predators consume many prey over a lifetime, and because roughly only a tenth of the energy at one trophic level becomes biomass at the next, fat-soluble substances that are stored rather than excreted become progressively concentrated. Apex predators, including birds of prey, marine mammals and humans, can accumulate concentrations millions of times higher than those in the surrounding water. Biomagnification is related to, but distinct from, bioaccumulation, which describes uptake from the environment within a single organism over its lifetime.

Examples

The classic case is DDT, whose metabolite DDE caused eggshell thinning and population crashes in peregrine falcons, bald eagles and brown pelicans; Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) brought the phenomenon to public attention and helped spur the ban of DDT in many countries. Methylmercury biomagnifies in aquatic food webs, reaching hazardous levels in large predatory fish such as tuna and swordfish and causing the poisoning disaster at Minamata, Japan. Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and other persistent organic pollutants show similar behavior, particularly in Arctic food chains.
