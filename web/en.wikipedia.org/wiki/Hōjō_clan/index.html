Hōjō clan

The Hōjō clan (北条氏, Hōjō-shi) was a Japanese samurai family that controlled the Kamakura shogunate as hereditary regents (shikken) from 1203 until the shogunate's fall in 1333. Claiming descent from the Taira clan, the Hōjō rose by backing Minamoto no Yoritomo, founder of the shogunate, and then ruled Japan through a succession of shoguns whom they installed and deposed as puppets. They are distinct from the later Odawara Hōjō of the Sengoku period.

Rise to the regency

Hōjō Tokimasa supported his son-in-law Yoritomo in the Genpei War and became the first regent in 1203 after Yoritomo's death, outmanoeuvring the young shogun Yoriie. His daughter Masako, Yoritomo's widow — the "nun shogun" — was a central political figure. Under the third regent, Hōjō Yasutoki (regent 1224–1242), the regency defeated the imperial court in the Jōkyū War of 1221, tightening Kamakura's grip on Kyoto, and issued the Jōei Shikimoku (1232), the first samurai legal code, prized for its practical fairness.

The Mongol invasions and decline

The regent Hōjō Tokimune (1268–1284) refused Mongol demands for submission and organised the defence against Kublai Khan's invasions of 1274 and 1281, both broken up in part by typhoons — the famed kamikaze. But the cost of defence, with no conquered land to reward the warriors, strained the regime; internal feuds within the Hōjō and resentment among provincial samurai grew. In 1333 the forces of Emperor Go-Daigō and the turncoat Ashikaga Takauji destroyed the shogunate; the last regent, Hōjō Takatoki, and hundreds of clan members died at Kamakura's fall.
