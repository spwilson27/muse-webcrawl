Terry v. Ohio

Decided: June 10, 1968
Citation: 392 U.S. 1
Court: Supreme Court of the United States

Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States in which the Court ruled that the Fourth Amendment's prohibition on unreasonable searches and seizures is not violated when a police officer stops a suspect on the street and frisks them without probable cause to arrest, if the officer has a reasonable suspicion that the person has committed, is committing, or is about to commit a crime and has a reasonable belief that the person "may be armed and presently dangerous."

Background

John W. Terry and two other men were observed by Cleveland detective Martin McFadden repeatedly walking past a store window, leading him to suspect they were "casing" it for a robbery. McFadden stopped and grabbed Terry, patted down the outside of his clothing, and found a revolver. Terry was convicted of carrying a concealed weapon and appealed, arguing the frisk violated the Fourth Amendment.

Decision

In an 8–1 decision written by Chief Justice Earl Warren, the Court held that the stop and frisk were reasonable under the Fourth Amendment. The decision created the "stop and frisk" exception — brief investigative stops based on reasonable suspicion rather than probable cause — and the resulting police practice is often called a "Terry stop."

Legacy

The ruling remains foundational to American criminal procedure but has been criticized for its role in enabling widespread stop-and-frisk policing and racial profiling.
