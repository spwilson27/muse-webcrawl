Keswick theology

Keswick theology, also known as the Keswick movement or the Higher Life movement, is a tradition within evangelical Protestant Christianity associated with the Keswick Convention, an annual gathering held in Keswick in England's Lake District since 1875. It teaches a distinctive doctrine of sanctification: that Christians can experience a "second blessing" — a decisive post-conversion work of the Holy Spirit, received by faith and surrender — through which the power of indwelling sin is counteracted and the believer enters a life of greater holiness, victory over sin, and service.

Origins

The movement grew out of the 19th-century Holiness movement and the meetings promoted by figures such as Robert Pearsall Smith and Hannah Whitall Smith, author of The Christian's Secret of a Happy Life (1875). Conventions at Broadlands and Oxford in 1874 preceded the first Keswick Convention, founded under the leadership of Canon T. D. Harford-Battersby. Early teachers included Evan Hopkins, whose exposition shaped the movement's theology, and later figures such as F. B. Meyer, Andrew Murray (influentially, though South African), and missionary statesman Hudson Taylor's circle.

Teaching

Keswick theology is sometimes summarised as "let go and let God": the believer ceases striving, fully surrenders to Christ, and by faith receives the filling of the Holy Spirit. Classic Keswick teaching denies that the sinful nature is eradicated (unlike Wesleyan perfectionism), holding instead that the Spirit counteracts it as the believer maintains an attitude of surrender. The movement strongly promoted foreign missions, stimulating the Student Volunteer Movement and the recruitment of missionaries, including the "Cambridge Seven" to China.

Influence and criticism

Keswick teaching spread through conventions worldwide and through figures such as Watchman Nee. It has been criticised by some Reformed theologians — notably B. B. Warfield, and later J. I. Packer (while sympathetic) — for a "quietistic" passivity and for dividing Christians into classes. The Keswick Convention continues today, somewhat moderated in emphasis, as a major fixture of British evangelical life.
