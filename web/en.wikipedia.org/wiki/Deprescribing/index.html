Deprescribing

Deprescribing is the planned and supervised process of dose reduction or discontinuation of medications that may be causing harm or are no longer providing benefit. It is an important part of managing polypharmacy, particularly in older adults and people with frailty, multimorbidity, or limited life expectancy.

Rationale

As patients age, the balance of benefit and harm from long-term medicines can shift: renal and hepatic function decline, drug interactions multiply, and prescribing cascades — new drugs added to treat the adverse effects of others — can accumulate. Common targets for deprescribing include benzodiazepines and related hypnotics, anticholinergics, proton pump inhibitors without an ongoing indication, sulfonylureas in tight glycemic control, and preventive medicines whose time-to-benefit exceeds life expectancy.

Process

Deprescribing typically involves reviewing the complete medication list, identifying potentially inappropriate medications (using tools such as the Beers Criteria or STOPP/START criteria), prioritizing drugs for withdrawal, tapering where necessary to avoid withdrawal or rebound effects, and monitoring the patient for recurrence of symptoms. Shared decision-making with the patient and caregivers is central, since fear of stopping long-standing medicines is a common barrier.

Evidence

Studies associate structured medication review and deprescribing with reduced drug burden and falls, and possibly improved cognition and quality of life, though evidence for mortality benefit remains mixed.
