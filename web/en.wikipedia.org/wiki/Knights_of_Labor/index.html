The Knights of Labor, officially the Noble and Holy Order of the Knights of Labor, was an American labor federation founded in 1869 by Philadelphia garment cutters led by Uriah Smith Stephens. It was the largest and one of the most important American labor organizations of the 19th century.

Organization and aims

Originally a secret society — a common precaution against employer retaliation — the Order dropped its secrecy in 1881. Unlike craft unions, the Knights sought to organize all "producers": skilled and unskilled workers, farmers and small business owners, in a single movement, and it admitted women and Black workers on a scale unusual for its time (though it supported the Chinese Exclusion Act). Its platform demanded the eight-hour day, abolition of child labor, equal pay for equal work, a graduated income tax, and worker cooperatives that would eventually replace the wage system. Under Grand Master Workman Terence V. Powderly, who favoured education and arbitration over strikes, membership soared after the Order's successful 1885 strike against Jay Gould's Wabash Railroad, reaching roughly 700,000 in 1886.

Decline

The Knights declined rapidly after 1886: failed strikes, internal factionalism, competition from the newly founded American Federation of Labor, and public backlash following the Haymarket affair — with which the Knights were unfairly associated — drained the membership. By the mid-1890s the Order was a shadow of itself, though a vestigial organization persisted into the 20th century.

Legacy

The Knights pioneered mass industrial unionism in the United States and carried labor's cause into national politics, influencing later movements from the Populists to the industrial unions of the 1930s.
