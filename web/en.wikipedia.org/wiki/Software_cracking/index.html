Software cracking

Software cracking (known as "breaking" mostly in the 1980s) is the modification of software to remove or disable features which are considered undesirable by the person cracking the software, especially copy protection features including protection against the manipulation of software, serial numbers, hardware keys, date checks and disc checks.

Disassembly and debugging tools are central to the practice of software cracking

Description

A crack refers to the means of achieving software cracking, for example a stolen serial number or a tool that performs that act of cracking. Some of these tools are called keygen, patch, or loader. A keygen is a handmade product serial number generator that often offers the ability to generate working serial numbers in your own name. A patch is a small computer program that modifies the machine code of another program. A loader modifies the startup flow of a program and does not remove the protection; it is widely used for circumventing protection schemes such as online activation.

History

Software cracking grew alongside the home computer software market of the early 1980s. On systems such as the Commodore 64 and ZX Spectrum, groups of crackers competed to be first to release "cracked" versions of games, adding introductory screens ("cracktros") that later fed into the demoscene. The spread of dial-up bulletin board systems and later the Internet industrialised distribution through the warez scene.

Methods

  • Binary patching: altering conditional jumps or return values in the executable so licence checks always pass.
  • Key generation (keygens): reverse-engineering the serial-validation algorithm and reproducing it.
  • Loaders: launching the target and modifying it in memory at run time.
  • Server emulation: replacing activation servers with local substitutes.

Legal aspects

The legality of software cracking varies by jurisdiction. Circumvention of technological protection measures is restricted under laws such as section 1201 of the U.S. Digital Millennium Copyright Act and Article 6 of the EU Copyright Directive. Distributing cracks is commonly treated as copyright infringement facilitation, while limited exceptions exist for interoperability, security research and preservation.
