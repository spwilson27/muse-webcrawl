A head-up display, or HUD, is any transparent display that presents data without requiring the user to look away from their usual viewpoint. The origin of the name stems from a pilot being able to view information with the head positioned "up" and looking forward, instead of angled down looking at lower instruments. A HUD also has the advantage that the pilot's eyes do not need to refocus to view the outside after looking at the optically nearer instruments.

Principles

A typical HUD combines a projector unit, a combiner (a partially reflective glass plate), and a computer generating symbology. The image is collimated — focused at optical infinity — so that flight data such as airspeed, altitude, attitude, and a flight-path vector appear superimposed on the outside world. Helmet-mounted displays extend the same concept to the pilot's helmet visor.

History

HUDs evolved from the reflector gunsights of Second World War fighters. The first true HUDs entered service in military aircraft in the late 1950s and 1960s (notably the Blackburn Buccaneer), spreading to fighters such as the F-14 and F-16, and later to civil airliners, where head-up guidance systems aid low-visibility approaches.

Automotive and other uses

General Motors introduced the first production automotive HUD in the 1988 Oldsmobile Cutlass Supreme, projecting speed onto the windshield; such systems now commonly display navigation prompts and driver-assistance warnings, including augmented-reality variants. HUD-style interfaces also appear in video games, smart glasses, and diving masks.
