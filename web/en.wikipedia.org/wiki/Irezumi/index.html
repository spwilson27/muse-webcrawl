Irezumi (入れ墨, literally "inserting ink") is the traditional Japanese style of tattooing, and the word also refers to tattoos in general in Japanese. Full irezumi compositions can cover the entire back, arms and legs, forming a body suit designed as a single unified image flowing with the body's contours. The art form is especially associated with the craftsmen, labourers, firefighters and, notoriously, the yakuza of the Edo and Meiji periods.

History

Tattooing in Japan dates to the Jōmon and Yayoi periods, and by the Edo period decorative full-body tattooing flourished among urban working men, inspired in part by the illustrated edition of the Chinese novel Water Margin (Suikoden), whose heroes bore elaborate tattoos. Skilled tattoo masters (horishi) such as the Horiuno line of Asakusa elevated the craft. Tattoos were banned in the Meiji period (1872) as Japan sought Western approval, driving the art underground; the ban was lifted under the American occupation in 1948. During the Meiji ban, Japanese masters famously tattooed visiting Western sailors and even royalty, including the future George V.

Technique and motifs

Traditionally irezumi is applied by tebori — hand-poking with a bundle of needles mounted on a bamboo or metal handle — using soot-based black ink and a limited palette of colours. Classic imagery draws on folklore and ukiyo-e: dragons symbolising wisdom, koi for perseverance, tigers, peonies, cherry blossoms, crashing waves (seigaiha) and Buddhist deities. Though increasingly mainstream in Japan, tattoos still carry stigma: many onsen, sentō and gyms restrict entry for tattooed bathers.
