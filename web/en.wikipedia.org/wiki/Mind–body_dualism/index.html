Mind–body dualism

Mind–body dualism is the view in the philosophy of mind that mental phenomena are non-physical, or that the mind and body are distinct and separable. It stands opposed to monist positions such as physicalism and neutral monism.

Cartesian dualism

René Descartes gave the view its classic form: the mind is a thinking, unextended substance (res cogitans), while the body is an extended, unthinking substance (res extensa). Descartes proposed that the two interact in the pineal gland. Critics, beginning with Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, pressed the interaction problem: how can an immaterial substance causally affect a material one?

Variants and criticism

Variants include property dualism, which holds that mental properties are irreducible to physical properties, and epiphenomenalism. Dualism remains a minority position in contemporary philosophy of mind but continues to be defended against physicalist accounts of consciousness.
