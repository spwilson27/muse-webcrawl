Coverage (filmmaking)

Coverage, in filmmaking, is the practice of filming a scene from multiple camera angles, shot sizes and positions so that the editor has a variety of takes and viewpoints from which to assemble the finished scene. The term also refers to the total amount of usable footage obtained for a scene.

Standard coverage

A typical coverage pattern for a dialogue scene begins with a master shot — a wide shot that contains the entire scene from beginning to end. The director then shoots progressively closer material: medium shots, close-ups of each actor, over-the-shoulder shots, two-shots and insert shots of important props or actions. Because the whole scene is repeated for each setup, the editor can cut between angles for emphasis, compress time, or rescue a performance by using the best portions of different takes.

Purpose and trade-offs

Coverage gives the production control in the editing room: it allows pacing to be adjusted, reactions to be highlighted, and continuity errors to be worked around. The downside is cost — every new camera setup requires relighting and repositioning, so extensive coverage takes time. Some directors, such as Clint Eastwood, are known for minimal coverage and few takes, while others shoot large amounts of coverage to maximize editorial options. In television, where schedules are tight, coverage is often planned carefully and shot quickly, sometimes with two cameras simultaneously.

Script coverage

The term "coverage" also has an unrelated meaning in the film industry: a written analysis and summary of a screenplay, prepared by a reader for producers, studios or agencies evaluating whether to develop the project.
