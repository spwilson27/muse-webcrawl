The Skunk Works is an official pseudonym for Lockheed Martin's Advanced Development Programs (ADP), a division responsible for developing advanced and secret aircraft and technologies. Founded in 1943 under Clarence "Kelly" Johnson, it pioneered the model of a small, empowered, semi-autonomous team working with minimal bureaucracy — an approach so influential that "skunk works" became a generic business term for any isolated innovation group.

History

In June 1943, the U.S. Army Air Forces urgently needed a jet fighter to counter German jets. Johnson's hand-picked team designed and built the XP-80 Shooting Star prototype in just 143 days, working in a rented circus tent beside a malodorous plastics factory in Burbank — the smell prompting the nickname, borrowed from the "Skonk Works" in Al Capp's Li'l Abner comic strip. The spelling was later changed to "Skunk Works" and trademarked.

Under Johnson's famous "14 rules" of management, the division produced a string of landmark aircraft: the U-2 spyplane (1955), the Mach 3+ SR-71 Blackbird (1964) and its A-12 predecessor for the CIA, the subsonic F-104 Starfighter, and the radar-evading F-117 Nighthawk stealth attack aircraft (first flight 1981, revealed 1988). Johnson's successor Ben Rich led the stealth era and wrote the memoir Skunk Works (1994).

Later programmes

The division contributed to the F-22 Raptor and F-35 Lightning II, the X-35 demonstrator, unmanned systems such as the RQ-170 Sentinel, the X-47 and Sea Ghost concepts, hypersonic research (SR-72 concept), and the X-59 QueSST low-boom supersonic demonstrator for NASA, which first flew in 2025. Its work remains largely classified.

Philosophy

Kelly Johnson's rules stress trust between customer and contractor, tiny project staffs, simple drawings and release systems, rigorous cost control, and giving the project manager near-total authority. The Skunk Works model has been widely imitated in technology and defence industries.
