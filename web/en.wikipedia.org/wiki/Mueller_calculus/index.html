Mueller calculus

Mueller calculus is a matrix method in optics for describing the polarisation of light and the effect of optical elements on it. Introduced by Hans Mueller in the 1940s, it represents the polarisation state of a light beam by a four-component Stokes vector and each optical element by a 4×4 real matrix, the Mueller matrix. Multiplying the input Stokes vector by the matrices of successive elements gives the output polarisation state.

Stokes vectors and Mueller matrices

The Stokes vector S = (S0, S1, S2, S3) describes total intensity and the amounts of linear and circular polarisation. Unlike the Jones calculus, which works with electric-field amplitudes and applies only to fully polarised, coherent light, Mueller calculus works with intensities and can describe unpolarised and partially polarised light, as well as depolarising elements such as scattering media. A Mueller matrix has up to 16 independent elements encoding diattenuation, retardance, polarisance and depolarisation.

Applications

Mueller calculus is widely used in polarimetry: ellipsometry of thin films, remote sensing, biomedical imaging (where polarised light reveals tissue structure), and the characterisation of optical components. Complete Mueller-matrix polarimeters measure all 16 elements of a sample's matrix, from which physical properties such as birefringence and scattering behaviour can be extracted. The formalism requires Mueller matrices to satisfy physical-realisation constraints, notably those expressed through the associated coherency matrix being positive semidefinite.
