Technetium-99m is the workhorse tracer of nuclear medicine imaging

Technetium-99m (symbol 99mTc) is a metastable nuclear isomer of technetium-99. It is the most commonly used radioisotope in diagnostic nuclear medicine, employed in tens of millions of procedures each year — by some estimates around 80% of all nuclear medicine scans worldwide.

Properties

Technetium-99m decays by isomeric transition with a half-life of about 6.0 hours, emitting a gamma ray of 140.5 keV — an energy well suited to gamma cameras and SPECT scanners — with very little accompanying particulate radiation, which keeps patient dose low. The six-hour half-life is long enough to prepare and administer radiopharmaceuticals but short enough that radioactivity largely leaves the body within days; the decay product, technetium-99, is itself only very weakly radioactive.

Production and supply

Because of its short half-life, technetium-99m cannot be stockpiled. It is obtained on-site from a molybdenum-99/technetium-99m generator — a shielded column of alumina loaded with parent molybdenum-99 (half-life 66 hours) from which the daughter isotope is "milked" by elution with saline, typically daily. Molybdenum-99 is produced mainly by fission of uranium targets in a small number of ageing research reactors, making the supply chain fragile: reactor outages in 2009–2010 caused worldwide shortages, spurring efforts to develop cyclotron and non-reactor production routes.

Medical uses

Technetium-99m is chelated to a variety of carrier molecules to image different organs: pertechnetate itself for thyroid and salivary glands; sestamibi (Cardiolite) for myocardial perfusion and parathyroid scans; MDP for bone scans; MAG3 for renal function; HMPAO for cerebral blood flow; sulfur colloid for liver, spleen, and sentinel-node studies; and labelled red cells for blood-pool imaging. Its versatile chemistry — technetium can adopt many oxidation states and coordination complexes — is a major reason for its dominance.

History

The isotope was identified in 1938 by Emilio Segrè and Glenn Seaborg at Berkeley, shortly after the discovery of technetium itself. Its medical career began in the early 1960s with the development of the generator at Brookhaven National Laboratory, and expanded rapidly as gamma cameras improved.
