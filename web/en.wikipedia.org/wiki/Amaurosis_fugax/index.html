Amaurosis fugax

Vision loss often described as a descending shade
SpecialtyOphthalmology, neurology
SymptomsPainless transient monocular vision loss, seconds to minutes
Usual causeRetinal embolism from carotid artery disease
SignificanceWarning sign of impending stroke (a retinal TIA)

Amaurosis fugax (from Greek amaurosis, "darkening", and Latin fugax, "fleeting") is a painless, temporary loss of vision in one eye, typically lasting seconds to a few minutes, caused by reduced blood flow to the retina. Patients classically describe a grey or black shade descending (or ascending) over the vision of one eye, after which sight returns completely. The term is often used loosely for transient monocular visual loss of any cause.

Causes and evaluation

The most important cause is embolism: small fragments of atherosclerotic plaque or clot from a stenosed carotid artery (or, less often, the heart) lodge briefly in the retinal circulation. Hollenhorst plaques — bright cholesterol emboli — may be seen on fundoscopy. Other causes include vasospasm, giant cell arteritis (a particular concern in the elderly), severe carotid stenosis with hypoperfusion, and raised intracranial or intraocular pressure. Because amaurosis fugax is effectively a transient ischaemic attack of the eye, and carries a significant short-term risk of stroke, it prompts urgent evaluation: carotid imaging, cardiac rhythm and echocardiographic assessment, blood pressure and lipid management, and consideration of antiplatelet therapy and carotid endarterectomy for severe symptomatic stenosis.

Prognosis

Vision itself usually recovers fully, though repeated episodes can cause permanent retinal infarction. The episode's main importance is as a warning event: patients with embolic amaurosis fugax have an increased annual risk of stroke and myocardial infarction, and prompt risk-factor treatment substantially lowers it.
