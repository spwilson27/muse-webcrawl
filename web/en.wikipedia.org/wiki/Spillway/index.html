Spillway

A spillway is a structure used to provide the controlled release of water from a dam or levee into a downstream area, typically the riverbed of the dammed river itself. Spillways ensure that water does not damage structures that are not designed to convey it, and prevent the dam from being overtopped, which could lead to its failure. In the United Kingdom, spillways are sometimes called overflow channels.

Types

Controlled spillways are fitted with gates that can be raised or lowered to regulate the discharge. Uncontrolled spillways discharge automatically whenever the reservoir level rises above the spillway crest. The most common profile for a dam spillway is the ogee, an S-shaped curve designed to match the underside of a freely falling nappe of water. Other types include chute spillways, which carry water down an open channel; shaft (morning glory) spillways, which funnel water into a vertical shaft, as at Monticello Dam in California; side-channel and labyrinth spillways, the latter using a folded plan shape to increase crest length; siphon spillways; and stepped spillways, which dissipate energy along their steps. Most dams also have a lower-capacity auxiliary spillway, and an emergency spillway designed to be used only in extreme floods.

Design considerations

Spillways are sized for a design flood, up to the probable maximum flood. Discharge must be passed without eroding the dam or its foundations, so high-velocity flows are usually delivered to a stilling basin or flip bucket at the toe. Damage can occur through cavitation — the formation and collapse of vapour bubbles in high-velocity flow — which famously damaged the spillway tunnels of Hoover Dam during the 1983 floods. The 2017 Oroville Dam crisis, in which erosion of both the main and emergency spillways forced the evacuation of some 188,000 people downstream, illustrated the consequences of spillway failure on a large dam.
