Protein folding

Protein folding is the physical process by which a protein, consisting of a linear chain of amino acids, folds into its characteristic and functional three-dimensional structure, known as the native structure. When proteins are synthesized as ribosome products, they emerge as long, flexible, unstructured chains of amino acids, called polypeptides, and must fold into their final shape in order to become biologically functional. The folding process is driven by hydrophobic interactions, intramolecular hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and opposing entropic forces, and may be assisted in the cell by molecular chaperones.

Although the amino acid sequence of a protein—its primary structure—contains all the information needed to specify its native fold, understanding exactly how that information maps onto a three-dimensional shape, the so-called protein folding problem, has been one of the central challenges of molecular biology since the 1960s. Incorrectly folded proteins can aggregate and cause diseases including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and the prion diseases. Remarkable progress in predicting folds computationally came with AlphaFold in 2020.

History

The idea that a protein's structure is encoded in its sequence dates to experiments by Christian B. Anfinsen in the early 1960s on the enzyme ribonuclease A. Anfinsen showed that the enzyme, whose function had been destroyed by denaturation with urea and reduction of its disulfide bonds, spontaneously refolded into its active form when the denaturing conditions were removed—even though the disulfide bonds could in principle have re-formed in many incorrect pairings. He concluded that the amino acid sequence itself determines the native conformation, a principle later called Anfinsen's dogma (or the thermodynamic hypothesis). Anfinsen shared the 1972 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

In 1969 Cyrus Levinthal pointed out a puzzle—Levinthal's paradox—showing that folding cannot proceed by random search of all possible conformations. During the 1980s and 1990s, energy landscape theory, developed by researchers including Peter Wolynes and José Onuchic, reframed folding as a biased diffusion on a funnel-shaped energy surface rather than a search through discrete pathways.

The folding process

Levels of structure

Protein structure is conventionally described at four levels. The primary structure is the amino acid sequence. Local hydrogen-bonded patterns—alpha helices, beta sheets, and turns—form the secondary structure. The overall three-dimensional arrangement of a single chain is the tertiary structure, and the assembly of multiple folded subunits is the quaternary structure. Folding proper refers to the acquisition of secondary and tertiary structure.

Kinetics

Small single-domain proteins may fold in microseconds to milliseconds, whereas large or multi-domain proteins can take minutes. Folding often begins while the chain is still being synthesized on the ribosome (co-translational folding). Many proteins fold through intermediate states, such as the compact but loosely packed molten globule, before reaching the native state. The rate-limiting steps commonly include the formation of slow proline cis–trans isomerizations and the correct pairing of disulfide bonds.

Driving forces

The native state is (usually) the conformation of lowest Gibbs free energy under physiological conditions, but the margin is small—typically only about 5–15 kcal/mol—so proteins are only marginally stable. The principal contributions are:

  • Hydrophobic effect. Nonpolar side chains are buried in the protein's interior, away from water; the resulting increase in the entropy of the surrounding water is the dominant driving force for collapse into a compact globule.
  • Hydrogen bonds. Backbone carbonyl and amide groups form hydrogen bonds in helices and sheets; unfulfilled buried hydrogen bonds are strongly destabilizing.
  • Van der Waals forces. The tight packing of the core gives favourable dispersion interactions.
  • Electrostatic interactions. Salt bridges and charge–dipole interactions contribute modestly and often confer specificity rather than bulk stability.
  • Conformational entropy. Folding restricts the chain's freedom, which opposes folding and must be paid for by the interactions above.

Levinthal's paradox and energy landscapes

Levinthal estimated that a protein of about 100 residues, if each residue could adopt only a handful of conformations and the chain sampled them at random, would require vastly longer than the age of the universe to find its native state—yet real proteins fold in seconds or less. The resolution is that folding is not a random search: local structure forms rapidly and guides the chain, and the energy landscape is funnel-shaped, so that most partially folded conformations are progressively closer in energy to the native state. This "folding funnel" picture, formalized by energy landscape theory and the principle of minimal frustration, reconciles fast folding with the vastness of conformational space.

Chaperones and cellular folding machinery

Inside the crowded cell, exposed hydrophobic surfaces of folding intermediates can cause aggregation. Molecular chaperones assist folding without forming part of the final structure. The Hsp70 family binds exposed hydrophobic stretches of nascent chains and, using ATP, helps prevent aggregation. The barrel-shaped chaperonins, exemplified by bacterial GroEL/GroES, enclose a single folding chain in a protected chamber. Other machinery includes protein disulfide isomerases, which catalyse correct disulfide pairing, and peptidyl-prolyl isomerases, which accelerate proline isomerization. When folding fails irretrievably, proteins are targeted for degradation by the proteasome as part of protein quality control.

Protein misfolding and disease

Failure to fold, or to remain folded, underlies a large class of diseases. Misfolded proteins often expose sticky surfaces that assemble into ordered aggregates called amyloid fibrils, rich in cross-beta structure. Examples include the amyloid-beta and tau aggregates of Alzheimer's disease, alpha-synuclein in Parkinson's disease, and huntingtin in Huntington's disease. The prion protein PrP is unusual in that its misfolded form can template the same misfold in normal copies, making the diseases—Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, kuru, and bovine spongiform encephalopathy—transmissible. Loss-of-function misfolding also occurs: in cystic fibrosis, the common ΔF508 variant of CFTR is retained and degraded rather than reaching the cell surface.

Experimental methods

The folded structures themselves are determined chiefly by X-ray crystallography, NMR spectroscopy, and cryo-electron microscopy, with structures deposited in the Protein Data Bank. Folding dynamics are studied with stopped-flow and temperature-jump kinetics, circular dichroism, fluorescence and FRET, hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry, and single-molecule techniques such as optical tweezers. Ultra-fast-folding proteins and the distributed computing project Folding@home have been used to reach the microsecond-to-millisecond timescales on which folding occurs.

Computational prediction

Predicting a protein's structure from its sequence alone—protein structure prediction—was assessed for decades through the biennial CASP experiments. Physics-based molecular dynamics can simulate folding directly for small, fast-folding proteins but remains limited by timescale. At CASP14 in 2020, DeepMind's AlphaFold 2 achieved median accuracy comparable to experimental methods for many targets, widely regarded as effectively solving the single-chain structure prediction problem; its developers Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared part of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Predicting folding pathways and dynamics, the effects of mutations, and structures of complexes and disordered regions remain active problems.
