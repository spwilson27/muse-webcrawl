Self-determination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Self-determination is the principle of international law and politics that a people has the right to freely choose its sovereignty and political status, and to pursue its economic, social and cultural development without external compulsion. Enshrined in the UN Charter (1945) and the twin human-rights covenants (1966, common Article 1: "All peoples have the right of self-determination"), it was the legal engine of decolonisation — and the most contested right in the international order, because it collides permanently with the rival principle of states' territorial integrity.

History and hard cases

The idea descends from the American and French Revolutions; Woodrow Wilson made national self-determination the slogan of the post-1918 settlement, applied selectively in Europe and denied to the colonies — a hypocrisy the colonised world noted and later repaid. The UN's 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence treated colonial peoples' independence as self-determination's settled meaning, and dozens of states were born under it (including Nkrumah's Ghana). The question that never closes is who counts as a "people" and whether the right includes secession from a non-colonial state: international practice admits it, if at all, only as a last resort against extreme oppression ("remedial secession"). Quebec's referendums (Canada's Supreme Court 1998: no unilateral right, but a duty to negotiate after a clear vote), Kosovo's contested 2008 independence (ICJ 2010: the declaration itself violated no law), Scotland 2014 (consensual, lost), Catalonia 2017 (unconsented, suppressed) and Crimea 2014 (a referendum under occupation, rejected by most states) are the modern casebook. Indigenous peoples won a qualified version in the UN Declaration of 2007. The principle remains simultaneously the charter of liberation and the standing invitation to redraw maps — the hinge between the nation state ideal and the world's refusal to match it.
