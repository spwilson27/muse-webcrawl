Externalism

Externalism is a group of related doctrines in the philosophy of mind and philosophy of language holding that some features of mental states, meanings, or justified beliefs depend essentially on factors outside the individual's body or brain. Its opposite, internalism, holds that such features are wholly determined by what is inside the subject.

Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment is the classic argument for semantic externalism.

Semantic externalism

Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment (1975) imagines a planet identical to Earth except that its "water" is a different chemical, XYZ rather than H2O. A speaker there means something different by "water" even though the speakers are internally identical — so meaning is not fixed by internal states alone. Tyler Burge extended the argument to beliefs shaped by the speaker's linguistic community ("social externalism"), as in his "arthritis" thought experiment.

Content externalism

Content externalism applies the same conclusion to mental states: two molecule-for-molecule identical individuals in different environments can have thoughts with different contents, because content is partly constituted by causal and social relations to the environment. This raises a well-known tension with "privileged access" — how a person can know their own thoughts if those thoughts depend on external facts.

Epistemological externalism

In epistemology, externalism about justification — most prominently reliabilism — holds that a belief can be justified by reliable processes even if the believer cannot show from the inside that those processes are reliable. This contrasts with internalist demands that justification be accessible to reflection.

Active externalism

Andy Clark and David Chalmers' "extended mind" thesis (1998) proposes an active externalism in which cognitive processes themselves can extend into notebooks, phones and other environmental props — a claim distinct from, but often discussed alongside, content externalism.
