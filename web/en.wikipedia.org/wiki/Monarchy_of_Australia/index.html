The monarchy of Australia is a key component of Australia's form of government, embodied by the Australian sovereign and head of state. The Australian monarchy is a constitutional one, modelled principally on the Westminster system of parliamentary government, while incorporating features unique to the constitution of Australia.

The present monarch is Charles III, who ascended the throne on the death of Elizabeth II on 8 September 2022. Because the monarch resides in the United Kingdom, most of the sovereign's constitutional and ceremonial duties in Australia are performed by the governor-general, appointed by the monarch on the advice of the Australian prime minister. Each Australian state also has a governor representing the sovereign in that state.

History

Australia's connection to the Crown dates to British colonisation from 1788. The separate colonies federated in 1901 as the Commonwealth of Australia, a dominion of the British Empire. The Statute of Westminster 1931, adopted by Australia in 1942, confirmed legislative independence, and the Royal Style and Titles Acts progressively established a distinct title for the sovereign "of Australia." The Australia Acts 1986 severed remaining constitutional links with the United Kingdom.

Constitutional role

Under the constitution, executive power is vested in the monarch and exercised by the governor-general; by convention, however, both act on the advice of ministers responsible to Parliament. The governor-general assents to legislation, appoints the prime minister and ministry, and may — in exceptional circumstances — exercise reserve powers, most controversially in the 1975 dismissal of the Whitlam government.

Republic debate

Australia held a referendum in 1999 on becoming a republic with a president appointed by Parliament; the proposal was defeated. Debate over an Australian head of state continues, though no new referendum has been scheduled.
