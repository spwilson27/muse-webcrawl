Lord of Galloway

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lord of Galloway was the title held by the rulers of Galloway, a semi-independent Gaelic-speaking lordship in south-western Scotland, during the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The lords of Galloway maintained a distinct identity from the Scottish crown to the north, frequently asserting their independence while intermarrying with both Scottish and English royal houses.

Origins

The lordship emerged in the aftermath of the fragmentation of the kingdom of Strathclyde and the Norse-Gaelic world of the Irish Sea. Its first historically secure ruler was Fergus of Galloway (died 1161), who appears in charters of David I of Scotland but seems to have ruled Galloway with considerable autonomy. Fergus founded the Cistercian abbey of Dundrennan and was the ancestor of the dynasty that ruled Galloway for the next two centuries.

The dynasty

On Fergus's death the lordship was divided between his sons Gille Brigte and Uhtred, whose rivalry ended with Uhtred's murder in 1174. Gille Brigte's line continued through his son Donnchadh, who received the earldom of Carrick, while Uhtred's son Lochlann (Roland) reunited Galloway proper. Lochlann's son Alan of Galloway (died 1234) was the most powerful of the line, serving as Constable of Scotland and acquiring interests in Ulster and the Isle of Man.

End of the lordship

Alan left no surviving legitimate sons, and on his death Alexander II divided Galloway among Alan's three daughters and their husbands, Roger de Quincy, John de Balliol and William de Forz. The Galwegians resisted the partition, but a revolt in 1235 was crushed, and Galloway was thereafter absorbed into the ordinary structures of the Scottish kingdom, bringing the distinct lordship to an end.
