Choquet theory

Choquet theory represents points of a compact convex set as averages of its extreme points.

In functional analysis and convex analysis, Choquet theory, named after Gustave Choquet, is an area of mathematics concerning measures on compact convex sets. Its central result, the Choquet integral representation theorem, states that every point of a compact convex subset of a locally convex topological vector space can be represented as the barycenter (resultant) of a probability measure supported on the set of extreme points of the set.

Background

The Krein–Milman theorem states that a compact convex set is the closed convex hull of its extreme points, so every point of the set is a limit of finite convex combinations of extreme points. Choquet theory strengthens this picture for metrizable compact convex sets: not only can every point be approximated by convex combinations of extreme points, but the representing measure can actually be chosen to live on the extreme points themselves (more precisely, on a measurable superset of them of full measure).

Choquet's theorem

Let K be a compact convex subset of a metrizable locally convex space. Then for every point x in K there exists a probability measure μ on K, supported on the set of extreme points of K, such that x is the barycenter of μ: that is, f(x) = ∫ f(y) dμ(y) for every continuous linear functional f. A compact convex set for which this representing measure is unique for every point is called a Choquet simplex; examples include finite-dimensional simplices and the infinite-dimensional Poulsen simplex.

Applications

Choquet theory has applications in potential theory (Choquet's integral and capacitability), ergodic theory (ergodic decomposition of invariant measures), operator algebras, and the theory of function algebras, where states of a function algebra are represented by measures on its Shilov boundary.
