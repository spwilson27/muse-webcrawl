Universal Credit

Universal Credit is a United Kingdom social security payment that combines six means-tested benefits and tax credits into a single monthly payment for people in or out of work. It was introduced under the Welfare Reform Act 2012 and is administered by the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP). It gradually replaced income-based Jobseeker's Allowance, income-related Employment and Support Allowance, Income Support, Housing Benefit, Child Tax Credit, and Working Tax Credit — the so-called "legacy benefits."

Design

Universal Credit is paid monthly in arrears and is designed to taper gradually as earnings rise, so that claimants are better off in work. The claim is normally made and managed online through a digital account. The standard allowance varies by age and whether the claim is single or joint, with additional elements for children, disability or limited capability for work, caring responsibilities, and housing costs.

History and criticism

The policy was championed by Iain Duncan Smith, Secretary of State for Work and Pensions, as the centrepiece of the coalition government's welfare reforms. Rollout proved far slower than planned, with repeated timetable revisions and major IT difficulties. The benefit has been controversial: critics, including the National Audit Office and parliamentary committees, have highlighted the minimum five-week wait for a first payment, the two-child limit on the child element, sanctions, and links to increased food bank use and rent arrears. Supporters argue it simplifies the system and improves work incentives compared with the legacy benefits it replaced.

By the mid-2020s several million households were receiving Universal Credit, and the managed migration of remaining legacy-benefit claimants proceeded through the decade.
