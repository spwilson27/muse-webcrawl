Sino-Korean vocabulary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sino-Korean vocabulary (Korean: 한자어, hanja-eo, "Hanja words") is the set of Korean words that originated in Chinese, or were coined from Chinese-derived morphemes, read with Sino-Korean pronunciations. Sino-Korean words account for roughly 57 percent of the Korean lexicon in dictionary counts, and a still larger share of formal, technical and academic vocabulary, though native Korean words dominate the most frequent everyday items.

History

Chinese vocabulary entered Korean over many centuries of contact, above all through the adoption of literary Chinese as the language of government, scholarship and Buddhism on the peninsula. The bulk of the stratum reflects Middle Chinese pronunciation as it was systematised during the Unified Silla and Goryeo periods, which is why Sino-Korean readings often preserve features — such as final -p, -t and -k consonants — that have disappeared from Mandarin. After the invention of Hangul in the fifteenth century, Sino-Korean words continued to be written with Hanja characters until twentieth-century script reforms; South Korea now writes them mostly in Hangul, while they remain instantly recognisable to educated readers.

Characteristics

Sino-Korean words are read with a fixed correspondence between each Hanja character and a Korean syllable. Because the mapping of Middle Chinese sounds into Korean collapsed many distinctions, Sino-Korean vocabulary contains large numbers of homophones, distinguished in writing (when Hanja are used) and by context in speech. The stratum supplies most Korean terms for government, science, religion and abstraction, and Sino-Korean numerals are used alongside native numerals in complementary grammatical contexts. Some words were coined in Korea itself, or in Japan during the colonial period and adopted into Korean, from the same Chinese morpheme stock.

Comparison

Sino-Korean, Sino-Japanese and Sino-Vietnamese vocabularies overlap extensively because all three languages drew on the same written Chinese tradition; cognate compounds for concepts such as "society", "science" and "telephone" can be recognised across the three languages, albeit with language-specific pronunciations.
