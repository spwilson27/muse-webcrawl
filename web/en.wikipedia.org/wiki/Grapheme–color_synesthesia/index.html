Grapheme–color synesthesia

For a grapheme–color synesthete, each letter or digit is consistently associated with a specific colour.

Grapheme–color synesthesia is a form of synesthesia in which an individual's perception of numerals and letters is associated with the experience of colors. Like all forms of synesthesia, grapheme–color synesthesia is involuntary, consistent and memorable.

Characteristics

People with this condition report that letters and digits evoke particular hues — for example, "A" might always seem red and "7" always green. The associations are idiosyncratic: large studies, notably by Julia Simner and colleagues, find some population-level tendencies (for instance, A is commonly red, and higher digits may be associated with brighter colours), but no two synesthetes share the same palette. The colour may be experienced as mentally associated with the grapheme, or, for "projectors", perceived as if printed on the page; "associators" experience the colour in the mind's eye.

Research history

The phenomenon was described in the 19th century — Francis Galton reported cases in the 1880s — but fell from scientific favour during the behaviourist era. It returned to mainstream research from the 1980s onward through the work of Richard Cytowic, Simon Baron-Cohen, Vilayanur Ramachandran and others. Consistency testing (giving subjects unannounced re-tests of their associations months later) distinguishes genuine synesthetes, who score near-perfectly, from controls relying on memory.

Mechanisms

Neuroscientific explanations invoke cross-activation or disinhibited feedback between adjacent cortical areas: the grapheme region and colour area V4 both lie in the fusiform gyrus, making grapheme–color the most common form of synesthesia. Learning during childhood (e.g. coloured letter toys) may influence individual associations.
