Sanbenito

A sanbenito (Spanish: sambenito; from saco bendito, "blessed sack") was a penitential garment, typically of yellow cloth, worn by heretics condemned by the Spanish Inquisition during the public ceremony of the auto-da-fé, and afterward as a mark of infamy. Together with the conical cap called a coroza, it formed the distinctive dress of those "reconciled" to the Church and of those sentenced to be burned.

Description

The design of the sanbenito indicated the wearer's sentence. Those who had repented and been reconciled wore a garment marked with the red saltire (cross of Saint Andrew), sometimes with flames pointing downward, signifying they had escaped the fire. Those condemned to death wore black or yellow garments painted with flames pointing upward and with demons, signifying impenitent heresy; if the condemned showed repentance at the last moment they were strangled before burning. The accompanying coroza bore similar imagery and often the charge.

Infamy after punishment

After the ceremony, the sanbenitos of the condemned — bearing their names — were hung permanently in parish churches as a public record of dishonour, shaming the penitent's family for generations and barring descendants from offices and professions. The obligation to wear the garment could itself form part of the sentence for reconciled heretics, ranging from months to life. The practice persisted in varying form in Spain, Portugal, and their colonies in the Americas into the 18th century; sanbenitos were still being displayed in some churches long after the Inquisition's abolition in 1834.
