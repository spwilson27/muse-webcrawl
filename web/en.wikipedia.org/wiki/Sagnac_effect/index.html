Sagnac effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sagnac effect, named after French physicist Georges Sagnac, is a phenomenon encountered in interferometry: when a beam of light is split and sent in opposite directions around a closed loop mounted on a rotating platform, the beam travelling with the rotation returns slightly later than the counter-rotating beam. The resulting phase or time difference is proportional to the area enclosed by the loop and to the rate of rotation.

History

Sagnac demonstrated the effect in 1913 with a rotating interferometer, interpreting it (incorrectly) as evidence for the luminiferous aether. The result is in fact fully consistent with special relativity: the rotating frame is non-inertial, and the effect was later confirmed on a grand scale by Michelson, Gale and Pearson in 1925, who measured the Earth's rotation with a large fixed interferometer. Sagnac corrections must also be applied in satellite navigation and time-transfer systems.

Applications

The effect is the operating principle of optical gyroscopes: ring laser gyroscopes and fibre-optic gyroscopes measure rotation without moving parts and are used in aircraft inertial navigation and spacecraft attitude control. Large ring lasers at geodetic observatories monitor minute variations in the Earth's rotation. In the Global Positioning System, Sagnac corrections of up to roughly 100 nanoseconds are significant at the accuracy levels required.
