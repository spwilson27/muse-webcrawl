Conflict of laws

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conflict of laws, also called private international law in civil law jurisdictions, is the set of legal principles that determine which jurisdiction's law should apply to a dispute with connections to more than one legal system. Despite its name, it is largely domestic law: each state has its own conflict-of-laws rules, developed nationally and, in some regions, harmonised by treaty or regulation.

The three principal questions

  1. Jurisdiction — does the court have the power to hear the case, or should it decline in favour of a foreign court (for example on forum non conveniens grounds)?
  2. Choice of law — which jurisdiction's substantive law governs the issue?
  3. Recognition and enforcement — will the resulting foreign judgment be recognised and enforced locally?

Choice-of-law method

Classical method proceeds in stages. The court first characterises the issue (as contract, tort, property, family law, and so on), then applies its choice-of-law rule for that category to identify the governing law by a connecting factor — e.g., the place of the wrong (lex loci delicti), the place where property is situated (lex situs), the parties' domicile or nationality, or the law chosen by the parties (lex voluntatis). Incidental complications include renvoi, where the foreign system's own conflict rules refer the question back to the forum or onward to a third system; most jurisdictions reject or limit renvoi outside areas such as succession and title to land.

In the United States, the First Restatement (1934), associated with Joseph Beale, applied rigid territorial rules; the Second Restatement (1971) adopted a flexible "most significant relationship" test. Other American approaches include Brainerd Currie's governmental interest analysis. In the European Union, the Rome I and Rome II Regulations harmonise choice-of-law rules for contractual and non-contractual obligations, and the Brussels I Regulation governs jurisdiction and judgments.

Scope

Conflict of laws arises in cross-border contracts and torts, international marriages and divorces, succession to property in several countries, multinational corporate disputes, and — within federal states such as the United States, Canada, and Australia — between subnational jurisdictions.
