Mongol conquest of China

Date1205–1279
LocationPresent-day China and Mongolia
ResultMongol victory; establishment of the Yuan dynasty
BelligerentsMongol Empire (later Yuan dynasty) vs. Western Xia, Jin dynasty, Dali Kingdom, Southern Song dynasty

The Mongol conquest of China was a series of major military efforts by the Mongol Empire to invade and conquer various Jin, Song, and other dynasties in China. It spanned nearly seven decades in the 13th century and involved the defeat of the Western Xia, the Jin dynasty, the Dali Kingdom, and the Southern Song. The conquest, completed under Kublai Khan in 1279, brought all of China under Mongol rule for the first time and made it part of the wider Mongol world empire.

Background

In 1206, Temüjin was proclaimed Genghis Khan, uniting the Mongol and Turkic tribes of the steppe. China at the time was divided among the Tangut-led Western Xia in the northwest, the Jurchen Jin dynasty in the north, the Southern Song south of the Yangtze, and the Dali Kingdom in the southwest.

Conquest of Western Xia and Jin

After campaigns between 1205 and 1209, Western Xia became a Mongol vassal; it was destroyed in 1227 during Genghis Khan's final campaign, in which he died. War with the Jin began in 1211. Mongol forces won a decisive victory at the Battle of Yehuling, captured the Jin capital Zhongdu (Beijing) in 1215, and — after an interlude while Genghis campaigned in Central Asia — completed the conquest under Ögedei Khan, taking Caizhou in 1234 with Southern Song assistance.

Conquest of Dali and the Song

In 1253, Möngke Khan's brother Kublai conquered the Dali Kingdom in Yunnan, outflanking the Song. Möngke's invasion of the Song in Sichuan ended with his death in 1259 at the siege of Diaoyu Fortress. As khan, Kublai founded the Yuan dynasty in 1271, conquered the twin fortress cities of Xiangyang and Fancheng after a five-year siege (1268–1273), and advanced down the Yangtze. The Song capital Lin'an (Hangzhou) surrendered in 1276, and the last resistance ended at the naval Battle of Yamen in 1279, where the boy emperor Zhao Bing died.

Consequences

The wars were devastating: census figures suggest China's population fell by tens of millions through warfare, famine, and dislocation, though registration changes account for part of the decline. Under Yuan rule China was reunified, linked into Eurasian trade networks, and administered through a hierarchy that placed Mongols and Central Asians above northern and southern Chinese.
