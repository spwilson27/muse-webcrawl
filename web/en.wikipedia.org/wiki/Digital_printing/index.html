Digital printing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digital printing is a method of printing from a digital-based image directly to a variety of media, using large-format or high-volume laser or inkjet printers. Unlike conventional processes such as offset lithography, digital printing requires no printing plates: the image is formed anew for every impression. This generally means a higher cost per page than offset for long runs, but the saving on plates, set-up, and make-ready makes digital the economical choice for short runs, on-demand work, and jobs where every copy differs (variable-data printing).

History and technologies

The breakthrough products were launched in 1993: the Indigo E-Print 1000 (using liquid-toner electrophotography) and the Xeikon DCP-1 (dry-toner) demonstrated commercial digital colour printing at the IPEX exhibition. The dominant technologies today are:

  • Electrophotography (laser/LED) — a charged photoconductor drum is exposed by laser or LED array and developed with toner; used by HP Indigo, Xerox iGen, and many office printers.
  • Inkjet — droplets of ink are propelled onto the substrate; continuous inkjet and drop-on-demand systems range from desktop photo printers to web-fed production presses and wide-format signage machines.
  • Dye-sublimation — heat transfers dye into coated media, used for photo prints and textiles.
  • Direct-to-garment and direct imaging variants for fabrics and other materials.

Applications

Digital printing is used for books printed on demand (each copy produced only when ordered), personalised direct mail in which text and images vary per recipient, short-run packaging and labels, photo books, proofing, architectural plans, and wide-format graphics such as posters and banners. Because files flow directly from computer to press, digital workflows integrate naturally with web-to-print ordering systems.

Comparison with offset

Offset presses offer very low unit cost at high volume and a wide range of spot colours and substrates, but require plates and lengthy set-up. Digital printing offers near-zero set-up, no minimum run, and variable data, at higher unit cost and historically lower (though now comparable) image quality. In practice, commercial printers route work to whichever process suits the run length and turnaround required.
