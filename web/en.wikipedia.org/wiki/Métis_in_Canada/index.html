Métis in Canada

The Métis (Canadian French: [meˈt͡sɪs]; Michif: Maetii) are an Indigenous people of Canada (and parts of the northern United States) whose ethnogenesis began in the 18th century from unions between First Nations women — principally Cree, Ojibwe, and Saulteaux — and European fur traders, mainly French and Scottish. They developed a distinct culture, language (Michif), and national identity centred on the Red River Colony and the historic Métis homeland of the northern Plains. The Métis are recognized in section 35 of Canada's Constitution Act, 1982 as one of the three Aboriginal peoples of Canada, alongside First Nations and Inuit.

History

Fur trade origins

Métis families formed around trading posts of the North West Company and Hudson's Bay Company. Métis hunters supplied the trade with pemmican — dried bison meat pounded with fat and berries — and organized communal buffalo hunts under elected captains with strict laws. The 1816 Battle of Seven Oaks, led by Cuthbert Grant, is often taken as an early assertion of Métis nationhood.

Red River and resistance

When Canada acquired Rupert's Land in 1869 without consulting its inhabitants, the Métis of Red River, led by Louis Riel, formed a provisional government and negotiated Manitoba's entry into Confederation through the Manitoba Act, 1870. Promised land grants were largely lost through delay and scrip speculation, and many Métis moved west to settlements such as Batoche in present-day Saskatchewan. In 1885, grievances erupted in the North-West Resistance; after battles at Duck Lake, Fish Creek, and Batoche, Canadian forces prevailed, and Riel was tried and hanged for high treason in Regina — an execution that remains a national controversy.

Recognition and rights

Alberta created the Métis Settlements in 1938, the only legislated Métis land base in Canada. The Constitution Act, 1982 recognized Métis Aboriginal rights; the Supreme Court's R v Powley (2003) established a test for Métis harvesting rights, and Daniels v Canada (2016) declared that Métis and non-status Indians fall under federal jurisdiction. In 2013, Manitoba Metis Federation v Canada found the Crown had failed to implement the Manitoba Act land promise honourably. Since 2019, Canada has signed self-government agreements with the Métis Nation of Alberta, Métis Nation of Ontario, and Métis Nation–Saskatchewan.

Culture

Métis culture blends Indigenous and European elements: the Michif language (French nouns with Cree verbs), fiddle music and jigging, intricate floral beadwork ("the Flower Beadwork People"), the woven sash, and the Red River cart of wood and rawhide. The blue infinity flag, first raised in 1816, symbolizes the joining of two cultures and a nation that will live forever.
