Retinoblastoma

Retinoblastoma (Rb) is a rare cancer of the retina that rapidly develops from immature retinal cells, most commonly affecting young children, with the majority of cases diagnosed before the age of three. It occurs in approximately 1 in 15,000–20,000 live births and may affect one eye (unilateral) or both (bilateral).

Genetics

Retinoblastoma is caused by inactivation of both copies of the RB1 tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 13. It was the paradigm for Alfred Knudson's "two-hit hypothesis" of carcinogenesis (1971). In heritable cases (about 40%), one defective copy is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern and the second is lost somatically; these children often develop bilateral and multifocal tumours and carry an increased lifetime risk of other cancers, such as osteosarcoma. Non-heritable cases require two somatic mutations in the same retinal cell and are almost always unilateral.

Signs and treatment

The commonest presenting sign is leukocoria — a white reflex in the pupil visible in photographs — followed by strabismus. Treatment depends on the extent of disease and includes enucleation of the affected eye, systemic or intra-arterial chemotherapy, focal therapies such as laser photocoagulation, cryotherapy and thermotherapy, plaque brachytherapy and, less commonly now, external beam radiotherapy. Survival exceeds 95% in high-income countries where diagnosis is early, but is much lower where presentation is delayed and extraocular spread has occurred.
