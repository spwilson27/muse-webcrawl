Timothy Hackworth (22 December 1786 – 7 July 1850) was an English steam locomotive engineer who lived in Shildon, County Durham. A contemporary and rival of George Stephenson, he was involved in the earliest days of railway development and built the locomotive Sans Pareil, which competed at the Rainhill trials of 1829. He was locomotive superintendent of the Stockton and Darlington Railway, the world's first public railway to use steam locomotives.

Early life

Hackworth was born in Wylam, Northumberland, the son of a blacksmith, and was apprenticed as a blacksmith at Wylam Colliery, where the early locomotive Puffing Billy was built. He became foreman smith and worked with Christopher Blackett and William Hedley on colliery locomotive experiments.

Stockton and Darlington Railway

In 1825 Hackworth was appointed locomotive superintendent of the newly opened Stockton and Darlington Railway. There he designed and built locomotives including the Royal George (1827), which introduced improvements such as the separate steam blast pipe that greatly increased boiler draught and efficiency.

Rainhill trials

At the Rainhill trials of October 1829, held to select locomotives for the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, Hackworth's Sans Pareil was one of the principal competitors to Stephenson's Rocket. The Sans Pareil performed well but was handicapped by excessive fuel consumption and suffered a cracked cylinder and pump problems, and the prize went to Rocket. Hackworth afterwards supplied locomotives to railways in Britain, Russia and Canada; his Samson of 1838, built for the Albion Mines Railway in Nova Scotia, is preserved as one of the oldest surviving locomotives in Canada.

Legacy

Hackworth's home in Shildon is now a museum, and the town is home to the Hackworth Park and part of the National Railway Museum's Locomotion museum. His son, John Wesley Hackworth, continued the family engineering business.
