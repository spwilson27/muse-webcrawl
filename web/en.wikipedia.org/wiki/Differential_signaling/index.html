Differential signalling

Differential signalling is a method for electrically transmitting information using two complementary signals. The technique sends the same electrical signal as a differential pair of signals, each in its own conductor. The pair of conductors can be wires in a twisted-pair or ribbon cable, or traces on a printed circuit board.

In differential signalling, the receiver responds to the difference between the two signals.

Electrically, the two conductors carry signals equal in amplitude but opposite in polarity. The receiver responds to the difference between the two signals, which results in a signal with a magnitude twice as large. Noise that couples equally into both conductors (common-mode noise) is rejected by the receiver, which gives differential links excellent immunity to interference and allows them to operate at low voltages and high speeds.

Comparison with single-ended signalling

Single-ended signalling measures a signal against a common ground reference, so ground noise and crosstalk appear directly in the received signal. Differential signalling costs an extra conductor per signal but provides higher noise immunity, reduced electromagnetic emissions because the fields of the two wires cancel, and a defined return path. It also permits lower supply voltages, since the effective signal swing at the receiver is doubled.

Applications

Differential pairs are used by most modern high-speed interfaces, including USB, Ethernet over twisted pair, HDMI, PCI Express, Serial ATA, and LVDS (low-voltage differential signalling) links. Balanced audio connections in professional sound equipment use the same principle, as do the RS-422 and RS-485 industrial standards.
