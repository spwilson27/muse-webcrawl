Single malt Scotch

Single malt Scotch is a type of single malt whisky, made at one specific distillery in Scotland from a fermented mash of malted barley, distilled in pot stills and matured in oak casks for at least three years. Under the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009, single malt Scotch must be wholly produced in Scotland at a single distillery, using only water and malted barley (to which only whole grains of other cereals may not be added, unlike grain whisky), batch-distilled in pot stills, matured in oak casks of no more than 700 litres for at least three years, and bottled in Scotland at a minimum strength of 40% alcohol by volume. Famous single malts include Glenfiddich, The Macallan, Lagavulin, Laphroaig, Glenlivet and Talisker.

Definition

The term "single" means the whisky is the product of one distillery only; it is not blended with whisky from other distilleries (though a bottling may combine casks of different ages from that distillery, in which case any age statement must refer to the youngest whisky in the bottle). "Malt" indicates that the spirit was distilled from malted barley alone. Other categories of Scotch include blended Scotch (a mix of malt and grain whiskies), blended malt, single grain and blended grain.

Production

Barley is malted — steeped, germinated and dried, traditionally sometimes over peat smoke, which imparts the smoky phenolic character associated especially with Islay malts — then milled, mashed with hot water, fermented into a beer-like "wash", and double (occasionally triple) distilled in copper pot stills. The new-make spirit is diluted and filled into oak casks, most often ex-bourbon barrels or ex-sherry butts, where it matures for at least three years and commonly 10–21 years or more, drawing colour and flavour from the wood. Roughly two per cent of the volume is lost to evaporation each year — the "angels' share".

Regions

Single malts are conventionally grouped by region: the Highlands (the largest area, with diverse styles), Speyside (home to more than half of Scotland's distilleries and noted for fruity, often sherry-influenced malts), the Lowlands (typically lighter and unpeated), Islay (famed for heavily peated, maritime whiskies), Campbeltown (once a major centre, now with three distilleries), and the Islands (a sub-grouping of Highland including Skye, Orkney, Mull, Jura and Arran).

History and market

Although malt whisky had been distilled in Scotland for centuries, single malts were historically mostly consumed by blenders; their emergence as premium products in their own right dates from the 1960s, when Glenfiddich pioneered the marketing of single malt abroad. From the 1980s onward, interest in single malts grew rapidly worldwide, with distilleries releasing extensive ranges of age-statement, cask-finish and limited-edition bottlings, and Scotch whisky became one of the United Kingdom's leading exports.
