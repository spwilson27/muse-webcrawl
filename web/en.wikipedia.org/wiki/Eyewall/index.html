Eyewall

The eyewall is a ring of towering thunderstorms that surrounds the calm central eye of a mature tropical cyclone. It is the region of the storm with the highest wind speeds, the heaviest rainfall, and the most violent weather. Eyewalls typically converge at an altitude of one to three kilometres above the surface and slope outward with height.

Structure and dynamics

Air spirals inward at the surface, rises rapidly in the eyewall's deep convection, and flows outward at the top of the troposphere. Latent heat released in the eyewall maintains the storm's warm core. The most intense hurricanes develop a "stadium effect", in which the eyewall clouds curve outward like the walls of a sports stadium. Secondary eyewalls sometimes form outside the original; during an eyewall replacement cycle, the outer eyewall contracts and chokes off the inner one, temporarily weakening the storm but often leaving it larger, after which it may re-intensify — a process documented in storms such as Hurricane Katrina (2005).

Mesovortices

Small-scale rotational features called eyewall mesovortices can occur within the eyewall, locally enhancing wind damage. Tornado-like vortices in the eyewall were observed by reconnaissance aircraft in several major hurricanes.
