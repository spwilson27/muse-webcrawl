Reification (Marxism)

Reification
Key thinkerGyörgy Lukács
Key workHistory and Class Consciousness (1923)
Related conceptsCommodity fetishism, alienation

In Marxist theory, reification (German: Verdinglichung, "making into a thing") is the process by which social relations between people come to be perceived as relations between things — in particular, by which commodity exchange and abstract labour come to dominate social life, so that human qualities and social relations appear as properties of objects governed by apparently natural laws.

Origins

The concept builds on Karl Marx's analysis of commodity fetishism in Capital. It was developed most influentially by the Hungarian philosopher György Lukács in his 1923 essay "Reification and the Consciousness of the Proletariat" in History and Class Consciousness. For Lukács, reification under capitalism extends from the factory — where labour is fragmented, measured and contemplated passively — to consciousness itself, shaping law, bureaucracy, science and philosophy.

Influence

Reification became a central category of Western Marxism and influenced the critical theory of the Frankfurt School, including Adorno and Horkheimer's critique of instrumental reason. Later thinkers, including Axel Honneth, have recast reification as a form of forgotten or distorted recognition between persons.
