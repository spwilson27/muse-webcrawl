Lithic core

In archaeology, a lithic core is a distinctive mass of stone (usually flint, chert, obsidian or another fine-grained, conchoidally fracturing rock) that has been shaped by the removal of flakes for use as tools or as blanks for tool production. The core is the piece from which flakes are struck; the flakes removed from it may themselves be used as tools or further retouched, while the core is typically discarded when exhausted — though some cores, such as those of bifacial handaxes, were also used as tools in their own right.

How cores are formed

A knapper strikes a core with a hammerstone or a softer hammer (antler, bone or wood) at a suitable striking platform. Because suitable stone fractures conchoidally, each blow detaches a flake, leaving a negative scar on the core. By rotating the core, choosing new platforms, and preparing the surface, the knapper controls the size and shape of subsequent removals. The pattern of scars records the sequence and strategy of reduction, which archaeologists study through lithic analysis.

Types of core

  • Unidirectional and bidirectional cores — flakes struck from one or two opposing platforms.
  • Levallois cores — carefully prepared cores of the Middle Palaeolithic whose surface shaping predetermines the form of the final flake.
  • Blade cores — prismatic or conical cores from which long, parallel-sided blades are removed, characteristic of the Upper Palaeolithic and later.
  • Discoidal cores — disc-shaped cores flaked around their circumference.
  • Globular and amorphous cores — expedient cores reduced from multiple directions with little preparation, typical of simple flake industries such as the Oldowan.
  • Bifacial cores — pieces worked on both faces; in some industries the biface itself was the intended tool (a handaxe or cleaver).

Archaeological significance

Cores are diagnostic of technology: the way a core is prepared and reduced reveals planning, skill and tradition, and changes in core forms mark major transitions in prehistory — from Oldowan chopper-cores to Levallois technology to blade production. Refit studies, in which flakes are reassembled onto cores, can reconstruct episodes of knapping at a site almost blow by blow.
