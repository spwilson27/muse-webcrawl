Basso continuo

Basso continuo, also called thoroughbass or figured bass, is a musical practice of the Baroque era (roughly 1600–1750) in which a bass line, reinforced by a chord-playing instrument, provides the harmonic foundation of a composition. The Italian term means "continuous bass".

Practice

The continuo part was typically played by a bass melody instrument — cello, viola da gamba or bassoon — together with a chordal instrument such as harpsichord, organ, lute or theorbo. The chord player did not receive fully written parts: instead, figures (numbers and accidentals) written under the bass notes indicated the intervals to build above them, a shorthand called figured bass, which the performer realised improvisationally.

History

Emerging from late-Renaissance accompaniment practice around 1600, the continuo became the backbone of nearly all Baroque music — opera, oratorio, cantata, sonata and concerto — and persisted into the Classical period in sacred music and operatic recitative before falling out of use around 1800. Modern historically informed performances revive the practice.
