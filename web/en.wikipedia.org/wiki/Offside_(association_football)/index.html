Offside (association football)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Offside is one of the laws of association football, codified in Law 11 of the Laws of the Game. A player is in an offside position if any part of the head, body or feet is nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent (usually the goalkeeper and one defender), and is in the opponents' half of the field. Being in an offside position is not an offence in itself; a player is penalised only if, at the moment the ball is played by a teammate, they become involved in active play — for example by playing the ball, interfering with an opponent, or gaining an advantage from a rebound.

Application

There is no offside offence if a player receives the ball directly from a goal kick, throw-in or corner kick. The sanction for an offside offence is an indirect free kick to the opposing team from where the offence occurred. Assistant referees, positioned level with the second-last defender, flag for offside; in professional competitions the video assistant referee (VAR) reviews close calls with calibrated lines, and semi-automated offside technology has been used at World Cups since 2022.

History

Offside rules descend from the much stricter laws of early English football, which effectively outlawed forward passing. The 1866 relaxation introduced a three-player rule, and in 1925 the requirement was reduced from three opponents to two, causing a sharp rise in goals and the development of new formations. Later tweaks included "level is onside" (1990) and guidance on what counts as interfering with play, intended to favour attackers.

Tactics

Defences exploit the law with the "offside trap", stepping up in unison to strand attackers offside — a tactic associated with teams such as Arrigo Sacchi's AC Milan and, in an extreme high-line form, with various modern pressing sides. Forwards counter it with timed, curved runs from onside positions.
