Raynaud syndrome

Raynaud syndrome, also known as Raynaud's phenomenon, is a medical condition in which the arteries supplying the fingers and toes (and occasionally the nose, ears or lips) go into spasm in response to cold or emotional stress. During an attack the affected part typically turns white (pallor), then blue (cyanosis), and finally red and painful as blood flow returns.

Primary and secondary Raynaud's

Primary Raynaud's (Raynaud's disease) occurs on its own, usually beginning in the teens or twenties and affecting women more often than men; it is generally mild. Secondary Raynaud's accompanies another condition, most often connective tissue diseases such as scleroderma, systemic lupus erythematosus or rheumatoid arthritis, but also vibration injury (hand–arm vibration syndrome), certain drugs, and arterial disease. Secondary cases can be more severe, occasionally progressing to digital ulcers or, rarely, gangrene.

Mechanism and management

The colour changes reflect episodic vasoconstriction of the digital arteries. Diagnosis is usually clinical, supplemented by nailfold capillaroscopy and blood tests to detect an underlying autoimmune disease. Management centres on keeping the extremities warm and avoiding triggers such as smoking and cold exposure. Calcium channel blockers such as nifedipine are the standard drug treatment for more troublesome cases; phosphodiesterase inhibitors and prostacyclin analogues are used in severe secondary disease.

The condition is named after the French physician Maurice Raynaud, who described it in his 1862 doctoral thesis.
