Credit score in the United States

Image: Chart showing the distribution of FICO scores in the United States (placeholder)

A credit score is a number that lenders in the United States use to assess the creditworthiness of an individual. It is calculated from the information in a consumer's credit reports, which are compiled by the three major credit bureaus — Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. The best-known scoring model is the FICO Score, developed by Fair Isaac Corporation (introduced in 1989); a competing model, VantageScore, was created by the three bureaus in 2006.

Score ranges and factors

Generic FICO Scores range from 300 to 850, with higher scores indicating lower lending risk; the average American score has risen gradually to around 718 in the early 2020s. The FICO model weights five categories: payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%) and credit mix (10%). VantageScore uses a similar 300–850 range in its current versions but somewhat different weightings.

Uses and regulation

Scores are used in decisions on mortgages, credit cards, auto loans, apartment rentals and sometimes employment and insurance pricing. Consumers' rights are governed by the Fair Credit Reporting Act (1970), which entitles them to free annual credit reports, and by the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, which prohibits discrimination in lending. The FACT Act and subsequent rules allow consumers to obtain their scores and dispute errors. Unlike the reports, the scores themselves are not free by law, though many banks and card issuers now provide them to customers.

Criticism

Credit scoring is criticised for opacity of models, errors in underlying reports, disparate impact on minority and low-income consumers, and the exclusion of "credit invisible" people with thin files. Reforms and newer models increasingly incorporate rent, utility and bank-account data, and medical debt has been progressively removed from many scores.
