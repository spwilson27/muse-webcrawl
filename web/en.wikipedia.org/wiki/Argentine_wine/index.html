Argentine wine is made mainly in the western regions of Argentina, in the foothills of the Andes. Argentina is one of the largest wine producers in the world — historically the fifth-largest — with viticulture centred on the province of Mendoza, which accounts for roughly two-thirds of national production. The country's signature grape is Malbec, a French variety that found ideal conditions in Mendoza's high-altitude, sun-drenched vineyards; the white grape Torrontés is Argentina's characteristic aromatic white.

History

Vines were introduced to Argentina by Spanish colonists in the 1550s, with cuttings of the Criolla grape arriving via Chile and Peru. Jesuit missionaries established vineyards, and production remained oriented toward domestic consumption and church wine for centuries. In the 19th century, European immigrants — particularly Italians and Spaniards — expanded plantings dramatically, and the arrival of the railway linking Mendoza to Buenos Aires in 1885 opened a mass market. A quality revolution from the 1990s onward, aided by flying winemakers such as Michel Rolland, repositioned Argentine wine on export markets.

Regions

  • Mendoza — includes Luján de Cuyo, Maipú, and the high-altitude Uco Valley.
  • San Juan — second-largest producer, known for Syrah and table grapes.
  • Salta — home to Cafayate, some of the world's highest vineyards, famed for Torrontés.
  • La Rioja and Catamarca — historic north-western regions.
  • Patagonia (Río Negro, Neuquén) — cool-climate Pinot Noir and Merlot.

Climate and terroir

The Andes cast a rain shadow over the wine regions, making them semi-arid; irrigation is supplied by snowmelt channeled through canals. High elevations moderate temperatures and produce large diurnal ranges, preserving acidity and aromatic intensity. Calcareous and alluvial soils are common.
