Viking program

OperatorNASA / JPL
SpacecraftViking 1, Viking 2
Launched1975
Mars landings20 July 1976; 3 September 1976
Program costAbout US$1 billion

The Viking program consisted of a pair of identical American space probes, Viking 1 and Viking 2, each consisting of an orbiter and a lander, launched toward Mars in 1975. Each orbiter photographed the planet to help select landing sites; each lander then touched down on the surface, becoming the first spacecraft to operate successfully on Mars.

Mission

Viking 1 was launched on 20 August 1975 and its lander touched down in Chryse Planitia on 20 July 1976 — the first successful Mars landing. Viking 2 was launched on 9 September 1975 and landed in Utopia Planitia on 3 September 1976. The orbiters mapped the planet and relayed communications, while the landers conducted imaging, meteorology, seismology (on Viking 2), and a celebrated suite of biology experiments.

Biology experiments

Each lander carried instruments designed to search for signs of life in the Martian soil, including the Labeled Release experiment, which initially returned results that met the pre-set criteria for detecting metabolism. However, the gas chromatograph–mass spectrometer found no significant organic molecules in the soil, and the scientific consensus became that the reactions observed were caused by non-biological chemical oxidants in the soil rather than by living organisms.

Legacy

The Viking landers operated far beyond their planned 90-day lifetimes: Viking 2's lander worked until 1980 and Viking 1's until 1982. The program returned the first detailed colour views from the Martian surface, long-term weather records, and a wealth of orbital imagery that transformed understanding of Mars, at a total cost of roughly one billion US dollars.
