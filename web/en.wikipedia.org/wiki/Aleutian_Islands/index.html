The Aleutian Islands are a chain of more than 300 small volcanic islands forming an island arc in the northern Pacific Ocean, stretching about 1,200 miles (1,900 km) westward from the Alaska Peninsula toward Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula. They separate the Bering Sea to the north from the Pacific Ocean to the south and mark part of the boundary between North America and Asia. Most belong to the U.S. state of Alaska; the westernmost Commander Islands belong to Russia.

Geography and geology

The islands are the partially submerged crest of a volcanic mountain range produced by subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the North American Plate — part of the Pacific "Ring of Fire." The chain includes dozens of historically active volcanoes, including Shishaldin on Unimak Island, the chain's largest. The climate is oceanic: cool, windy, foggy and treeless, with grassland vegetation.

History

The Unangax̂ (Aleut) people have inhabited the islands for thousands of years, developing a sophisticated maritime culture of sea hunting in skin boats (baidarkas). Russians reached the islands in 1741 during Vitus Bering's expedition and exploited the sea otter fur trade, with devastating effects on the Aleut population. The islands passed to the United States with the Alaska Purchase of 1867.

Second World War and today

The Aleutian Islands Campaign of 1942–43 saw Japanese forces occupy Attu and Kiska — the only incorporated American territory invaded in the war — before U.S. and Canadian forces retook them; wartime evacuation interned many Aleut villagers in camps in southeast Alaska. Today the population is small and concentrated in fishing ports such as Unalaska/Dutch Harbor, one of the United States' busiest fishing ports by volume. Most of the chain lies within the Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge, a haven for seabirds and marine mammals.
