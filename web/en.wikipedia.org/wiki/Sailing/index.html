Sailing employs the wind — acting on sails, wingsails or kites — to propel a craft on water, land or ice. For millennia sailing was the principal means of maritime trade, transport, warfare and fishing, before steam and internal-combustion engines replaced it commercially. Today most sailing is recreational: cruising, racing, day sailing and sail training.

Physics

A sail acts as a wing. Wind flowing across its curved surface generates lift, part of which drives the boat forward; the keel or centreboard resists sideways drift ("leeway") and converts the force into forward motion. By adjusting sail trim and heading relative to the wind — the "points of sail", from close-hauled (sailing toward the wind at roughly 45°) to running (wind directly astern) — a vessel can travel in almost any direction, zig-zagging ("tacking") to make progress directly upwind.

History

Depictions of sailing vessels appear in Egyptian art from the fourth millennium BCE. Austronesian peoples used sails to settle the Pacific, culminating in Polynesian voyaging canoes that reached Hawaii, Easter Island and New Zealand. In Europe and Asia, square and lateen rigs culminated in the great ocean-going sailing ships of the 15th–19th centuries; fast clippers and windjammers carried cargo into the early 20th century. Steam shipping took over most trade by 1900.

Sailing as a sport

Yacht racing is organised in classes from small dinghies to ocean racers. Major events include the Olympic sailing regatta, the America's Cup, the Vendée Globe solo round-the-world race, and The Ocean Race. Sailing has been an Olympic sport since 1900 (except 1904), with classes such as the ILCA (Laser), 470, 49er, Nacra 17 and, from 2024, kiteboarding and windfoiling classes.
