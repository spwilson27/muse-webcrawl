Gaétan Gatian de Clérambault (2 July 1872 – 17 November 1934) was a French psychiatrist. He is chiefly remembered for describing the delusional condition that bears his name — de Clérambault's syndrome, or erotomania — and as the teacher of Jacques Lacan, who called him "my only master in psychiatry".

Life and career

Clérambault studied medicine in Paris and also took a degree in law and studied decorative arts. From 1905 he worked at the special infirmary for the insane of the Paris police prefecture, succeeding his mentor Paul Sérieux as chief physician in 1920. His post exposed him to thousands of cases of acute mental disturbance, from which he developed his theories of mental automatism — the idea that certain thoughts, voices and impulses are experienced as imposed from outside the self, forming the core of chronic hallucinatory psychoses.

De Clérambault's syndrome

In his 1921 work on "passionate psychoses", Clérambault described erotomania: a delusion in which the patient, usually a woman, is convinced that another person — typically of higher social status and often famous — is in love with her. The condition, also named in the related notion of the Kandinsky–Clérambault syndrome (with Victor Kandinsky) covering the experience of thoughts and actions being controlled by an external force, remains part of modern psychiatric classification.

Other interests and death

Clérambault was also an accomplished painter and photographer; his thousands of photographs of draped fabrics, taken in Morocco and Paris, are now held by French museums and are admired as studies of folds and textiles. Afflicted by failing eyesight and chronic pain following wartime wounds and unsuccessful operations, he took his own life in 1934.
