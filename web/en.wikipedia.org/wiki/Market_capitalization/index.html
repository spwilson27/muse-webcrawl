Market capitalization, sometimes referred to as market cap, is the total value of a publicly traded company's outstanding shares of stock, calculated by multiplying the current share price by the total number of shares outstanding. It is a standard measure of a company's size as valued by the market and a key input to stock indices and portfolio classification.

Categories

  • Large-cap — typically US$10 billion or more.
  • Mid-cap — roughly US$2–10 billion.
  • Small-cap — roughly US$300 million–2 billion.
  • Micro-cap and nano-cap — below US$300 million and US$50 million respectively.

Index providers often use free-float market capitalization, counting only shares available for public trading and excluding those held by insiders and governments. The S&P 500 and MSCI indices rank constituents on this basis. Market capitalization differs from enterprise value, which adds debt and subtracts cash, and from book value, which is an accounting measure.

Largest companies

The largest market capitalizations in history have been held by technology companies. Apple became the first company to reach US$1 trillion (2018), US$2 trillion (2020), and US$3 trillion (2022) in market value. In the mid-2020s, chipmaker NVIDIA and Microsoft joined or contested the top positions, driven by the artificial-intelligence boom, with valuations around or above US$3 trillion; Tencent has been China's most valuable listed company for much of the past decade. PetroChina briefly exceeded US$1 trillion on its 2007 debut before falling sharply.

Limitations

Market cap reflects the marginal trading price applied to all shares; it does not mean the company could actually be bought or sold for that amount, and acquiring control typically commands a premium. Prices can also detach from fundamentals during bubbles and crashes, so market capitalization is a snapshot of market sentiment as much as of underlying value — and high price liquidity can amplify both rises and falls.
