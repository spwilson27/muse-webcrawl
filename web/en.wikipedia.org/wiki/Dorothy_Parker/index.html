Dorothy Parker

BornDorothy Rothschild, August 22, 1893, Long Branch, New Jersey, U.S.
DiedJune 7, 1967 (aged 73), New York City, U.S.

Dorothy Parker (née Rothschild; August 22, 1893 – June 7, 1967) was an American poet, writer, critic, wit, and satirist whose work was based mainly on her experiences in New York City and her association with the Algonquin Round Table. Famous for her mordant wit and wisecracks — "Men seldom make passes / At girls who wear glasses" — she wrote verse, short fiction, drama criticism for Vanity Fair and The New Yorker, and screenplays in Hollywood, twice receiving Academy Award nominations.

Parker's wit made her a legend of the Algonquin Round Table, though she later disparaged her reputation as a wisecracker.

Life and career

Born in New Jersey and raised on Manhattan's Upper West Side, Parker lost both parents young and supported herself by playing piano at a dance school before joining Vogue and then Vanity Fair, where she became drama critic. From 1919 she lunched daily at the Algonquin Hotel with Robert Benchley, Alexander Woollcott, and other wits whose repartee was chronicled in newspaper columns. Her first book of verse, Enough Rope (1926), was a bestseller; her short story "Big Blonde" (1929) won the O. Henry Award.

In the 1930s Parker went to Hollywood with her husband Alan Campbell, writing for films including A Star Is Born (1937), for which she was nominated for an Academy Award. Her left-wing activism — support for Sacco and Vanzetti, anti-fascist causes, and the Screen Writers Guild — led to her being blacklisted in the McCarthy era. She died in New York in 1967, leaving her estate to Martin Luther King Jr. and, after his assassination, to the NAACP.
