Caesarea

Caesarea most commonly refers to Caesarea Maritima, the ancient port city built by Herod the Great between 25 and 13 BCE on the Mediterranean coast of present-day Israel and named in honour of Caesar Augustus. The modern town of Caesarea (Kesarya) stands near the archaeological site.

Herod's city

Herod transformed a modest Phoenician anchorage called Straton's Tower into one of the great cities of the eastern Mediterranean, with a massive artificial harbour (Sebastos) built using hydraulic concrete and submerged wooden forms, a theatre, an amphitheatre/hippodrome, temples, palaces, and a grid of streets with sewers. The city became the Roman administrative capital of Judaea; Pontius Pilate resided there, and an inscription bearing his name was found in the theatre. Caesarea features repeatedly in the Acts of the Apostles, and later became a major centre of early Christian scholarship associated with Origen and Eusebius.

Later history

The city flourished under Byzantine rule, declined after the Arab conquest, and was refortified by Crusaders, who built the citadel and walls whose remains are still visible. It was finally abandoned after the Mamluk conquest in 1265. Systematic excavation since the 1950s — on land and underwater in the harbour — has made Caesarea National Park one of Israel's most visited archaeological sites.

The name Caesarea was also borne by many other ancient cities, including Caesarea Mazaca in Cappadocia (modern Kayseri) and Caesarea in Mauretania (modern Cherchell, Algeria).
