The Comics Code Authority (CCA) was formed in 1954 by the Comics Magazine Association of America as an alternative to government regulation of the comic book industry. Its code, the most restrictive content code in American mass media, governed the content of American comic books for more than five decades. Comics approved by the authority carried its seal on their covers.

Background

In the early 1950s, crime and horror comics — notably those published by EC Comics — drew intense public criticism. Psychiatrist Fredric Wertham's 1954 book Seduction of the Innocent argued that comics caused juvenile delinquency, and Senate subcommittee hearings on the subject followed. Fearing federal censorship, publishers created the CCA to police themselves.

The Code

The original code forbade, among other things: sympathetic depictions of criminals; any suggestion that authority figures could be corrupt; "sex perversion or any inference to same"; and the words "horror" or "terror" in a comic's title. Vampires, werewolves, ghouls and zombies were banned outright — restrictions that effectively destroyed the horror comics genre and drove EC Comics out of the comic book business.

Revisions and decline

The Code was revised in 1971, after Marvel Comics published an anti-drug storyline in The Amazing Spider-Man without the seal at the request of the Nixon administration's health authorities — the original code had prohibited any depiction of drug use, even in condemnation. The revision loosened rules on horror monsters and narcotics. Further revisions came in 1989. From the 1970s onward, publishers increasingly bypassed the Code through direct-market distribution and "mature readers" imprints.

End

Marvel withdrew from the Code in 2001 in favour of its own rating system. In January 2011, DC Comics and Archie Comics — the last two participating publishers — both announced they would no longer submit titles for approval, ending the Code after 56 years.
