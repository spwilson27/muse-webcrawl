Barbizon school

The Barbizon school of painters formed part of an influential movement in art toward Realism in landscape painting. Active roughly from 1830 to 1870, it took its name from the village of Barbizon, France, on the edge of the Forest of Fontainebleau, where many of the artists gathered to paint directly from nature.

Years activec. 1830–1870
LocationBarbizon, France
Major figuresThéodore Rousseau, Jean-François Millet, Charles-François Daubigny, Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot

Style and aims

Rejecting the idealized classical landscapes of the Academy, Barbizon painters made landscape itself the subject, working en plein air with oil sketches later finished in the studio. Their tones were naturalistic and often sombre, celebrating rural labour and unspoiled nature at a time of rapid industrialization. Influences included Dutch Golden Age landscape and English painter John Constable.

Artists and legacy

Théodore Rousseau was the movement's leader; Jean-François Millet's The Gleaners and The Angelus made peasant life monumental. Others included Daubigny, Diaz de la Peña, Jules Dupré, and Constant Troyon; Corot was a revered precursor. The school paved the way for Impressionism and influenced landscape painting across Europe and America, including the Hudson River School's later Barbizon-influenced painters. See also Realism.
