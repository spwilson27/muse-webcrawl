Katsura Imperial Villa

The Katsura Imperial Villa (桂離宮, Katsura Rikyū) is an imperial residence with associated gardens and outbuildings in the western suburbs of Kyoto, Japan. Built in the 17th century for the Hachijō-no-miya branch of the imperial family, it is regarded as one of the masterpieces of Japanese architecture and garden design, and had a significant influence on modernist architecture in the West.

History

The site, on the west bank of the Katsura River, had been a moon-viewing retreat associated with the Heian court and features in The Tale of Genji. Prince Hachijō Toshihito (1579–1629) began building a villa there in about 1615; his son Toshitada added the tea houses and completed the garden. After the Hachijō line died out in 1681, the property reverted to the imperial household and was maintained by the court; it is now administered by the Imperial Household Agency, and visits are by guided tour only.

Buildings and garden

The residence consists of three shoin (drawing rooms) — the Old Shoin, Middle Shoin and New Palace — arranged in a staggered "geese in flight" diagonal, built in the sukiya style with plain wood, white plaster, tatami rooms and sliding fusuma and shōji. Scattered through the stroll garden around an irregular pond are tea houses including the Shōkintei, celebrated for its bold blue-and-white checkered fusuma, and the Shōkatei. Stepping-stone paths, borrowed scenery, and carefully staged views sequence the visitor's movement through the grounds.

Influence

Introduced to the West in the 1930s, notably by the architect Bruno Taut, who praised it lavishly, Katsura became a touchstone for modernists such as Walter Gropius and Kenzo Tange, whose 1960 book with photographs by Yasuhiro Ishimoto cemented its international reputation. Its modular tatami planning, asymmetry and frank use of materials were read as anticipating modern architecture by three centuries.
