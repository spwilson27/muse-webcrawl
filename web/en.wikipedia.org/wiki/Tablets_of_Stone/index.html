According to the Hebrew Bible, the Tablets of the Law (also Tablets of Stone, or Tablets of the Covenant; Hebrew: לוחות הברית, Luchot HaBrit) were the two stone tablets inscribed with the Ten Commandments that were given to Moses by God on Mount Sinai. They are described as written by "the finger of God" and were kept in the Ark of the Covenant.

Biblical account

The Book of Exodus relates that Moses ascended Mount Sinai and received the tablets after forty days and nights. Descending and seeing the Israelites worshipping the golden calf, he smashed the first tablets in anger (Exodus 32). After interceding for the people, Moses carved a second pair, and God re-inscribed them (Exodus 34); these were placed in the Ark alongside, according to Deuteronomy, the testimony kept before the Lord. The tablets are described as inscribed on both sides, and Jewish tradition holds the lettering miraculously readable through the stone.

In tradition and art

The tablets became one of the most recognisable symbols of Judaism and of divine law generally. In art from the medieval period onward they are conventionally shown as two rounded-top stones with the first words of each commandment, an image popularised by works such as Rembrandt's Moses with the Tablets of the Law. Rabbinic tradition adds that the broken first tablets were also preserved in the Ark — a symbol that the broken and the whole both have a place in the covenant. The tablets appear in synagogue decoration and over the arks of many congregations, and on the façade of the United States Supreme Court building in Washington, D.C.
