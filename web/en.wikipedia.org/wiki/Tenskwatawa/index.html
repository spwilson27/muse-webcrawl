Tenskwatawa

Tenskwatawa (January 1775 – November 1836), also known as the Shawnee Prophet, was a Native American religious and political leader of the Shawnee tribe. Born Lalawethika ("the Rattle" or "Noisemaker"), he was the younger brother of Tecumseh, with whom he co-founded a pan-Indian confederacy and the settlement of Prophetstown in present-day Indiana.

Early life and transformation

Lalawethika was one of triplets, grew up in poverty, lost an eye in a hunting accident, and in his early adulthood was regarded as a boastful and dissolute man with a drinking problem. In 1805, during an illness — described as a trance or near-death experience, reportedly following an alcoholic collapse — he awoke with a vision of the Master of Life. Renaming himself Tenskwatawa, "the Open Door", he began preaching a doctrine of purification: Native Americans must reject alcohol, European goods and customs, witchcraft and intertribal conflict, and return to traditional ways.

The revival and Prophetstown

The Prophet's preaching drew thousands of followers from many tribes and supplied the spiritual foundation for Tecumseh's political and military confederacy. His credibility soared in 1806 when he correctly predicted a solar eclipse, confounding Governor William Henry Harrison's challenge to demonstrate supernatural power. In 1808 the brothers founded Prophetstown at the confluence of the Wabash and Tippecanoe rivers.

Decline

While Tecumseh was away recruiting allies in the South in 1811, Tenskwatawa — contrary to Tecumseh's instructions — engaged Harrison's army at the Battle of Tippecanoe. His warriors were defeated and Prophetstown burned, and the Prophet's prestige, based partly on claims that his medicine would make warriors invulnerable, collapsed. He fled to Canada during the War of 1812, later returned to the United States, and in 1826 led his remaining followers to a reservation in Kansas, where he died in 1836.
