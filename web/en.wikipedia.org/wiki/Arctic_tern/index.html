The Arctic tern (Sterna paradisaea) is a tern in the family Laridae. This bird has a circumpolar breeding distribution covering the Arctic and sub-Arctic regions of Europe, Asia, and North America. The species is famous for its long-distance migration; it travels from its Arctic breeding grounds to the Antarctic and back again each year, the longest regular migration by any known animal.

An Arctic tern in breeding plumage

Migration

Arctic terns migrate about 70,900 km (44,100 mi) on average each year, and some individuals travel considerably more. Because of this twice-yearly journey between the northern and southern summers, the Arctic tern sees two summers per year and more daylight than any other creature on the planet. A typical individual may fly around 2.4 million km (1.5 million mi) over its lifetime of up to about 30 years.

Description

The Arctic tern is a medium-sized bird, predominantly white with a black cap, a deeply forked tail, and a blood-red bill and feet in the breeding season. Its diet consists mainly of fish and marine invertebrates, which it catches by plunge-diving. It closely resembles the common tern but has a shorter bill, longer tail streamers, and a different primary moult pattern.

Breeding

Arctic terns breed in colonies on coasts and islands, laying one to three mottled eggs in a scrape on the ground. They are long-lived birds, with many individuals returning to the same breeding colony each year. Both parents incubate the eggs, which hatch after about three weeks.

Conservation

The species is classified as Least Concern by the IUCN, with a large global population estimated at about one million individuals, although numbers have declined in some parts of its range, including Iceland, where declines have been linked to reductions in sand eel stocks.
