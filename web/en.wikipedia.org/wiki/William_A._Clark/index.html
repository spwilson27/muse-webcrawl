William A. Clark

William Andrews Clark Sr. (January 8, 1839 – March 2, 1925) was an American entrepreneur, copper magnate, and politician from Montana. One of the three "Copper Kings" of Butte, Montana, he was among the richest Americans of his era. His election to the United States Senate became notorious for bribery, and the scandal contributed to the passage of the Seventeenth Amendment providing for direct election of senators.

Business career

Clark moved west in the 1860s, working in gold mines in Colorado and Montana before moving into trade, banking, and eventually copper mining. Based in Butte and Deer Lodge, he built a fortune from copper mines, smelters, railroads, newspapers, and utilities, rivalling Marcus Daly and F. Augustus Heinze. He also developed the townsite of Clarkdale, Arizona, and founded Las Vegas, Nevada, through his railroad interests; Clark County, Nevada, is named for him.

Senate scandal

In 1899 the Montana legislature elected Clark to the U.S. Senate, but evidence that his agents had bribed legislators led the Senate to refuse him the seat; he resigned in 1900 before a formal vote. Appointed to a subsequent vacancy and then elected again in 1901, he served a full term until 1907. Mark Twain wrote a scathing essay about Clark, calling him "the most putrid human carrion in the state of Montana".

Legacy

Clark's Fifth Avenue mansion in New York was among the grandest private homes in America. His art collection and mansion bequest formed a core of the Corcoran Gallery of Art in Washington, D.C. His younger daughter, Huguette Clark, became famous posthumously as a reclusive heiress whose estate was widely reported on.
