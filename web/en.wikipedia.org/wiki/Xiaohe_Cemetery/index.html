Xiaohe Cemetery

The Xiaohe Cemetery (Chinese: 小河墓地), also known as Ördek's Necropolis, is a Bronze Age burial site near the dried-up Lop Nur region of Xinjiang, China, in the Taklamakan Desert. Dating to roughly 2000–1000 BCE, it is famous for its extraordinarily well-preserved mummies and distinctive boat-shaped coffins.

Discovery

The site was first noted in the 1930s by Swedish archaeologist Folke Bergman, working with the explorer Sven Hedin's expeditions, and was named after a nearby "little river." It was then effectively lost until its rediscovery in 2000 using satellite imagery. Major excavations by the Xinjiang Institute of Archaeology took place from 2002 to 2005.

Burials

The cemetery contains more than 300 burials arranged in layers on a sand dune. Graves are marked by upright wooden poles — phallic-shaped for women and oar-shaped for men. Coffins were fashioned from poplar planks covered with cattle hides, resembling inverted boats, an image some scholars connect to the solar barque. Bodies were naturally mummified by the arid, saline conditions; grave goods included woven baskets, ephedra twigs, wheat grains, cheese, ornaments, and felt caps.

Significance

Genetic studies of the Xiaohe population have suggested a long-isolated group with ancient Siberian-related ancestry, informing debates about early populations of the Tarim Basin and the spread of farming, dairying, and Indo-European languages into Central Asia.
