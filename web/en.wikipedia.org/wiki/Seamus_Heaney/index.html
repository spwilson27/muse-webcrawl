Seamus Heaney

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Seamus Heaney in later life: the Nobel laureate smiling, white-haired, in a Donegal tweed jacket]

Seamus Justin Heaney (13 April 1939 – 30 August 2013) was an Irish poet, playwright and translator, widely regarded as one of the major poets of the twentieth century and awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1995 'for works of lyrical beauty and ethical depth, which exalt everyday miracles and the living past' (as the citation is usually summarised).

Born on a farm at Mossbawn, County Londonderry, the eldest of nine children, Heaney made the ground of his childhood — digging, thatching, the bog — into poetry's territory: Death of a Naturalist (1966), North (1975), written through the Troubles with bog-body poems as dark mirrors, Field Work (1979), Station Island (1984) and Seeing Things (1991). His translation of Beowulf (1999) was a best-seller; his rendering of Sophocles' Philoctetes became The Cure at Troy (1990).

Career

Heaney taught at Queen's Belfast (among the 'Belfast Group' of poets), moved south to the Republic in 1972 as violence worsened, and held chairs at Harvard and Oxford (Professor of Poetry, 1989–94). His criticism (The Government of the Tongue, The Redress of Poetry) argued poetry's power to redress reality without propaganda — a position tested publicly every time the conflict demanded a tribal spokesman.

Death and standing

Heaney died in Dublin in 2013; his last words to his wife, by family account, were a texted Latin phrase meaning 'do not be afraid'. Ireland mourned a national figure; the Seamus Heaney Listen Now Again exhibition in Dublin and the HomePlace centre in Bellaghy curate his archive and landscape.
