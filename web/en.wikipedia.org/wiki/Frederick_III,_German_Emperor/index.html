Frederick III (German: Friedrich III; 18 October 1831 – 15 June 1888) was German Emperor and King of Prussia for 99 days between March and June 1888, the so-called Year of the Three Emperors. Already terminally ill with cancer of the larynx when he succeeded his father Wilhelm I, he died before he could implement the liberal reforms he and his wife Victoria, eldest daughter of Queen Victoria, had long envisaged. He was succeeded by his son, Wilhelm II.

Early life and military career

Frederick was born in Potsdam, the son of Prince Wilhelm of Prussia and Princess Augusta of Saxe-Weimar-Eisenach. Educated partly by liberal tutors, he studied at the University of Bonn. In 1858 he married Victoria, Princess Royal of Great Britain, a marriage that reinforced his liberal and Anglophile outlook and brought him into frequent conflict with Chancellor Otto von Bismarck.

As Crown Prince he proved a capable and popular army commander in Prussia's wars of unification: he led the Second Army in the Austro-Prussian War of 1866, winning at Königgrätz, and commanded the Third Army in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, victorious at Wörth and Sedan and present at the siege of Paris.

Reign and death

Frederick had suffered from throat problems for years; by the time of his accession in March 1888, cancer of the larynx had robbed him of speech, and he communicated largely in writing. The diagnosis and treatment of his illness — involving the British specialist Morell Mackenzie and bitter disputes with German doctors — became an international controversy. During his short reign he dismissed the reactionary interior minister Robert von Puttkamer but had no time for broader change. He died on 15 June 1888, after a reign of just 99 days.

Legacy

Frederick's brief reign has generated enduring "what if" speculation: whether a longer-lived Frederick might have steered Germany toward parliamentary liberalism and away from the confrontations of Wilhelm II's reign remains debated by historians, many of whom caution that his power to reshape the system was limited. The Kaiser-Friedrich-Museum in Berlin (now the Bode Museum) was named in his memory.
