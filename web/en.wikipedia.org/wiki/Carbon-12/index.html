Carbon-12 (12C) is the more abundant of the two stable isotopes of carbon (carbon-13 being the other), amounting to 98.93% of element carbon on Earth; its abundance is due to the triple-alpha process by which it is created in stars. Carbon-12 is of particular importance in its use as the standard from which atomic masses of all nuclides are measured, thus, its atomic mass is exactly 12 daltons by definition. Carbon-12 is composed of 6 protons, 6 neutrons, and 6 electrons.

History as a measurement standard

Before 1961, physicists and chemists used different atomic mass scales based on oxygen. In 1961 the International Union of Pure and Applied Physics and Chemistry unified the scales by defining the atomic mass unit as exactly 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. This remained the basis of the mole until the 2019 redefinition of the SI base units, when the Avogadro constant was fixed at an exact value; the dalton remains defined relative to carbon-12.

Nucleosynthesis

Carbon-12 is synthesised in stars by the triple-alpha process, in which three helium-4 nuclei fuse. Fred Hoyle famously predicted that carbon-12 must possess an excited state — the Hoyle state at 7.65 MeV — to explain the observed abundance of carbon, since without the resonance the reaction would proceed too slowly. The state was subsequently discovered experimentally, and is often cited as an example of anthropic reasoning in physics.

Properties

The ground state of carbon-12 has spin and parity 0+. Because its nucleus is exceptionally stable and abundant, carbon-12 also serves as the reference for the relative isotopic masses used throughout chemistry, and the 12C/13C ratio is a standard measure in isotope geochemistry and in studies of photosynthesis.
