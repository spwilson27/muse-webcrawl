Allioideae is a subfamily of bulbous monocot flowering plants in the family Amaryllidaceae, order Asparagales. It was formerly treated as a separate family, Alliaceae. The subfamily is named for the type genus Allium.

Description

Members are herbaceous perennials arising from bulbs (occasionally rhizomes), with linear leaves that often smell of onion or garlic when crushed, owing to sulfur compounds such as allicin precursors. The flowers are borne in an umbel subtended by papery bracts; the ovary is superior — a feature distinguishing Allioideae from the other subfamilies of Amaryllidaceae.

Genera and distribution

The subfamily contains about 18 genera and roughly 1,000 species, the great majority in the single genus Allium, which includes onion, garlic, leek, chive and many ornamental species. Other notable genera include Tulbaghia, Gilliesia, Ipheion and Nothoscordum. The group is nearly cosmopolitan, with centres of diversity in the Mediterranean basin, Central Asia, and both North and South America.

Economic importance

Allioideae contains some of the world's oldest cultivated vegetables — onions and garlic have been grown for more than 5,000 years — as well as leeks, shallots, scallions and Chinese chives. Many Allium species are grown as ornamentals for their showy spherical flower heads.
