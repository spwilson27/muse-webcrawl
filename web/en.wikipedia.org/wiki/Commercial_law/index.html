Commercial law

Commercial law, also known as business law or trade law, is the body of law that applies to the rights, relations, and conduct of persons and businesses engaged in commerce, merchandising, trade, and sales. It is often considered a branch of civil law and deals with issues of both private law and public law.

Commercial law
ScopeContracts, sales, agency, negotiable instruments, corporations
Key sourcesUniform Commercial Code (US), Sale of Goods Act (UK), lex mercatoria

History

Commercial law developed in medieval Europe through the lex mercatoria, a body of customs applied by merchant courts at fairs and ports. In England, Lord Mansfield incorporated many of these customs into the common law in the 18th century. In the United States, the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), first published in 1952, harmonised sales and commercial transactions law across most states.

Areas

Contracts and sales

Rules govern formation, performance, and remedies for contracts for the sale of goods, distinct in many systems from general contract law. See contract and consumer protection.

Agency and partnership

Agency law regulates when a principal is bound by an agent's acts; partnership law governs co-owned unincorporated businesses.

Negotiable instruments and banking

Cheques, promissory notes, bills of exchange, secured transactions, and letters of credit are central commercial instruments.

Corporate and insolvency law

Although often treated separately, company formation, directors' duties, and bankruptcy interact closely with commercial transactions.

International commercial law

The United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG, 1980), UNCITRAL model laws, Incoterms, and World Trade Organization agreements provide a framework for cross-border trade. See also consumer choice.
