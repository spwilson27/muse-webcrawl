Via Appia

The Via Appia (Appian Way) is one of the earliest and strategically most important Roman roads of the ancient republic. It connected Rome to Brindisi, in southeast Italy. Begun in 312 BC by the censor Appius Claudius Caecus, after whom it is named, it was known to the Romans as the regina viarum, the "queen of roads".

The original basalt paving of the Via Appia near Rome, lined with tombs and pine trees.

History

The road was originally built during the Samnite Wars to move troops quickly toward Capua. It was progressively extended: to Venusia by 291 BC, to Tarentum by 272 BC, and finally to the Adriatic port of Brundisium (Brindisi) by about 264 BC, giving a total length of some 350 miles (560 km). Later emperors, notably Trajan, built a variant route, the Via Traiana, to ease the passage over the Apennines.

The Appian Way is famous in history as the road along which the captured followers of Spartacus were crucified in 71 BC — some 6,000 crosses lining the route from Capua to Rome. Because Roman law forbade burial within the city, the road outside the walls became lined with tombs and catacombs, including the early Christian catacombs of San Callisto and San Sebastiano.

Today

Long stretches of the original road survive. The first miles outside Rome are protected as the Appian Way Regional Park (Parco Regionale dell'Appia Antica), popular with walkers and cyclists, with the original basalt paving stones, Roman tombs, and aqueduct ruins visible along the route. In 2024, the Via Appia was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
