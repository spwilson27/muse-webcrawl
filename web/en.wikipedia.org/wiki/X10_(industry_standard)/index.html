X10 (industry standard)

X10 is a protocol for communication among electronic devices used for home automation (domotics). It primarily uses power line wiring for signalling and control, where the signals involve brief radio frequency bursts representing digital information. A wireless radio protocol is also defined in the X10 system.

History

X10 was developed in 1975 by Pico Electronics of Glenrothes, Scotland, and takes its name from being the company's tenth project. Products based on the technology reached the consumer market in 1978, sold through retailers such as Radio Shack and Sears, making X10 one of the earliest and most widely adopted home automation systems.

Technology

X10 controllers send signals over the household mains wiring by transmitting a 120 kHz carrier burst at the zero crossings of the AC waveform. Each device has a house code (A–P) and a unit code (1–16), allowing up to 256 addresses. Commands include on, off, dim, bright and "all lights on". Because the signals travel over the electrical wiring, installations require no new cables, but the system is susceptible to electrical noise and signal attenuation between phases of a split-phase supply, which may require couplers or repeaters.

Two-way modules can report their status, and computer interfaces allow scheduling and conditional control. Later additions included wireless remotes and motion sensors operating by radio.

Legacy

Although limited in speed and reliability compared with newer technologies such as Z-Wave, Zigbee and Insteon, X10 remained in use for decades because of its low cost, simplicity and large installed base, and modules remain available for hobbyists and legacy installations.
