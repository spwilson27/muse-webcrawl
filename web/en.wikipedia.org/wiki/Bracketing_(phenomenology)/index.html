Bracketing (phenomenology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Edmund Husserl, founder of phenomenology.

Bracketing (German: Einklammerung), also called phenomenological reduction or epoché, is a foundational operation in the philosophical method of phenomenology, developed by Edmund Husserl. It involves suspending—or "putting in brackets"—the natural attitude's assumption that the world exists independently of consciousness, together with all scientific theories and received beliefs about the objects of experience, so that the phenomenologist can describe phenomena exactly as they present themselves to consciousness.

The natural attitude and its suspension

In everyday life and in the natural sciences, Husserl argued, we inhabit the "natural attitude": we take for granted that the world and its objects exist "out there" whether or not we perceive them. Phenomenology cannot simply adopt this assumption if it is to become a rigorous science of consciousness, because the existence of the world is itself given only through conscious experience. The epoché therefore neither doubts nor denies the world's existence—as Cartesian doubt does—but sets the question aside: the phenomenologist "brackets" the being of the world and attends instead to the world as phenomenon, as meant and given in experience.

Stages of the reduction

Husserl described several related reductions. The phenomenological epoché excludes judgements about the independent existence of things. The eidetic reduction moves from particular facts to essences (eide), grasping the invariant structures of a kind of experience through imaginative variation. The transcendental reduction leads back from the bracketed world to transcendental subjectivity—the field of pure consciousness in which objects are constituted. What remains after the reduction is sometimes called the "phenomenological residuum": consciousness and its intentional correlates, analysed through the structures of intentionality, noesis and noema.

Reception and use

Bracketing became a key concept for later phenomenologists and existentialists such as Martin Heidegger, Jean-Paul Sartre, and Maurice Merleau-Ponty, though each transformed it—Heidegger, for instance, questioned whether presuppositions can ever be fully suspended. Hermeneutic thinkers argued that bracketing is an ideal that inquiry can approach but never completely achieve, since interpretation always begins from fore-understandings. Beyond philosophy, bracketing has been adopted as a methodological term in qualitative research, particularly in phenomenological psychology, nursing, and psychotherapy research, where it denotes the researcher's disciplined effort to set aside prior assumptions in order to describe participants' lived experience faithfully.
