Cello Concerto (Elgar)

Edward Elgar's Cello Concerto in E minor, Op. 85, his last notable work, is a cornerstone of the solo cello repertoire. Unlike his earlier, more expansive Violin Concerto, the Cello Concerto is for the most part spare and elegiac, reflecting the aftermath of the First World War. The work was composed during the summer of 1919 at Elgar's cottage near Fittleworth, Sussex, where his wife Alice recorded that he hummed the first movement's theme after recovering from an operation.

Premiere and early reception

The concerto received its first performance on 27 October 1919 by Felix Salmond and the London Symphony Orchestra conducted by the composer, in the opening concert of the orchestra's season at Queen's Hall. The performance was under-rehearsed and poorly received, and the work languished for decades. Elgar's own 1920 recording with Beatrice Harrison preserved his interpretation. Pablo Casals championed the concerto, but it achieved wide popularity only with Jacqueline du Pré's celebrated 1965 recording conducted by Sir John Barbirolli, which made it a signature piece of her artistry.

Structure

  1. Adagio – Moderato (E minor) — opening with a declamatory recitative for the solo cello, followed by the flowing main theme.
  2. Lento – Allegro molto (G major) — a nimble scherzo-like movement of delicate figuration.
  3. Adagio (B♭ major) — a deeply expressive slow movement sung largely by the soloist.
  4. Allegro – Moderato – Allegro, ma non troppo – Poco più lento – Adagio (E minor) — a finale whose bustle gives way to a return of the first movement's recitative and theme before a brief, decisive close.

Legacy

The concerto is now regarded as one of the finest in the cello repertoire, frequently recorded and performed, and strongly associated with du Pré, whose interpretation remains a benchmark. It stands as Elgar's farewell to large-scale composition.
