1951 Refugee Convention

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees, commonly known as the 1951 Refugee Convention or the Geneva Refugee Convention, is a United Nations multilateral treaty that defines the term “refugee”, outlines the rights of displaced persons and the legal obligations of states to protect them. Adopted on 28 July 1951 and in force from 22 April 1954, it originally covered persons displaced by events before 1951 (with an optional Europe limitation); the 1967 Protocol removed the temporal and geographic limits, universalising the regime.

Its core is non-refoulement (Article 33): no state may return a refugee to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened on account of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. The refugee definition (Article 1A(2)) — a well-founded fear of persecution on those five grounds, outside one's country and unable or unwilling to avail oneself of its protection — is interpreted through UNHCR's Handbook and national case law (extending gradually to gender-based and gang-related claims in some jurisdictions, restrictively in others). With about 146–149 parties to the Convention and Protocol combined, it anchors a regime strained by mass displacement and non-cooperative politics, debated between defenders of the post-war settlement and advocates of expanded or, conversely, curtailed protection.
