Roman à clef

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A roman à clef (French for "novel with a key") is a novel about real-life events that is overlaid with a façade of fiction. The fictitious names in the novel represent actual people, and the "key" is the relationship between the fiction and the non-fiction. The technique allows writers to depict real, often famous or scandalous, persons and events while maintaining plausible deniability.

History and examples

The form dates to 17th-century France, where writers such as Madeleine de Scudéry represented courtiers under classical pseudonyms, with keys circulated among insiders. Later examples include Aldous Huxley's Point Counter Point, Ernest Hemingway's The Sun Also Rises, Truman Capote's Answered Prayers, and The Bell Jar by Sylvia Plath, published under a pseudonym. Political and Hollywood novels frequently use the device, as in Primary Colors, based on Bill Clinton's 1992 presidential campaign.

Purpose

Authors use the roman à clef to satirise, to protect themselves from libel actions, to fictionalise their own lives in autobiographical novels, or to gossip with a measure of safety. Readers' pleasure often lies in decoding the identities behind the characters, and publishers sometimes include or omit literal keys listing the correspondences.
