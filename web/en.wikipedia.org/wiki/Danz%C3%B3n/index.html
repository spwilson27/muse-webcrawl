Danzón

Danzón is the official genre of music and dance of Cuba. Written in 2/4 time, it arose in Matanzas in the 1870s from a fusion of European contradanza traditions with African rhythmic elements, and became the island's first distinctly national ballroom dance. It is the direct ancestor of mambo and cha-cha-chá.

History

The first danzón recognised by name, "Las alturas de Simpson", was composed by Miguel Faílde and performed in Matanzas on 1 January 1879. Early danzón was played by orquestas típicas — wind-heavy ensembles — and later by the charanga francesa, which combined French-style flute and violins with African-derived percussion such as the güiro and timbales.

Musical structure

A classic danzón follows a rondo-like sectional form with repeated refrains alternating with contrasting sections (including a violin trio and a flute solo). Its rhythm is built on syncopated patterns derived from the cinquillo. In the 1920s and 1930s, musicians such as Antonio María Romeu expanded the form; in 1938 Orestes López added a syncopated final section — the nuevo ritmo — in his danzón "Mambo", giving birth to mambo. Danzón also flourished in Mexico, especially in Veracruz and Mexico City, and remains a cherished social dance in both countries.
