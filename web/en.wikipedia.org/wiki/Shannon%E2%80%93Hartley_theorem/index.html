Shannon–Hartley theorem


Field: Information theory
Formula: C = B log₂(1 + S/N)
Named for: Claude Shannon, Ralph Hartley

In information theory, the Shannon–Hartley theorem gives the maximum rate at which information can be transmitted over a communications channel of a specified bandwidth in the presence of noise. This maximum, the channel capacity C, equals the bandwidth B multiplied by the base-2 logarithm of one plus the signal-to-noise ratio S/N.

Statement

Formally, C = B log₂(1 + S/N), where C is in bits per second, B is the channel bandwidth in hertz, and S/N is the ratio of signal power to noise power. Below this rate, communication with arbitrarily low error is theoretically possible; above it, it is not.

History and implications

The result descends from Ralph Hartley's 1928 work on telegraph transmission and was completed by Claude Shannon in his 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication". It sets a fundamental limit approached by modern error-correcting codes such as turbo and LDPC codes, and explains why capacity grows only logarithmically with signal power but linearly with bandwidth.
