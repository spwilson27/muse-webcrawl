The Y chromosome is one of two sex chromosomes in therian mammals and some other organisms. It is the sex-determining chromosome in many species, since the presence or absence of Y typically determines the sex of offspring: in the XY system, males have one X and one Y chromosome, while females have two X chromosomes. The human Y chromosome spans roughly 57 to 63 million base pairs and represents about 2% of the total DNA in a male cell.

The Y is one of the smallest human chromosomes and carries far fewer genes than the X chromosome. Its most famous gene is SRY, which triggers the development of testes and thus male sex differentiation. Much of the Y consists of repetitive, non-recombining DNA; only the small pseudoautosomal regions pair and recombine with the X during meiosis. Full sequencing was long hindered by its repetitive structure—the male-specific region is rich in palindromic ampliconic sequences—and a complete telomere-to-telomere assembly was published only in 2023.

Evolution and degeneration

The X and Y chromosomes evolved from an ordinary pair of autosomes roughly 160–200 million years ago in early mammals. After the sex-determining gene arose, recombination between the proto-X and proto-Y was progressively suppressed, and the Y lost most of its genes in successive "strata" of degeneration. Despite predictions of continued decay, comparative studies show the remaining human Y genes have been under strong purifying selection and largely stable for tens of millions of years.

Inheritance and genealogy

Because the Y chromosome passes almost unchanged from father to son, its variation is used in genetic genealogy and population history through Y-chromosomal haplogroups. The most recent common male-line ancestor of all living humans is informally known as "Y-chromosomal Adam," estimated to have lived well over 100,000 years ago in Africa. Y-linked (holandric) inheritance is rare; most Y genes concern sex determination and spermatogenesis, and large deletions in the AZF regions are a known cause of male infertility.
