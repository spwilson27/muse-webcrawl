Cholinesterase inhibitor

Cholinesterase inhibitor

Cholinesterase inhibitors (also called anticholinesterases) are chemicals that inhibit the enzymes acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase, which normally break down the neurotransmitter acetylcholine. Blocking these enzymes increases the level and duration of action of acetylcholine in the central and peripheral nervous systems.

Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase prolongs the action of acetylcholine at cholinergic synapses.

Medical uses

Reversible cholinesterase inhibitors are used to treat Alzheimer's disease — donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine modestly improve symptoms by compensating for the loss of cholinergic neurons. Physostigmine and neostigmine are used in myasthenia gravis and to reverse non-depolarising neuromuscular blockade after surgery; pyridostigmine is a mainstay of myasthenia treatment. Echothiophate and other agents were historically used for glaucoma.

Toxicity

Excessive cholinergic stimulation produces the SLUDGE/DUMBELS syndrome: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, gastrointestinal cramps, emesis, miosis, bronchospasm, and bradycardia, progressing in severe poisoning to seizures, respiratory failure, and death. Treatment includes atropine, pralidoxime (an enzyme reactivator), and benzodiazepines.

Pesticides and chemical weapons

Many insecticides — organophosphates such as malathion and carbamates such as carbaryl — act as cholinesterase inhibitors, and accidental or deliberate exposure is a major cause of poisoning worldwide. The most potent anticholinesterases are the nerve agents (sarin, VX, soman, tabun), which phosphorylate acetylcholinesterase essentially irreversibly ("aging") and were developed from pesticide research in the 1930s–1950s.

Natural compounds

Plants produce several cholinesterase inhibitors, including physostigmine from the Calabar bean, huperzine A from Chinese club moss, and galantamine from snowdrop bulbs.
