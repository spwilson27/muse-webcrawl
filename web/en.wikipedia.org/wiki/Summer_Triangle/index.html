Summer Triangle
TypeAsterism
StarsVega (Lyra), Deneb (Cygnus), Altair (Aquila)
Best visibleJune to August (Northern Hemisphere)

The Summer Triangle is an astronomical asterism in the northern celestial hemisphere consisting of three bright stars — Vega, Deneb and Altair — which form the vertices of a large triangle spanning the constellations Lyra, Cygnus and Aquila respectively. The asterism dominates the summer evening sky at northern temperate latitudes and is a useful signpost for locating other stars and deep-sky objects, including the Milky Way, which runs through it along the length of Cygnus.

The stars

Vega (Alpha Lyrae), at magnitude 0.03, is the brightest star in the triangle and the fifth-brightest star in the night sky; it lies only about 25 light-years away. Altair (Alpha Aquilae), magnitude 0.76, is about 17 light-years distant and one of the nearest bright stars. Deneb (Alpha Cygni), though the faintest of the three at about magnitude 1.25, is by far the most luminous — a distant blue-white supergiant roughly 2,600 light-years away.

Visibility and history

The term "Summer Triangle" was popularised in the mid-20th century, notably by the British astronomer Sir Patrick Moore, although the grouping had been recognised informally earlier; American military navigators in the 1940s called the three stars the "Navigator's Triangle". The asterism is visible from most inhabited latitudes, appearing in the southern winter sky (inverted) for Southern Hemisphere observers. When the Summer Triangle is high in the evening sky, skywatchers use it to find the Ring Nebula in Lyra, the Coathanger cluster, and the Great Rift of the Milky Way.
