Maximilien de Béthune, Duke of Sully

Maximilien de Béthune, 1st Duke of Sully, Marquis of Rosny and Nogent, Count of Muret and Villebon, Viscount of Meaux (13 December 1560 – 22 December 1641) was a French nobleman, soldier, statesman, and counsellor of King Henry IV of France. A devout Huguenot, Sully was one of Henry's most trusted ministers, and he is particularly remembered for his efforts to improve French agriculture and for building the French road and canal network.

Early life

Maximilien de Béthune was born at the Château de Rosny near Mantes-la-Jolie into a branch of the House of Béthune. He was the second son of François de Béthune, Baron de Rosny, and Charlotte Dauvet. In 1572, at age eleven, he was presented to Henry of Navarre (the future Henry IV) and remained permanently in the household of the future king. The young Béthune accompanied Henry to Paris on the eve of the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre in August 1572, and escaped death himself.

Career under Henry IV

When Henry became king of France in 1589, Béthune steadily rose in his service. In 1596 he entered the council of finances, and in 1598 he became sole superintendent of finances (surintendant des finances), a post he held until Henry's assassination in 1610. Among his achievements were:

  • Paying off a large part of the royal debt and accumulating a reserve in the Bastille.
  • Lowering taxes, especially the taille, while increasing overall revenue.
  • Promoting agriculture — he famously declared that "labourage et pâturage sont les deux mamelles de la France" ("tillage and grazing are the two breasts of France").
  • Building roads and canals, including the Briare Canal.
  • Reorganizing the army and strengthening the frontier fortresses.
  • Restoring order in the provinces and suppressing abuses in tax collection.

In 1606 Henry raised him to Duke of Sully and peer of France.

Later life

After Henry IV's assassination in 1610, Sully's position weakened as the regent Marie de' Medici favored other advisers. He resigned his offices in 1611 and retired to his estates, where he composed his famous memoirs, the Mémoires des sages et royales économies d'État (published in part from 1638), a major source for the reign of Henry IV, though written with considerable self-justification. He died at the Château de Villebon on 22 December 1641.
