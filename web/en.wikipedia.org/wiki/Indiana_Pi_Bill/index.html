Indiana Pi Bill

The Indiana Pi Bill is the popular name for bill No. 246 of the 1897 session of the Indiana General Assembly, one of the most famous attempts to establish a mathematical result by legislative fiat. The bill, written by amateur mathematician Edwin J. Goodwin, purported to solve the ancient problem of squaring the circle; had it passed, it would in effect have enshrined several inconsistent values of π (pi) in law.

Background

Goodwin, a physician from Solitude, Indiana, believed he had squared the circle and published his construction in the American Mathematical Monthly in 1894. He offered the "discovery" to Indiana free of royalties for use in its schools if the state would officially adopt his "new mathematical truth". The bill was introduced by Representative Taylor I. Record at Goodwin's request.

Legislative history

The bill passed the Indiana House unanimously on 5 February 1897, after committees treated it as a patent-like measure rather than scrutinising its mathematics. In the Senate, however, Purdue University mathematics professor C. A. Waldo, who happened to be at the Statehouse on other business, explained the bill's errors to the senators. The Senate postponed the bill indefinitely on 12 February 1897, and it was never enacted.

Mathematical content

Goodwin's claims implied, among other things, that a circle's circumference-to-diameter ratio should be 3.2 and that √2 should equal 2 — values mutually inconsistent and both wrong. The episode is frequently retold (sometimes exaggerated) as a warning about legislating scientific facts, and it is often inaccurately said to have set π equal to 3.
