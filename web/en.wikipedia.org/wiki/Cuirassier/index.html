Cuirassier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A French cuirassier of the Napoleonic Wars, wearing steel cuirass and helmet.

A cuirassier was a cavalryman equipped with a cuirass — a piece of armour comprising a breastplate and backplate — and a helmet, armed typically with a long straight sword, pistols, and later a carbine. Cuirassiers were the heaviest cavalry of early modern and 19th-century European armies, descended from the armoured men-at-arms and the demi-lancers of the 16th century, and were used as battlefield shock troops intended to break enemy formations by the charge.

History

As firearms made full plate armour impractical, cavalry armour shrank during the 17th century to a helmet and cuirass, often proofed against pistol shot. Cuirassiers fought in the Thirty Years' War — Gottfried Heinrich Graf zu Pappenheim's regiments were famous — and in the English Civil War, where the London lobsters were among the few fully armoured units. By the 18th century most armies retained cuirassier regiments as their elite heavy cavalry, though Britain abandoned the cuirass on campaign until after Waterloo.

Cuirassiers reached their zenith in the Napoleonic Wars. Napoleon maintained up to fourteen regiments, whose massed charges — such as those at Eylau and Borodino — were decisive battlefield events. French, Austrian, Prussian, and Russian cuirassiers all wore polished steel or brass-faced cuirasses that were both protection and spectacle. Their charges, delivered at the trot or gallop in close formation with the sword, could shatter infantry that had not formed square and sweep away lighter cavalry.

Decline and survival

The increasing range and accuracy of rifled firearms and artillery made armoured shock charges ever costlier through the 19th century, and in 1870–71 French cuirassiers suffered heavily in heroic but futile charges against Prussian infantry. France sent cuirassiers to war in 1914 still wearing the breastplate (covered in cloth) and helmet, but they soon discarded armour in the trenches. Germany and Russia likewise converted their cuirassiers to ordinary cavalry or disbanded the armour.

The cuirass survives today only in ceremonial dress: the British Household Cavalry (Life Guards and Blues and Royals) wear cuirasses on state occasions, as do the French Republican Guard and the Italian Corazzieri, who guard the President of Italy — a role whose name preserves the same tradition.
