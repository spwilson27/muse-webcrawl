The Khufu ship is an intact full-size solar barque preserved from ancient Egypt, buried in a pit at the foot of the Great Pyramid of Giza around 2500 BC. It is among the oldest, largest, and best-preserved vessels from antiquity, and is thought to have been built for Khufu, the second pharaoh of the Fourth Dynasty—whether for use in life or for his journey in the afterlife remains debated.

Discovery and reconstruction

In May 1954 the Egyptian archaeologist Kamal el-Mallakh uncovered a sealed pit on the south side of the pyramid containing the ship dismantled into 1,224 pieces, carefully stacked in thirteen layers. The restorer Ahmed Youssef Moustafa spent years reassembling the vessel: 43.4 metres long and built chiefly of Lebanese cedar planks sewn together with rope, without a single nail. Rope rigging, oars, and a deckhouse of wood and—originally—gilded fittings survived. A second pit beside it, long left sealed, was opened to study in the 2010s; its timbers showed evidence the second boat had been used on water.

Design and purpose

The ship's hull is a shell-first, "sewn" construction whose curved profile rises gracefully at bow and stern, echoing papyrus rafts. Though seaworthy in form, the first ship showed few signs of actual use, supporting the interpretation that it was a ritual "solar barque" meant to carry the resurrected king with the sun god Ra across the heavens; others argue the ships ferried Khufu's body or pilgrims during his lifetime before being dismantled for burial. Five boat pits are associated with the pyramid.

Display

From 1982 the ship was exhibited in a purpose-built museum over its pit at Giza. In August 2021 it was transported in a specially designed vehicle to the Grand Egyptian Museum, where it forms a centrepiece exhibit alongside the treasures of Tutankhamun.
