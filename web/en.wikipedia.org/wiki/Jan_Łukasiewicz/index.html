Jan Łukasiewicz

Jan Łukasiewicz (21 December 1878 – 13 February 1956) was a Polish logician and philosopher who is best known for Polish notation and Łukasiewicz logic. A central figure of the Lwów–Warsaw school of logic, he pioneered many-valued logic, made fundamental contributions to the history of logic — particularly the rediscovery of Stoic propositional logic and the study of Aristotle's syllogistic — and served briefly as Poland's Minister of Education in 1919.

Life and career

Łukasiewicz was born in Lwów (then in Austrian Galicia) and studied mathematics and philosophy at the University of Lwów under Kazimierz Twardowski, obtaining his doctorate in 1902. He taught at Lwów and, from 1915, at the University of Warsaw, where he twice served as rector. In 1919 he was Minister of Education in the government of Ignacy Paderewski. With Stanisław Leśniewski and Alfred Tarski, he made interwar Warsaw one of the world's leading centres of mathematical logic. During the Second World War he worked in the Warsaw municipal archives; in 1944 he and his wife left Poland, eventually settling in Dublin in 1946 at the invitation of Irish premier Éamon de Valera. He became a member of the Royal Irish Academy and worked there until his death in 1956; he is buried in Mount Jerome Cemetery, Dublin.

Work

In 1920 Łukasiewicz introduced a three-valued logic, adding "possible" (indeterminate) as a third truth value alongside true and false — motivated by Aristotle's problem of future contingents, such as tomorrow's sea battle. He later generalised this to logics with finitely and infinitely many values. His logical research was facilitated by Polish notation (prefix notation), a parenthesis-free way of writing logical and arithmetical formulas that later found application in computer science, notably in stack-based calculators and the programming language Lisp's descendants. His historical studies, including Aristotle's Syllogistic from the Standpoint of Modern Formal Logic (1951), transformed the understanding of ancient logic, and he was the first to recognise that the Stoics had developed a propositional logic comparable to that of Frege.

Legacy

Łukasiewicz's axioms for propositional calculus, his work on modal logic and his axiomatic rejection-systems (a formal method for rejecting unprovable formulas) remain foundational in non-classical logic. The Institute of Philosophy at the University of Warsaw and several awards in logic bear his name.
