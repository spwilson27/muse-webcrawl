Stout

Stout
TypeDark ale
ColourVery dark brown to black
Alcohol by volumeTypically 4–12%
OriginLondon, England (from porter)

Stout is a type of dark beer brewed with roasted malt or roasted barley, hops, water, and yeast. It developed in 18th-century London from porter: the strongest porters were called "stout porters", and the name eventually denoted a distinct style. Stouts are characteristically dark — often opaque black — with flavours of coffee, chocolate, and roasted grain, and typically a creamy head.

Styles

Dry (Irish) stout, epitomised by Guinness Draught, is bitter and coffee-like, served with nitrogen for a smooth texture. Sweet or milk stout contains lactose for residual sweetness. Oatmeal stout uses oats for a silky body. Imperial stout, originally brewed strong for export to the Russian imperial court, can exceed 9–10% alcohol. Modern craft variants include coffee, chocolate, and pastry stouts.

History

The first known use of "stout" for beer dates to 1677. By the 19th century, Dublin's Guinness had become the dominant stout brewer; its Extra Stout and Foreign Extra Stout were exported worldwide. Stout declined in Britain in the later 20th century but revived strongly with the craft beer movement.
