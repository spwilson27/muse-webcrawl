Exclusivism

Exclusivism is the religious stance that only one particular religion, faith tradition, or belief system is true, and — in its soteriological form — that salvation, liberation or enlightenment is available only through that tradition. In the theology of religions, exclusivism is one of three classic positions, contrasted with inclusivism (one religion is uniquely true, but others participate in its truth or its members may be saved through it) and religious pluralism (multiple religions offer valid responses to the divine).

In theology of religions

The threefold typology was systematised by theologian Alan Race in 1983. Exclusivists typically ground their position in scripture and doctrinal authority, holding that truth claims of religions are mutually incompatible where they conflict, and that genuine adherence requires rejecting rival claims. The stance may be epistemic (only my religion's teachings are true), soteriological (only my religion's adherents are saved), or both.

Examples

Certain strands of Christianity have taught extra Ecclesiam nulla salus ("outside the Church there is no salvation"), and some evangelical and fundamentalist traditions maintain that explicit faith in Christ is necessary for salvation. In Islam, doctrines about the finality of Muhammad's prophethood and the supersession of earlier revelations can take exclusivist form, though interpretations vary widely. Exclusivist positions also appear in some forms of Judaism regarding covenant, and in various new religious movements. By contrast, Karl Rahner's notion of the "anonymous Christian" is a famous inclusivist alternative, and John Hick championed pluralism.

Criticism and defence

Critics argue that exclusivism is epistemically arrogant given the "accident of birth" — most people inherit the religion of their culture — and that it has historically fuelled intolerance, colonialism and conflict. Defenders respond that truth is by nature exclusive (contradictory claims cannot all be true), that taking one's own faith seriously entails believing it, and that exclusivism about belief is compatible with tolerance and respect in practice.

Broader usage

Outside theology, "exclusivism" is used more loosely for any doctrine or practice that draws sharp in-group/out-group boundaries — for example ethnic or national exclusivism, or exclusionary social policies.
