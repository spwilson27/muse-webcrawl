Swan

Swans are birds of the family Anatidae within the genus Cygnus. They are among the largest flying birds, closely related to geese and ducks. The six living species include the mute swan, trumpeter swan, tundra swan, whooper swan, black swan, and black-necked swan, distributed mainly across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, with species also in Australia and South America.

Description and behaviour

Swans are recognised by their long, curved necks, heavy bodies, and — in the mute swan — an orange bill with a black knob. They mate for life, though "divorce" occasionally occurs, and both parents care for the grey-brown young, called cygnets. Swans feed mainly on aquatic vegetation, upending in shallow water, and graze on land. They are strong fliers, migrating in V formation at speeds up to 60 mph, and despite their graceful reputation can be aggressively territorial, hissing and beating their wings at intruders.

In culture

Swans feature prominently in mythology and art: the Greek myth of Leda and the swan, the "swan song" believed to be sung before death, Tchaikovsky's ballet Swan Lake, Wagner's Lohengrin, and Hans Christian Andersen's "The Ugly Duckling". The swan maiden folktale, told worldwide, concerns a supernatural woman who transforms by means of a swan feather garment. In the United Kingdom, unmarked mute swans on open water traditionally belong to the Crown, a right exercised in the annual Swan Upping census.
