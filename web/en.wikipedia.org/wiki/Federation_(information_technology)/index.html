Federation (information technology)

ConceptInteroperation of independently administered systems
ExamplesEmail, XMPP, ActivityPub, federated identity

In information technology, federation is the practice of allowing independently owned and administered systems to interoperate as a single larger system through agreed standards and protocols, without any central operator controlling the whole. Each participant keeps control of its own users and data while communicating across the federation.

Examples

Email is the classic federation: servers run by different organizations exchange mail using SMTP, and any user can reach any other regardless of provider. Federated identity works similarly — with protocols such as SAML and OpenID Connect, an organization can accept logins vouched for by another trusted identity provider, enabling single sign-on across domains. Instant messaging's XMPP, and the ActivityPub protocol behind Mastodon and the "Fediverse," federate social networking so that users on different servers can follow and message one another. Federated databases likewise present data held in separate databases through one unified interface.

Advantages and challenges

Federation avoids vendor lock-in and single points of failure or control, and lets each community set its own policies — for example, its own moderation rules. The costs are coordination overhead, uneven feature support between implementations, and the difficulty of policing abuse that crosses administrative boundaries. The contrast between federated systems and centralized "walled garden" platforms is a recurring theme in debates over the structure of the internet.
