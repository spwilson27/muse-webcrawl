Biogeochemistry

Biogeochemistry studies how chemical elements cycle through living and non-living parts of the Earth system.

Biogeochemistry is the scientific discipline that involves the study of the chemical, physical, geological, and biological processes and reactions that govern the composition of the natural environment, including the biosphere, the hydrosphere, the pedosphere, the atmosphere, and the lithosphere. It focuses in particular on the cycles of chemical elements, such as carbon and nitrogen, and their interactions with and incorporation into living things.

History

The field was founded by the Russian and Ukrainian scientist Vladimir Vernadsky, whose 1926 book The Biosphere outlined the role of living matter as a geological force. The British scientist G. Evelyn Hutchinson is widely credited with developing the discipline's scope and concepts in the mid-twentieth century, and later figures such as W. H. Schlesinger produced influential syntheses of global cycles.

Biogeochemical cycles

Biogeochemists quantify the reservoirs in which an element is stored and the fluxes between them. Key cycles include the carbon cycle, nitrogen cycle, water cycle, oxygen cycle, phosphorus cycle and sulfur cycle. Human activities have profoundly altered several of these: fossil fuel combustion has increased atmospheric carbon dioxide, industrial nitrogen fixation now rivals natural fixation, and phosphorus mining has doubled the flow of phosphorus to the oceans.

Research and methods

Research combines field measurements, isotope geochemistry, remote sensing and computer modeling of the Earth system. Isotope ratios act as fingerprints that reveal reaction pathways and timescales, from the dating of groundwater to the reconstruction of ancient climates from sediment cores.
