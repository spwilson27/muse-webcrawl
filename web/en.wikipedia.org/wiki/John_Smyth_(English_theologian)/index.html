John Smyth (c. 1554 – c. 28 August 1612) was an English Anglican clergyman who became a Separatist and is generally regarded as the founder of the Baptist movement in England.

Life

Smyth was educated at Christ's College, Cambridge, and ordained in the Church of England, holding a lectureship at Lincoln. His Puritan views brought him into conflict with the authorities, and by 1606 he had joined a Separatist congregation at Gainsborough. Facing persecution, the group fled to Amsterdam in 1608.

The first Baptist church

In Amsterdam Smyth concluded that baptism should be administered only to professing believers, not infants. In 1609 he baptised himself (by pouring, earning the nickname "the Se-Baptist") and then his followers, including Thomas Helwys, forming the first Baptist church. Smyth soon sought reconciliation with the Dutch Mennonites and applied for membership, a move Helwys opposed; Helwys led part of the congregation back to England in 1611–12, founding the first Baptist church on English soil at Spitalfields, London. Smyth died in Amsterdam in 1612 while his application to the Mennonites was pending.

Theology

Smyth's writings, including The Differences of the Churches of the Separation (1608) and his final confession of faith, defended believer's baptism, a gathered church of visible saints, and liberty of conscience. His Propositions and Conclusions (1612) asserted that magistrates should not meddle in religion — one of the earliest English statements of full religious freedom.
