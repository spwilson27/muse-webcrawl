Inspector general
An official charged with auditing and investigating an organization.

An inspector general is an official oversight officer within a government agency, military organization, or large institution, responsible for auditing operations, investigating fraud, waste, abuse, and misconduct, and reporting findings to both the head of the organization and, typically, a legislature.

United States federal inspectors general

In the United States federal government, Offices of Inspector General (OIGs) were established on a government-wide basis by the Inspector General Act of 1978, initially in 12 agencies and later expanded to more than 70 statutory offices. Federal inspectors general are appointed in one of two ways: by the President with Senate confirmation for cabinet departments and large agencies, or by the agency head for smaller designated federal entities. They conduct independent audits and investigations, issue semiannual reports to Congress, operate whistleblower hotlines, and may refer criminal matters for prosecution. The Council of the Inspectors General on Integrity and Efficiency (CIGIE) coordinates the community. Removal protections and required notifications to Congress have been amended over time, notably by the Inspector General Reform Act of 2008 and subsequent legislation.

Other uses

Many U.S. states and cities maintain inspectors general, as do universities, corporations, and international bodies. In military usage, an inspector general investigates complaints by service members and inspects readiness; the concept dates to the inspecteurs généraux of the French army and to Baron von Steuben, the first Inspector General of the Continental Army. Other countries use analogous officials, sometimes styled ombudsmen or auditors general.
