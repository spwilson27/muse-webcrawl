Retroactivity (law)

Retroactive legislation is legislation that operates on matters, transacted in the past, or rights and obligations that existed previously. Many legal systems generally frown upon retroactive laws, as they are seen as unjust: they change the legal consequences of acts already performed. A law that is applied retrospectively is said to have retroactive effect.

Ex post facto laws

Retroactivity is closely related to the prohibition of ex post facto laws. Article I of the United States Constitution forbids Congress and the states from passing ex post facto laws — laws that criminalize acts committed before the law's enactment, increase the punishment for a crime after its commission, or otherwise disadvantage a criminal defendant retroactively. The U.S. Supreme Court's test derives from Calder v. Bull (1798).

Civil retroactivity

In civil law, retroactive statutes are sometimes permissible. In the United States, courts apply a presumption against retroactivity (Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 1994) unless Congress clearly states otherwise. Retroactive tax laws and curative statutes that remedy defects in earlier laws are common examples.

Other jurisdictions

The European Convention on Human Rights (Article 7) prohibits retroactive criminal liability, and many countries' constitutions contain similar protections. Courts in the United Kingdom apply a common-law presumption against retrospectivity, though Parliament may legislate retroactively if it uses clear words.
