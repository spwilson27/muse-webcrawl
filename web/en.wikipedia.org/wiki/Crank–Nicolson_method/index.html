Crank–Nicolson method

In numerical analysis, the Crank–Nicolson method is a finite difference scheme used for numerically solving partial differential equations, particularly the heat equation and other parabolic (diffusion-like) problems. Developed by the British physicists John Crank and Phyllis Nicolson in 1947, the method is a second-order scheme in both time and space. It is the average of the forward Euler method at time step n and the backward Euler method at step n + 1 — equivalently, it applies the trapezoidal rule in time — which makes it implicit but unconditionally stable for linear diffusion problems.

Formulation

For the one-dimensional heat equation ∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x², the centred difference in space is evaluated at both the old and the new time level and averaged. Writing r = αΔt/(Δx)², the scheme is:

−r·ui−1n+1 + (2 + 2r)·uin+1 − r·ui+1n+1 = r·ui−1n + (2 − 2r)·uin + r·ui+1n

At each time step a tridiagonal linear system must be solved (efficiently, by the Thomas algorithm), so the cost per step is higher than for an explicit method, but the unconditional stability — there is no constraint on Δt from the CFL-type condition that limits explicit schemes — permits much larger time steps for stiff problems.

Accuracy and stability

The local truncation error is O(Δt²) + O(Δx²), making the method second-order accurate in both variables, in contrast to the first-order-in-time Euler schemes. Von Neumann stability analysis shows the amplification factor never exceeds unity in magnitude for the linear heat equation, so the scheme is unconditionally stable and A-stable. A practical caveat is that for very large time steps relative to the spatial mesh, non-physical oscillations can appear near sharp initial gradients, because the method is not L-stable; this can be mitigated by taking a few small backward-Euler starting steps (Rannacher smoothing).

Applications

Crank–Nicolson is a standard solver for diffusion equations in heat transfer and groundwater flow, for the Black–Scholes equation in mathematical finance, and, in split form, for the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, where its unitarity preserves the norm of the wavefunction. Variants such as the Douglas–Gunn alternating-direction implicit scheme extend the approach to two and three spatial dimensions while retaining tridiagonal solves.
