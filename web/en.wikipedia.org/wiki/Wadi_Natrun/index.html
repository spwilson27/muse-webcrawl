Wadi Natrun (Arabic: وادي النطرون Wādī n-Naṭrūn, "Valley of Natron"; Coptic: Ϣⲓϩⲏⲧ Šihēt, "measure of the hearts"; Greek: Σκετις or Σκετες) is a depression in northern Egypt, located in Beheira Governorate. It is included in the UNESCO tentative list under the name "Monasteries of the Arab Desert and Wadi Natrun".

The valley lies about 23 metres (75 ft) below sea level and 38 metres (125 ft) below the level of the Nile, northwest of Cairo between the capital and Alexandria. It contains a chain of alkaline lakes rich in natron, a naturally occurring mixture of sodium carbonate and sodium bicarbonate used since antiquity.

History

In ancient Egypt, natron from Wadi Natrun was essential to the mummification process, as a drying and preserving agent; it was also used in glass-making and ceramics and as a cleansing agent. The valley's mineral wealth made it an important economic resource for millennia.

Wadi Natrun is far better known, however, as one of the birthplaces of Christian monasticism. Known in antiquity as Scetis, it was settled from the 4th century AD by hermits such as Macarius the Great, and together with Nitria and Kellia formed one of the three great centres of early desert monasticism in Egypt. The sayings and lives of its monks were recorded in works such as the Apophthegmata Patrum (Sayings of the Desert Fathers).

Monasteries

Of the dozens of monasteries that once flourished in the valley, four ancient Coptic Orthodox monasteries survive today: the Monastery of Saint Macarius the Great (Deir Abu Maqar), the Monastery of Saint Pishoy (Deir Anba Bishoy), the Monastery of the Syrians (Deir el-Surian), and the Monastery of the Romans (Deir el-Baramus). These remain active centres of Coptic monastic life and pilgrimage; the Monastery of Saint Pishoy is a favoured retreat of the Coptic popes, and Pope Shenouda III is buried there.
