Gas mantle

A gas mantle (also incandescent gas mantle or Welsbach mantle) is a device for generating bright white light when heated by a flame. It consists of a mesh bag of refractory oxides — historically about 99% thorium dioxide and 1% cerium dioxide — suspended over a gas burner. The flame itself burns almost invisibly; the mantle glows incandescently, converting much more of the fuel's energy into visible light than a bare flame.

History

The Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach patented the incandescent mantle in the 1880s and commercialised it in the 1890s after discovering that a mixture of thorium and cerium oxides gave an exceptionally bright, white glow and a durable ash skeleton. Mantle lighting transformed gas street lighting and domestic lamps, allowing gas to compete with early electric light for several decades. Gas mantles remain in production for camping lanterns and pressure lamps burning kerosene or liquefied petroleum gas.

Manufacture and operation

Mantles are made by knitting or weaving a fabric of cotton, silk or rayon, impregnating it with thorium and cerium nitrates, and shaping it into a bag. On first use the organic fabric burns away, leaving a fragile ceramic skeleton of the oxides that shrinks to its working size. In operation the mantle must be handled gently, as the ash structure crumbles easily.

Safety

The thorium in traditional mantles is weakly radioactive; while the exposure from a single lantern mantle is small, manufacturers came under pressure to substitute non-radioactive materials, and many modern mantles use yttrium or other rare-earth oxides instead. Thorium mantles are still sold in some markets.
