The Tyndale Bible generally refers to the body of biblical translations by William Tyndale (c. 1494–1536) into Early Modern English, made from the Hebrew and Greek texts. Tyndale's was the first English translation to be produced using printing, and the first to draw directly on the original-language texts rather than the Latin Vulgate.

Tyndale's New Testament was first printed at Worms in 1526, after an attempted printing at Cologne in 1525 was interrupted; copies were smuggled into England, where they were condemned and burned by the authorities. He went on to translate the Pentateuch (1530) and other portions of the Old Testament, though he was arrested near Brussels and executed for heresy in 1536 before completing the Old Testament.

Influence

Though banned in his lifetime, Tyndale's translation became the basis of all subsequent English Bibles of the Reformation era. A very large proportion of the King James Version (1611) reproduces Tyndale's wording in passages he translated, and he is credited with coining or popularising many English expressions still in use, including "Passover", "scapegoat", and "let there be light" phrasing traditions. His work also deeply influenced the Geneva Bible and the Great Bible.

Surviving copies

Only a few complete copies of the 1526 Worms New Testament survive; the British Library holds one of the best-known examples. The Cologne fragment of 1525 is unique.
