Marcasite is an iron sulfide mineral with the chemical formula FeS2. It is a polymorph of pyrite: both minerals have the same composition, but marcasite crystallises in the orthorhombic system, while pyrite is cubic. Its name derives from the Arabic and medieval Latin word marqashītā, originally applied to pyrite and related minerals.

Properties and occurrence

Marcasite is pale brass-yellow and slightly lighter in colour than pyrite. It is brittle and forms distinctive spear-shaped twinned crystals, sometimes in radiating groups, and also occurs in concretions and massive forms. It is found in sedimentary rocks — particularly chalk, limestone, and clays — and in coal measures, often in association with pyrite. Marcasite is metastable at low temperatures and gradually transforms to pyrite.

Uses and problems

Because it decays readily in moist air, oxidising to iron sulfate and sulfuric acid, marcasite was historically used for making fire by striking it with flint (the name "fire stone" was sometimes applied), and in the early development of crystal radio receivers as a detector crystal. Its tendency to crumble and damage collections makes well-preserved marcasite specimens of particular interest to mineral collectors.

In jewellery, the name "marcasite" is also applied to pyrite cut as small faceted stones set in silver — the traditional "marcasite jewellery" of the Victorian era — although most of this material is actually pyrite rather than true marcasite.
