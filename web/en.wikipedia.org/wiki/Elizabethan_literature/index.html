Elizabethan literature

Elizabethan literature refers to bodies of work produced during the reign of Queen Elizabeth I (1558–1603) and is one of the most celebrated eras of English literature. During this period, the English language matured and writers explored new forms, influenced by the Renaissance and the rediscovery of classical authors. From Marlowe and Shakespeare to Jonson and Donne, the age produced landmark drama and poetry; prose, too, flourished in chronicle, pamphlet, romance, and translation.

The First Folio of Shakespeare (1623), the capstone of the Elizabethan dramatic legacy.

Drama

The period saw the building of the first permanent public theatres in London — The Theatre (1576), the Globe (1599) — and an explosion of popular drama. Christopher Marlowe pioneered mighty blank verse in plays such as Tamburlaine and Doctor Faustus. William Shakespeare, writing for the Lord Chamberlain's Men, produced histories, comedies, tragedies, and romances that are generally considered the summit of English literature. Contemporaries include Thomas Kyd (The Spanish Tragedy), Ben Jonson (whose career extends into the Jacobean era), John Webster, Thomas Dekker, and Thomas Middleton.

Poetry

Poetry in the Elizabethan age was dominated by the sonnet sequence and lyric verse. Edmund Spenser's epic The Faerie Queene (1590–1596) allegorized the Elizabethan court; his Amoretti joined sonnet sequences by Sir Philip Sidney (Astrophil and Stella, published 1591), Shakespeare's sonnets (1609), Samuel Daniel, and Michael Drayton. Spenser also reinvigorated the pastoral and invented the nine-line Spenserian stanza. Songbooks of madrigals and lute airs by composers such as Thomas Campion and John Dowland flourished alongside print miscellanies.

Prose and translation

Prose writers include John Lyly, whose Euphues (1578) gave English the ornate "euphuistic" style; Sidney's prose romance Arcadia; Richard Hakluyt's Principal Navigations, celebrating English voyages; and chronicles by Holinshed that supplied Shakespeare with plots. Great translations shaped the language: the Geneva Bible (1560) and Bishops' Bible, North's Plutarch, and (just after Elizabeth's death) the King James Bible of 1611, translated by committees formed under James I but rooted in Elizabethan scholarship.

Context

Literary production was enabled by expanding literacy, the printing press, grammar-school education in Latin classics, and court patronage — while being constrained by censorship, licensing by the Master of the Revels, and periodic suppression of satire (the Bishops' Ban of 1599). The era shades into Jacobean literature after 1603.
