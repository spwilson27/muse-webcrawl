Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1537–1598) was a Japanese samurai and daimyō who completed the unification of Japan begun by his lord Oda Nobunaga. Of peasant origin, he rose through ability alone to become imperial regent (kampaku) — one of the most remarkable social ascents of pre-modern history — and launched Japan's invasions of Korea in the Imjin War.

Rise under Nobunaga

Born in Nakamura in Owari Province to a peasant foot-soldier's family, Hideyoshi entered Nobunaga's service — legend begins him as a sandal-bearer — and rose to become one of his most trusted generals, famed for feats such as building Sunomata Castle in a single night and his patient siege warfare in the Chūgoku region. When Nobunaga was assassinated at Honnō-ji in 1582, Hideyoshi rushed back, defeated the assassin Akechi Mitsuhide at Yamazaki, and outmanoeuvred rival generals at Shizugatake (1583) to take control of the Oda inheritance.

Unification and rule

From his great base at Osaka Castle, Hideyoshi subdued Shikoku (1585) and Kyushu (1587) and destroyed the Hōjō at Odawara in 1590, completing unification. Unable as a commoner to become shōgun, he had himself adopted into the Fujiwara and took the titles kampaku and later taikō, and the new clan name Toyotomi from the emperor. His government fixed Japan's social order: the sword hunt of 1588 disarmed the peasantry, nationwide land surveys (Taikō kenchi) standardised taxation, and merchants and missionaries fell under strict control, culminating in the martyrdom of the Twenty-six Martyrs of Japan in 1597.

Korean invasions and death

In 1592 Hideyoshi invaded Korea with the ultimate ambition of conquering Ming China; after early successes the war bogged down against Korean guerrillas, Admiral Yi Sun-sin's navy and Ming intervention, and a second invasion in 1597 also failed. Hideyoshi died in September 1598, ordering the withdrawal; power passed to a Council of Five Elders for his young son Hideyori, but within two years Tokugawa Ieyasu seized supremacy at Sekigahara.
