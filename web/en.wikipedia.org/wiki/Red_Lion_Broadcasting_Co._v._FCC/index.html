Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. Federal Communications Commission, 395 U.S. 367 (1969), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States upholding the constitutionality of the fairness doctrine in broadcasting. The Court held that because broadcast frequencies are scarce, the government may require licensees to present controversial issues of public importance in a balanced manner and to give a right of reply to persons personally attacked on air.

Background

Red Lion Broadcasting, owner of radio station WGCB in Red Lion, Pennsylvania, had aired a 1964 broadcast by the Reverend Billy James Hargis attacking journalist Fred J. Cook, author of a book critical of Senator Barry Goldwater. Invoking the FCC's personal-attack rules, Cook demanded free reply time; the station refused. The FCC ordered the station to provide reply time, and Red Lion sued, arguing the rules violated the First Amendment.

Decision

Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice Byron White upheld the regulations. The opinion famously reasoned: "It is the right of the viewers and listeners, not the right of the broadcasters, which is paramount." Because the scarcity of the electromagnetic spectrum makes broadcasting a uniquely regulable medium, broadcasters act as trustees of a public resource and may be required to share it with others. The Court found no First Amendment right to monopolise a frequency.

Legacy

Red Lion remained the principal authority for reduced First Amendment protection of broadcast media and was distinguished in cases extending stronger protection to print (Miami Herald v. Tornillo, 1974) and the internet (Reno v. ACLU, 1997). The FCC itself repealed the fairness doctrine in 1987, concluding it chilled speech, though the personal-attack rule survived until 2000. Red Lion has never been overruled.
