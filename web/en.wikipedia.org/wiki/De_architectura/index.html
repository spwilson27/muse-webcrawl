De architectura (On Architecture, published as Ten Books on Architecture) is a treatise on architecture written by the Roman architect and engineer Marcus Vitruvius Pollio and dedicated to his patron, the emperor Augustus, around 30–20 BC. It is the only major work on architecture to survive from classical antiquity and one of the few complete technical texts from the Roman world.

Contents

The ten books cover the education of the architect; building materials; temples and the classical orders; public buildings including theatres and baths; private houses; floors, stucco and painting; water supply and aqueducts; astronomy and sundials; and civil and military machines. Vitruvius insists a building must satisfy firmitas (solidity), utilitas (usefulness) and venustas (beauty) — the "Vitruvian triad" — and derives architectural proportion from the human body, the source of Leonardo da Vinci's Vitruvian Man.

Influence

Rediscovered by Renaissance humanists — Poggio Bracciolini located a manuscript at St Gall in 1414 — the treatise profoundly shaped Alberti, Palladio and Renaissance and neoclassical architecture generally, and remains a primary source for Roman construction technology.
