Axin

Axin is a family of scaffold proteins that play a central role in the Wnt signalling pathway. In vertebrates there are two axin genes, AXIN1 and AXIN2, encoding the proteins axin 1 and axin 2 (also known as conductin). Axin acts as a tumour suppressor by organising the "destruction complex" that regulates levels of the transcriptional co-activator β-catenin.

Function in Wnt signalling

In the absence of a Wnt signal, axin binds β-catenin together with the kinases glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) and casein kinase 1 (CK1), and the tumour suppressor APC. Within this complex, β-catenin is sequentially phosphorylated, which marks it for ubiquitination and degradation by the proteasome. When Wnt ligands engage their Frizzled and LRP5/6 receptors, the destruction complex is inhibited, β-catenin accumulates, and it enters the nucleus to activate target gene transcription with TCF/LEF factors.

Axin is the concentration-limiting component of the destruction complex and is itself regulated: its abundance is controlled by the poly-ADP-ribosylating enzyme tankyrase, whose inhibition stabilises axin and suppresses Wnt signalling — a strategy explored in cancer drug development.

Clinical significance

Mutations in AXIN1 are associated with hepatocellular carcinoma and other cancers, while mutations in AXIN2 can cause tooth agenesis (oligodontia) and predispose to colorectal cancer. Axin was originally identified in the mouse through the fused locus, whose mutation causes axial duplications during embryonic development.
