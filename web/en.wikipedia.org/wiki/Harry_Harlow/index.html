Harry Harlow

Harry Frederick Harlow (October 31, 1905 – December 6, 1981) was an American psychologist best known for his maternal-separation, dependency needs, and social isolation experiments on rhesus monkeys at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. His work demonstrated the importance of caregiving and companionship in social and cognitive development.

Surrogate mother experiments

In the 1950s Harlow separated infant monkeys from their mothers and offered them two surrogate mothers: one made of bare wire that provided food, and one covered in soft terry cloth that did not. The infants spent most of their time clinging to the cloth mother and visited the wire mother only to feed, showing that "contact comfort", not food alone, drives attachment — a finding that challenged behaviourist accounts and influenced attachment theory.

Criticism

Harlow's later isolation experiments, including the "pit of despair", caused severe psychological harm to the monkeys and are widely cited in debates about animal research ethics; they contributed to the strengthening of animal welfare regulation in the United States.
