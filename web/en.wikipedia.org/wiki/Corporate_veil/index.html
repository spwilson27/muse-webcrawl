Corporate veil

The corporate veil is a legal metaphor describing the separate legal personality of a corporation: a company is treated in law as distinct from its shareholders, directors and employees, so that the company's rights, duties and liabilities are its own. The doctrine was firmly established in English law by the House of Lords in Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897], which held that a duly registered company is a legal person separate from the individuals who formed it, even where one person effectively controls it.

Lifting or piercing the veil

In exceptional circumstances courts may "lift", "pierce" or look behind the corporate veil and treat the company and its controllers as one, typically where the corporate form is used as a façade to evade existing legal obligations or perpetrate fraud. In English law the scope of the doctrine was narrowed by Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013], in which the Supreme Court distinguished the "concealment principle" (looking behind the veil to identify facts hidden by it) from true "evasion", where a person interposes a company to defeat an existing liability.

Undercapitalisation, failure to observe corporate formalities, and intermingling of personal and corporate assets may also lead courts in the United States to pierce the veil, especially in close corporations and corporate groups. Statutes may likewise impose liability directly, for example for unpaid taxes or wages.

Related doctrines

Related concepts include limited liability, which the veil underpins; enterprise liability within corporate groups; and the rule that directors who trade wrongfully while a company is insolvent may incur personal liability.
