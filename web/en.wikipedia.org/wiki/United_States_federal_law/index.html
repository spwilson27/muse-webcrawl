United States federal law

United States federal law is the body of law created by the federal government of the United States. The Constitution of the United States, which replaced the Articles of Confederation in 1789, is the supreme law of the land; federal statutes enacted by Congress, treaties, and regulations issued by executive agencies operate within its framework.

Sources

Federal law originates from several sources: the Constitution and its amendments; statutes passed by Congress and signed by the President, published in the Statutes at Large and codified in the United States Code; regulations promulgated by agencies, published in the Federal Register and codified in the Code of Federal Regulations; and case law produced by the federal judiciary.

Federalism and supremacy

The Tenth Amendment reserves powers not delegated to the federal government to the states or the people, so most everyday law — criminal law, property, contracts, family law — is state law. Under the Supremacy Clause of Article VI, however, valid federal law preempts conflicting state law. Congress's authority rests chiefly on the enumerated powers of Article I, including the Commerce Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause.

Federal courts

The federal judiciary, headed by the Supreme Court, consists of 94 district courts, 13 courts of appeals, and specialized courts such as the Court of Federal Claims. Federal courts hear cases arising under the Constitution and federal statutes, and cases between citizens of different states meeting jurisdictional thresholds.
