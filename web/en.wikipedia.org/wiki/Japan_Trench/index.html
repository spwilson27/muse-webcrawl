Japan Trench

The Japan Trench is an oceanic trench, part of the Pacific Ring of Fire, in the floor of the northern Pacific Ocean off northeastern Japan. It extends about 800 kilometres (497 mi) from the Kuril Islands to the northern Izu Islands, and reaches a maximum depth of about 8,000 metres.

Geology

The trench marks the subduction zone where the Pacific Plate slides westward beneath the continental Okhotsk Plate (often treated as part of the North American Plate), moving at roughly 8–9 cm per year. North of the trench lies the Kuril–Kamchatka Trench; to the south it meets the Izu–Ogasawara Trench.

Seismicity

The subduction zone is one of the most seismically active on Earth. On 11 March 2011 it ruptured in the magnitude 9.0–9.1 Tōhoku earthquake, the most powerful ever recorded in Japan, which generated a devastating tsunami and triggered the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster. Earlier great earthquakes along the trench include those of 869 (Jōgan), 1896 (Meiji-Sanriku) and 1933 (Shōwa-Sanriku).

Exploration

In 2022 the trench was visited by crewed submersibles exploring its hadal depths, and the caldera-forming geology of the region has been studied by the drilling vessel Chikyū.
