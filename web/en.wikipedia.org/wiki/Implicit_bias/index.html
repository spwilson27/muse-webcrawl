Implicit bias
Also calledImplicit stereotype, unconscious bias
FieldSocial psychology, cognitive psychology
Common measureImplicit Association Test
Contrasts withExplicit bias (consciously endorsed attitudes)

Implicit bias (also called implicit stereotype or unconscious bias) refers to attitudes or stereotypes that affect a person's understanding, actions and decisions in an unconscious manner. These associations are held to operate automatically, outside conscious awareness and control, and may conflict with a person's explicitly endorsed beliefs and values.

Concept

Psychologists propose that the mind forms associations between groups of people and traits (for example, age and competence) through repeated exposure to cultural messages and personal experience. Such associations can be activated automatically when a person encounters a member of a group, potentially influencing judgements in hiring, policing, medicine and education. Implicit biases are distinguished from explicit biases, which are deliberate and self-reported.

Measurement

The most common measure is the Implicit Association Test (IAT), which infers association strength from categorisation speed. Other methods include evaluative priming and the affect misattribution procedure. Scores on these measures correlate only weakly with one another and with explicit attitude measures, and their ability to predict individual discriminatory behaviour is contested.

Debate and interventions

Implicit-bias training has been widely adopted by employers, universities and police forces, though evidence that it produces lasting behavioural change is mixed; some reviews find small, short-lived effects on measures but little effect on behaviour. Researchers instead emphasise structural and procedural remedies, such as blind review and standardised decision criteria. The scientific debate continues over the construct's predictive validity, reliability and practical significance.
