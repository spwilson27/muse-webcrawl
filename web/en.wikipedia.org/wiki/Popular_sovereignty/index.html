Popular sovereignty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Popular sovereignty is the principle that the authority of a state and its government is created and sustained by the consent of its people, who are the source of all political power. It is the normative foundation of modern democracy and republicanism: rulers are the people's agents or trustees, not their owners. The doctrine was developed by the social-contract theorists — given classic form by Rousseau's theory of the general will — and proclaimed in the American Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789): “governments are instituted among men, deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed”.

In constitutional practice popular sovereignty underlies constituent power — the people's authority to make and remake constitutions, exercised through conventions and referendums — and coexists, sometimes tensely, with constitutional limits on the people's present-day representatives and even on popular majorities themselves (entrenched rights, amendment rules). In nineteenth-century America the phrase also named the policy of letting territorial settlers decide the slavery question, a usage associated with Stephen A. Douglas and the Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854).
