Fluvial processes

Fluvial processes are the physical interactions between flowing water in rivers and streams and the Earth's surface — the erosion, transport and deposition of sediment that shape valleys, floodplains, alluvial fans and deltas. Fluvial geomorphology studies these processes and the landforms they produce. Moving water is the most important agent of landscape change on the continents.

Erosion and transport

Rivers erode by hydraulic action, abrasion (corrasion), attrition, solution and cavitation, wearing down their beds and banks. Eroded material — the sediment load — travels as dissolved load, suspended load, and bed load (saltation and traction). Hjulström's curve relates particle size to the flow velocities needed to erode, transport and deposit grains: fine clays require high velocities to erode (because of cohesion) but settle only in near-still water, while sands move easily. A stream's competence (largest particle it can move) and capacity (total load it can carry) increase sharply with discharge, so most geomorphic work is done in floods.

Channel patterns and landforms

Where sediment supply and slope favour it, channels braid into multiple shifting threads; where fine sediment and gentle gradients prevail, channels meander, eroding cut banks on the outside of bends and depositing point bars inside, eventually cutting off oxbow lakes. At mountain fronts, rivers drop coarse sediment in alluvial fans; where rivers enter standing water they build deltas. Downcutting produces V-shaped valleys, terraces and, where uplift rejuvenates a landscape, incised meanders and gorges. Key landforms include levees, floodplains, potholes, plunge pools, riffles and pools, and yardang-like fluvial features on other planets, notably Mars, where dry channels record ancient flowing water.

Deposition and drainage basins

Deposition occurs wherever flow competence falls — on the inside of meanders, in expanding reaches, at lakes and seas. The drainage basin (watershed) organises fluvial processes: precipitation falling within the basin converges through tributaries (ordered by the Strahler system) to the main channel, whose longitudinal profile typically declines from steep headwaters to a graded lower course approaching base level.
