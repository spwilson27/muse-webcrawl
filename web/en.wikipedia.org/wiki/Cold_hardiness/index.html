Cold hardiness

Cold hardiness is the ability of a plant to survive low temperatures, particularly sub-freezing temperatures, without serious injury. It is a central concept in horticulture and agriculture, determining which perennial plants can be grown outdoors in a given climate. Hardiness is usually expressed as the minimum temperature a plant can tolerate, and is summarised for gardeners by hardiness zone systems such as the USDA plant hardiness zones, which divide regions by average annual minimum winter temperature.

Mechanisms

Hardy plants acclimate to cold in autumn in response to shortening days and falling temperatures — a process called cold acclimation or hardening off. Cells accumulate soluble sugars and protective proteins (including dehydrins), membrane lipid composition changes to remain fluid, and in many woody plants water is withdrawn from cells so that ice forms in intercellular spaces rather than lethally inside the cells. Some species tolerate deep supercooling of tissues. Hardiness is lost (deacclimation) as temperatures rise in spring, so late frosts after a warm spell can be especially damaging.

Factors

Hardiness varies by species, cultivar, plant age, health, soil drainage and snow cover, which insulates roots. A plant's roots are generally less hardy than its top growth, a consideration for container-grown plants.
