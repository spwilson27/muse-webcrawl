Somali Rebellion

The Somali Rebellion was an armed uprising against the regime of President Siad Barre that developed during the 1980s and culminated in the outbreak of the Somali Civil War. Dissatisfaction with Barre's authoritarian, clan-based rule — sharpened by Somalia's defeat in the Ogaden War against Ethiopia (1977–78) — led to the formation of several clan-based insurgent movements.

Origins

After the Ogaden defeat, failed coup attempts and growing repression pushed opposition groups into exile, mainly in Ethiopia. The Somali Salvation Democratic Front (SSDF), largely Majeerteen, began armed operations in the northeast from 1978. The Somali National Movement (SNM), largely Isaaq, was founded in 1981 and launched operations in the northwest. The United Somali Congress (USC), largely Hawiye, formed in 1989 and built strength in the central regions and Mogadishu.

Escalation

Barre's forces responded with severe repression, most notoriously the 1988 campaign against the northern cities of Hargeisa and Burao, which were bombed and largely destroyed, with tens of thousands of Isaaq civilians killed — violence later described by investigators as genocidal. By 1990 the insurgencies controlled most of the countryside, and in December 1990 USC forces entered Mogadishu.

Fall of Barre

Siad Barre fled the capital on 26 January 1991, ending his 21-year rule. The rebellion's victors, however, failed to agree on a successor government: USC factions fought each other, the SNM proclaimed the independence of Somaliland in May 1991, and the country fragmented into clan-based fiefdoms, beginning the decades-long Somali Civil War and a famine that drew international intervention in 1992.
