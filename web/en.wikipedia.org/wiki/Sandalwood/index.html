Sandalwood

Principal speciesSantalum album (Indian sandalwood)
Other speciesSantalum spicatum (Australian sandalwood) and others
ProductsWood, essential oil, incense, carvings
FragranceSoft, warm, woody, milky

Sandalwood is a class of woods from trees in the genus Santalum, most famously Indian sandalwood, Santalum album. The heartwood is yellow, fine-grained and exceptionally fragrant — uniquely, it retains its scent for decades — and both the wood and its distilled oil have been prized for millennia in perfumery, incense, carving and religious ritual.

Uses

Sandalwood oil, rich in the sesquiterpene alcohols alpha- and beta-santalol, is a classic base note and fixative in fine perfumery and soap. Sandalwood paste and incense are integral to Hindu ritual, and the wood is used in Buddhist and Jain practice, in traditional Chinese and Ayurvedic medicine, and for fine carving and furniture in India. In Australia, S. spicatum is harvested from wild and plantation stands, mainly for oil and incense.

Cultivation and conservation

Sandalwood trees are hemiparasitic, drawing nutrients from the roots of host plants, and take 15 years or more to develop fragrant heartwood. Decades of over-harvesting have made Indian sandalwood scarce; S. album is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN, and Indian law long restricted felling and trade. Commercial plantations have been established in northern Australia and elsewhere to supply demand sustainably.

Substitutes

Because of cost and scarcity, perfumers widely use synthetic sandalwood odorants such as polysantol and ebanol, as well as unrelated "sandalwood" woods like West Indian sandalwood (Amyris balsamifera), which differ chemically from true Santalum oil.
