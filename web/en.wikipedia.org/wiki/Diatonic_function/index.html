Diatonic function

Image: Diatonic chords in C major

In music theory, a diatonic function (also called harmonic function) is the role that a chord plays within a key in tonal music. Each of the seven degrees of a diatonic scale can serve as the root of a chord, and each chord is understood to have one of three broad functions: tonic (T), dominant (D), or predominant (PD, also called subdominant function).

Functional categories

The tonic function represents stability and rest, centred on the tonic chord (I in major, i in minor). The dominant function, carried primarily by V and vii°, creates tension that demands resolution to the tonic. Predominant chords — such as ii and IV in major — prepare the dominant, typically moving the harmony from tonic stability toward dominant tension.

Origins

The theory of harmonic function was developed by Hugo Riemann in the late nineteenth century, building on the work of Jean-Philippe Rameau and Moritz Hauptmann. Riemann proposed that all chords in a key relate to one of three functions — Tonika, Dominante, and Subdominante — and introduced function symbols still used in German music theory.
