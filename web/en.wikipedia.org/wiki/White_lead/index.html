White lead

White lead is a basic lead carbonate, 2PbCO3·Pb(OH)2, a complex salt of lead carbonate and lead hydroxide. It was the most important white pigment in Western art and the standard base of house paint for centuries.

Production

White lead was traditionally made by the "Dutch process", in use since antiquity: thin sheets or coils of lead were suspended over pots of vinegar in a closed stack filled with fermenting manure or bark. Over several weeks, the acetic acid vapour, heat and carbon dioxide corroded the lead into a white crust of basic lead carbonate, which was scraped off, washed and ground.

Uses and properties

The pigment has excellent covering power, a warm white tone, and produces durable, weather-resistant paint films that dry quickly — properties that made it indispensable to painters from the ancient world through the 19th century. It was also used in cosmetics, famously by women seeking a pale complexion, and in ceramic glazes and putty.

Toxicity and decline

White lead is highly toxic. Workers who made and ground it suffered lead poisoning, and children were poisoned by chewing leaded paint. Through the 20th century it was progressively restricted and replaced by titanium dioxide and zinc oxide pigments; most countries now ban lead paint in homes, and artists' "flake white" is one of the few remaining uses, itself increasingly substituted.
