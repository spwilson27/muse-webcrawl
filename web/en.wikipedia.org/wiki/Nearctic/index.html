Nearctic

The Nearctic is one of the eight terrestrial biogeographic realms of the Earth. It covers most of North America, including Greenland, central Florida, and the highlands of Mexico, an area of roughly 22.9 million square kilometres. Together with the Palearctic realm of Eurasia, it is sometimes combined into the larger Holarctic realm, reflecting the strong similarities and shared history of the two regions' biotas.

Geography and climate

The Nearctic extends from the Arctic tundra of northern Canada and Alaska south to the subtropical regions of Florida and the Mexican plateau. Its major biomes include tundra, boreal forest (taiga), temperate deciduous and coniferous forests, grasslands, Mediterranean chaparral, and deserts such as the Sonoran and Mojave. North–south mountain ranges, particularly the Rocky Mountains, create corridors and rain shadows that shape species distributions.

Flora and fauna

Characteristic Nearctic animals include bison, pronghorn, moose, caribou, grizzly bears, beavers, and many endemic families such as the pronghorn family (Antilocapridae) and the pocket gophers. The region shares many groups — wolves, bears, deer, and numerous bird families — with the Palearctic, a legacy of the Bering land bridge that repeatedly connected North America and Asia during the Ice Ages, allowing faunal exchange in both directions.

History

North America was joined to Eurasia as part of Laurasia before the opening of the North Atlantic separated the continents. Later, the formation of the Isthmus of Panama about three million years ago connected the Nearctic with the Neotropical realm of South America, triggering the Great American Interchange, in which northern species such as big cats and deer spread south while opossums, armadillos, and other southern forms moved north.
