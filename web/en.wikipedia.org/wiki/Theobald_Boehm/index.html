Theobald Boehm

Born: 11 April 1794, Munich

Died: 25 November 1881, Munich

Occupation: Flutist, composer, instrument maker

Known for: The Boehm system flute

Theobald Boehm (11 April 1794 – 25 November 1881) was a German inventor and musician, best known for developing the modern Western concert flute and its system of fingering, the "Boehm system", which is also applied — through the work of Adolphe Sax and others — to instruments including the clarinet family, oboe and bassoon.

Life and career

Boehm was born in Munich, the son of a goldsmith, and trained in his father's trade while teaching himself the flute. He became flutist in the Bavarian royal orchestra and a virtuoso of international reputation, touring Europe as a soloist. His work as a jeweller and his studies of acoustics, conducted with the physicist Carl von Schafhäutl, gave him the practical and theoretical tools to redesign the flute from first principles.

The Boehm flute

In 1832 Boehm produced a flute with ring keys that freed the placement of tone holes from the limits of the player's fingers. His definitive model of 1847 adopted a cylindrical bore (with a parabolic head joint), large tone holes placed at acoustically correct positions, and a system of padded keys on rod axles operated by a rational fingering scheme. In his treatise Über den Flötenbau (1847) and the later Die Flöte und das Flötenspiel (1871) he set out the acoustic reasoning. Boehm also experimented with flutes made of silver, and the modern silver flute with Boehm fingering became the world standard.

As a composer he wrote concertos, fantasies and studies for his instrument, of which the Grand Polonaise, Op. 16, is still performed.
