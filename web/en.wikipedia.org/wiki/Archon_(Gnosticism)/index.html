Archon (Gnosticism)

In Gnosticism, an archon (Greek: ἄρχων, "ruler") is one of a class of powerful beings who govern the material world and stand between humanity and the transcendent true God. The archons are typically portrayed as the servants, offspring, or emanations of the Demiurge, the ignorant or malevolent creator of the material cosmos, and as jailers who imprison the divine spark within human beings and obstruct the soul's ascent after death.

Origins and role

The Gnostic use of the term draws on the Greek political sense of archon as a magistrate, on Jewish traditions of angels ruling the nations, and on astrological beliefs in planetary powers governing fate (heimarmenē). In many systems there are seven archons, corresponding to the seven planetary spheres, each guarding a heaven through which the soul must pass; in others, notably the Sethian texts of the Nag Hammadi library, their number and names vary widely.

Principal figures

The chief archon is commonly identified with the Demiurge and called Yaldabaoth, Saklas ("fool"), or Samael ("blind god"). In the Apocryphon of John, Yaldabaoth fashions the other archons and, with them, creates Adam's body, scheming to keep the divine power placed within humanity under his control. Other named archons include Athoth, Eloaios, Astaphaios, Yao, Sabaoth, Adonin, and Sabbataios. Sabaoth, having repented, is in some accounts exalted above the seventh heaven and becomes a figure of relative good.

In Ophite and related diagrams described by Origen and Irenaeus, the archons take animal forms — lion, bull, serpent, eagle, bear, dog, and ass — and the ascending soul must address each with passwords and seals, a ritual pattern also found in the so-called Mithras Liturgy and in later Hermetic and magical texts.

Interpretation

Scholars read the archons variously as a mythological expression of political oppression under empire, of the experienced hostility of cosmic fate, and of the Gnostic conviction that the created order and its rulers are alien to the true God. The concept left traces in the New Testament language of "the rulers of this age" and "the god of this world", and in later dualist movements such as Manichaeism.
