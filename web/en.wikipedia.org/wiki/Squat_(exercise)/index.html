Squat (exercise)

The squat is a strength training exercise in which the trainee lowers their hips from a standing position and then stands back up. During the descent, the hip and knee joints flex while the ankle joint dorsiflexes; in the ascent the same joints extend and plantarflex. The squat is considered the most important exercise in strength training because it recruits the largest muscle groups of the lower body and trunk, and it is one of the three lifts in the sport of powerlifting, alongside the deadlift and bench press.

Technique

In the barbell back squat, the bar rests across the upper back (high-bar) or lower on the rear deltoids (low-bar), held in a rack. The lifter steps back, braces the trunk, and descends by flexing at the hips and knees until the tops of the thighs are at least parallel to the floor—competition standards require the hip crease to pass below the knee—then drives upward to a standing position. Key coaching points include keeping the weight over the mid-foot, tracking the knees over the toes, maintaining a neutral spine, and controlling the descent. Common faults include the knees caving inward (valgus collapse) and excessive forward lean.

Variations

  • Front squat — bar held on the front of the shoulders; more upright torso, greater quadriceps emphasis; used in Olympic weightlifting training.
  • Overhead squat — bar held overhead with a wide grip; demanding of mobility and stability.
  • Goblet squat — a dumbbell or kettlebell held at the chest; popular for beginners.
  • Bodyweight (air) squat — unloaded squats used in conditioning and calisthenics.
  • Split squat and Bulgarian split squat — unilateral variants training one leg at a time.
  • Box squat and safety-bar squat — variants altering load and emphasis.

Benefits and safety

Squats build lower-body strength and muscle mass, improve bone density, and transfer to athletic movements such as jumping and sprinting. Properly performed squats are considered safe and beneficial for healthy knees; research indicates deep squats do not inherently damage the knee joint in trained individuals. Risks arise mainly from poor technique, excessive loading, or pre-existing conditions, and supervised progressive training is recommended. The world record for the equipped squat exceeds 500 kg, while raw (unequipped) records are above 400 kg.
