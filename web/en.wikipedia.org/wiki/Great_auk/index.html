Great auk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The great auk (Pinguinus impennis), illustration by John James Audubon.

The great auk (Pinguinus impennis) is a species of flightless alcid that became extinct in the mid-19th century. It was the only modern species in the genus Pinguinus. It was also called the garefowl, or in French, the grand pingouin — the word "penguin" was originally used for the great auk, and was later applied to the superficially similar birds of the Southern Hemisphere.

Description

The great auk stood about 75 to 85 centimetres (30 to 34 in) tall and weighed around 5 kilograms (11 lb). It had black plumage on its back and a white underside, a large, hooked, grooved bill, and small wings used for swimming. It was a powerful swimmer that hunted fish and crustaceans in the cold waters of the North Atlantic.

Distribution and habitat

Great auks bred in dense colonies on rocky, isolated islands with sloping shorelines in the North Atlantic, including Funk Island off Newfoundland, islands in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, and colonies near Iceland, the Faroe Islands, and Britain — notably St Kilda. Outside the breeding season they ranged over the open ocean.

Extinction

Humans hunted the great auk for centuries for its meat, eggs, oil, and feathers. As the species grew rare in the early 19th century, specimens and eggs became prized by collectors, hastening its end. The last two confirmed individuals were killed on Eldey Island, Iceland, in June 1852; the last widely accepted sighting of a live bird was later that year. About 78 skins, along with skeletons and eggs, survive in museum collections. The great auk has become one of the best-known symbols of human-caused extinction.

Cultural references

The bird appears in literature and popular culture, from Charles Kingsley's The Water-Babies to modern conservation writing, and its scientific name survives in that of Antarctic penguins only by transfer of the common name.
