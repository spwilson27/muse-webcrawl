Paraprotein

Also calledMonoclonal protein, M protein, M spike
DefinitionAn abnormal immunoglobulin (or fragment) produced by a single clone of plasma cells or B lymphocytes
Detected bySerum protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, free light chain assay
Associated conditionsMGUS, multiple myeloma, Waldenström macroglobulinaemia, AL amyloidosis

A paraprotein is an abnormal immunoglobulin protein (or protein fragment) found in the blood or urine, produced by a single clonal population of plasma cells or B lymphocytes. It is also called a monoclonal protein, M protein, or M spike. Unlike normal immunoglobulins, which are polyclonal and diverse, a paraprotein consists of identical molecules of one immunoglobulin class and light-chain type (for example IgG kappa or IgA lambda), sometimes only a free light chain (Bence Jones protein).

Serum protein electrophoresis: a sharp monoclonal "M spike" in the gamma region indicates a paraprotein.

Detection

Paraproteins are detected by serum protein electrophoresis, on which they appear as a narrow, dense band or "spike," and characterised by immunofixation electrophoresis, which identifies the heavy- and light-chain class. Serum free light chain assays measure kappa and lambda light chains not bound in intact immunoglobulins and are especially useful when the paraprotein is small or light-chain only. Urine electrophoresis can detect Bence Jones protein (free light chains excreted in urine).

Clinical significance

The presence of a paraprotein indicates a plasma cell dyscrasia, which may be benign or malignant:

  • Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) — a common, usually asymptomatic finding (present in roughly 3% of people over 50), with a small risk (about 1% per year) of progression to malignancy.
  • Multiple myeloma — a malignant proliferation of plasma cells, typically with a larger paraprotein plus end-organ damage (anaemia, bone lesions, renal failure, hypercalcaemia).
  • Waldenström macroglobulinaemia — an IgM paraprotein associated with lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma; high IgM levels can cause hyperviscosity.
  • AL amyloidosis — misfolded light chains deposit as amyloid in organs such as the heart and kidneys, sometimes with only a small paraprotein.

Paraproteins can cause symptoms through hyperviscosity, deposition in tissues, interference with coagulation, or autoimmune activity (for example, cold agglutinins or anti-MAG neuropathy).
