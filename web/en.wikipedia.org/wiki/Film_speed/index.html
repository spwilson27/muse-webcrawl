Film speed

Film speed is the measure of a photographic film's sensitivity to light, determined by sensitometry and measured on various numerical scales, the most recent being the ISO system (ISO 5800:1987). A closely related ISO system is used to describe the relationship between exposure and output image lightness in digital cameras.

The ISO scale

The ISO scale combines the older ASA (American) and DIN (German) scales: the arithmetic ASA number and the logarithmic DIN degree are combined as, for example, ISO 400/27°. Higher numbers indicate more sensitive ("faster") film, requiring less light for a correct exposure. Film speeds commonly range from ISO 25 to ISO 3200 or higher. Each doubling of the speed corresponds to one stop of exposure.

Grain and quality

Faster films use larger silver halide grains, which produce more visible grain and lower resolution than slow films. Consequently, slow, fine-grained films are preferred when light is plentiful and maximum detail is wanted, while fast films are chosen for low-light photography, sports, and photojournalism.

History

Many competing systems preceded ISO, including the Hurter and Driffield numbers, Weston ratings, General Electric values, the ASA scale (from 1943), and the DIN scale (from 1934). The ISO standard unified ASA and DIN in 1974 for colour film and 1987 in its current form.
