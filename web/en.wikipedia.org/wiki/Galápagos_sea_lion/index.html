Galápagos sea lion

The Galápagos sea lion (Zalophus wollebaeki) is a species of sea lion that lives and breeds on the Galápagos Islands and, in smaller numbers, on Isla de la Plata in Ecuador. It is the largest land animal in the archipelago and one of its most conspicuous, famous for its playful behaviour and lack of fear of humans. It is closely related to the California sea lion and was formerly treated as a subspecies of it.

Description

The species is strongly sexually dimorphic. Adult males reach about 2.5–2.7 m in length and 250–400 kg, developing a prominent sagittal crest and a lighter-coloured mane; females are smaller and more slender, around 1.8–2.4 m and 60–110 kg. Pups are born dark brown and lighten with age. Like other eared seals, Galápagos sea lions have external ear flaps and can rotate their hind flippers forward to move on land.

Behaviour and breeding

Colonies haul out on sandy beaches and rocky shores throughout the islands. During the breeding season (roughly May to the following January), dominant bulls defend aquatic and beach territories, while females give birth to a single pup and alternate nursing ashore with foraging trips at sea. The species feeds mainly on sardines, myctophids (lanternfish), squid and octopus, diving typically to depths of 100–300 m. Males that fail to hold territories form bachelor groups.

Conservation

The population, estimated at roughly 20,000–40,000 individuals, fluctuates sharply with El Niño events, when warm, nutrient-poor water reduces prey and causes mass pup mortality. The species is classified as Endangered by the IUCN; threats include disease, introduced predators, fisheries interaction and climate-driven oceanographic change.
