Problem solving

Problem solving is the process of achieving a goal by overcoming obstacles, a frequent part of most activities. Problems in need of solutions range from simple personal tasks (e.g. how to turn on an appliance) to complex issues in business and technical fields. The former is an example of simple problem solving (SPS) addressing one issue, whereas the latter is complex problem solving (CPS) with multiple interrelated obstacles. Another classification of problem-solving tasks is into well-defined problems with specific obstacles and goals, and ill-defined problems in which the current situation is troublesome but it is not clear what kind of resolution to aim for.

Problem solving was studied by Gestalt psychologists in Germany, such as Karl Duncker and later by researchers involved in the cognitive psychology and computer simulation movements, notably Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon. In the 1960s the problem-solving approach became central to artificial intelligence, where systems such as the General Problem Solver attempted to model human strategies.

Definition

There are two different types of problems: ill-defined and well-defined; different approaches are used for each. Well-defined problems have specific goals, clearly defined solutions, and clear expected solutions. Ill-defined problems have no clear goals, solutions, or expected solutions. A problem itself may be ill-defined when the problem solver has no means to assess the problem other than trial and error.

Strategies

Problem-solving strategies are the steps taken to find solutions to problems. Commonly used strategies include:

  • Algorithms: step-by-step procedures that will always produce a correct solution when followed properly.
  • Heuristics: general strategies or "rules of thumb" that speed up the process of finding a satisfactory solution, though they do not guarantee correctness.
  • Trial-and-error: trying possible solutions and discarding those that fail.
  • Insight: the sudden realisation of a problem's solution, often after a period of incubation.
  • Means–ends analysis: repeatedly reducing the difference between the current state and the goal state.
  • Working backwards: beginning from the goal state and working towards the starting conditions.

Barriers

Common barriers to successful problem solving include functional fixedness (using an object only in its conventional way), the Einstellung or mental set effect (applying previously successful strategies to new problems), confirmation bias, and unnecessary constraints imposed by the solver. George Pólya's 1945 book How to Solve It popularised a four-step method: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, and look back.

Applications

Problem solving is studied and applied in education, engineering design, medicine (clinical reasoning), management, and computer programming. In mathematics education, problem solving is treated both as a skill to be learned and as a vehicle for learning other content. See also Heuristic, Cognition, and Decision-making.
