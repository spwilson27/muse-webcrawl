Radioactive decay

Radioactive decay (also known as nuclear decay, radioactivity, radioactive disintegration, or nuclear disintegration) is the process by which an unstable atomic nucleus loses energy by radiation.

TypesAlpha, beta, gamma, neutron emission, fission
DiscoveredHenri Becquerel, 1896
Key conceptHalf-life

Types

Common modes include alpha decay (emission of a helium nucleus), beta decay (electron or positron emission, changing a neutron to a proton or vice versa), gamma emission, electron capture, and spontaneous fission in heavy nuclei.

Decay law and half-life

Decay is random for a single atom but exponential for a large population: N(t)=N₀e-λt. The half-life is the time for half the nuclei to decay, ranging from fractions of a second to billions of years, e.g. uranium-238 at 4.5 billion years, used in radiometric dating.

History

Radioactivity was discovered by Henri Becquerel in 1896 and investigated by Marie and Pierre Curie and Ernest Rutherford, work central to nuclear physics. See Glenn Seaborg.

Applications and hazards

Uses include medicine, power generation, smoke detectors and dating. Ionising radiation can damage living tissue, requiring shielding and regulation.
