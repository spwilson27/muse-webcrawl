Argentine literature is the body of literary work produced in Argentina, written chiefly in Spanish. It is one of the most prominent national literatures of Latin America, home to figures of world stature such as Jorge Luis Borges and Julio Cortázar.

Origins and the 19th century

Colonial writing was sparse; the literature of the young republic was dominated by political conflict between unitarians and federalists. Domingo Faustino Sarmiento's Facundo (1845) set civilization against "barbarism" in a foundational national essay. The gauchesque tradition culminated in José Hernández's epic poem Martín Fierro (1872/1879), Argentina's national poem. Esteban Echeverría's short story "El matadero" and José Mármol's novel Amalia protested the Rosas dictatorship.

Modernism and the avant-garde

Leopoldo Lugones led the modernista movement, while the 1920s saw the playful rivalry of the Florida and Boedo literary groups. Borges emerged as the central figure of Argentine letters, transforming the short story with collections such as Ficciones (1944) and The Aleph (1949).

Later 20th century and beyond

Cortázar's experimental novel Hopscotch (Rayuela, 1963) became a landmark of the Latin American Boom. Other major writers include Ernesto Sabato, Adolfo Bioy Casares, Manuel Puig, Juan José Saer, Ricardo Piglia, César Aira, and Mariana Enríquez.
