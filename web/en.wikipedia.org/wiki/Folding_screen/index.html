Folding screen

A folding screen is a piece of furniture consisting of several frames or panels connected by hinges, used to divide a room, screen a bed or doorway, or decorate an interior. Folding screens originated in China, where the earliest known examples date to the Han dynasty (206 BC – AD 220). From China they spread to Japan, where they became a celebrated art form known as byōbu, and later reached Europe, where they were objects of great luxury in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

History

Screens appear in Chinese literature and tomb paintings from the Han dynasty. The Japanese byōbu, often painted with landscapes, seasons, or scenes from literature on paper or silk backed with gold leaf, flourished from the Muromachi period onward. After the Portuguese and Dutch opened trade routes in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, Chinese and Japanese export screens (sometimes called Coromandel screens, carved and lacquered) became fashionable in European palaces and were produced in large numbers for export.

Construction

Screens typically consist of two to twelve panels. Chinese screens were often made of lacquered wood panels, sometimes inlaid with mother-of-pearl or painted; Japanese byōbu use a wooden lattice frame covered with paper or silk, joined by paper hinges so the panels can fold flat.

Uses

Beyond their practical role as room dividers and privacy screens, folding screens serve as movable decoration, draft shields, and backdrops in photography and theatre. See also Byōbu and Coromandel screen.
