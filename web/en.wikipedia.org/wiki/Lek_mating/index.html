Lek mating

A lek is an aggregation of male animals gathered to engage in competitive displays and ritualized fighting to entice visiting females which are surveying prospective partners to mate with. The term derives from the Swedish word lek, meaning play, and was first used for the black grouse and capercaillie of Scandinavia. A lekking species is characterised by strong sexual dimorphism, male aggregation, no male parental care, and the fact that females gain nothing from males other than genes.

In a classic lek, males gather on a traditional display ground with no resources of value to females. Each male defends a tiny territory — sometimes only a few centimetres across — and displays with postures, calls, and plumage ornaments. Females visit, watch, and freely choose a mate. Typically a small number of males, often the most dominant and centrally positioned, obtain the great majority of copulations, producing exceptionally strong sexual selection.

Criteria

Classical lekking is defined by four characteristics: (1) males provide no parental care; (2) males aggregate at specific sites; (3) the display sites contain no resources females need, apart from the males themselves; and (4) females can exercise mate choice. Species meeting all four criteria include many grouse, the ruff, the great snipe, manakins, birds-of-paradise, the kakapo, and several antelopes such as the Uganda kob and topi. Lek-like systems also occur in insects, including some moths, wasps, and flies, and in fish.

Costs and benefits

Lekking is an evolutionary puzzle because intense female choice should quickly exhaust genetic variation in male display traits (the so-called "lek paradox"). Proposed resolutions include indirect benefits through "good genes", mutation maintaining variation, and females gaining fitter or more attractive sons. For males, the cost of constant display is high, but the reproductive payoff for the few winners is enormous; most males at a lek never mate.

Hotshot and hotspot models

Two classic explanations for why leks form are the hotspot model, in which males cluster where female travel routes converge, and the hotshot model, in which subordinate males cluster around a highly attractive male to intercept females drawn to him. Kin selection may also play a role, since males at leks are often relatives.
