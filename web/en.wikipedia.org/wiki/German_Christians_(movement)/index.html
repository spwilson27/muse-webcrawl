German Christians (movement)

The German Christians (German: Deutsche Christen) was a movement within the German Protestant church during the Nazi era that sought to align Protestantism with National Socialist ideology, including its racial antisemitism. Founded in 1932 and strongly supported by the Nazi regime after 1933, the movement attempted to "de-Judaize" Christianity and create a unified Reich Church under Nazi control. Its excesses provoked the formation of the oppositional Confessing Church.

Origins and rise

The movement grew out of nationalist and völkisch circles in Thuringia in the late 1920s and was formally organized in 1932 under pastor Joachim Hossenfelder. Its program combined loyalty to Adolf Hitler, German nationalism, and the demand to apply the Nazi "Aryan paragraph" within the church, excluding clergy of Jewish descent. In the church elections of July 1933, held under heavy regime pressure and propaganda, the German Christians won majorities in most regional churches. Their candidate Ludwig Müller was installed as Reich Bishop of the new German Evangelical Church.

Program and controversy

German Christian theologians promoted a "heroic" Jesus portrayed as an Aryan opponent of Judaism, demanded removal of the Old Testament from the canon, and sought to purge Jewish elements from liturgy and hymnody. In November 1933 a mass rally at the Berlin Sportpalast, addressed by Reinhold Krause, openly called for this program, scandalizing many church members and triggering a backlash. Pastors led by Martin Niemöller formed the Pastors' Emergency League, which developed into the Confessing Church; its Barmen Declaration of 1934, chiefly drafted by Karl Barth, rejected the subordination of the church to Nazi ideology.

Decline

Hitler, frustrated by church infighting and Müller's incompetence, progressively distanced the regime from the movement; from 1935 church affairs were handled by Hanns Kerrl's Ministry for Church Affairs, and the regime's long-term aim increasingly appeared to be the marginalization of Christianity itself. The German Christians fragmented into rival factions and lost influence, though a radical wing persisted — including the Institute for the Study and Elimination of Jewish Influence on German Church Life, founded at Eisenach in 1939, which produced a dejudaized New Testament. After 1945 the movement dissolved; the Protestant churches' Stuttgart Declaration of Guilt (1945) acknowledged complicity and failure during the Nazi period.
