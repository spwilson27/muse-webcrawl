Religious pluralism

Religious pluralism is an attitude or policy regarding the diversity of religious belief systems co-existing in society. It can indicate one or more of the following:

  • The acceptance that different religions can co-exist peacefully in the same society;
  • The theological position that one's own religion is not the sole and exclusive source of truth, and that some level of truth and value exists in other religions;
  • The promotion of understanding between different religions for the benefit of social harmony.

Definitions and usage

Religious pluralism is distinct from religious toleration, which may simply endure difference, and from religious diversity, which is the demographic fact of plurality. As a theological stance, pluralism is associated especially with the philosopher of religion John Hick, who proposed a "Copernican revolution" in theology: rather than treating one tradition as the centre, all major traditions can be seen as responses to the same ultimate Reality ("the Real"). Critics, including many exclusivists and inclusivists, argue that pluralism flattens genuine doctrinal differences or undermines truth claims.

Pluralism in the world's religions

Ideas resembling pluralism appear in many traditions: the Hindu concept that truth is one but the wise call it by many names (Rig Veda 1.164.46), the Bahá'í teaching of progressive revelation, and modern interfaith movements that emerged after the 1893 Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago. In the political sphere, pluralism underlies constitutional arrangements protecting freedom of religion, such as the First Amendment to the United States Constitution.

Interfaith dialogue

Religious pluralism is promoted through interfaith dialogue, shared civic projects between congregations, and academic study of comparative religion. Organisations such as the Elijah Interfaith Institute and local interfaith councils work to reduce conflict and build cooperation between faith communities.
