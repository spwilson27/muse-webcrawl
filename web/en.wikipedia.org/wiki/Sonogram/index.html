Sonogram

A sonogram is an image produced by ultrasonography (medical ultrasound). Ultrasonography uses high-frequency sound waves, typically between 2 and 18 megahertz, generated by a transducer placed on the skin. Pulses of sound travel into the body and echoes returning from tissue boundaries are recorded and converted into a visual display of internal structures.

How it works

The strength and timing of each returning echo determine the brightness and depth of each point in the image (B-mode imaging). Because the sound pulses are generated and received many times per second, sonograms can show moving structures in real time, including the beating heart and blood flow (with Doppler techniques). Unlike X-ray imaging, ultrasonography uses no ionising radiation.

Uses

Sonograms are widely used in obstetrics to monitor fetal development, estimate gestational age and detect congenital abnormalities. They are also used to image the abdomen, pelvis, heart (echocardiography), blood vessels, thyroid, breast, muscles and tendons, and to guide needle biopsies and other procedures.

History

Medical ultrasound developed from wartime sonar research. In the 1950s the Scottish physician Ian Donald and engineer Tom Brown pioneered obstetric ultrasound in Glasgow, demonstrating that echoes could distinguish normal tissue from cysts and tumours. Grey-scale imaging, real-time scanners and Doppler ultrasound followed, making the sonogram a routine part of medical practice worldwide.
