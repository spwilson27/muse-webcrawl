Phreatomagmatic eruption

Phreatomagmatic eruptions are volcanic eruptions driven by the interaction of magma with water—groundwater, surface water, seawater, or water in a crater lake. The water flashes to steam, expanding enormously in volume, and the resulting explosion fragments the magma into fine ash far more efficiently than purely magmatic degassing. The term combines "phreatic" (relating to groundwater) with "magmatic," since fresh juvenile magma is erupted, unlike purely phreatic eruptions, which eject only old rock.

Mechanism

When hot magma (around 1,100–1,200 °C) contacts water at an optimal ratio, heat transfer occurs almost explosively—a fuel–coolant interaction. Steam expansion shatters the melt into very fine particles, increasing surface area and sustaining the explosion. Deposits are characteristically fine-grained, well-bedded, and rich in accretionary lapilli, and the eruptions generate fast-moving, ground-hugging base surges.

Landforms

Phreatomagmatic eruptions excavate broad, low craters. Where explosions occur below the surface in sediment or rock, they form maars—wide craters cut into the ground, often filled by lakes, ringed by low tuff rims. Repeated explosions at a fixed vent build tuff rings and tuff cones. The 1963 eruption of Surtsey, Iceland, began as an explosive phreatomagmatic phase in shallow seawater and turned effusive once the growing island isolated the vent from the sea.

Examples

Famous examples include the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa (in part), the 1965 and 2020 eruptions of Taal Volcano in the Philippines, whose crater-lake setting produced lethal base surges; and the 15 January 2022 eruption of Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai, in which seawater interacted with magma to produce one of the most powerful explosions of the modern era, generating a volcanic tsunami and a plume reaching the mesosphere.

Hazards

Base surges—turbulent, dilute pyroclastic density currents—are the chief hazard, along with fine ash fall, ballistics, and, in coastal or submarine settings, tsunamis. Because water is commonly present at volcanoes, many otherwise modest eruptions (e.g., at Eyjafjallajökull in 2010, where meltwater flooded the vent) are amplified by phreatomagmatic explosivity.
