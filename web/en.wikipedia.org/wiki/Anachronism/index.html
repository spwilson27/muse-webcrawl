Anachronism

Anachronism
[Image: A painting of a medieval knight checking a wristwatch — a deliberate visual anachronism]
EtymologyGreek ana- ("against/again") + chronos ("time")
TypesParachronism, prochronism
Found inHistorical error, art, literature, film

An anachronism is a chronological inconsistency in some arrangement, especially a juxtaposition of people, events, objects, language terms or customs from different time periods. The most common type is an object misplaced in time, but it may also be a verbal expression, a technology, a philosophical idea, a musical style, a material, or any other thing associated with a particular period that is placed outside its proper temporal domain.

Types

A parachronism is something that appears in a time period in which it has already passed out of general use or been superseded — for example, a character in a modern novel lighting a room with an oil lamp as a matter of routine. A prochronism is the opposite error: something appearing earlier than its actual invention or occurrence, such as a Roman soldier wearing a wristwatch. Anachronisms may be unintentional errors, or deliberate artistic devices used for satire, comedy or effect, as in Shakespeare's references to clocks in ancient Rome in Julius Caesar.

In scholarship and art

Detecting anachronism is a basic tool of historical and textual criticism: Lorenzo Valla's exposure of the Donation of Constantine as a forgery rested partly on its anachronistic Latin. In the visual arts, medieval and Renaissance paintings routinely dressed biblical figures in contemporary clothing, which contemporaries did not perceive as an error. Modern historical fiction and film are routinely scrutinised for anachronisms, while works such as steampunk embrace them deliberately.
