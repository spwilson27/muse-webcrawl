Bank engine

RoleAssisting locomotive on steep gradients
Also known asBanking locomotive, pusher engine, helper
PlacementUsually at the rear of the train ("banking")
EraCommon in steam age; still used on some heavy-haul lines

A bank engine (also banker or banking locomotive) is a railway locomotive that temporarily assists a train up a steep gradient ("bank"), typically by pushing from the rear. Once the summit is reached, the banker detaches—sometimes on the move—and returns down the incline to await the next train.

Operation

Banking is needed where a gradient is too steep for the train's own locomotive to haul the load unaided, but the rest of the route is easier, making a second engine for the whole journey uneconomic. The banker may be attached at the rear, or in some practices run "buffering up" without coupling, dropping away as the train crests the grade. Historically, famous British banks included the Lickey Incline near Birmingham—served by the unique 0-10-0 locomotive "Big Bertha"—and the climb to Shap summit on the West Coast Main Line.

Modern practice

Diesel and electric multiple-unit operation and distributed power have replaced traditional banking on many routes, but pushers remain in use on North American mountain grades—where they are called helpers or pushers—and on heavy mineral lines worldwide. In North America the assisting locomotives may be attached mid-train or at the rear and controlled remotely by the lead engine's driver.

Other applications

The term was also used for locomotives piloting trains through long tunnels or over sections with special restrictions. On rack railways and cable-hauled inclines, stationary engines sometimes performed the equivalent function.
