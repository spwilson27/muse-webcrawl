Durable good

Durable goods
Also called: hard goods
Opposite: nondurable goods / consumables

In economics, a durable good (or hard good) is a good that does not quickly wear out, or more specifically one that yields utility over time rather than being completely consumed in one use. Items like cars, furniture, household appliances, sports equipment, and toys are durable goods, whereas food, fuel, and toiletries are nondurable goods.

Characteristics

Statistical agencies commonly define durability by an expected service life of more than three years. Because purchases of durables can be postponed, spending on them is highly sensitive to economic conditions, and orders for durable goods are closely watched as a leading economic indicator — for example in the United States Census Bureau's monthly durable goods report.

Durability and policy

Durability raises questions of planned obsolescence, repairability, and sustainability; a durable good that cannot be repaired may still become waste quickly. Economists note that services flowing from a durable good continue over its lifetime, distinguishing the purchase of the stock from the consumption of its service flow. See good (economics).
