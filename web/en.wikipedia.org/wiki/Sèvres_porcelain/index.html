Sèvres porcelain is porcelain produced by the Manufacture nationale de Sèvres, one of the principal European porcelain factories. Founded at Vincennes in 1740 and moved to Sèvres, outside Paris, in 1756, the factory became the foremost producer of luxury porcelain in 18th-century Europe, closely associated with the French court, King Louis XV and his mistress Madame de Pompadour.

History

The manufactory began as a private enterprise at the Château de Vincennes, experimenting with soft-paste porcelain to rival the hard-paste wares of Meissen in Saxony and imports from China. Royal patronage grew; in 1753 the king took a controlling interest, and the factory received the privilege of using the royal mark of interlaced L's. In 1756 it moved to purpose-built premises at Sèvres, on the road between Paris and Versailles and near Madame de Pompadour's Château de Bellevue. In 1759 Louis XV bought the factory outright, making it a royal manufacture.

After the French Revolution the factory passed to the state. It developed true hard-paste porcelain from the late 1760s, using kaolin discovered near Limoges. Through the 19th and 20th centuries Sèvres continued to produce diplomatic gifts, state table services and works designed by leading artists, while also supplying the official services of the French Republic. The manufacture still operates today under the French Ministry of Culture, alongside its national museum of ceramics.

Characteristics

Sèvres soft-paste porcelain was famed for its rich ground colours, developed by the factory's chemists, including bleu de roi (royal blue), turquoise (bleu céleste), apple green, and the celebrated pink known as rose Pompadour. Coloured grounds were combined with reserves painted with flowers, landscapes, birds or figures, framed by elaborate gilding. Famous products include dinner services for royalty and diplomats, pot-pourri vases in the shape of ships (vaisseau à mât), and unglazed biscuit-porcelain figures modelled after François Boucher and others.

Marks

Pieces were typically marked with the interlaced L's of the royal cipher in underglaze blue, enclosing a date letter; painters' and gilders' marks were added in enamel or gold.
