Bookbinding

Bookbinding is the process of physically assembling a book of codex format from an ordered stack of paper sheets that are folded together into sections or sometimes left as a stack of individual sheets. The stack is then bound by stitching together the folds of the sections with thread, by adhesive under a layer of hide or other material, or by a variety of other simple devices.

Historically, bookbinding combines aspects of craft and industrial manufacture. Traditionally, binding and publication took place in separate workshops, but by the nineteenth century the process was largely mechanised in printing houses, with machines for folding, gathering, sewing and casing-in.

History

The codex form, in which folded sheets are sewn together, arose in late antiquity and gradually replaced the scroll. Early surviving examples include Coptic bindings from Egypt. Medieval monastic binders produced elaborate treasure bindings decorated with metalwork and jewels. With the invention of printing in the fifteenth century, binding became a distinct trade in Europe, producing hand-sewn bindings in leather over wooden boards. The nineteenth century saw the introduction of cloth cases and publisher's bindings, separating the decorative case from the text block for the first time.

Structure

A typical sewn book consists of signatures (gatherings of folded sheets) sewn through the fold onto cords or tapes; the sewn text block is then glued, trimmed, and attached to a cover. Other structures include Smyth sewing, perfect binding (adhesive), saddle stitching for pamphlets, and spiral or ring binding.

Styles and craft

Conservation binding, fine binding, and artist's books preserve hand techniques such as forwarding (preparing the text block), paring leather, gold tooling, and edge gilding. Institutions such as the American Institute for Conservation recognise bookbinding as a specialist craft discipline.
