Sack of Rome (390 BC)

The sack of Rome in 390 BC (by some chronologies 387 BC) was a catastrophic defeat and occupation of the city of Rome by the Senones, a Gallic tribe led by Brennus. It followed the Battle of the Allia, fought on 18 July 390 BC at the confluence of the Allia and Tiber rivers, about 16 kilometres (10 miles) north of Rome. The disaster left Rome defenceless and led to the only sack of the city for some 800 years; the day of the battle, the dies Alliensis, was thereafter regarded as unlucky in the Roman calendar.

Background

Gallic peoples had migrated into northern Italy and came into conflict with the Etruscan city of Clusium. According to Roman tradition, Roman envoys sent to negotiate took up arms against the Gauls, violating the law of nations. The Gauls demanded the envoys be surrendered for punishment; when Rome refused and even honoured them with office, Brennus turned his army toward Rome itself.

Battle of the Allia and the sack

The Roman army met the Gauls at the Allia and was routed; the survivors fled to Veii or into Rome. With the gates left open and the city largely abandoned, the Gauls entered Rome a few days later and plundered and burned much of it. The last defenders held out on the Capitoline Hill, which tradition says was saved from a night attack when the sacred geese of Juno raised the alarm — an episode associated with Marcus Manlius Capitolinus.

Ransom and withdrawal

Starving, the Romans agreed to ransom the city for 1,000 pounds of gold. Livy records that when the Romans complained about the weights used to measure the gold, Brennus threw his sword onto the scales and uttered the famous phrase Vae victis — "Woe to the vanquished." Roman tradition further held that the exiled commander Marcus Furius Camillus arrived with an army, annulled the ransom and defeated the Gauls, though modern historians doubt elements of the account.

Aftermath

Rome was rebuilt in haste and without a clear plan, which ancient authors blamed for the city's irregular street layout. The trauma of the sack spurred military and political reforms, and Rome rapidly reasserted itself in Latium and central Italy in the following decades.
