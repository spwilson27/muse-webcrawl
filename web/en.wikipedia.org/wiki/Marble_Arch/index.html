Marble Arch is a 19th-century white Carrara marble triumphal arch in London, England. Designed by John Nash in 1827 and completed in 1833, it was originally erected as the monumental state entrance to the courtyard of Buckingham Palace. Modelled on the Arch of Constantine in Rome, its facades carry sculptural reliefs (by E. H. Baily and Sir Richard Westmacott) celebrating Britain's victories in the Napoleonic Wars.

Relocation

When Buckingham Palace was enlarged in the 1840s–50s, the arch was dismantled and in 1851 rebuilt at its present site at the north-east corner of Hyde Park, near Speakers' Corner, at the junction of Oxford Street, Park Lane, and Edgware Road. Tradition holds that only members of the royal family and the King's Troop, Royal Horse Artillery are permitted to pass through its central archway. The arch contains a tiny three-room interior once used as a police station.

The surrounding area

The name "Marble Arch" also denotes the surrounding district and a London Underground station on the Central line (opened 1900). The site is close to the former location of the Tyburn gallows, London's principal place of public execution until 1783. The nearby street plan and the Edgware Road's Arab and Lebanese restaurants make the area a noted cultural quarter.
