Salt pan (geology)

A salt pan is a flat expanse of ground covered with a crust of salt and other minerals, formed where a shallow body of water evaporates and leaves its dissolved minerals behind. Salt pans are characteristic of arid basins and desert playas, where evaporation exceeds precipitation, and they include some of the flattest natural surfaces on Earth.

Formation

Salt pans develop in closed (endorheic) drainage basins, where water flows in but has no outlet to the sea. After rain or flooding, a shallow temporary lake — a playa lake — collects dissolved salts leached from surrounding rocks. As the water evaporates under desert sun and wind, the minerals precipitate in sequence of solubility: carbonates first, then gypsum, then halite (common salt), and finally — in the most concentrated brines — potassium and magnesium salts. Repeated cycles of flooding and evaporation build up a hard, flat crust, which cracks into polygonal plates as it dries and buckles. Beneath the crust there is often deep soft mud, making pans hazardous to cross in vehicles after rain.

Notable salt pans

  • Salar de Uyuni, Bolivia — the world's largest salt flat, about 10,582 km², sitting atop vast lithium-rich brines
  • Bonneville Salt Flats, Utah, United States — famous for land speed records
  • Etosha Pan, Namibia — a huge mineral pan that floods seasonally and attracts flamingos
  • Makgadikgadi Pan, Botswana — among the largest salt pan complexes in the world
  • Badwater Basin, Death Valley, California — the lowest point in North America
  • Rann of Kutch, India/Pakistan — a vast seasonal salt marsh
  • Lake Eyre (Kati Thanda), South Australia — normally a salt pan, occasionally filling to become Australia's largest lake

Uses and ecology

Salt pans have long been exploited for salt production by solar evaporation, and several — Uyuni, Atacama, and the "lithium triangle" flats of South America — overlie brines rich in lithium, boron, and potash of major economic importance. Their extreme flatness makes them suited to land-speed trials and to the calibration of satellite altimeters. Although seemingly barren, flooded pans support specialised life, including brine shrimp, algae, and the flamingos that feed on them, while halophilic microorganisms thrive in the brines themselves.
