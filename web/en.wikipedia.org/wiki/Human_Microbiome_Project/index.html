Human Microbiome Project

The Human Microbiome Project (HMP) was a United States National Institutes of Health research initiative launched in 2007 to improve understanding of the microbial flora involved in human health and disease. It aimed to characterise the human microbiome and analyse its role in health and disease.

Phase one

The first phase (2007–2012) sampled 300 healthy volunteers at multiple body sites — gut, mouth, skin, nose, and vagina — using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and whole-genome shotgun metagenomics. Landmark papers in 2012 established a reference catalogue of the healthy human microbiome and showed that microbial composition varies strongly by body site and by individual.

Phase two

The second phase, the Integrative Human Microbiome Project (iHMP, 2014–2019), followed cohorts in three conditions — pregnancy and preterm birth, inflammatory bowel disease, and type 2 diabetes — collecting longitudinal multi-omics data on both host and microbes.

Significance

The HMP created reference datasets, standardised methods, and bioinformatics tools that underpin modern microbiome research, and it shifted medicine toward viewing humans as ecosystems of human and microbial cells.
