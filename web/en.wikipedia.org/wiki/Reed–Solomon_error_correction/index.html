Reed–Solomon codes treat a block of data as coefficients of a polynomial evaluated at many points.

Reed–Solomon error correction is a family of error-correcting codes invented in 1960 by Irving S. Reed and Gustave Solomon of MIT's Lincoln Laboratory. Reed–Solomon (RS) codes are non-binary cyclic codes that operate on blocks of symbols rather than individual bits, making them especially effective against burst errors. They are among the most widely deployed error-correction schemes in consumer and industrial technology.

Principle

An RS encoder takes a block of k data symbols and interprets them as the coefficients of a polynomial over a finite (Galois) field, typically GF(28), whose elements are bytes. The polynomial is evaluated at n distinct points, producing an n-symbol codeword. Because a polynomial of degree less than k is uniquely determined by any k of its values, the receiver can reconstruct the data even if some symbols are corrupted or lost: an RS(n, k) code can correct up to (n − k)/2 symbol errors at unknown positions, or up to n − k erasures whose positions are known. RS codes achieve the maximum possible minimum distance for their length and dimension, and are therefore maximum-distance-separable (MDS) codes.

Practical decoders — beginning with the Berlekamp–Massey algorithm and related methods of the late 1960s — made decoding fast enough for real-time hardware, opening the way to mass application.

Applications

  • Optical discs — cross-interleaved Reed–Solomon coding (CIRC) protects audio CDs; DVDs and Blu-ray discs use RS product codes, allowing scratched discs to play perfectly.
  • Barcodes — QR codes use RS correction to remain readable when partly damaged or obscured.
  • Communications — deep-space missions (including Voyager), satellite links, DSL and cable modems, and digital television broadcasting.
  • Storage — RAID 6 arrays and distributed storage systems use RS-style erasure coding to survive disk failures.
