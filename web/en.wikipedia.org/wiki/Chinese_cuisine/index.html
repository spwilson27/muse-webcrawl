Chinese cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Chinese banquet table with shared dishes and a wok dish being served]

Chinese cuisine comprises the culinary traditions originating from China, one of the world's oldest and most varied food cultures. It is organised into regional schools — traditionally the "Eight Cuisines" of Shandong, Sichuan, Cantonese (Guangdong), Fujian, Jiangsu, Hunan, Anhui and Zhejiang — shaped by geography, climate and history.

Core elements include rice in the south and wheat noodles and buns in the north, stir-frying in the wok, steaming in bamboo baskets, soy sauce and fermented pastes, and the balance of flavour, aroma, colour and texture (se xiang wei). Chinese food travelled with the diaspora to create Chinatowns and adapted cuisines worldwide.

History

Chinese cooking is documented from the Zhou dynasty; Confucius is credited with culinary maxims, and chopsticks spread as fuel-saving cooking favoured small pieces quickly cooked. The Silk Road brought spices and techniques; the Columbian exchange added chillies, which transformed Sichuan and Hunan cooking in the Ming and Qing dynasties. Tea culture originated in China and spread across Asia.

Regional cuisines

Cantonese cuisine prizes freshness and delicate seasoning, dim sum and roast meats; Sichuan cuisine is famous for ma la — the numbing heat of Sichuan pepper and chilli; Shandong is the oldest documented school, strong in seafood and soups; Hunan's food is hot and sour; Jiangsu cooking is refined and banquet-oriented.

Dishes

Famous dishes include Peking duck, kung pao chicken, mapo Tofu, hot pot, Dumplings (jiaozi), spring rolls, fried rice and countless Noodle dishes from hand-pulled lamian to dan dan mian.
