Victor Vasarely

Victor Vasarely (born Győző Vásárhelyi; 9 April 1906 – 15 March 1997) was a Hungarian-French artist widely regarded as the father of Op art. His work, based on systematic arrangements of geometric forms and colors, produced striking optical illusions of movement, depth, and vibration on flat surfaces.

Early life and training

Vasarely was born in Pécs, Hungary, and briefly studied medicine in Budapest before turning to art. At the Műhely school, sometimes called the "Budapest Bauhaus," he was introduced to constructivist ideas by Sándor Bortnyik. In 1930 he moved to Paris, where he worked successfully as a graphic designer while developing his abstract painting.

Op art

From the 1940s onward Vasarely refined a method he called the "plastic alphabet": a modular system of forms and colors that could be combined mathematically to generate compositions. Works such as Zebra and his later Vega series, in which warped grids of colored squares seem to bulge and swell, made him internationally famous and defined the Op art movement of the 1960s. He believed art should be reproducible and accessible to all, and he applied his designs to architecture and urban decoration.

Legacy

The Vasarely Museum in Aix-en-Provence, France, housed in a building designed to his specifications, opened in 1976. Another museum in Budapest preserves a major collection of his work. Vasarely died in Paris in 1997.
