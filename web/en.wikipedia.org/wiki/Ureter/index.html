Ureter

The ureters are paired muscular tubes that propel urine from the kidneys to the urinary bladder by peristalsis. In adults each ureter is about 25–30 cm (10–12 in) long and 3–4 mm in diameter. They arise from the renal pelvis — downstream of the renal calycies — descend retroperitoneally, and enter the bladder obliquely at the ureterovesical junction.

Structure

The ureter has three layers: an inner mucosa lined with transitional epithelium (urothelium), a muscularis of smooth muscle that generates peristaltic waves roughly every few seconds, and an outer adventitia. Three physiological narrowings — at the ureteropelvic junction, where it crosses the pelvic brim and iliac vessels, and at the bladder entry — are the most common sites where kidney stones lodge.

Function

Urine drains from the renal pelvis into the ureter and is moved to the bladder independently of gravity. The oblique entry of the ureter through the bladder wall functions as a flap valve, preventing reflux of urine toward the kidney during bladder contraction.

Clinical significance

Ureteric obstruction from stones (ureterolithiasis) causes renal colic. Other conditions include vesicoureteral reflux, congenital duplication of the ureter, strictures, and transitional cell carcinoma. Imaging includes ultrasound, CT urography, and retrograde pyelography; treatments range from stenting and ureteroscopy to surgical repair.
