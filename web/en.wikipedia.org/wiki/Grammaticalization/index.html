Grammaticalization

Grammaticalization is the process of language change by which words representing objects and actions (nouns and verbs) become grammatical markers, and existing grammatical items become more grammatical. Typical outcomes include lexical verbs becoming auxiliaries, nouns becoming adpositions or case affixes, and independent words becoming clitics and then inflectional affixes. The term was introduced by the French linguist Antoine Meillet in 1912.

Mechanisms

Several recurrent mechanisms drive grammaticalization. Semantic bleaching is the loss of concrete meaning in favour of abstract grammatical meaning. Phonetic erosion reduces the form (as in English going to → gonna). Decategorialization is the loss of the morphosyntactic properties of the source category, and extension is the use of the item in new contexts. Christian Lehmann proposed a set of parameters measuring the increasing bondedness and decreasing autonomy of grammaticalizing forms.

Examples

The Romance future tense grew from a Latin periphrasis with habere "to have" (e.g. cantare habeo "I have to sing") into a synthetic suffix. English modals such as will derive from full verbs of volition. In many languages, verbs meaning "go" become future markers, nouns meaning "back" become locative adpositions, and demonstratives become definite articles or complementizers. The English discourse marker development of indeed, and the rise of quotative be like, illustrate ongoing grammaticalization in progress.

Unidirectionality

Grammaticalization is generally held to be unidirectional: items move from lexical to grammatical, not the reverse. Apparent counterexamples, termed degrammaticalization, are rare and disputed. The theory is central to historical linguistics and linguistic typology, and to usage-based accounts of grammar associated with scholars such as Joan Bybee, Paul Hopper, and Elizabeth Traugott.
