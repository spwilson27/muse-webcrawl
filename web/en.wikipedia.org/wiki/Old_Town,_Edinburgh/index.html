The Old Town is the name popularly given to the oldest part of Scotland's capital city of Edinburgh. The area has preserved much of its medieval street plan and many Reformation-era buildings. Together with the 18th/19th-century New Town, it forms a protected UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Layout

The Old Town grew along the ridge — the "crag and tail" left by glacial erosion — running east from the volcanic Castle Rock. Its spine is the Royal Mile, descending from Edinburgh Castle through the Lawnmarket and High Street to the Canongate and the Palace of Holyroodhouse. Narrow closes and wynds run down the steep sides of the ridge like the bones of a herring, giving access to courts and to some of medieval Europe's tallest residential buildings: hemmed in by the town walls and the Nor Loch, Edinburgh built upward, with stone "lands" of ten or more storeys housing rich and poor on different floors of the same tenement.

History

By the 18th century the cramped, unsanitary Old Town held tens of thousands of people. After the Acts of Union (1707) civic leaders planned first the draining of the Nor Loch and then the Georgian New Town to the north, to which the wealthy moved. Fires and improvement schemes cleared some Old Town slums in the 19th century (the Great Fire of 1824, the creation of George IV Bridge and Chambers Street), while Patrick Geddes pioneered its conservation a century later. St Giles' Cathedral, the John Knox House, Mary King's Close (a real street sealed beneath later buildings), and the modern Scottish Parliament Building at Holyrood anchor the district, which each August fills with the Edinburgh Festival Fringe, the world's largest arts festival.
