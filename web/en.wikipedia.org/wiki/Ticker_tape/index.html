Ticker tape

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ticker tape was the earliest form of digital electronic communication medium in finance, transmitting stock price information over telegraph lines from the late 19th century through the 1960s. The tape consisted of a paper strip that ran through a machine called a stock ticker, which printed abbreviated company names (the origin of the term "ticker symbol"), followed by stock transaction prices and volume figures.

History

Stock tickers were developed in the 1860s and 1870s. Edward A. Calahan invented an early version in 1867 for the Gold and Stock Telegraph Company, and Thomas Edison developed the influential "Universal Stock Printer" in 1869, which he sold to help finance his Menlo Park laboratory. The name "ticker" came from the ticking sound the machines made as they printed. By receiving the same printed quotations simultaneously, brokers across the country could follow prices in near-real time for the first time.

Decline and legacy

As trading volumes grew, ticker systems fell behind the market during busy periods; on the day of the 1929 stock market crash, the tape ran hours late. Computerized quotation systems replaced paper tickers in the 1960s. The terms "ticker symbol" and the scrolling electronic "ticker" seen on financial news channels survive as legacies of the paper tape. Shredded ticker tape thrown from office windows onto celebratory parades gave rise to the ticker tape parade, a New York City tradition.
