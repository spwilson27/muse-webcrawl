Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model is a foundational model of economic growth in macroeconomics. It extends the Solow–Swan model by endogenising the savings rate: forward-looking households choose consumption and saving to maximise lifetime utility, so saving behaviour is derived from microeconomic optimisation rather than assumed.

History

The model builds on Frank Ramsey's 1928 paper on optimal saving, one of the earliest applications of the calculus of variations in economics. David Cass and Tjalling Koopmans independently incorporated Ramsey's framework into the neoclassical growth model in 1965.

Structure

Infinitely lived households maximise a discounted stream of utility from consumption subject to a budget constraint and a neoclassical production function. The equilibrium is characterised by the Keynes–Ramsey rule, linking consumption growth to the difference between the interest rate and the rate of time preference, and typically displays saddle-path stability toward a steady state where capital per worker and consumption are constant (absent technological progress).

Significance

The model underpins much of modern macroeconomics, including real business cycle theory and dynamic general equilibrium analysis, and provides the standard framework for studying fiscal policy, growth transitions, and intergenerational welfare.
