Multan is a city in Punjab, Pakistan, on the bank of the Chenab River, and one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in South Asia. Historically a great centre of trade at the meeting of routes from Central Asia, Persia and India, it is famed as the "City of Saints" for its extraordinary concentration of Sufi shrines, whose blue-tiled domes and tombs — above all those of Bahauddin Zakariya and Shah Rukn-e-Alam — dominate its skyline and draw pilgrims from across the region.

History

Multan's origins reach into antiquity: it has been identified with the city Alexander the Great besieged in 326 BCE, where he was gravely wounded. In the early medieval period its great Sun Temple made it a place of pilgrimage and a rich prize. Arab armies under Muhammad ibn al-Qasim conquered it in 712 CE, making it an outpost of Islam in the Indus valley; later it passed among Ghaznavid, Ghurid, Delhi Sultanate, Mughal, Sikh and finally British hands, witnessing campaigns by rulers from Mahmud of Ghazna to Ahmad Shah Durrani. Under the Mughals it flourished as a commercial and mint city; the British stormed it after a hard siege in 1848–49 during the Second Anglo-Sikh War.

Economy and culture

Today Multan is the commercial heart of southern Punjab, trading in cotton, wheat, sugarcane and Pakistan's celebrated mangoes, and known for blue pottery, camel-skin lamps, embroidered khussa shoes and the sweet sohan halwa. It is the principal centre of the Saraiki language and culture. The city's summer heat is proverbial — Multan is often counted among the hottest inhabited places in Pakistan — a fact summed up in the old saying of its "dust, heat, graveyards and beggars".
