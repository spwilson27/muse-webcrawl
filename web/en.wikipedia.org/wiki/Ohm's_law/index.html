Ohm's law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The Ohm's law triangle: V = IR.

Ohm's law states that the electric current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points. Introducing the constant of proportionality, the resistance, one arrives at the three equivalent forms of the law:

I = V/R   ·   V = IR   ·   R = V/I

where I is the current in amperes, V the voltage in volts, and R the resistance in ohms (Ω). More generally, Ohm's law states that the current density in a material is proportional to the electric field, J = σE, where σ is the electrical conductivity. The law is named after the German physicist Georg Ohm, who published it in 1827 in his work Die galvanische Kette, mathematisch bearbeitet.

Scope and limitations

Ohm's law is an empirical law that holds for many materials and devices — called ohmic — over a wide range of conditions, provided physical conditions such as temperature remain constant. It does not hold for devices with strongly non-linear current–voltage characteristics, such as diodes, transistors and gas discharge tubes, nor for superconductors, whose resistance vanishes. Even for metals, resistance varies with temperature, so the law applies at a given temperature. At the microscopic level it can be derived from the Drude model of conduction, in which electrons scatter from the ionic lattice; modern treatments use quantum mechanical band theory.

Circuit analysis

Together with Kirchhoff's circuit laws, Ohm's law forms the basis of DC circuit analysis. For alternating current circuits, the relation generalises by replacing resistance with complex impedance: V = IZ. Resistors obeying the law dissipate electrical energy as heat at the rate P = VI = I²R (Joule heating).
