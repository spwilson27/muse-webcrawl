1936–1939 Arab revolt in Palestine

DateApril 1936 – August 1939
LocationMandatory Palestine
CombatantsArab rebels vs. British authorities and Jewish forces
ResultRevolt suppressed; 1939 White Paper issued

The 1936–1939 Arab revolt in Palestine, later called the Great Revolt or Great Palestinian Revolt, was a nationalist uprising by Palestinian Arabs against British colonial rule and mass Jewish immigration to Palestine. It began with a six-month general strike in 1936 and developed into an armed insurgency that Britain suppressed with a large military deployment by 1939.

Causes

The revolt grew from Arab opposition to Jewish immigration, which had surged in the 1930s as Jews fled Nazi Germany, and to land purchases that displaced Arab tenant farmers. Arab leaders demanded an end to immigration, a ban on land sales to Jews, and the establishment of a national government responsible to a representative council.

General strike and first phase

Violence in April 1936 — including killings in Jaffa and attacks on Jewish travellers — led the Arab Higher Committee, headed by Haj Amin al-Husseini, the Grand Mufti of Jerusalem, to declare a general strike. Armed bands formed in the hills, attacking British forces, Jewish settlements, and infrastructure such as the Iraq Petroleum Company pipeline.

Peel Commission

Britain appointed the Peel Commission, which in 1937 became the first official body to propose partitioning Palestine into separate Jewish and Arab states. The proposal was rejected by the Arab leadership and the revolt resumed in the autumn of 1937, after the British deported Arab leaders and the district commissioner Lewis Andrews was assassinated.

Second phase

The second phase was a full peasant insurgency: rebels briefly held the Old City of Jerusalem in 1938 and controlled much of the countryside. Britain deployed over 20,000 troops, built Tegart forts, and used air power, mass arrests, and collective punishments. Palestinian casualties are estimated at around 5,000 killed, with thousands more wounded or imprisoned; the revolt also fractured Palestinian society through rebel violence against Arab rivals.

Consequences

By 1939 the revolt was crushed, its leadership exiled and its population disarmed. Britain then issued the White Paper of 1939, sharply limiting Jewish immigration and land purchase and promising an independent, majority-Arab state within ten years — a policy that shaped the final decade of the Mandate.
