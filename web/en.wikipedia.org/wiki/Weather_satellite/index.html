A weather satellite or meteorological satellite is a type of Earth observation satellite that is primarily used to monitor the weather and climate of the Earth. Satellites are typically of two types: geostationary satellites, which hover over a fixed point on the equator and image a full hemisphere, and polar-orbiting satellites, which circle the planet at low altitude and build up global coverage strip by strip.

History

The first successful weather satellite, TIROS-1, was launched by the United States on 1 April 1960 and returned the first television pictures of clouds from orbit. The Nimbus and ESSA series followed, and dedicated operational systems emerged with NOAA's polar orbiters and the SMS/GOES geostationary series of the 1970s. Since then a coordinated international system — coordinated through the WMO and CGMS — has provided continuous global coverage; the famous whole-disc images of hurricanes and the nightly weather-map animations both derive from these spacecraft.

Instruments and uses

Modern weather satellites carry multispectral imagers measuring visible, infrared and water-vapour channels, from which forecasters derive cloud motion winds, sea surface temperature, fog, volcanic ash and wildfire detections. Sounders profile atmospheric temperature and humidity for numerical weather prediction models, which assimilate satellite data as their single largest source of observations; microwave instruments see through clouds, and geostationary lightning mappers track convective storms in real time. Data are also essential for hurricane track and intensity forecasting, climate monitoring, aviation and agriculture.

Major systems

Current and recent systems include the US GOES series and NOAA/Metop polar orbiters, Europe's Meteosat and Metop, Japan's Himawari, China's FengYun, India's INSAT and Russia's Meteor and Elektro-L satellites.
