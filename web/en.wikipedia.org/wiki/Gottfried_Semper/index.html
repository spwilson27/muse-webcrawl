Gottfried Semper

Gottfried Semper (29 November 1803 – 15 May 1879) was a German architect, art critic, and professor of architecture who designed and built the Semper Opera House in Dresden between 1838 and 1841. In 1849 he took part in the May Uprising in Dresden and was put on the government's wanted list; he fled first to Zurich and later to London. Later in life he returned to Switzerland and designed many significant buildings, including works in Vienna undertaken with Karl Freiherr von Hasenauer. Semper wrote extensively about the origins of architecture, especially in his major theoretical work Der Stil in den technischen und tektonischen Künsten (Style in the Technical and Tectonic Arts, 1860–1863), and is regarded as one of the most influential architectural theorists of the 19th century.

Life

Education and early career

Semper was born in Altona (then under Danish rule, now part of Hamburg) into a prosperous family of manufacturers. He studied law briefly at Göttingen, then mathematics and architecture, traveling to Paris in 1826 to work under Franz Christian Gau and later Jacques Hittorff. A study tour of Italy and Greece (1830–1833) fueled his research into ancient polychromy — he argued, controversially at the time, that Greek temples had been painted in vivid colors. In 1834, on the recommendation of Franz Christian Gau, he became professor of architecture at the Dresden Academy of Fine Arts.

Dresden and exile

In Dresden Semper designed the Hoftheater (the first Semperoper, 1838–1841), the Zwinger gallery (Sempergalerie), a synagogue, and other landmarks, helping transform the city. When the May Uprising of 1849 broke out, Semper — a committed republican sympathizer — helped design barricades; when the revolt was crushed he fled, eventually reaching London, where he participated in circles around the Great Exhibition of 1851 and the South Kensington museum project under Henry Cole. His study of the Crystal Palace exhibits informed his theory of industrial design.

Zurich and Vienna

In 1855 Semper became professor at the new Polytechnikum (now ETH Zürich) in Switzerland, where he designed its main building and the Winterthur Stadthaus, among other works. In the 1870s, summoned to Vienna, he collaborated with Karl von Hasenauer on the monumental Ringstrasse projects: the twin Kunsthistorisches and Naturhistorisches museums and the Burgtheater. Semper died in Rome in 1879 during a trip to Italy.

Theory

Semper's major work, Der Stil in den technischen und tektonischen Künsten ("Style in the Technical and Tectonic Arts," 1860–1863), sought the laws governing artistic form. He proposed that architecture originated in four elements — the hearth, the mound (earthwork), the roof, and the lightweight enclosing "wall" derived from woven textiles. His concept of Bekleidung ("dressing" or cladding) held that the visible surface or textile-like covering, rather than the hidden structure, was the fundamental bearer of architectural meaning. His materialist but historically grounded theory influenced generations of architects and theorists, including Otto Wagner and, through him, early modernism; Alois Riegl both drew on and criticized Semper's approach.
