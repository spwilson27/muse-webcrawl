Legendre polynomials

The first six Legendre polynomials P0 to P5

In mathematics, Legendre polynomials, named after Adrien-Marie Legendre (1752–1833), are a system of complete and orthogonal polynomials that arise in solving Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates. They are solutions of Legendre's differential equation and appear throughout physics and engineering wherever problems possess spherical symmetry — in electrostatics, gravitation, heat conduction, and quantum mechanics.

Definition

The Legendre polynomial Pn(x) can be defined by Rodrigues' formula:

Pn(x) = (1 / 2n n!) · dn/dxn [ (x² − 1)n ]

The first few polynomials are P0(x) = 1, P1(x) = x, P2(x) = (3x² − 1)/2, P3(x) = (5x³ − 3x)/2, and P4(x) = (35x⁴ − 30x² + 3)/8. They satisfy Bonnet's recursion relation (n + 1)Pn+1(x) = (2n + 1)xPn(x) − nPn−1(x), by which all higher polynomials can be generated.

Orthogonality and expansion

On the interval [−1, 1] the Legendre polynomials are orthogonal with unit weight: the integral of Pm(x)Pn(x) over the interval equals 2/(2n + 1) when m = n and vanishes otherwise. Consequently any (suitably well-behaved) function can be expanded in a series of Legendre polynomials, the analogue of a Fourier series adapted to the sphere. Taking x = cos θ connects the polynomials to expansions in polar angle.

Applications

The gravitational or electrostatic potential of an axisymmetric mass or charge distribution is naturally expressed as a series in Legendre polynomials — the multipole expansion. In quantum mechanics, the angular part of the hydrogen atom's wavefunction involves the associated Legendre functions, close relatives of the polynomials, which combine with radial functions to form spherical harmonics. Legendre series also underpin numerical methods such as Gauss–Legendre quadrature, whose nodes are the roots of Pn.
