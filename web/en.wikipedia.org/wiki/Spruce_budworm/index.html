The spruce budworm (Choristoneura fumiferana) is a moth of the family Tortricidae native to the boreal forests of eastern North America. Its caterpillars feed on the new foliage of spruce and balsam fir, and periodic population outbreaks — recurring roughly every 30 to 40 years — defoliate and kill trees over millions of hectares, making it one of the most destructive forest insects in North America.

Life cycle

Adults emerge in mid-summer and lay egg masses on needles; the tiny larvae overwinter in silken shelters without feeding, then emerge in spring to mine old needles and buds before feeding on the opening new growth. Heavily defoliated trees weaken after two or three years and may die after four to seven years of attack.

Economic and ecological impact

Outbreaks cause major losses to the pulp and timber industries of Canada and the northern United States, and are managed with aerial spraying of insecticides such as Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), early-intervention strategies, and salvage harvesting. Ecologically, budworm cycles shape forest succession, and the caterpillars are an important food source that drives breeding booms of birds such as the bay-breasted warbler.
