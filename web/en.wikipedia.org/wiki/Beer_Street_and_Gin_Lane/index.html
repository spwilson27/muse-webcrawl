Beer Street and Gin Lane

Gin Lane (1751), Hogarth's depiction of the evils of cheap spirits.

Beer Street and Gin Lane are two prints issued in 1751 by the English artist William Hogarth in support of what would become the Gin Act 1751. They were published alongside his earlier moralising works and the political pamphlets of his friend Henry Fielding.

Description

The two engravings form a deliberate contrast. In Beer Street, happy, prosperous citizens drink wholesome English beer: tradesmen thrive, buildings are repaired, and only the pawnbroker's shop is in disrepair. In Gin Lane, set in the slum district of St Giles, a population ruined by cheap gin suffers starvation, madness and death; a drunken mother lets her baby fall, the pawnbroker prospers, and buildings collapse. Verses by James Townley beneath each print underline the moral.

Context and legacy

The prints responded to the "gin craze" of the first half of the eighteenth century, when cheap, untaxed spirits were blamed for crime and social breakdown in London. Sold cheaply at a shilling each, the prints were aimed at a wide audience rather than collectors. Gin Lane in particular has become one of the most famous images of eighteenth-century London and is still reproduced in discussions of public health and drug policy.
