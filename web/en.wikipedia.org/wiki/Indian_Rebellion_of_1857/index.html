Indian Rebellion of 1857

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Residency at Lucknow, whose siege became emblematic of the rebellion.

The Indian Rebellion of 1857 was a major uprising in India in 1857–58 against the rule of the British East India Company. It began as a mutiny of sepoys of the Company's army in the garrison town of Meerut on 10 May 1857 and soon spread into a wider rebellion across the northern and central regions, though much of the south and the recently annexed Punjab remained quieter. In India it is also called the First War of Independence.

Causes

Grievances accumulated over decades: the Doctrine of Lapse annexations of princely states, heavy land-revenue settlements, missionary activity feared as forced Christianisation, and sepoys' resentment of low pay and overseas-service orders. The immediate spark was the new Enfield rifle cartridge, rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat — offensive to Hindu and Muslim soldiers alike, who had to bite it open.

Course of the rebellion

Mutineers marched from Meerut to Delhi and proclaimed the aged Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah II their leader. Major fighting centred on Delhi, besieged and stormed by the British in September 1857; Kanpur, where Nana Sahib's forces massacred British captives; Lucknow, defended at the Residency; and Jhansi, defended by the Rani Lakshmibai, who died fighting in 1858. Guerrilla resistance under Tantia Tope continued until 1859.

Consequences

The Government of India Act 1858 dissolved the East India Company and transferred its possessions to the Crown, inaugurating the British Raj. Bahadur Shah was exiled to Burma. The army was reorganised with a higher proportion of British troops, and official policy turned away from aggressive Westernising reform. The rebellion became a founding epic of Indian nationalism, memorialised in novels, films, and Mangal Pandey's popular legend.
