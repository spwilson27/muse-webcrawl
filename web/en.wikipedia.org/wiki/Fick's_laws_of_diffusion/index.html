Fick's laws of diffusion

Fick's laws of diffusion describe diffusion and were derived by Adolf Fick in 1855. They can be used to solve for the diffusion coefficient, D. Fick's first law can be used to derive his second law, which in turn is identical to the diffusion equation.

Fick's first law

Fick's first law relates the diffusive flux to the gradient of concentration. It postulates that the flux goes from regions of high concentration to regions of low concentration, with a magnitude proportional to the concentration gradient:

J = −D (dφ/dx)

where J is the diffusion flux, D is the diffusivity (in m²/s), φ is the concentration, and x is position. The negative sign reflects flow down the gradient.

Fick's second law

Fick's second law predicts how diffusion causes the concentration to change with time:

∂φ/∂t = D (∂²φ/∂x²)

In multiple dimensions the right-hand side becomes D times the Laplacian of the concentration. The equation is mathematically the same as the heat equation, and its solutions underlie much of transport theory.

History and applications

Adolf Fick, a German physiologist, formulated the laws by analogy with Fourier's law of heat conduction and Ohm's law. The laws are applied throughout physics, chemistry, biology and engineering — for example to gas exchange in the lungs, dopant diffusion in semiconductors, and drug release. Extensions such as the Maxwell–Stefan equations handle multi-component systems where Fick's simple form is inadequate.
