Chu Ci

The Chu Ci (Chinese: 楚辭; "Verses of Chu"), traditionally attributed largely to Qu Yuan, is an anthology of Chinese poetry dating from the Warring States period to the Han dynasty. Together with the Shijing (Classic of Poetry), it forms one of the two great founding collections of Chinese verse.

The anthology takes its name from the southern state of Chu, and its style — the sao style — is distinguished from northern poetry by its exuberant imagery, shamanistic journeys, lush descriptions of flora, and the use of the exclamatory particle xi (兮) within lines.

Contents

The most famous piece is Li Sao ("Encountering Sorrow"), Qu Yuan's long autobiographical poem of political frustration and spiritual quest, written after his exile from the Chu court. Other major sections include the Jiu Ge ("Nine Songs"), a cycle of shamanic hymns; Tian Wen ("Heavenly Questions"), posing over 170 questions about mythology and the cosmos; the Jiu Zhang ("Nine Pieces"); and later Han dynasty additions such as the Jiu Bian ("Nine Changes") by Song Yu and pieces by the anthology's compiler.

Compilation

The collection was compiled in its received form by Wang Yi of the Eastern Han dynasty (2nd century CE), whose commentary is the earliest surviving. Qu Yuan (c. 340–278 BCE), a minister of Chu who drowned himself in the Miluo River — an act commemorated by the Dragon Boat Festival — has been its central figure ever since.

Influence

The Chu Ci strongly influenced the development of the fu rhapsody of the Han dynasty and Chinese literature generally, and it introduced themes of the exiled loyal minister and the goddess encounter that recur throughout later poetry.
