Fourdrinier machine

The Fourdrinier machine is the basis of most modern papermaking. It has been developed to create a continuous web of paper, using a moving woven mesh to drain a pulp slurry and form a matted sheet, which is then pressed and dried.

History

The continuous paper machine was patented in France in 1799 by Louis-Nicolas Robert and later developed in England with financing from the stationer brothers Sealy and Henry Fourdrinier, after whom it is named, and engineering by Bryan Donkin. The first successful machine was installed at Frogmore Mill, Hertfordshire, in 1803.

Process sections

A Fourdrinier machine has four main sections. In the forming section (the "wet end"), pulp slurry flows from the headbox onto a moving wire mesh, where water drains away — aided by hydrofoils, vacuum boxes and a dandy roll — leaving a wet web. The press section squeezes out more water between felted rollers; the dryer section passes the sheet over steam-heated cylinders; and the calender section smooths and finishes the paper before it is wound onto reels.
