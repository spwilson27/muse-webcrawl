Taksin the Great (Thai: ตากสินมหาราช, 17 April 1734 – 7 April 1782) was king of Siam and founder of the Thonburi Kingdom. A general of mixed Thai-Chinese descent, he reunified Siam after the catastrophic fall of Ayutthaya to the Burmese in 1767.

Reunification of Siam

Born Sin, the son of a Chinese immigrant tax farmer and a Thai mother, he rose through government and military service and was governor of Tak. Before Ayutthaya fell, he broke out of the besieged city with a small force, rallied support on the eastern coast, and within months retook the capital region from the Burmese garrisons. He established his capital at Thonburi, across the river from modern Bangkok, and was crowned in 1767.

Over the following years Taksin subdued the rival regional lords who had fragmented the former kingdom, repelled further Burmese invasions, extended Siamese power over Chiang Mai and the Lao kingdoms, and asserted suzerainty over Cambodia and Malay states. He rebuilt trade — especially with China — restored administration, and patronised religion and the arts, ordering the collection of texts lost in Ayutthaya's destruction.

Downfall and legacy

In his later reign Taksin showed signs of mental instability, harshly disciplining monks and officials and reportedly claiming divine status. In 1782 a rebellion deposed him; his general Chao Phraya Chakri, returning from Cambodia, took the throne as Rama I, founding the Chakri dynasty, and Taksin was executed. In Thailand he is officially honoured as one of the great kings ("the Great"), and his equestrian monument at Wongwian Yai in Thonburi is a major landmark, with 28 December, his coronation day, commemorating him.
