Homeschooling, also known as home education or elective home education, is the education of school-aged children at home or a variety of places other than a school. Usually conducted by a parent, tutor, or online teacher, many homeschool families use less formal, more personalized and individualized methods of learning that are not always found in schools. Many homeschoolers are educated by online programs or by tutors hired by parents.

Before the introduction of compulsory school attendance laws, most childhood education was imparted by families and local communities. By the early 19th century, in many countries, attending a primary school had become the norm. In the 1960s and 1970s, educational reformers such as John Holt began questioning the effectiveness of traditional schooling and helped spark the modern homeschooling movement.

History

For most of human history, children were educated at home or through apprenticeships. The rise of public schooling in the 19th century shifted education into institutions. In the United States, compulsory attendance laws spread from Massachusetts (1852) to all states by 1918. The modern homeschooling movement grew in the 1970s, drawing both religious families seeking faith-based instruction and secular families influenced by Holt's critique of schooling. By 1993, homeschooling was legal in all 50 US states in some form.

Methods

Homeschooling approaches range along a spectrum from highly structured to unstructured:

  • Structured/traditional — follows a school-like schedule and commercial curriculum.
  • Classical education — based on the trivium of grammar, logic, and rhetoric.
  • Charlotte Mason method — emphasizes "living books" and nature study.
  • Montessori and Waldorf-inspired approaches adapted for home use.
  • Unschooling — child-led, interest-driven learning without a set curriculum.
  • Online and virtual schooling — delivered through accredited online programs.

Motivations

Common motivations include dissatisfaction with the local school environment, a desire to provide religious or moral instruction, concern about school safety, the wish to customize the curriculum to a child's pace and interests (including gifted children and those with special needs), and family lifestyle or travel considerations. Surveys in the United States, such as those by the National Center for Education Statistics, consistently list concern about the school environment and a desire for moral instruction among the most-cited reasons.

Outcomes and criticism

Research on academic outcomes is mixed and contested, with studies often limited by self-selection bias. Supporters point to evidence that homeschooled students score well on standardized tests and succeed in college; critics raise concerns about socialization, regulatory oversight, gaps in instruction, and the protection of children's interests.

Legality around the world

Homeschooling is legal, with varying degrees of regulation, in countries including the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand. It is restricted in some European countries: Germany generally prohibits it except in exceptional circumstances, and Sweden permits it only under extraordinary circumstances. Laws in France were tightened in 2021 to require authorization. International interpretations of the right to education and parental rights continue to be debated.
