River Wey

CountryEngland, United Kingdom
Lengthabout 140 km (87 mi) including both branches
SourcesAlton, Hampshire; Blackdown, West Sussex
MouthRiver Thames at Weybridge, Surrey

The River Wey is a tributary of the River Thames in south-east England. It has two main branches: the northern branch rises at Alton in Hampshire and flows through Farnham, and the southern branch rises at Blackdown in West Sussex and flows through Haslemere and Godalming; the two join at Tilford in Surrey. The united river continues through Guildford and joins the Thames at Weybridge, which takes its name from the river.

The Wey Navigation

The Wey was among the first rivers in England to be made navigable. Under an Act of 1651 the Wey Navigation was constructed between Guildford and Weybridge, opening in 1653 — more than a century before the great canal age — with 12 locks that overcame the fall of the river. In 1764 the navigation was extended upstream to Godalming. Managed for centuries by barge traffic carrying corn, timber, coal and gunpowder from the Chilworth mills, the navigations were given to the National Trust in the 20th century, which still maintains them; the towpath now forms part of a popular walking route, and the Dapdune Wharf visitor centre in Guildford interprets the waterway's history.

Industry and landscape

The Wey valley powered an exceptional concentration of mills — over 40 mill sites are recorded, producing flour, paper, cloth, iron and gunpowder. The river passes the ruins of Waverley Abbey, the first Cistercian abbey in England, near Farnham; Newark Priory on an island near Ripley; and the grounds of Guildford Castle. Its banks include protected watermeadows at Broadford and the Unstead wetlands. The Wey supports brown trout, grayling and coarse fish, and its clear chalk-influenced springs at the headwaters sustain a modest chalk-stream character in the northern branch below Alton.
