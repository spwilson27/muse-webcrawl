Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove

Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove
Location: Osogbo, Osun State, Nigeria
Area: 75 hectares
UNESCO World Heritage Site: inscribed 2005 (cultural, criteria ii, iii, vi)

The Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove is a sacred forest along the banks of the Osun River, on the outskirts of the city of Osogbo in Osun State, Nigeria. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, inscribed in 2005. The grove is dedicated to Osun (Oshun), the Yoruba goddess of fertility, and is one of the last remnants of primary high forest in southern Nigeria.

Description

The grove covers about 75 hectares of dense forest containing some 40 shrines, sculptures and art works in honour of Osun and other Yoruba deities. Two palaces, five sacred places and nine worship points line the riverbanks, along paths used by priests and worshippers. Sacred groves dedicated to Osun were once a feature of every Yoruba settlement; Osun-Osogbo is the largest and best preserved, and the only one still functioning as an active religious site of its scale.

The New Sacred Art movement

In the mid-20th century the grove fell into neglect: the annual festival shrank, poaching and logging threatened the forest, and shrines decayed. From the 1950s, the Austrian artist Susanne Wenger (1915–2009), who settled in Osogbo and took the Yoruba name Adunni Olorisha, worked with local artists of the New Sacred Art movement to restore the grove, creating monumental mud-and-cement sculptures and shrines that fused Yoruba cosmology with modern technique. Their work protected the grove from developers — the sculptures made it too sacred and too celebrated to destroy — and helped revive the Osun festival.

Osun-Osogbo Festival

Each August, the grove hosts the culmination of the two-week Osun-Osogbo festival, held in honour of the river goddess. The festival commemorates the mythical pact between the founders of Osogbo and Osun, who granted the town protection and fertility. Its climax is the procession of the Arugba, a young virgin votary who carries the sacrificial offerings (the calabash of appeasement) to the goddess at the river, accompanied by the Ataoja (king) of Osogbo and thousands of devotees and visitors.

Significance

UNESCO recognised the grove as "the largest and perhaps the only remaining example of a once widespread phenomenon that used to characterise every Yoruba settlement" and as a living testimony to Yoruba religion and artistic tradition.
