Optimal foraging theory

Optimal foraging theory
FieldBehavioural ecology
Key worksMacArthur & Pianka (1966); Emlen (1966)
Core predictionNatural selection favours foraging behaviour that maximises net energy (or fitness) gained per unit time

Optimal foraging theory (OFT) is a behavioural ecology model that helps predict how an animal behaves when searching for food. Although obtaining food provides the animal with energy, searching for and capturing it also requires energy and time. OFT proposes that organisms forage in such a way as to maximise their net energy intake per unit time — that is, they behave as if solving an optimisation problem — because animals that do so gain a fitness advantage over those that forage less efficiently.

Models

The classic prey-choice (diet breadth) model, developed independently by Robert MacArthur and Eric Pianka and by J. M. Emlen in 1966, predicts which food types a forager should accept or ignore based on each item's profitability (energy gained divided by handling time) and encounter rates: a forager should add a lower-ranked food to its diet only if doing so raises its overall rate of energy intake. The marginal value theorem, formulated by Eric Charnov (1976), predicts how long a forager should stay in a depleting patch before moving on: it should leave when its rate of intake in the patch drops to the average rate for the habitat as a whole.

Evidence and criticism

Many experiments, from shore crabs selecting mussels to bees visiting flowers, broadly support OFT predictions, though rarely exactly. Critics note that animals may optimise something other than energy — nutrients, safety from predators, or water — face imperfect information, and are constrained by physiology and cognition. Modern behavioural ecology treats OFT as a framework for generating testable hypotheses about foraging decisions rather than a claim that animals compute optima.
