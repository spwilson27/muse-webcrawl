Ebla (Sumerian: 𒌈𒆷 eb-la; Arabic: إبلا) was one of the earliest kingdoms in Syria. Its remains constitute a tell named Tell Mardikh, about 55 km (34 mi) southwest of Aleppo near the village of Mardikh. Ebla was an important centre throughout the 3rd millennium BC and in the first half of the 2nd millennium BC. Its discovery provided evidence that the Levant was a centre of ancient civilisation comparable to Egypt and Mesopotamia, contradicting earlier views that it was a cultural backwater.

History

Ebla was first settled around 3500 BC and grew through trade in timber, textiles and agricultural produce. The first Eblaite kingdom (c. 2380–2250 BC) controlled an empire stretching from the Euphrates to the Mediterranean; it was destroyed, most likely by the Akkadian king Naram-Sin or by its former vassal Mari. A second kingdom arose in the later 3rd millennium, and a third, Amorite-era Ebla flourished in the early 2nd millennium BC before being destroyed around 1600 BC, probably by the Hittite king Mursili I. A reduced Iron Age settlement persisted into the 7th century BC.

The Ebla tablets

In 1974–75, Italian excavators under Paolo Matthiae discovered the royal palace archives: some 17,000 clay tablets and fragments written in cuneiform, dating to about 2350 BC. The tablets are written in Sumerian and in a previously unknown Semitic language, Eblaite, and include administrative records, royal decrees, treaties — including one of the oldest known treaties, with Abarsal — dictionaries and bilingual word lists that made decipherment possible. The archive transformed understanding of the political, economic and religious world of Bronze Age Syria.

Economy and society

Ebla's prosperity rested on agriculture, animal husbandry and long-distance trade in textiles, metals and timber; tens of thousands of sheep and goats supported a major wool industry. The chief deity (EN) of the city was Kura, and the pantheon shared many gods with northern Mesopotamia and the Canaanite world.
