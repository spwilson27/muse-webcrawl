The LGP-30 was an early off-the-shelf computer manufactured by the Librascope company of Glendale, California (a division of General Precision), and sold and serviced by the Royal Precision Electronic Computer Company. It was introduced in 1956 and cost $47,000, equivalent to a much larger sum today. The initials stand for Librascope General Purpose 30.

The LGP-30 was a desk-sized machine weighing about 800 pounds (360 kg). It was one of the first computers intended to be operated by a single person without a specialized staff, and it achieved respectable sales for its era, with roughly 493 units produced.

Design

The computer was a binary, 31-bit word machine with a magnetic drum memory holding 4,096 words. It used 113 vacuum tubes, 1,450 diodes, and 34 plug-in cards, an unusually small parts count achieved through a bit-serial architecture in which arithmetic was performed one bit at a time as words rotated on the drum. Input and output were through a Flexowriter electric typewriter, with paper tape as the storage medium.

Users and legacy

Notable users included Edward Lorenz, whose early weather models on an LGP-30 helped lead to chaos theory, and Mel Kaye, the programmer celebrated in the folklore story "Mel, the Real Programmer." The machine was also used by Dartmouth College, where the ACT-IV compiler was written for it.
