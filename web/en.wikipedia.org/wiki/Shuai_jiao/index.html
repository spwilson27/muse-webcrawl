Shuai jiao

Shuai jiao (Chinese: 摔跤; pinyin: shuāijiāo) is the term for several styles of Chinese wrestling, the most common of which descends from the jacket wrestling system of the Qing dynasty. It is considered the oldest of the Chinese martial arts, with legendary antecedents said to reach back more than 4,000 years to the military exercises of the Yellow Emperor's era, and is recognized as a foundational influence on grappling techniques found across Chinese kung fu and, through turbulent centuries of contact, on Mongolian wrestling and Japanese jujutsu and judo.

History

Early forms of wrestling, known as jǐao dǐ (角抵, "horn butting"), were performed in the Han dynasty as both military training and court spectacle, with wrestlers sometimes wearing horned helmets in imitation of a mythical horned rebel, Chiyou. Under the Song and Ming dynasties, wrestling remained a standard component of military examinations. In the Qing dynasty, the Manchu imperial guard maintained an elite wrestling unit in the Shan Pu Ying ("Bureau of Fine Wrestling") in Beijing, whose jacket throws became the core of the modern Beijing style. Regional styles developed in Tianjin, Baoding, and Shandong, each with characteristic emphasis — speed and flashy finishes in Baoding's kuaizi ("fast") style, solid stances in Beijing style.

Technique and rules

Shuai jiao emphasizes throws, trips, sweeps, and off-balancing, using the opponent's jacket (traditionally a thick short-sleeved garment), belt, and limbs as grips. A match is won by throwing the opponent so that any part of the body above the knees touches the ground while the thrower remains standing. Modern sport bouts take place on mats or in a ring; strikes are excluded in pure shuai jiao competition, distinguishing it from the combined striking-and-throwing format of sanda. Training traditionally includes stance work, gripping drills, solo throwing practice with weighted implements, and conditioning exercises such as lifting stone locks.
