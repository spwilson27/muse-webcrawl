Montague grammar

[Image placeholder: syntax–semantics mapping]

Montague grammar is an approach to natural language semantics based on formal logic, especially lambda calculus and intensional logic. It was developed by the American logician Richard Montague in the late 1960s and published in a series of papers, most famously "The Proper Treatment of Quantification in Ordinary English" (1973). Against the prevailing view that natural languages were too irregular for formal treatment, Montague declared: "I reject the contention that an important theoretical difference exists between formal and natural languages."

Principles

The framework rests on the principle of compositionality: the meaning of a complex expression is a function of the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined. Montague specified a fragment of English with an explicit syntax and a translation into a typed intensional logic, in which noun phrases are treated as generalised quantifiers and verbs receive meanings relative to possible worlds and times. The mapping between syntactic rules and semantic operations is a homomorphism, guaranteeing that every derivation yields exactly one interpretation.

Influence

Montague grammar founded modern formal semantics in linguistics. Barbara Partee and others adapted its model-theoretic machinery for linguistic theory, and it underlies much work in computational semantics and natural language processing, including categorial grammar approaches that inherit its type-driven composition.
