Republic (Plato)

The Republic (Greek: Πολιτεία, Politeia; Latin: De Re Publica) is a Socratic dialogue by the Athenian philosopher Plato, written around 375 BC. It concerns justice (δικαιοσύνη), the order and character of the just city-state and the just man, and is Plato's best-known work, widely regarded as one of the most influential texts in philosophy and political theory.

Structure and argument

In ten books, Socrates discusses the nature of justice with interlocutors including Glaucon, Adeimantus, Polemarchus, Cephalus and the sophist Thrasymachus, who argues that justice is "the advantage of the stronger". To define justice in the individual, Socrates constructs an ideal city in speech, the Kallipolis, divided into producers, auxiliaries (warriors) and philosopher-kings as guardians, and maps its three classes onto the three parts of the soul: reason, spirit and appetite. Justice is each part performing its proper function in harmony.

Famous passages

The dialogue contains the allegory of the cave, the analogy of the sun and the divided line (Book VI–VII), the myth of the metals or "noble lie", the ship of state analogy, the critique and banishment of imitative poetry (Book X) and the closing Myth of Er, an account of the afterlife. The philosopher-king thesis — that cities will have no rest from evils until philosophers rule as kings — remains its most debated political claim.

Influence

The Republic shaped Aristotle's Politics, Cicero's De re publica, Augustine, and centuries of utopian and educational thought. Karl Popper's The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945) attacked it as a blueprint for totalitarianism, a reading many classicists contest.
