A bookmaker, often simply called a bookie, is an individual or organization that takes bets on sporting and other events at agreed-upon odds. Bookmakers originated with horse racing in Britain in the 18th and 19th centuries and are now a global industry, operating through betting shops, racecourse pitches, telephone accounts, and online platforms.

How bookmaking works

Rather than betting on an outcome itself, a bookmaker aims to accept wagers in proportions that guarantee a profit regardless of the result. Odds are set so that the implied probabilities sum to more than 100% — the excess is the overround, which constitutes the bookmaker's margin. The bookie adjusts odds in response to the weight of money (and information) to "balance the book," and may lay off risky positions with other bookmakers or on betting exchanges. Major modern firms — such as Flutter (Paddy Power, Betfair), Entain (Ladbrokes, Coral), and William Hill — employ traders and statistical models to set and move prices.

History and legality

Off-course cash betting shops were legalized in the United Kingdom by the Betting and Gaming Act 1960 (shops opened from 1961), transforming the industry. In the United States, bookmaking was long restricted largely to Nevada, and illegal "bookies" operated widely; the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Murphy v. NCAA striking down the federal sports-betting ban led most states to legalize regulated sportsbooks. Fixed-odds betting terminals and online/in-play betting have been major modern growth areas and regulatory concerns.
