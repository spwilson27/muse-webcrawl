Macbeth (Medieval Gaelic: Mac Bethad mac Findlaích; Scottish Gaelic: MacBheatha mac Fhionnlaigh; anglicised as Macbeth, died 15 August 1057) was King of Scotland from 1040 until his death in 1057. He ruled as mormaer (earl) of Moray before killing his cousin, King Duncan I, in battle and taking the throne.

Life and reign

Macbeth's father, Findláech, was mormaer of Moray in northern Scotland. Macbeth himself became mormaer by about 1032 and married Gruoch, granddaughter of a previous king. In 1040 Duncan I — who had attacked Moray — was killed by Macbeth's forces near Elgin; later tradition of murder in bed is a fabrication. Macbeth's seventeen-year reign was, by the standards of the time, stable and prosperous: he was generous to the Church, and in 1050 made a pilgrimage to Rome where he was said to have scattered alms "like seed".

Fall

Duncan's son Malcolm (later Malcolm III, "Canmore") found refuge in England and, with the support of Siward, Earl of Northumbria, invaded in 1054. Macbeth held on for three more years before being killed at the Battle of Lumphanan in Aberdeenshire in 1057 (some accounts say he died at Scone). He was succeeded briefly by his stepson Lulach, who was killed the following year, clearing the way for Malcolm III.

Legend and Shakespeare

The historical Macbeth bears little resemblance to the tyrant of William Shakespeare's tragedy Macbeth (c. 1606), which drew on the unreliable narratives of Holinshed's Chronicles, written to flatter the Stuart king James VI and I, a descendant of the play's virtuous Banquo. Shakespeare's witches, the murder of the sleeping Duncan, and Lady Macbeth are largely inventions, but the play has permanently fixed Macbeth's popular image.
