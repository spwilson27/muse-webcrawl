Tombs of the Kings (Jerusalem)

LocationNablus Road, East Jerusalem
TypeRock-cut burial complex
Period1st century CE (Second Temple period)
Associated withHelena of Adiabene

The Tombs of the Kings (Hebrew: קברי המלכים, Keveri HaMlakhim; French: Tombeau des Rois) are a rock-cut funerary complex in East Jerusalem, north of the Old City walls, dating to the Second Temple period. Despite the name, the tombs are not those of the kings of Judah but are traditionally attributed to Helena of Adiabene, a first-century CE queen of Adiabene in northern Mesopotamia who converted to Judaism and moved to Jerusalem.

Description

The complex is entered through a large sunken courtyard cut into the bedrock, approached by a wide stairway. Behind a porch framed by a carved frieze of grapes, wreaths and fruits lies a labyrinth of burial chambers containing some thirty burial niches and stone sarcophagi. A rolling stone once sealed the entrance. The scale of the complex — including ritual baths, a cistern system and reception rooms — made it one of the grandest burial sites of the ancient city.

Excavation and ownership

The site was excavated in the 1860s by the French archaeologist Félicien de Saulcy, who initially identified it as the tomb of the kings of Judah. Sarcophagi found there, one bearing an Aramaic inscription naming "Queen Saddan" — often identified with Helena — were taken to the Louvre in Paris. The property was purchased in the 1870s by the French-Jewish Péreire brothers and later came into the possession of the French state, which administers it. The site has periodically been opened to visitors and was reopened in 2019 after renovation, amid ongoing legal and religious disputes over access and ownership. A different site, the Tombs of the Kings at Paphos in Cyprus, shares the name but is unrelated.
