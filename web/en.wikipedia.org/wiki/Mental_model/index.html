Mental model

A mental model is an internal representation of how something works.

A mental model is an internal representation of external reality that people use to understand phenomena, reason, anticipate events and make decisions. Mental models are necessarily simplified: they omit detail, are incomplete and unstable, and differ between individuals. The concept has been influential in cognitive psychology, human–computer interaction, systems thinking and management science.

Origins

The term was introduced by the Scottish psychologist Kenneth Craik in The Nature of Explanation (1943), who proposed that the mind constructs small-scale models of reality — much as an engineer builds a physical model — and uses them to try out alternatives before acting. The idea was developed by Philip Johnson-Laird, whose Mental Models (1983) presented a theory of reasoning in which deduction proceeds by constructing and testing models of the premises rather than by applying formal rules of inference. Dedre Gentner and Albert Stevens' edited volume Mental Models (1983) collected work on naive physics and other domains.

Characteristics

Mental models are often based on a handful of salient facts and qualitative relations rather than quantitative laws. They can incorporate misconceptions — for example, the widespread "impetus" model of motion resembling medieval physics rather than Newtonian mechanics. In human–computer interaction, Donald Norman emphasized that users form models of devices from their visible structure and behaviour; good design makes the correct model easy to form.

Applications

In systems dynamics, shared mental models of managers are treated as a key lever for organisational learning (Peter Senge). Team cognition research studies "shared mental models" as predictors of team performance. Charlie Munger popularised the idea of a "latticework of mental models" drawn from many disciplines as a tool for better judgement in investing and decision-making.
