The Alphabet of Ben Sira (Alphabetum Siracidis) is an anonymous medieval Hebrew text, conventionally dated between the 8th and 10th centuries CE. It consists of two collections of acrostic proverbs — one in Aramaic and one in Hebrew, each arranged by the letters of the alphabet — together with a narrative framework purporting to tell the life of Ben Sira, the sage traditionally credited with the wisdom book Ecclesiasticus (Sirach). The work is widely regarded as a satire, and it is famous today chiefly as the earliest source of the story of Lilith as the first wife of Adam.

Content

The frame story presents Ben Sira as a miraculously precocious infant who speaks from birth, and recounts episodes at the court of Nebuchadnezzar, in which the sage answers the king's questions and heals his household. The proverbs themselves are often earthy, irreverent, and scatological, and scholars debate whether the text was meant as entertainment, parody of rabbinic midrash, or covert commentary on its times.

The Lilith story

In the most famous episode, Ben Sira is summoned to cure Nebuchadnezzar's son and relates the story of Lilith: created from the earth at the same time as Adam, she refuses to lie beneath him, insisting that they are equal; she pronounces the Ineffable Name of God and flies away to the Red Sea. Pursued by three angels — Senoy, Sansenoy, and Semangelof — she refuses to return and accepts that one hundred of her demon offspring will die daily. This account, far more developed than earlier scattered references to lilith as a night demon, is the origin of the modern image of Lilith as Adam's rebellious first wife.
