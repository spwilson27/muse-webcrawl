Groupthink

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Groupthink is a psychological phenomenon that occurs within a group of people in which the desire for harmony or conformity in the group results in an irrational or dysfunctional decision-making outcome. Cohesiveness, or the desire for cohesiveness, in such a group may produce a tendency among its members to agree at all costs. This causes the group to minimize conflict and reach a consensus decision without critical evaluation.

The term was coined by social psychologist Irving Janis in 1972, adapting George Orwell's "doublethink". Janis developed the theory by studying American foreign-policy disasters, including the Bay of Pigs Invasion, the failure to anticipate the attack on Pearl Harbor, and the escalation of the Vietnam War.

Symptoms

Janis identified eight symptoms of groupthink: an illusion of invulnerability; collective rationalisation of warnings; an unquestioned belief in the group's inherent morality; stereotyped views of out-groups; direct pressure on dissenters; self-censorship; an illusion of unanimity; and "mindguards" who shield the group from contrary information. Antecedent conditions include high group cohesiveness, insulation from outside views, directive leadership and high stress.

Prevention and criticism

Proposed remedies include assigning a devil's advocate, encouraging leaders to withhold their preferences, consulting outside experts, and holding "second chance" meetings. Later research has qualified Janis's account: cohesiveness alone does not reliably produce defective decisions, and some of his case studies have been re-examined as products of political and organisational factors as much as social pressure. Nonetheless groupthink remains a widely taught framework in psychology, management and political science.
