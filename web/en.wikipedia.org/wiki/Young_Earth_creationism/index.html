Young Earth creationism (YEC) is a form of creationism which holds as a central tenet that the Earth and its lifeforms were created by the direct acts of God between approximately 6,000 and 10,000 years ago. Its adherents typically interpret the Book of Genesis literally: creation in six days, a global Noachian flood that explains most of the geological record, and the separate creation of "kinds" of organisms, with variation occurring only within kinds. The position is held by a significant minority of Christians, principally within evangelical and fundamentalist traditions, and is rejected by mainstream science, which dates the Earth to about 4.54 billion years.

History

Chronologies based on biblical genealogies, such as Archbishop James Ussher's dating of creation to 4004 BC, were widely accepted in early modern Europe but were progressively displaced during the 18th and 19th centuries by geological evidence for deep time. Modern young Earth creationism coalesced in the 20th century, drawing on the "flood geology" of Seventh-day Adventist writer George McCready Price and reaching wide influence with John C. Whitcomb and Henry M. Morris's The Genesis Flood (1961). Morris founded the Institute for Creation Research (1970); Ken Ham's Answers in Genesis later built the Creation Museum (2007) and Ark Encounter (2016) in Kentucky.

Beliefs

  • Recent creation: the universe, Earth and life originated within the last 10,000 years.
  • Flood geology: a worldwide flood in Noah's time laid down most sedimentary rock layers and fossils rapidly.
  • Created kinds (baraminology): organisms descend, with limited variation, from originally created kinds; macroevolution between kinds is denied.
  • Challenge to radiometric dating: YEC researchers argue decay rates may have been accelerated in the past (e.g., the RATE project), so radiometric ages are unreliable.

Criticism and legal history

The scientific community regards young Earth creationism as pseudoscience: its central claims conflict with evidence from geology, astronomy, physics, chemistry, molecular biology and palaeontology, and its explanatory framework is not considered testable or falsifiable. In the United States, repeated court decisions — including Epperson v. Arkansas (1968), Edwards v. Aguillard (1987) and Kitzmiller v. Dover (2005, concerning intelligent design) — have held that teaching creationism in public-school science classes violates the Establishment Clause. Surveys in the US have repeatedly found that a substantial share of the public endorses a young Earth, though the figure varies with question wording.
