Limpieza de sangre (Spanish for "purity of blood") was a system of discriminatory statutes in early modern Spain and Portugal — and later in the Spanish Empire — that excluded descendants of Jewish and Muslim converts to Christianity (conversos and Moriscos) from offices, guilds, religious orders, universities and other institutions, regardless of their own beliefs.

Origins

The first major statute is generally dated to Toledo in 1449, when, in the wake of anti-converso riots, the city barred conversos from public office. The idea spread widely after the mass conversions and expulsions of 1492: although the converts were formally Christian, "Old Christians" suspected them of secretly practising Judaism, and the statutes shifted the criterion of exclusion from religion to ancestry.

Operation

Candidates for covered positions had to obtain a certificate of limpieza, based on formal inquiries into their genealogy, with witnesses attesting that no ancestor had been Jewish, Muslim, or punished by the Inquisition. The proofs were costly and time-consuming, generated a bureaucratic industry, and were open to bribery and forgery. In Spanish America the concept broadened into the casta system regulating status by racial ancestry.

Decline

Enlightenment critics attacked the statutes as unjust and irrational, but they were only gradually dismantled during the nineteenth century; requirements for some institutions lingered until the 1860s. Historians have treated limpieza de sangre as a landmark in the history of discrimination — an early transition from religious to genealogical, quasi-racial exclusion.
