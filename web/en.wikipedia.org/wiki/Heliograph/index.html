Heliograph

A heliograph (from Greek helios, "sun", and graphein, "to write") is a solar telegraph system that signals by flashes of sunlight reflected by a mirror. The flashes are produced by momentarily pivoting the mirror, or by interrupting the beam with a shutter.

History

Sun mirrors were used for signalling from antiquity, but the practical heliograph was developed in the 1860s and 1870s, notably by Sir Francis Ronalds and by Henry Christopher Mance of the British Army, whose design of 1869 was widely adopted. The instrument consisted of a mirror mounted on a tripod with a sighting rod; the operator aimed the reflected beam at the distant station and keyed messages in Morse code.

Use

The heliograph was extensively used by military forces in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in sunny colonial theatres — India, Africa, Australia and the American Southwest, where it was used during the Apache campaigns. Under good conditions a flash could be seen at ranges exceeding 80 kilometres (50 miles), and in 1894 a record distance of some 295 km (183 miles) was achieved between stations on Mount Ellen and Mount Uncompahgre in the United States. Its advantages were range, portability and freedom from wires; its limitations were dependence on sunlight and vulnerability to interception, since any observer in the beam's path could read the signals.

The heliograph remained in military service through the Second World War in some armies and survives today in the form of the emergency signalling mirror carried in survival kits. The term is also used for instruments that record sunshine duration and for an early photographic process invented by Nicéphore Niépce.
