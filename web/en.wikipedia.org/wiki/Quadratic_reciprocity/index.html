Quadratic reciprocity

In number theory, the law of quadratic reciprocity is a theorem about modular arithmetic that gives conditions for the solvability of quadratic equations modulo prime numbers. It relates the Legendre symbols of two distinct odd primes p and q, and — together with the supplementary laws for −1 and 2 — allows the evaluation of any Legendre symbol, making it a central tool in elementary and algebraic number theory.

Quadratic residues modulo small primes; reciprocity reveals a symmetry in such tables.

Statement

For distinct odd primes p and q, writing (p/q) for the Legendre symbol:

(p/q)(q/p) = (−1)((p−1)/2)·((q−1)/2)

That is, (p/q) = (q/p) unless both p and q are congruent to 3 modulo 4, in which case (p/q) = −(q/p). The supplementary laws state that (−1/p) = 1 if p ≡ 1 (mod 4) and −1 if p ≡ 3 (mod 4), and that (2/p) = 1 if p ≡ ±1 (mod 8) and −1 if p ≡ ±3 (mod 8).

History

Special cases were glimpsed by Fermat, and Euler conjectured the general law. Legendre introduced his symbol and gave an incomplete proof in 1798. Carl Friedrich Gauss gave the first complete proof in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801), calling the result the theorema fundamentale; he returned to it repeatedly, publishing six proofs in his lifetime, and hundreds of proofs are now known. The theorem was a driving force in the development of algebraic number theory, where it generalises to reciprocity laws in number fields — cubic and biquadratic reciprocity, and ultimately the Artin reciprocity law of class field theory.

Applications

Quadratic reciprocity makes it practical to determine whether an integer is a quadratic residue modulo a prime without computing square roots, which underlies tests used in primality proving and in the quadratic sieve factoring method.
