H II region

An H II region or HII region is a region of interstellar atomic hydrogen that is ionised. It is typically part of a large molecular cloud in which star formation is taking place: the short-lived, massive blue stars newly born in the cloud emit copious ultraviolet radiation that ionises the surrounding hydrogen gas. The name uses the spectroscopic notation in which "H I" is neutral atomic hydrogen and "H II" is ionised hydrogen (H+). H II regions may span several hundred light-years and are often associated with giant molecular clouds; they glow characteristically in the red light of the hydrogen-alpha (Hα) emission line at 656.3 nm.

Formation and physics

When a massive O- or early B-type star ignites inside a molecular cloud, its ultraviolet photons carve out a bubble of ionised gas — initially a compact Strömgren sphere whose radius balances ionisation against recombination. The hot (~10,000 K) plasma expands into the cooler neutral medium, sweeping up shells that may collapse and trigger further star formation. As free electrons recombine and cascade to lower energy levels they emit the nebula's spectral lines, including Hα, and forbidden lines of oxygen and nitrogen used by astronomers to measure temperature, density and chemical abundances.

Observations and significance

The Orion Nebula (M42), about 1,344 light-years away, is the nearest massive star-forming H II region and has been studied since its telescopic discovery in 1610–1611. Extragalactic H II regions, such as the enormous Tarantula Nebula in the Large Magellanic Cloud, are so luminous that their Hα emission is used to trace the star-formation rate and spiral structure of distant galaxies, and their spectra yield the gas-phase metallicities of galaxies across cosmic time. Because their ionising stars live only a few million years, H II regions mark the current locations of starbirth in the Milky Way's spiral arms.
