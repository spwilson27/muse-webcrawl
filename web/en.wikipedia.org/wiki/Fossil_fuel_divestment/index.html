Fossil fuel divestment

The divestment movement urges institutions to sell holdings in coal, oil and gas companies.

Fossil fuel divestment is the removal of investment assets — stocks, bonds and funds — from companies involved in extracting, refining or transporting fossil fuels, undertaken as a moral, political and financial response to climate change. The campaign, coordinated globally by the organisation 350.org from 2012 under the slogan "Do the Math", asks universities, religious institutions, pension funds and governments to withdraw support from the fossil fuel industry.

Rationale

Campaigners argue that proved reserves of coal, oil and gas far exceed the carbon budget compatible with limiting warming to 1.5–2 °C, so most reserves are "unburnable" and the companies holding them are overvalued — the "carbon bubble" thesis developed by Carbon Tracker. Divestment aims to stigmatise the industry, erode its political licence to operate (on the model of the 1980s South Africa divestment campaign), and protect portfolios from stranded assets.

Commitments

The movement spread from US college campuses such as Swarthmore and Unity College to large institutions: the Rockefeller Brothers Fund (2014), the University of Oxford and Stanford (coal), Norway's Government Pension Fund Global, which excluded many coal companies from 2015, the World Council of Churches, the British Medical Association, New York City pension funds, and the Republic of Ireland, which in 2018 became the first country to legislate full divestment of its sovereign wealth fund. By the early 2020s, institutions representing well over 40 trillion US dollars in assets had made some form of divestment commitment.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that selling shares merely transfers ownership to less scrupulous investors, that divested funds have negligible effect on companies' cost of capital, and that engagement and shareholder activism may achieve more. Some studies find divestment announcements impose reputational and eventually financial costs on fossil fuel firms; others note the movement's largest impact may be normative — shifting debate on the legitimacy of fossil fuel expansion. Many institutions adopted partial measures, such as excluding thermal coal or tar sands while retaining diversified energy holdings.
