Tombstoning

TypeRecreational jumping into water from height
HazardsImpact injury, hidden rocks, cold water shock, rip currents

Tombstoning is the act of jumping in a straight, upright vertical posture into the sea or other body of water from a high platform, such as a cliff, pier or harbour wall. The name derives from the shape of a tombstone, reflecting the vertical position of the jumper during the fall. The activity is popular in some coastal areas of the United Kingdom, particularly in the south west of England, but is regularly condemned by coastguard, police and safety organisations because of the serious injuries and deaths associated with it.

Risks

Entering the water from heights of ten metres or more can subject the body to forces comparable to hitting concrete. Jumps are rarely into water of known depth: tides change quickly, and submerged rocks may be hidden even in familiar spots. Cold water shock, strong currents and difficulty exiting the water compound the danger. Coastguard and Royal National Lifeboat Institution campaigns have highlighted cases of spinal injuries, broken bones and fatalities, including among teenagers.

Safety campaigns and regulation

Local authorities in resorts such as Newquay, Plymouth and Weymouth have responded with signage, CCTV, barriers and seasonal patrols. Safety campaigns often use the slogan of checking water depth — roughly five metres of water is needed for a jump from five metres — while emphasising that conditions can never be guaranteed safe. Some harbour authorities treat tombstoning as an offence under local byelaws.
