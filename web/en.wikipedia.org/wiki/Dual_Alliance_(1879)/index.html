The Dual Alliance (German: Zweibund) was a defensive alliance between the German Empire and Austria-Hungary, signed in Vienna on 7 October 1879. It was the first and most durable of the peacetime alliances constructed by Chancellor Otto von Bismarck after German unification, and it remained in force until the collapse of both empires at the end of the First World War in 1918.

Background and terms

After the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 and the Congress of Berlin, relations between Austria-Hungary and Russia deteriorated over the Balkans, while Bismarck feared that Germany's previous understanding with Russia (the Three Emperors' League) was breaking down and that St. Petersburg might turn toward France, leaving Germany facing a two-front threat. Negotiated by Bismarck and the Austro-Hungarian foreign minister Gyula Andrássy, the treaty provided that if either party were attacked by Russia, the other would come to its aid with its entire military strength; if either were attacked by another power (meaning France), the other would maintain benevolent neutrality; and if the other power were supported by Russia, the full casus foederis applied. The alliance was initially concluded for five years and was repeatedly renewed.

Role in Bismarck's system

The Dual Alliance became the fixed point of Bismarck's alliance system: Germany simultaneously sought to keep Russia friendly through the revived Three Emperors' Alliance (1881) and the secret Reinsurance Treaty (1887), while the Dual Alliance gave Austria security against Russia and anchored Vienna to Berlin. In 1882 Italy joined to form the Triple Alliance, directed primarily against France.

Long-term consequences

After Bismarck's dismissal in 1890, the Reinsurance Treaty lapsed and Russia moved toward France (Franco-Russian Alliance, 1892), producing the rigid two-bloc system of pre-1914 Europe. The Dual Alliance's guarantee underlay Germany's "blank cheque" to Austria-Hungary during the July Crisis of 1914, committing the two Central Powers to war together. Historians debate how far the alliance's solidity encouraged Austro-Hungarian risk-taking in the Balkans.
