The Okefenokee Swamp is a shallow, 438,000-acre (1,770 km²), peat-filled wetland straddling the Georgia–Florida line in the United States. Climate researchers classify the Okefenokee as one of the world's largest intact freshwater ecosystems. The name derives from a Seminole phrase meaning "land of the trembling earth," referring to the floating peat mats — called "houses" — that quake underfoot.

Geography and hydrology

The swamp occupies a saucer-shaped depression that was once an Atlantic Ocean bay. Unlike most swamps it is not fed mainly by rivers: rain falling directly on its surface supplies most of its water, which drains slowly out through the Suwannee River southwest to the Gulf of Mexico and the St. Marys River southeast to the Atlantic. Within the swamp, open "prairies" of water lilies and grasses alternate with cypress domes, shrub swamps and sandy pine islands. Peat deposits up to 15 feet (4.6 m) thick blanket the floor, and decaying vegetation stains the slow water tea-brown and acidic.

History

Indigenous peoples lived in and around the swamp for thousands of years; the Seminole used it as a refuge during the Seminole Wars. In the 1890s the Suwannee Canal Company attempted to drain the swamp for logging and agriculture, digging the Suwannee Canal before going bankrupt. Large-scale cypress logging followed in the early twentieth century under the Hebard Lumber Company. Public alarm at the destruction led to federal protection, and in 1937 most of the swamp became the Okefenokee National Wildlife Refuge.

Wildlife

The swamp shelters American alligators in large numbers, Florida black bears, sandhill cranes, wood storks, carnivorous pitcher plants and giant cypress trees. Its floating peat islands support trees and shrubs rooted in mats that rise and fall with the water level.

In culture

The Okefenokee became famous nationwide as the home of Pogo, the philosophising opossum of Walt Kelly's comic strip, whose slogan "We have met the enemy and he is us" became an environmental catchphrase.
