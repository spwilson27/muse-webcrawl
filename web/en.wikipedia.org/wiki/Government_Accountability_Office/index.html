The United States Government Accountability Office (GAO) is a legislative branch government agency that provides auditing, evaluation, and investigative services for the United States Congress. It is sometimes called the "congressional watchdog". GAO was established as the General Accounting Office by the Budget and Accounting Act of 1921; its name changed to Government Accountability Office in 2004.

Role and work

GAO examines how taxpayer dollars are spent and provides Congress and federal agency heads with timely, fact-based, non-partisan information that can be used to improve government performance. Its work includes financial audits, program evaluations, policy analyses, and investigations. GAO also issues legal decisions on bid protests concerning federal contracts and publishes generally accepted government auditing standards, known as the "Yellow Book".

Leadership

The head of GAO, the Comptroller General of the United States, is appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate for a single 15-year term. The long, non-renewable term is intended to insulate the office from political pressure.
