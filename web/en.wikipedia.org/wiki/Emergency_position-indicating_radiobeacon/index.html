An emergency position-indicating radiobeacon (EPIRB) is a type of emergency locator beacon, a portable, battery-powered radio transmitter used in emergencies to locate airplanes, vessels, and persons in distress and in need of immediate rescue. In the event of an emergency, such as a ship sinking or an aircraft crash, the transmitter is activated and begins transmitting a continuous radio signal, which is used by search-and-rescue teams to locate the emergency and render aid.

Operation

Modern EPIRBs transmit a digital signal on 406 MHz that is detected by satellites of the international Cospas-Sarsat system. The signal carries a unique identifier registered to the vessel, allowing rescue coordination centres to identify the ship and contact its emergency contacts. Many units incorporate a GPS receiver and transmit their position, greatly narrowing the search area; without GPS, position is estimated from the Doppler shift of the signal as satellites pass overhead. Most EPIRBs also transmit a low-power 121.5 MHz homing signal for final approach by rescuers.

Types and activation

  • Category I: float-free, automatically deployed and activated by a hydrostatic release when submerged.
  • Category II: manually deployed and activated.

Related devices using the same system include ELTs (emergency locator transmitters) for aircraft and PLBs (personal locator beacons) carried by individuals on land and sea.

Regulation

Under the SOLAS Convention and the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS), most commercial vessels are required to carry EPIRBs, and carriage is mandated for many offshore and commercial fishing vessels by national rules. Beacons must be registered with the national authority — registration is free and significantly speeds rescue because false alerts can be checked by telephone.

History

The 406 MHz satellite system became operational in the 1980s; satellite processing of the old analogue 121.5 MHz distress signal was terminated on 1 February 2009, after which 121.5 MHz beacons could no longer be detected by satellite. Cospas-Sarsat credits the system with assisting in the rescue of tens of thousands of people worldwide.
