Climate of Australia

The climate of Australia is governed largely by the continent's size, its position straddling the tropic of Capricorn, and the surrounding oceans. Australia is the driest inhabited continent on Earth: the great majority of its interior is arid or semi-arid desert, while the northern fringe experiences a tropical monsoon climate and the southeast and southwest enjoy temperate conditions. Most of the population lives along the well-watered eastern, southeastern, and southwestern coastal fringes.

Regional patterns

The northern third of the continent — including Darwin and Cairns — has a tropical climate with a hot, humid wet season driven by monsoon troughs and tropical cyclones between roughly November and April, and a warm, almost rainless dry season. The interior "outback" receives less than 250 mm of rain in many places, with high evaporation and extreme summer heat; the highest temperature recorded in Australia was 50.7 °C at Oodnadatta in South Australia in 1960.

The populous southeast, including Sydney and Melbourne, has a temperate climate with rain distributed through the year, while Adelaide and Perth experience a Mediterranean pattern of wet winters and hot, dry summers. Tasmania is cooler and wetter, and snow falls regularly on the Australian Alps, which receive more precipitation than any other part of the mainland.

Variability

Australian rainfall is famously variable from year to year, driven chiefly by the El Niño–Southern Oscillation: El Niño events typically bring drought to eastern Australia, while La Niña brings heavy rain and flooding. Other drivers include the Indian Ocean Dipole and the Southern Annular Mode. Prolonged droughts, such as the Federation Drought and the Millennium Drought, have had major economic and social effects, and hot, dry conditions combine with eucalypt vegetation to produce severe bushfire seasons, most catastrophically on Black Saturday in 2009 and during the 2019–20 "Black Summer" fires.

Climate change

Australia's land area has warmed by roughly 1.4 °C since national records began in 1910, with increases in the frequency of extreme heat events and, in the southwest and southeast, declining cool-season rainfall. Warming of surrounding oceans has driven repeated mass bleaching of the Great Barrier Reef.
