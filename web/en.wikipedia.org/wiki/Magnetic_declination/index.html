Magnetic declination

Magnetic declination (also called magnetic variation) is the angle between magnetic north — the direction a compass needle points — and true north at a given location on Earth. By convention, declination is positive when magnetic north lies east of true north, and negative when it lies to the west.

Variation

Declination varies both from place to place and over time, because Earth's magnetic field is generated by movement in its liquid outer core and changes gradually (secular variation). Lines of equal declination are called isogonic lines; the line of zero declination is the agonic line. In parts of North America declination can exceed 20° east or west, while in much of central Europe it is near zero and slowly increasing eastward.

Navigation

Because compasses point to magnetic north, navigators correct charts and headings using the local declination, traditionally printed on nautical charts with its annual rate of change. Aircraft runways are numbered by magnetic heading, so runway numbers occasionally change as the field drifts. Modern models such as the World Magnetic Model, updated every five years by NOAA and the British Geological Survey, provide declination values used in navigation, surveying and smartphone compasses.

History

Chinese scholars understood the phenomenon by the 11th century, and European navigators measured declination from the 15th century onward; its variation was once hoped to provide a method of determining longitude at sea.
