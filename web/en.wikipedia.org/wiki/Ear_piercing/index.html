Ear piercing is the practice of creating a hole in the ear, most commonly the earlobe, through which jewellery such as earrings can be worn. It is one of the oldest and most widespread forms of body modification, documented in cultures across the world for thousands of years. The ice mummy Ötzi, dating to around 3300 BCE, had pierced ears, and ear ornaments appear in ancient Egyptian, Greek, Roman and South Asian art.

Types

The standard lobe piercing passes through the soft earlobe. Cartilage piercings include the helix (outer rim), forward helix, tragus and antitragus, conch, rook, daith and snug, while the "industrial" piercing connects two points of the upper cartilage with a single straight barbell. Stretching (gauging) gradually enlarges a piercing to accommodate plugs or tunnels. Multiple and asymmetric ear piercings became fashionable in Western youth culture from the late twentieth century.

Procedure and aftercare

Professional piercers use a hollow needle, considered cleaner and more precise than the spring-loaded piercing guns used in many retail settings. Initial jewellery is usually made of implant-grade titanium, niobium or surgical steel to reduce allergic reactions, nickel being a common sensitiser. Aftercare involves cleaning with sterile saline and avoiding unnecessary touching; lobes typically heal in six to eight weeks, cartilage considerably longer.

Health risks

Complications include bacterial infection, allergic contact dermatitis, keloid scarring, tearing of the lobe from heavy jewellery, and — particularly in cartilage piercings — perichondritis, an infection of the cartilage covering that can deform the ear if untreated. Unsterile equipment carries a risk of blood-borne infections such as hepatitis.

Cultural significance

Ear piercing has marked identity, status, beauty and rites of passage in many societies. In Hindu tradition the karnavedha ceremony pierces a child's ears; in parts of South and Southeast Asia, infants' ears are pierced within days of birth. In the modern West, piercing both ears became common for women in the twentieth century and later spread widely among men as well.
