Downhill folding

Downhill folding
FieldBiophysics, protein folding
ContrastTwo-state folding
Key featureNo significant free-energy barrier to folding

Downhill folding is a model of protein folding in which a protein reaches its native structure without crossing a significant free-energy barrier, descending a smooth, funnel-shaped energy landscape in a gradual, continuous process. It contrasts with the classical two-state model, in which a protein jumps abruptly between unfolded and folded states over a substantial energy barrier, with transition kinetics resembling a chemical reaction between two species.

Background

In the energy-landscape picture popularised in the 1990s, a folding protein explores a funnel of conformations biased toward the native state. For most small proteins the funnel is interrupted by a barrier, giving cooperative two-state behaviour: the molecule is either essentially unfolded or essentially folded, and intermediate states are scarcely populated. In the downhill scenario, the barrier shrinks to roughly the thermal energy (kBT) or vanishes altogether, so the protein population shifts gradually and continuously toward native-like conformations as conditions change.

Evidence and candidates

The BBL protein domain, a small bacterial protein, became the best-known candidate for global downhill folding after experiments in the 2000s showed its folding to be non-cooperative, with probes reporting gradual rather than abrupt changes. The interpretation was debated, with critics arguing that apparent downhill behaviour can arise from other effects; subsequent work has refined criteria for identifying true barrierless folding. Ultra-fast-folding proteins such as the villin headpiece subdomain, and engineered variants of the lambda repressor, fold on microsecond timescales close to the speed limit for protein folding.

Significance

Downhill folding matters because it tests the limits of folding theory: if there is no barrier, there is no single rate-limiting transition state, and folding must be described as diffusion over a landscape rather than as a simple two-state reaction. It also informs the study of intrinsically disordered proteins, whose shallow landscapes resemble the downhill regime.
