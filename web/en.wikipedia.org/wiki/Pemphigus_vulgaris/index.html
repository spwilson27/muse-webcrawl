In pemphigus vulgaris, autoantibodies split the epidermis just above the basal layer, forming fragile blisters.

Pemphigus vulgaris is a rare, chronic autoimmune disease that causes painful blistering of the skin and mucous membranes. It is the most common form of pemphigus. It occurs when the immune system produces antibodies against desmogleins—proteins that hold skin cells (keratinocytes) together in desmosomes—causing the cells to separate (acantholysis) and blisters to form within the epidermis.

Signs and symptoms

The disease usually begins with painful erosions in the mouth, which may precede skin lesions by months. Skin blisters are flaccid and rupture easily, leaving raw, slow-healing erosions prone to infection. Gentle pressure can extend a blister or shear off the skin (Nikolsky's sign). Before modern treatment, pemphigus vulgaris was often fatal through infection and fluid loss.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by skin biopsy showing intraepidermal acantholysis and by direct immunofluorescence or ELISA detecting anti-desmoglein antibodies. Long-term corticosteroids transformed survival; today rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, combined with a shorter steroid course, is a first-line therapy in many guidelines, with steroid-sparing immunosuppressants such as azathioprine or mycophenolate as adjuncts.
