General anaesthesia

General anaesthesia (UK) or general anesthesia (US) is a medically induced loss of consciousness accompanied by loss of protective reflexes, resulting from the administration of one or more general anaesthetic agents. It aims to produce a reversible state of unconsciousness, amnesia, analgesia, and akinesia (lack of movement) so that surgical procedures can be performed without pain, distress, or recall for the patient. It can be contrasted with sedation and regional anaesthesia, in which the patient remains conscious.

History

Modern general anaesthesia began with the public demonstration of ether anaesthesia by William T. G. Morton in Boston in 1846, followed by James Young Simpson's use of chloroform in 1847. Nitrous oxide, described earlier by Humphry Davy, also came into use. Intravenous agents followed in the twentieth century, including thiopental (1934), muscle relaxants such as curare (1942), and propofol (introduced clinically in the 1980s).

Technique

A typical general anaesthetic proceeds through pre-operative assessment, induction (commonly intravenous propofol, or inhalational sevoflurane in children), securing the airway with an endotracheal tube or supraglottic airway, maintenance with volatile agents or total intravenous anaesthesia, and emergence and recovery. Neuromuscular blocking agents provide muscle relaxation and are reversed at the end of surgery. Throughout, the anaesthetist monitors ventilation, oxygenation, circulation, temperature, and depth of anaesthesia.

Risks and side effects

Common side effects include nausea and vomiting, sore throat, shivering, and transient confusion. Serious complications are rare in healthy patients but include malignant hyperthermia, anaphylaxis, aspiration, and anaesthesia awareness (estimated at roughly 1–2 per 1,000 general anaesthetics). Risk is stratified pre-operatively using the ASA physical status classification.
