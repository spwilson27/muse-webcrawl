Sophistry

Sophistry is the use of fallacious arguments, especially arguments that sound plausible but are deliberately deceptive or logically unsound, typically with the intention of deceiving or manipulating others. The term derives from the Sophists (Greek: σοφισταί, sophistai), a category of itinerant teachers in ancient Greek culture who, from the second half of the 5th century BC, taught excellence (aretē) and the skills of rhetoric and argument for fees — particularly in Athens, where public speaking was a path to political power.

The historical Sophists

Notable Sophists include Protagoras of Abdera, famous for the dictum that "man is the measure of all things"; Gorgias of Leontini, author of the paradoxical Encomium of Helen and On Non-Existence; Prodicus of Ceos; Hippias of Elis; Thrasymachus; and Antiphon. They taught rhetoric, grammar, politics, ethics and the art of argument, and are associated with relativism about truth and morality — the claim that moral and political truths are conventions rather than absolutes.

Plato and Aristotle's critique

Plato, through the character of Socrates, portrayed the Sophists as mercenary purveyors of deceptive rhetoric who made "the weaker argument appear the stronger" and cared for persuasion rather than truth. Aristotle, in his Sophistical Refutations, catalogued the logical fallacies used in eristic argument. These critiques fixed the pejorative meaning of "sophism" and "sophistry" in later philosophy.

Modern reassessment

Since the work of scholars such as G. B. Kerferd and W. K. C. Guthrie, the Sophists have been reassessed as serious thinkers — the first Greek humanists — who contributed to linguistics, ethics, political theory and the theory of knowledge, and whose relativism anticipated later debates in philosophy.
