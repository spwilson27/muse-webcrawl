Affirming the consequent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For the legal concept, see Affinity (law); for the linguistic term, see Affix — both unrelated.


Form of the fallacy (placeholder image).

Affirming the consequent, sometimes called the converse error, is a formal fallacy of inferring the converse from the original statement. It takes the form:

If P, then Q. Q is true. Therefore P is true.

The argument is invalid because the truth of the consequent Q does not guarantee the truth of the antecedent P: other causes may also produce Q. For example: "If it rained, the grass is wet. The grass is wet. Therefore it rained" — the grass might instead have been watered by a sprinkler.

Form and counterexamples

In propositional logic the schema is ((P → Q) ∧ Q) → P, which is not a tautology. Affirming the consequent is related to, but distinct from, the valid inference modus tollens (denying the consequent) and from denying the antecedent, its sister fallacy.

Use and near-valid reasoning

Reasoning from an observed outcome to a hypothesised cause resembles abduction — inference to the best explanation — which is legitimate as a heuristic but is not deductively valid: a confirmed prediction raises confidence in a hypothesis without proving it, since rival hypotheses may predict the same result. The fallacy appears frequently in everyday argument, pseudoscience and flawed diagnostic reasoning.
