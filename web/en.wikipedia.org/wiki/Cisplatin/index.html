Cisplatin is a chemotherapy drug used to treat a number of cancers, including testicular, ovarian, bladder, lung and stomach cancers. It is a platinum coordination complex, cis-diamminedichloridoplatinum(II), given by intravenous infusion. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism of action

After entering the cell, cisplatin loses its chloride ligands and binds to DNA, forming intra-strand crosslinks, mainly between adjacent guanine bases. These adducts distort the double helix, block DNA replication and transcription, and ultimately trigger apoptosis. Cancer cells with defective DNA repair are particularly sensitive to this damage.

History

The compound was first described by Michele Peyrone in 1845 and is sometimes called Peyrone's salt. Its biological activity was recognised in the 1960s by Barnett Rosenberg, who noticed that electrolysis products from platinum electrodes inhibited bacterial division. Cisplatin was approved for medical use in the United States in 1978 and dramatically improved cure rates for testicular cancer.

Side effects

Important adverse effects include kidney damage (nephrotoxicity), severe nausea and vomiting, hearing loss (ototoxicity), peripheral neuropathy and electrolyte disturbances. Aggressive hydration and anti-emetic regimens are used to reduce toxicity.
