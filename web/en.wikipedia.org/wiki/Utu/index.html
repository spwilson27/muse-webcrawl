Utu (Sumerian), known in Akkadian as Shamash, is the ancient Mesopotamian god of the sun, and by extension of justice, truth and moral order: because the sun sees everything that happens by day, nothing can be hidden from him, and at night he was believed to judge the dead as he passed through the underworld. He is the son of the moon god Nanna and the goddess Ningal, and the twin brother of Inanna (Ishtar). His main temples were the Ebabbar ("Shining House") at Larsa in the south and at Sippar in the north.

Myths and functions

Every morning Utu rises from behind the twin-peaked Mount Mashu in the east, cutting his way out with a great saw — depicted on seals as a rayed, serrated weapon — and crosses the sky in his chariot, attended by his vizier Bunene; at dusk he descends in the west to spend the night judging the dead and decreeing the fates of ghosts. In myth he is the great helper of heroes and the wronged: he opens the earth so that Dumuzi can flee his pursuers, gives Etana guidance, and in the Epic of Gilgamesh he favours Gilgamesh and Enkidu, binding the winds against the ogre Humbaba and answering Gilgamesh's prayers before the Cedar Forest expedition. The dying also invoke him, as the being who travels the road between the worlds.

God of justice and law

Shamash is the divine judge par excellence: hymns call him the one who "sees the wrongdoer" and protects the widow, the orphan and the honest merchant against the corrupt judge and the false witness. Oaths were sworn before him, contracts concluded in his temple, and extispicy (divination from entrails) addressed its questions to Shamash and Adad as "lords of divination." His association with lawgiving is immortalised on the stele of the Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BC), whose relief shows the Babylonian king standing before the enthroned, rayed Shamash and receiving the rod and ring of authority — a scene reproduced on countless textbooks. The great Akkadian Hymn to Shamash, nearly 400 lines long, is one of the masterpieces of Babylonian religious poetry.

Iconography

Utu/Shamash is shown with rays or flames rising from his shoulders, holding his saw-weapon or the rod-and-ring, one foot set on a mountain as he rises between its peaks while two minor gods open the gates of heaven. The solar disc — a circle with a four-pointed star and wavy rays — served as his emblem on boundary stones and seals for two millennia.
