Jayavarman VII (c. 1125 – c. 1218) was king of the Khmer Empire from 1181 until about 1218. Generally regarded as the greatest of the Khmer kings, he reversed the empire's humiliation by the kingdom of Champa, made Mahayana Buddhism the state religion, and carried out the most prodigious building programme in Cambodian history, including the new capital Angkor Thom and the great temple of the Bayon.

Accession and war with Champa

In 1177 a Cham fleet sailed up the Mekong and Tonlé Sap and sacked the Khmer capital Yasodharapura. Jayavarman, a prince then in his fifties or sixties, rallied the Khmer, defeated the Chams in battles on land and on the Tonlé Sap lake, and was crowned in 1181. He later invaded Champa itself in 1190 and for a time made it a Khmer dependency.

Building programme

Jayavarman's constructions transformed Angkor. He walled the city of Angkor Thom, raised the Bayon with its towers of giant serene faces — usually identified with the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara, perhaps bearing the king's own features — and built the great monastery-temples of Ta Prohm (dedicated to his mother) and Preah Khan (dedicated to his father), as well as Banteay Kdei, Ta Som and Neak Pean. Inscriptions boast of public works: 102 hospitals, resthouses along the roads, and hundreds of reservoirs across the empire.

Beliefs and legacy

A devout Mahayana Buddhist influenced by his queens Jayarajadevi and her sister Indradevi, Jayavarman presented kingship as compassionate service to his suffering people. Later tradition in Cambodia venerates him as the model Buddhist monarch. After his death there was a reaction against Buddhism under his successors, and some of his Buddhist imagery was defaced or converted.
