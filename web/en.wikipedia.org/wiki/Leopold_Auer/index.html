Leopold von Auer (Hungarian: Auer Lipót; 7 June 1845 – 15 July 1930) was a Hungarian violinist, academic, conductor, and composer. Renowned in his own right as a soloist, he is best remembered as one of the greatest violin teachers in history: his class at the St. Petersburg Conservatory produced a generation of virtuosi that defined twentieth-century violin playing.

Early life and career

Auer was born in Veszprém, in western Hungary, and showed talent early, studying at the Budapest Conservatory and then in Vienna with Jakob Dont and with Joseph Joachim in Hanover. After concert tours and posts as concertmaster in Düsseldorf and Hamburg, he was invited in 1868 to succeed Henryk Wieniawski as professor of violin at the Imperial Conservatory in St. Petersburg, a chair he held for forty-nine years.

In Russia, Auer became a central figure in musical life: he was violinist to the Tsar, director of the Imperial Russian Musical Society's concerts, and a conductor of the court orchestra. Though his own playing was admired for its elegance and musicianship rather than raw pyrotechnics, he premiered or championed many works, and Tchaikovsky dedicated works in his orbit — the Violin Concerto was originally intended for Auer, who declined it as unplayable, a judgment he later reversed by teaching a slightly revised version to his students.

Teaching

Auer's pupils include Jascha Heifetz, Mischa Elman, Nathan Milstein, Efrem Zimbalist, Miron Polyakin, and Kathleen Parlow — a roster unmatched by any other teacher. His method emphasized tone production, musical intelligence, and individual adaptation rather than rigid system; he set out his ideas in Violin Playing as I Teach It (1921) and in a graded course of violin literature. He also edited numerous concertos and wrote cadenzas, among them for the Beethoven and Brahms violin concertos.

Later life

Auer left Russia after the Revolution, settling in the United States in 1918, where he taught at the Institute of Musical Art in New York (later part of Juilliard) and at the Curtis Institute of Music in Philadelphia. He became an American citizen in 1926 and died in 1930 while visiting Dresden. He played a 1690 Stradivari known as the "Auer" among other fine instruments over his career.
