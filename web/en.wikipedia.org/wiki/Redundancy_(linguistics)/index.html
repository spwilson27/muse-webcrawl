Redundancy (linguistics)

In linguistics, redundancy is information that is expressed more than once. It is a feature of natural language at every level — phonology, morphology, syntax and semantics — and is generally thought to help comprehension by making messages robust against noise and error.

Agreement features such as number can be marked redundantly across a sentence. [Image placeholder]

Examples

In English, agreement can mark the same information in several places: in "the three cats are sleeping", plurality is signalled by the numeral, the plural suffix on cats, and the plural form of the verb. In many languages case, gender, and number are similarly repeated across determiners, adjectives, and nouns. Semantically redundant constructions include phrases such as "free gift" or "past history", where one element repeats information already contained in another.

Function

Redundancy in language parallels redundancy in information theory: Shannon estimated English text to be substantially redundant, which is why readers can reconstruct words with missing letters. Linguists argue that redundancy facilitates language acquisition, speeds processing, and provides error-checking in spoken communication, where signals are transient and noisy.

Stylistic views

Style guides frequently advise trimming redundancy in formal writing — tautologies such as "advance planning" or "ATM machine" — though linguists note that much supposedly redundant marking is grammatically obligatory rather than decorative.
