Revolving door (politics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The revolving door is a situation in which personnel move between roles as legislators or regulators and roles as lobbyists or employees of the industries affected by the legislation and regulation. The practice is legal and common — former officials are valuable for their expertise and contacts — but is criticised as a channel of capture and soft corruption: officials may favour future employers while in office, and agencies may lose independence when staff anticipate industry careers.

Jurisdictions respond with cooling-off rules: US federal law bars former officials from lobbying their agencies for one to two years depending on seniority (18 U.S.C. § 207), with lifetime bans on particular matters; the UK vets post-office jobs through the Advisory Committee on Business Appointments (ACOBA, advisory only — its weakness regularly criticised after cases such as a former prime minister lobbying for Greensill in 2021); the EU imposes cooling-off periods on former commissioners, whose move to a bank chairmanship in 2016 provoked reform pledges. Japan's amakudari (“descent from heaven”) names the analogous practice of bureaucrats retiring into firms they regulated. Japan and France (pantouflage) show the phenomenon is structural in elite labour markets, not a party-political one.
