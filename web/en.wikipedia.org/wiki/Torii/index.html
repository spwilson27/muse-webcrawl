Torii

A torii (Japanese: 鳥居) is a traditional Japanese gate most commonly found at the entrance of a Shinto shrine, where it symbolically marks the transition from the mundane to the sacred.

Structure

A torii consists of two upright pillars (hashira) topped by a horizontal lintel (kasagi) and a second, slightly lower crossbeam (shimaki/nuki). Gates are traditionally made from wood — often painted vermilion — or from stone, concrete, or metal. Styles such as shinmei (straight lintels) and myōjin (curved lintels) vary by shrine.

Symbolism and use

Passing under a torii signifies entering ground inhabited by kami. The most famous examples include the great gate of Itsukushima Shrine, which appears to float at high tide, and the thousands of vermilion gates forming tunnels at Fushimi Inari-taisha in Kyoto, many donated by businesses and individuals as votive offerings.

Origins

The origin of the torii is debated: hypotheses link it to the Indian torana, the Chinese paifang, or indigenous Japanese ritual rope-bound posts (himorogi).
