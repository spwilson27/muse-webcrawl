Beatus manuscripts

The Beatus manuscripts are a group of illuminated manuscripts, mostly produced in Christian monasteries of northern Spain between the 10th and 13th centuries, containing the Commentary on the Apocalypse written around 776 by the monk Beatus of Liébana. Their vivid, stylised miniatures — depicting the visions of the Book of Revelation in bold flat colours blending late-antique, Visigothic, Carolingian and Islamic influences — make them among the most celebrated works of medieval Spanish art. About 34 manuscripts survive in whole or in part, 27 of them illuminated.

Contents

Following Beatus's compilation, each manuscript presents the Apocalypse text interleaved with patristic commentary, prefaces attributed to Jerome, and a famous world map (the "Beatus map") showing the world as the ancients knew it, with an additional landmass across the ocean. The illustration cycle — angels, beasts, the Whore of Babylon, the New Jerusalem — follows a shared pictorial tradition traceable to late-antique models, rendered by Mozarabic and Romanesque artists in an arresting, non-naturalistic style.

Notable examples

The earliest surviving illuminated copy is the Morgan Beatus (c. 940–945, Morgan Library, New York), painted by the monk Magius. The Girona Beatus (975) is unusual for having a named female illuminator, Ende. The Saint-Sever Beatus (c. 1050) is the only one made north of the Pyrenees, in Gascony; the Silos Apocalypse (1091–1109, British Library) is among the last great examples in the Romanesque style.
