Overview

Celestial navigation is the art of finding position by observing celestial bodies. A sextant measures a body's altitude above the horizon; with the exact time from a chronometer and tables in a nautical almanac, the navigator computes the circle on Earth's surface from which the body would appear at that altitude. One observation gives a position line; two or more, from different bodies or from the Sun at different hours, cross to give a fix.

The simplest of all sights is the meridian altitude: the Sun's height at local noon, which converts almost directly into latitude — the observation sailors had trusted for centuries before longitude became findable.

Decline and survival

Radio and then satellite navigation eclipsed celestial methods in the later twentieth century, and navies briefly stopped teaching them. They survive — and have been deliberately revived in several navies — because they need no signals from shore or space and cannot be jammed or spoofed: the sky is the one positioning system no adversary controls.
