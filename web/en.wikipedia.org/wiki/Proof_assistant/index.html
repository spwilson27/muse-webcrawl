Proof assistant

In computer science and mathematical logic, a proof assistant or interactive theorem prover is a software tool to assist with the development of formal proofs by human–machine collaboration. This involves some sort of interactive proof editor, or other interface, with which a human can guide the search for proofs, the details of which are stored in, and some steps provided by, a computer. The machine-checked proofs produced are generally regarded as far more reliable than even carefully refereed human proofs, at the cost of substantially greater effort.

Major systems

Well-known proof assistants include Coq (based on the calculus of inductive constructions), Isabelle (supporting higher-order logic and other logics), Lean (whose mathematical library, mathlib, has formalized large parts of undergraduate and research mathematics), HOL Light, Agda, and the Mizar system. They typically consist of a small trusted kernel that checks proof terms, surrounded by tactics and automation that help users construct them, illustrating the de Bruijn criterion that a prover should produce independently checkable evidence.

Notable formalizations

Landmark achievements include the machine-checked proof of the Four Color Theorem (Coq, 2005), the Feit–Thompson odd order theorem (Coq, 2012), the formal verification of the seL4 microkernel (Isabelle), the CompCert verified C compiler (Coq), and the Liquid Tensor Experiment, in which Lean was used to verify a key result in condensed mathematics proposed as a challenge by Peter Scholze.

Relation to automated theorem proving

Proof assistants differ from fully automated theorem provers (such as SAT and SMT solvers) in that the human directs the overall proof strategy; increasingly, however, assistants incorporate automation (hammers, decision procedures) that discharge routine goals. The Curry–Howard correspondence, linking proofs to programs, makes proof assistants simultaneously programming languages for verified software.
