Siegel zero

In mathematics, a Siegel zero (or Landau–Siegel zero) is a possible counterexample to the generalised Riemann hypothesis: a real zero of a Dirichlet L-function very close to s = 1. Siegel zeros are named after Carl Ludwig Siegel, who showed in 1935 that at most one such exceptional zero can exist for a given modulus.

Background

For a Dirichlet character χ of modulus q, the L-function L(s, χ) is known to have no zeros in a region near s = 1, except possibly for a single real zero β with 1 − c/log q < β < 1, where c is an effectively computable constant. Siegel proved a bound β < 1 − c(ε) q^(−ε), but with an ineffective constant, a result known as Siegel's theorem.

Consequences

The hypothetical existence of Siegel zeros has deep consequences: it would imply strong results in some directions (for example on the smallest prime in an arithmetic progression) while obstructing others. Results such as Linnik's theorem on the least prime in an arithmetic progression are proved by ruling out or controlling Siegel zeros.
