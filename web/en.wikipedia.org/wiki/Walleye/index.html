Walleye

The walleye (Sander vitreus, synonym Stizostedion vitreum) is a freshwater perciform fish native to Canada and the northern United States. It is a North American relative of the European zander, also known in Canada as the pickerel, yellow pickerel or dory. The walleye is named for its pearlescent, opaque eyes, caused by a reflective layer (the tapetum lucidum) that helps it see in low light.

Description

Walleyes are olive to gold-coloured, with dark mottling on the back shading to a white belly. The lower lobe of the tail fin has a distinctive white tip. Adults average 38–80 cm in length and 0.5–3 kg in weight, though exceptional fish exceed 10 kg. Their large, glassy eyes are adapted for feeding in dim water at dawn, dusk and night.

Distribution and habitat

The native range extends from the Mackenzie River basin and Hudson Bay drainages across Canada and the Great Lakes to the Mississippi basin in the northern United States. Walleyes prefer large, shallow, turbid lakes and slow rivers with rocky shoals. They have been widely stocked beyond their native range, sometimes harming native fish communities.

Life history

In spring, when water reaches about 6–11 °C, adults migrate to rocky reefs and gravel bars in lakes or upstream in tributaries to spawn, scattering eggs that receive no parental care. Young feed first on zooplankton and insects, then become piscivorous. Males mature at 2–4 years, females at 3–6 years; walleyes can live more than 20 years in northern waters.

Fishing

The walleye is one of North America's most prized sport and food fish, famed for its firm, white, mild flesh; it is the state fish of Minnesota, South Dakota and Vermont, and of national importance in Canada, where a commercial fishery operates on Lake Erie and other waters. Anglers typically fish with jigs and live bait or trolled crankbaits, often at night. The closely related sauger and their hybrid, the saugeye, are similar but smaller.
