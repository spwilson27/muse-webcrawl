Synonymy

Synonymy is the semantic relation between two or more words or expressions that have the same or very similar meanings. Words that are synonyms are said to be synonymous, and the state of being a synonym is called synonymy. The term derives from the Greek synonymia, meaning "a sameness of name".

Definition and degrees

Synonyms are words that can, in at least one context, be substituted for each other without significantly changing the truth conditions of a sentence. Absolute or perfect synonyms — words interchangeable in every context — are extremely rare; most synonyms differ in connotation, register, collocation, or shades of meaning. For example, begin and commence share a core meaning but differ in formality.

Linguists distinguish several types of synonymy:

  • Conceptual synonymy — words denote the same concept but differ stylistically (e.g., die, pass away, kick the bucket).
  • Near-synonymy (plesionymy) — words with overlapping but not identical meanings (e.g., mist and fog).
  • Contextual synonymy — words that are interchangeable only in certain contexts.

Synonymy in semantics

In lexical semantics, synonymy is one of several sense relations, alongside antonymy, hyponymy, and meronymy. Synonymy plays an important role in the theory of paraphrase: two sentences are paraphrases of each other partly when they use synonymous expressions. The philosopher Willard Van Orman Quine treated synonymy with suspicion, arguing in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) that no satisfactory criterion of synonymy could be given without circularity.

Uses

Synonym dictionaries, or thesauri, catalogue words by synonymy; the best-known English example is Roget's Thesaurus (1852). Writers use synonyms to avoid repetition and to fine-tune tone, though careless substitution can produce awkward "elegant variation". In natural language processing, synonymy is exploited by systems such as WordNet, which groups English words into sets of synonyms called synsets.
