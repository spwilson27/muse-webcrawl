The Tupolev Tu-4 (NATO reporting name: Bull) is a Soviet strategic bomber that served with the Soviet Air Force from the late 1940s to the mid-1960s. It was a reverse-engineered copy of the American Boeing B-29 Superfortress, built after three B-29s made emergency landings in Soviet territory in 1944 and were interned by the USSR.

Development

Joseph Stalin ordered Andrei Tupolev to produce an exact copy of the B-29 rather than develop an indigenous design. Soviet engineers meticulously reproduced every detail of the interned aircraft, in some cases even replicating defects and battle-damage patches. Because Soviet sheet metal was produced in metric thicknesses, panels were slightly thicker than the originals, making the Tu-4 heavier and somewhat slower than the B-29. The aircraft first flew on 19 May 1947 and was publicly revealed during the Tushino air parade in August 1947, stunning Western observers.

Design

The Tu-4 was a mid-wing, four-engined monoplane powered by Shvetsov ASh-73TK radial engines of 2,400 hp each, themselves derived from the Wright R-3350. Unlike the B-29's .50-calibre machine guns, the Soviet version carried 23 mm Nudelman-Suranov cannon in remotely controlled turrets. The bomber had a maximum speed of about 558 km/h (347 mph), a range of some 5,400 km, and could carry up to 8,000 kg of bombs.

Operational history

A total of 847 Tu-4s were built. The type gave the Soviet Union its first true intercontinental strike capability and, equipped with the RDS-1 copy of the Fat Man design, became the USSR's first nuclear delivery aircraft in the early 1950s. Variants included the Tu-4K missile carrier, the Tu-4D transport, and long-range reconnaissance versions. China received Tu-4s in the 1950s, re-engined some with turboprops as airborne early warning aircraft (the KJ-1), and kept the type in service until 1988. The Tu-4's airframe was further developed into the Tu-70 airliner, Tu-75 transport and the enlarged Tu-80 and Tu-85 bombers.
