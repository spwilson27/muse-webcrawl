Differential geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A curved surface with tangent planes and a geodesic curve]

Differential geometry is a mathematical discipline that studies the geometry of smooth shapes and spaces — curves and surfaces, and their higher-dimensional generalisations (manifolds) — using the techniques of differential and integral calculus and linear algebra.

Gauss's theory of surfaces (1827) discovered intrinsic curvature: a surface's geometry (its Gaussian curvature) can be measured entirely within the surface. Riemann's 1854 generalisation to manifolds of any dimension, with curvature as a tensor, gave the language Einstein needed for general relativity (1915), where gravity is spacetime curvature.

Concepts

Key ideas include tangent spaces, metrics (Riemannian geometry), geodesics (straightest/shortest paths), connections and parallel transport, and curvature tensors. Theorems such as Gauss–Bonnet connect local curvature to global topology.

Modern differential geometry interacts with topology (geometric analysis, Ricci flow — used in Perelman's 2003 proof of the Poincaré conjecture), physics (gauge theory, general relativity), and computer graphics and robotics (surface processing, motion).
