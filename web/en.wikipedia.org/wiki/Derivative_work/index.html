Derivative work

A derivative work is a work based on or derived from one or more pre-existing works, incorporating protected expression from the original in a new form. Classic examples include translations, film adaptations of novels, musical arrangements, dramatisations, abridgements, sequels using protected characters, and annotated editions. In copyright law, the right to prepare derivative works is one of the exclusive rights of the copyright owner; an unauthorised derivative work may infringe even though the new material in it is itself original.

Legal definition

Under the US Copyright Act, a derivative work is one "based upon one or more preexisting works" that recasts, transforms or adapts the original, including editorial revisions and elaborations that as a whole represent original authorship. The Berne Convention likewise grants authors the exclusive right to authorise translations and adaptations. Ideas, facts and stock elements are not protected, so a work drawing only on those elements of a predecessor is not derivative in the legal sense.

Copyright in derivative works

A lawful derivative work attracts its own copyright, but only in the new material contributed by its author; the copyright in the underlying work is unaffected, and making the derivative does not extend any right in the original. If the derivative was unauthorised, the creator may be denied protection for the parts that unlawfully use the original (US law, section 103(a)), though courts have differed on how much protection, if any, remains.

Fair use and transformation

Some derivative uses are privileged as fair use (US) or fair dealing (other common-law systems). Parody, criticism and transformative reworkings feature prominently in the case law: Campbell v. Acuff-Rose (1994) treated a commercial parody rap version of a song as potentially fair; Suntrust v. Houghton Mifflin allowed The Wind Done Gone, a retelling of Gone with the Wind from an enslaved character's perspective; while Salinger v. Colting enjoined an unauthorised sequel to The Catcher in the Rye. The Supreme Court's 2023 decision in Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith narrowed reliance on "transformativeness" where the new work shares the original's commercial purpose. In civil-law systems, the original author's moral rights may additionally limit how a work may be adapted.

Examples and disputes

Fan fiction, remixes, sample-based music and software ports all raise derivative-work questions, and licensing frameworks — from film options to open-content licences such as Creative Commons (whose ShareAlike condition expressly governs adaptations) — exist largely to authorise and regulate them in advance.
