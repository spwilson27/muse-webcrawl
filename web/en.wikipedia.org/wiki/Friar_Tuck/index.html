Friar Tuck

Friar Tuck, the jovial cleric of Robin Hood's band.

Friar Tuck is a fictional character in the legends of Robin Hood. He is depicted as a jovial friar and member of Robin's band of Merry Men, known for his love of food and ale, his considerable fighting ability, and his earthy humour. Unlike the stereotypical holy mendicant, Tuck is usually shown as a worldly, cheerful man of the Church who has left (or been expelled from) his order.

Origins

The character evolved outside the earliest Robin Hood ballads. A "Frere Tuk" appears as a character in a fragmentary play of about 1475, and Tuck features in the May Games and morris traditions of the 15th and 16th centuries. Records from the reign of Henry VIII mention a real outlaw chaplain styling himself Friar Tuck. By the 1590s Tuck was firmly part of the legend, appearing in Anthony Munday's plays about Robin Hood (as a Franciscan) and in the ballad Robin Hood and the Curtal Friar, in which Robin meets his match in a wrestling and trickery contest with the friar at Fountains Abbey, after which Tuck joins the outlaws.

Characterisation

Sir Walter Scott's Ivanhoe (1819) fixed much of the modern image by introducing the jolly "Clerk of Copmanhurst", a hermit-friar based on Tuck. In later adaptations — Howard Pyle's The Merry Adventures of Robin Hood (1883), the 1938 film with Eugene Pallette, Disney's 1973 animated film (where he is a badger), and many television versions — Tuck serves as the band's chaplain and conscience, performing marriages and burials, while remaining a formidable fighter with staff and sword and a trencherman at the outlaws' feasts.
