Acceleration

In mechanics, acceleration is the rate of change of the velocity of an object with respect to time. Acceleration is a vector quantity (it has magnitude and direction), and it is the derivative of velocity with respect to time and the second derivative of position with respect to time. The SI unit of acceleration is the metre per second squared (m/s²).

a = dv/dt = d²x/dt²

Average and instantaneous acceleration

The average acceleration over an interval is the change in velocity divided by the elapsed time, a = Δv/Δt. Instantaneous acceleration is the limit of this ratio as the interval approaches zero. Because velocity is a vector, an object can accelerate by changing its speed, its direction of motion, or both — a body in uniform circular motion experiences centripetal acceleration directed toward the center even when its speed is constant.

Newton's second law

Newton's second law of motion states that the net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration, F = ma. Thus a given force produces a larger acceleration on a smaller mass. Under Earth's gravity, freely falling objects accelerate at approximately g = 9.81 m/s² near the surface, neglecting air resistance.

Relativity and proper acceleration

In special relativity, coordinate acceleration behaves differently, but proper acceleration — the acceleration measured by an accelerometer carried with the object — remains a well-defined physical quantity. The rate of change of acceleration is called jerk; further derivatives are sometimes termed snap, crackle, and pop.
