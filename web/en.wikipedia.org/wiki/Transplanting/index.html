Transplanting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

For the medical procedure, see Organ transplantation.

In agriculture and gardening, transplanting (or replanting) is the technique of moving a plant from one location to another. Most often this takes the form of starting a plant from seed in favourable conditions indoors — in a greenhouse, cold frame or seed tray — and then replanting it in a final growing position outdoors once it has developed true leaves and conditions permit. Plants raised this way are called transplants or seedlings, and the practice is sometimes contrasted with direct seeding into the field.

Advantages

  • Longer growing season: tender crops such as tomatoes, peppers and aubergines can be started under cover weeks before the last frost and crop earlier.
  • Control: germination conditions, spacing and early growth are managed closely, wasting less seed and giving uniform stands.
  • Weed competition: a well-grown transplant starts with a size advantage over newly germinating weeds.
  • Succession and land use: beds can be replanted immediately with transplants while the previous crop finishes, intensifying production — a method central to market gardening and to rice cultivation, where seedlings are raised in nurseries and transplanted into flooded paddies.

Technique

Successful transplanting depends on minimising root disturbance and on hardening off — gradually acclimatising indoor-grown plants to outdoor light, wind and temperature over a week or more before planting out. Seedlings are usually watered well before handling, lifted with as much of the root ball intact as possible, set into the soil at the same depth they were growing (with exceptions such as tomatoes, which root along a buried stem), firmed in, and watered again. Planting in the late afternoon or on an overcast day reduces stress.

Transplant shock

Plants commonly wilt and pause in growth after transplanting because fine root hairs are damaged and water uptake temporarily fails to match loss from the leaves. This transplant shock is mitigated by careful handling, watering, shading and the use of cell trays or biodegradable pots that avoid root disturbance. Some plants — notably many tap-rooted species and cucurbits — resent disturbance and are often direct-sown instead.

Large plants and trees

Trees and shrubs are also transplanted, from nursery-grown standards planted in gardens to mature specimens moved with tree spades — machines that dig and transport a large root ball. Semi-mature tree transplanting is used in landscaping and occasionally to preserve notable trees threatened by development, though survival depends on retaining enough of the root system and on aftercare watering.
