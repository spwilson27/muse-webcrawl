Atonement in Christianity

Atonement in Christianity
Central eventCrucifixion and resurrection of Jesus
Main theoriesSubstitution, Christus Victor, moral influence, satisfaction

In Christian theology, the atonement is the reconciliation of God and humankind through the sacrificial death of Jesus Christ. Christians hold that through Christ's life, suffering, death on the cross and resurrection, human beings are forgiven their sins and restored to fellowship with God. The English word "atonement" was coined by William Tyndale to render Latin reconciliatio, literally expressing "at-one-ment."

Biblical background

The doctrine draws on Old Testament sacrificial imagery, including the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) rituals of Leviticus, the suffering servant of Isaiah 53, and the Passover lamb, as well as New Testament language of ransom, sacrifice, propitiation and reconciliation in the letters of Paul and the Epistle to the Hebrews.

Theories of the atonement

  • Ransom / Christus Victor – the patristic view: Christ's death liberates humanity from the powers of sin, death and the devil.
  • Satisfaction – Anselm of Canterbury's Cur Deus Homo (1098): Christ's death satisfies the demands of God's honour and justice.
  • Moral influence – associated with Peter Abelard: the cross moves sinners to repentance by displaying God's love.
  • Penal substitution – developed in the Reformation: Christ bears the punishment deserved by sinners in their place.
  • Governmental, scapegoat and participatory (recapitulation) models have also been proposed.

Eastern Orthodox theology emphasises the incarnation, victory over death and theosis rather than legal categories. The scope of the atonement (for all people or only the elect) has been a point of debate, notably between Arminian and Calvinist traditions.
