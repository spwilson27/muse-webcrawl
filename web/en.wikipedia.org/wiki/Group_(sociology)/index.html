Group (sociology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the social sciences, a social group is two or more humans who interact with one another, share similar characteristics, and have a collective sense of unity. A group is a basic unit of social organisation, distinct from a mere aggregate of people who happen to be in the same place, and from a social category of people who share a trait but do not interact.

Types of groups

Sociologist Charles Horton Cooley distinguished primary groups — small, long-lasting, face-to-face associations such as the family and close friends, characterised by intimate and cooperative relationships — from secondary groups, which are larger, more impersonal, and goal-oriented, such as a workplace or a university class. William Graham Sumner introduced the concepts of in-groups, to which an individual feels loyalty and belonging, and out-groups, toward which the individual may feel competition or hostility. Reference groups are groups against which individuals compare and evaluate themselves.

Group dynamics

Groups develop norms, roles, and status hierarchies that shape members' behaviour. Classic research includes Solomon Asch's conformity experiments, Muzafer Sherif's studies of norm formation and intergroup conflict, and studies of groupthink, social loafing, and group polarisation. Émile Durkheim treated society itself as resting on collective consciousness, and group cohesion remains a central variable in explaining cooperation and conflict.

Size and structure

Group properties change with size. Georg Simmel analysed the dyad (two members), which dissolves if one leaves, and the triad (three members), in which mediation and coalition become possible. As groups grow, interaction becomes less personal, formal rules and leadership structures typically emerge, and subgroups or cliques may form.
