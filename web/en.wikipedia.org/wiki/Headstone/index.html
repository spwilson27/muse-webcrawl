Headstone

A headstone, tombstone, or gravestone is a stone marker set above a grave, typically inscribed with the name of the deceased, dates, and an epitaph.

A headstone, tombstone, or gravestone is a stone marker, usually set upright at the head of a grave, commemorating the person buried there. It typically bears the deceased's name, dates of birth and death, and often an epitaph, religious symbol, or decorative carving. A matching smaller stone at the foot of the grave is called a footstone.

Materials and manufacture

Early markers were made of local slate, sandstone, or marble; from the late 19th century granite became dominant for its durability. Modern production uses diamond saws, sandblasting, and laser etching, which permits photographic portraits to be reproduced on the stone. Inscriptions may weather badly on softer stones, a problem for genealogists and historians who record monumental inscriptions.

History and symbolism

Burial markers descend from ancient stelae and Roman tombstones. In 17th- and 18th-century New England and Britain, headstones carried memento mori imagery — skulls, winged death's-heads, hourglasses — which gradually gave way to softer symbols such as cherubs, urns, willows, lambs (for children) and clasped hands. Jewish headstones (matzevot) carry Hebrew inscriptions and symbols such as the Star of David or the blessing hands of the Kohanim; Muslim graves traditionally use simple markers, sometimes with Qur'anic verses. Military cemeteries use uniform headstones, such as the white marble markers of American cemeteries and the Portland stone headstones of the Commonwealth War Graves Commission.
