Buddhist ethics

Buddhist ethics are traditionally based on what Buddhists view as the enlightened perspective of the Buddha. The term for ethics or morality used in the Pāli Canon is śīla (Pāli: sīla). Śīla is one of three sections of the Noble Eightfold Path — right speech, right action, and right livelihood — and is understood as a foundation for meditation and liberation rather than as commandments issued by a deity.

The Five Precepts

The most common ethical code for lay Buddhists is the Five Precepts (pañca-sīla): abstaining from killing living beings, from taking what is not given, from sexual misconduct, from false speech, and from intoxicants that cloud the mind. These are undertaken voluntarily as training rules.

Ten wholesome actions

Mahayana and Theravada sources alike describe ten wholesome courses of action (daśa-kuśala-karmapatha), covering body, speech, and mind, paired with ten unwholesome ones to be avoided, including greed, ill will, and wrong view.

Karma and intention

Buddhist ethics is fundamentally consequentialist and intention-centered: the Buddha declared "it is intention (cetanā) that I call karma." Actions rooted in greed, hatred, and delusion produce suffering; actions rooted in generosity, love, and wisdom lead toward awakening.

Mahayana developments

In the Mahayana, the bodhisattva ideal places compassion for all beings at the center, and the Bodhisattva Precepts — including rules against praising oneself and disparaging others — supplement the traditional codes. Concepts such as skillful means (upāya) allow flexible application of rules for compassionate ends.

Applied ethics

Modern Buddhist ethics addresses issues such as environmental protection, war and nonviolence, animal welfare and vegetarianism, bioethics, and social justice, notably through the engaged Buddhism movement.
