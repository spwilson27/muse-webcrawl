Famine in India

Victims of the Great Famine of 1876–1878 in Madras, one of the major famines of British India.

India has a long history of famines. The Indian subcontinent, heavily dependent on monsoon rains for agriculture, suffered numerous severe famines in pre-colonial, colonial, and — to a far lesser extent — post-independence periods. Major famines during British rule are generally attributed to a combination of drought, crop failure, and colonial economic and administrative policies, and they caused tens of millions of deaths.

Causes

The immediate cause of most Indian famines was failure of the monsoon rains and consequent crop failure. Economists and historians have debated the role of markets, transport, and state policy. Amartya Sen's entitlement theory argues that several famines, including Bengal in 1943, occurred amid adequate or only moderately reduced food availability, with starvation resulting from the collapse of people's ability to buy food.

Colonial famine policy

Following the famines of the 1870s, the British administration established Famine Commissions and Famine Codes that prescribed relief works, wage employment, and food distribution. Critics argue that relief was often late, inadequate, and governed by laissez-faire doctrine that discouraged interference with grain markets even during mass starvation.

After independence

Since independence in 1947, India has avoided large-scale famine mortality, a change often credited to democratic accountability, a free press, public distribution of grain, and employment programmes. Droughts such as those in Bihar (1966–67) and Maharashtra (1972) were met with large relief efforts, although chronic malnutrition remains widespread.
