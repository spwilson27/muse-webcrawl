Papal supremacy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Papal supremacy
TypeDoctrine of the Catholic Church
ClaimThe pope, by divine institution, has supreme, full, immediate and universal power over the whole Church
Key textsMatthew 16:18–19; Unam sanctam (1302); Pastor aeternus (1870)
Distinguished fromPapal primacy (of honour); papal infallibility

Papal supremacy is the doctrine of the Catholic Church that the pope, by reason of his office as Vicar of Christ and pastor of the whole Church, possesses "full, supreme, and universal power over the whole church, a power which he can always exercise unhindered" (Second Vatican Council, Lumen gentium). It goes beyond a primacy of honour: the pope's jurisdiction is held to be immediate and ordinary in every diocese, and his definitive judgments in faith and morals bind the entire Church.

Biblical and historical basis

Catholic teaching grounds supremacy in Christ's words to Peter in the Gospel of Matthew — giving him the "keys of the kingdom of heaven" and the power of binding and loosing — and in Peter's role among the apostles in Acts and John 21 ("feed my sheep"). Historically, Roman bishops intervened in other churches from an early date; Pope Leo I in the 5th century articulated a strong theory of Petrine jurisdiction, and the medieval reforming popes, especially Gregory VII (Dictatus papae, 1075), asserted sweeping claims. Boniface VIII's bull Unam sanctam (1302) gave the doctrine its most absolute medieval formulation.

Definition and ecumenical issues

The First Vatican Council's constitution Pastor aeternus (1870) dogmatically defined both papal supremacy of jurisdiction and papal infallibility; the Second Vatican Council reaffirmed supremacy while also teaching the collegiality of the bishops with the pope. Papal supremacy is rejected by Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox and Protestant churches, which variously accept only a primacy of honour for Rome or deny any universal jurisdiction; it therefore remains one of the principal obstacles in ecumenical dialogue, and was a focus of the Ravenna Document discussions between Catholics and Orthodox.
