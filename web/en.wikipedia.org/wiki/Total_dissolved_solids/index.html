Total dissolved solids

Total dissolved solids (TDS) is a measure of the combined content of all inorganic and organic substances dissolved in a liquid, expressed in milligrams per litre (mg/L) or parts per million (ppm). It is a standard indicator of water quality, encompassing minerals, salts, and ions such as calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, bicarbonates, chlorides, and sulfates.

Measurement

The reference method is gravimetric: a filtered sample is evaporated at 180 °C and the residue weighed. In practice TDS is usually estimated with a conductivity meter, since dissolved ions carry electric current; the instrument multiplies the electrical conductivity by an empirical factor (typically 0.5–0.7) to report an approximate TDS. Conductivity methods cannot detect uncharged dissolved substances such as sugars or many organics.

Significance

Fresh water generally has TDS below 1,000 mg/L; seawater averages about 35,000 mg/L. High TDS affects taste, causes scale in pipes and boilers, and can harm aquatic life and irrigation. Drinking-water guidelines commonly recommend TDS below about 500–600 mg/L for palatability, though TDS itself is a secondary (aesthetic) standard rather than a direct health measure.
