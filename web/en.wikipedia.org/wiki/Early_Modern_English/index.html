Early Modern English

Early Modern English (often abbreviated EModE) is the stage of the English language from the beginning of the Tudor period (1485) until about 1713, between Middle English and Late Modern English. It is the language of William Shakespeare, the King James Bible and John Milton's early work, and is largely intelligible to modern readers, though pronunciation, grammar and vocabulary differed in notable ways.

Historical background

The introduction of the printing press to England by William Caxton in 1476 helped standardise spelling on the Chancery Standard based on London English. The period saw the English Renaissance, an influx of Latin and Greek loanwords, and deliberate coinages during the "inkhorn" controversy over borrowed vocabulary.

Pronunciation

The Great Vowel Shift, a chain shift of the long vowels, was largely completed during this period, so that Shakespeare's contemporaries pronounced many words differently from modern speakers despite similar spellings. Rhymes and puns in early texts, together with the works of contemporary spelling reformers and phoneticians such as John Hart, allow linguists to reconstruct the sound system.

Grammar

Early Modern English retained the second-person singular pronouns thou, thee and thy, with verb forms such as hath and doth and endings in -est and -eth. Over the period, you increasingly displaced thou except in intimate, poetic or religious use, and the auxiliary do became standard in questions and negations.

Orthography

Spelling was variable but progressively standardised by printers; features such as the interchangeable use of u/v and i/j, the final silent -e, and the long s (ſ) are characteristic of printed texts of the era.
