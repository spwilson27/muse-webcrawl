Civil conscription

Civil conscription is the compulsory assignment of civilians by the state to perform labour, in contrast to military conscription, which drafts people into the armed forces. Civil conscripts may be required to work in agriculture, industry, construction, public services or emergency services. In some systems, civil service is offered as an alternative to military service for conscientious objectors; in others it is a general obligation imposed in war or peacetime.

Civil conscription directs civilian labour to tasks designated by the state.

Alternative service

In countries with military conscription, conscientious objectors are often assigned to a civilian alternative service (German: Zivildienst). Germany maintained such a system until conscription was suspended in 2011; objectors worked in hospitals, care homes and social services, typically for a period comparable to or longer than military service. Austria and Switzerland maintain similar alternative-service schemes. Finland's non-military service (siviilipalvelus) is another example.

Compulsory civil labour

During the Second World War, several states directed the labour of their own populations: Britain's "Bevin Boys" were young men selected by ballot to work in coal mines instead of military service, and the British government also directed women into essential war work. Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union used compulsory labour on a vast scale, including the forced labour of occupied peoples and prisoners, which international law now treats as a war crime when imposed on protected persons. North Korea is frequently cited as a contemporary state maintaining extensive compulsory labour mobilisation.

Legal framework

The International Labour Organization's Forced Labour Convention (No. 29) of 1930 prohibits forced or compulsory labour, with exceptions for military service, normal civic obligations, prison labour under supervision, emergencies, and minor communal services. The European Convention on Human Rights similarly excludes such categories from its ban on forced labour. International humanitarian law restricts the work that occupying powers may compel from protected civilians.
