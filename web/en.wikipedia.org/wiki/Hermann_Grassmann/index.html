Hermann Günther Grassmann (15 April 1809 – 26 September 1877) was a German polymath — a schoolteacher by profession — now recognised as one of the founders of modern linear and multilinear algebra and as a significant linguist and Sanskrit scholar. His Ausdehnungslehre ("Theory of Extension", 1844, revised 1862) introduced the exterior product and the algebra of extended quantities that now bears his name, including Grassmann numbers in physics and the Grassmannian in geometry. In his lifetime, however, his mathematics was largely ignored, and he won more contemporary recognition for his work on Indo-European linguistics, including Grassmann's law of dissimilation in Greek and Sanskrit.

Life

Grassmann was born in Stettin, where his father taught mathematics and physics at the Gymnasium. After studying theology and philosophy in Berlin, he returned to Stettin and spent nearly his whole career teaching at its Gymnasium, writing textbooks on arithmetic and languages while pursuing research privately. Repeatedly passed over for a university chair, he redirected much of his later energy to linguistics, producing a dictionary and a translation of the Rigveda and studies of German plant names and folk songs.

Mathematics

The Ausdehnungslehre presented a general calculus of quantities in n-dimensional space: vectors, their exterior (wedge) product, linear independence and subspaces — decades before linear algebra took standard form. Grassmann also anticipated ideas of vector spaces, algebras over a field and differential forms. His results entered the mathematical mainstream only late in the 19th century through the work of others, including Hankel, Peano and Élie Cartan. In physics his name survives in the anticommuting Grassmann variables used to describe fermions in quantum field theory and supersymmetry.

Other work

Grassmann formulated laws of colour mixture ("Grassmann's laws" of colourimetry, 1853), studied the theory of tides and crystals, and made contributions to electrodynamics. His linguistic "Grassmann's law" (1863), describing the dissimilation of aspirates in ancient Greek and Sanskrit, remains a standard part of Indo-European philology.
