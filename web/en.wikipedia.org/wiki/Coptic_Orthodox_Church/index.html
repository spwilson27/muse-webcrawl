The Coptic Orthodox Church is an Oriental Orthodox Christian church based in Egypt, with a large diaspora worldwide. According to its tradition it was founded by Mark the Evangelist in Alexandria in the 1st century AD. Its primate is the Pope of Alexandria, since 2012 Pope Tawadros II.

Interior of a Coptic church in Cairo.

History

The church split from the Chalcedonian churches after the Council of Chalcedon (451), rejecting the council's definition in favour of miaphysitism — the doctrine that Christ has one united nature, divine and human. Under Byzantine, then Arab, Fatimid, Mamluk, and Ottoman rule, the Copts remained the largest Christian community in Egypt, though subject to periods of persecution and gradual Arabisation. The Coptic language, the final stage of ancient Egyptian, survives principally as the church's liturgical language.

Theology and practice

The church observes seven sacraments, venerates Mary as Theotokos, and is known for long periods of fasting — over 200 days a year in total. Egyptian monasticism, founded by figures such as Anthony the Great and Pachomius, profoundly influenced worldwide Christian monastic practice. Copts form the largest Christian population in the Middle East, commonly estimated at around 10% of Egypt's population, though figures are politically contested.

Modern era

Under Pope Shenouda III (1971–2012) the church expanded globally through emigration, establishing dioceses across North America, Europe, and Australia. Egyptian Copts have faced sectarian violence in recent decades, including church bombings in 2011 and 2016–17, and attacks claimed by the Islamic State.
