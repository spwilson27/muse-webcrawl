In computer networking, a wireless access point (WAP or AP) is a networking hardware device that allows other Wi-Fi devices to connect to a wired network. The AP usually connects to a router (via a wired network) as a standalone device, but it can also be an integral component of the router itself. An AP is differentiated from a hotspot, which is the physical location where Wi-Fi access is available.

Operation

An access point broadcasts one or more network names (SSIDs), typically in the 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and increasingly 6 GHz bands under IEEE 802.11 standards (Wi-Fi 4 through Wi-Fi 7). Client devices associate with the AP, authenticate (using WPA2 or WPA3 security), and thereafter have their frames bridged onto the wired Ethernet network, where the AP behaves much like a switch port. Enterprise and campus networks deploy many APs with overlapping coverage, allowing clients to roam between cells; in large installations the APs may be centrally managed by a wireless LAN controller or a cloud service, which coordinates channel selection, transmit power, and load balancing.

Modes and related devices

Besides the standard infrastructure mode, access points can act as wireless repeaters, bridges between two wired segments, or clients of another AP. Wireless routers combine an AP with a router, DHCP server, and switch in one consumer unit. Mesh Wi-Fi systems link multiple AP-like nodes over a wireless backhaul to blanket a building. In ad hoc and Wi-Fi Direct modes, devices communicate without any AP.

Placement and security

Performance depends strongly on placement: APs are commonly ceiling-mounted away from metal obstructions and interference sources, and powered via Power over Ethernet. Security concerns include rogue access points installed without authorization, "evil twin" spoofing of legitimate SSIDs, and weak passphrase-based security; enterprise deployments therefore often use 802.1X authentication, management-frame protection, and wireless intrusion detection.
