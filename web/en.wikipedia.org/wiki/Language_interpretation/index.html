Language interpretation is the activity of facilitating spoken or signed communication between speakers of different languages, by rendering a message from a source language into a target language in real time. It is distinguished from translation, which deals with written texts and permits revision; interpretation is immediate and irreversible once delivered.

Modes

In simultaneous interpretation, the interpreter speaks almost at the same time as the source speaker, usually from a soundproof booth with headphones and a microphone; it is the standard mode at the United Nations and other international conferences, and interpreters normally work in pairs, rotating about every 30 minutes because of the intense concentration required. In consecutive interpretation, the interpreter speaks after the source speaker has finished a segment, often with the aid of a specialised note-taking system; it suits speeches, interviews and court proceedings. Whispered interpretation (chuchotage) is simultaneous interpretation whispered to one or two listeners. Signed-language interpretation applies the same principles between spoken and signed languages.

History

Interpreters have mediated diplomacy and trade since antiquity. The modern profession emerged in the early 20th century: consecutive interpretation dominated the League of Nations era, while simultaneous interpretation was pioneered at scale at the Nuremberg trials (1945–46), where it was essential for conducting proceedings in English, French, Russian and German, and was then adopted by the United Nations.

Work settings

Conference interpreters serve international organisations, summits and business meetings. Community interpreters work in hospitals, courts, police stations and schools, where accuracy can be critical to health and legal rights. Interpreters are bound by professional codes emphasising accuracy, impartiality and confidentiality. In recent years remote interpretation by video and telephone, and experiments with machine interpretation, have expanded rapidly.
