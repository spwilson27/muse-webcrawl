Gracia Mendes Nasi (1510–1569), known as Doña Gracia and in Hebrew sources simply as La Señora ("The Lady"), was one of the wealthiest women of Renaissance Europe. Born into a Portuguese converso (New Christian) family as Beatriz de Luna, she directed the international banking and trading house of Mendes–Benveniste, used her fortune to rescue persecuted Jews fleeing the Inquisition, and became a major patron of Jewish scholarship and settlement in the Ottoman Empire.

Life

Gracia married Francisco Mendes Benveniste in 1528; with his brother Diogo he ran a Lisbon bank trading in pepper, spices and precious stones, with a branch in Antwerp. Widowed in 1538, she left Portugal in 1537–1544 stages to escape tightening Inquisition scrutiny, settling in Antwerp with her daughter, sister Brianda and nephew João Micas (later Joseph Nasi). In Antwerp she co-directed the bank with Diogo until his death in 1542, resisting attempts by the authorities and by would-be suitors to control the family fortune.

When the Low Countries became unsafe, she moved through Venice (1549) — where she was denounced to the authorities as a Judaizer and briefly imprisoned before Ottoman intervention freed her — and Ferrara (1551), where she could live openly as a Jew. There she patronised Hebrew printing, most famously the Ferrara Bible (1553), a Ladino translation dedicated to her. She finally settled in Constantinople in 1553 at the invitation of Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, whose domains she helped enrich with trade and finance.

Philanthropy and the Tiberias project

From Constantinople, Doña Gracia financed synagogues, yeshivas, hospitals and printing, and organised rescue networks — ships, safe houses and bribes — that carried conversos out of Portugal and Italy to Ottoman lands. After the burning of crypto-Jews at Ancona in 1555–56 she organised a Jewish boycott of that papal port. In the 1560s, with her nephew and son-in-law Joseph Nasi (later Duke of Naxos), she obtained from the Sultan a lease on the ruined city of Tiberias in Galilee, rebuilding its walls and settling Jewish silk-workers there — an early, if short-lived, attempt at organised Jewish resettlement in the Land of Israel, a generation before modern Zionism. She died in 1569, on the eve of a planned move to Tiberias.

Legacy

Doña Gracia is the subject of numerous historical novels and of a museum in Tiberias (the Dona Gracia House). Her career is often presented as the archetype of the Sephardic "Court Jew" a century before the type flourished in northern Europe, and as a rare example of a woman directing a great banking house in the sixteenth century.
