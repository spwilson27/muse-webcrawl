Sinatra Doctrine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sinatra Doctrine was the name jokingly given by the Soviet government of Mikhail Gorbachev to its policy of allowing neighbouring Warsaw Pact states to determine their own internal affairs. The name alluded to Frank Sinatra's song "My Way" — the doctrine held that each country could do it its way — and marked the repudiation of the Brezhnev Doctrine, under which the Soviet Union had claimed the right to intervene to preserve communist rule, as it had in Czechoslovakia in 1968.

Origin

On 25 October 1989, Soviet Foreign Ministry spokesman Gennadi Gerasimov, asked in an interview about Soviet policy, explained: "We now have the Frank Sinatra doctrine. He has a song, 'I Did It My Way.' So every country decides on its own which way to take." Foreign Minister Eduard Shevardnadze had signalled the same principle in a speech the previous day, affirming freedom of choice for all states. Gorbachev himself had already renounced interference in a July 1989 address to the Council of Europe.

Consequences

The doctrine's practical effect was immediate: Moscow made clear it would not repeat the interventions of 1956 (Hungary) or 1968 (Czechoslovakia) to save unpopular communist governments. Reform and opposition movements across Eastern Europe — including Poland's Solidarity, whose electoral triumph in June 1989 even preceded the phrase — were thereby emboldened. Within weeks of Gerasimov's remark the Berlin Wall fell (9 November 1989); within two years the Warsaw Pact and the Soviet Union itself had dissolved.
