Banking (transport)

In transport engineering and vehicle dynamics, banking refers to the tilting or lateral inclination used in taking a curve. The term applies both to the deliberate leaning of a vehicle or aircraft in a turn and to the built-in tilt of the road or track itself — where it is also called superelevation or cant.

Banked roads and railways

On a banked curve the outer edge of the road or the outer rail of the track is raised relative to the inner edge. The horizontal component of the normal force then helps supply the centripetal force needed for the turn, reducing the reliance on tyre friction or flange contact and allowing higher speeds in greater comfort and safety. Highway engineers typically limit superelevation to about 4–12 percent depending on climate (since slow vehicles may slide inward on icy, steeply banked roads), while railways specify cant in millimetres of height difference between rails, with tilting trains compensating for "cant deficiency" at high speed. Steeply banked ovals — exceeding 30 degrees at some NASCAR superspeedways — allow stock cars to corner at full throttle.

Aircraft and two-wheelers

An aircraft turns by banking: the pilot rolls the wings so that part of the lift acts horizontally, curving the flight path; a coordinated turn uses rudder to counter adverse yaw. Motorcycles and bicycles bank by leaning into the turn, a manoeuvre initiated by countersteering above walking pace. In maritime usage, "banking" can also describe a vessel heeling under wind or in a turn, though the commoner terms are heel and list.
