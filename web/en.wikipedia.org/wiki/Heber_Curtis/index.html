Heber Doust Curtis (June 27, 1872 – January 9, 1942) was an American astronomer best known for his participation in the 1920 Great Debate with Harlow Shapley on the scale of the universe and the nature of spiral nebulae.

Biography

Curtis was born in Muskegon, Michigan, studied at the University of Michigan and taught school before earning a doctorate in astronomy at the University of Virginia in 1902. He joined the Lick Observatory, working in Chile and California on nebular photography and radial velocities.

The Great Debate

On April 26, 1920, at the Smithsonian Museum of Natural History, Curtis argued that spiral nebulae were independent island universes — other galaxies — outside the Milky Way, while Shapley argued they were smaller and nearby. Subsequent work by Edwin Hubble largely vindicated Curtis on the galactic nature of spirals, though Shapley was closer on the size of the Milky Way.

Career

Curtis later directed the Allegheny Observatory and then the University of Michigan Observatory, where he promoted the construction of the McMath–Hulbert Observatory and published work on nebulae with the Crossley reflector.

Legacy

The lunar crater Curtis is named in his honour. His debate helped set the stage for modern extragalactic astronomy.
