Gran Colombia

Gran Colombia (1819–1831) encompassed much of northern South America.

Gran Colombia (Spanish: Gran Colombia), officially the Republic of Colombia, was a state that encompassed much of northern South America and part of southern Central America from 1819 to 1831. It included the territories of present-day Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador and Panama, with smaller portions of what are now Guyana, Brazil and Peru.

History

The republic was created by Simón Bolívar during the Spanish American wars of independence. After the victory at the Battle of Boyacá in August 1819 secured the liberation of New Granada, the Congress of Angostura proclaimed the union of Venezuela and New Granada on 17 December 1819. The Congress of Cúcuta in 1821 adopted a constitution and made Bogotá the capital; Bolívar became president and Francisco de Paula Santander vice president. Quito (modern Ecuador) was incorporated after its liberation in 1822.

The union was always fragile. Regional loyalties, vast distances, poor communications, and personal rivalries — notably between Bolívar, who favoured a strong central government, and Santander and regional leaders such as José Antonio Páez in Venezuela — strained the state. The separatist movement known as La Cosiata (1826) signalled Venezuela's drift toward secession. Bolívar's attempt to hold the republic together, including his assumption of dictatorial powers in 1828 and the failed constitutional convention at Ocaña, could not prevent dissolution.

Dissolution

In 1830 Venezuela and Ecuador seceded, and Gran Colombia was formally dissolved in 1831. Its remaining territory became the Republic of New Granada. Bolívar, disillusioned, died near Santa Marta in December 1830.

Legacy

The flag of Gran Colombia — horizontal stripes of yellow, blue and red — is the basis of the modern flags of Colombia, Ecuador and Venezuela. The ideal of the union continues to be invoked in Latin American integrationist thought.
