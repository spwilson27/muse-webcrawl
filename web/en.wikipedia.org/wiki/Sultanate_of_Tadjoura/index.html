The Sultanate of Tadjoura was one of the traditional Afar sultanates of the Horn of Africa, centred on the ancient port town of Tadjoura on the northern shore of the Gulf of Tadjoura, in what is now Djibouti. The town, the oldest continuously inhabited settlement in Djibouti, served as a trading post linking the Afar lowlands and the Ethiopian highlands with Arabia across the Red Sea.

History

Tadjoura's sultan, drawn from the Adali (Ad'al) clan confederation of the Afar, governed the town and its surrounding territory as one of several small Afar polities along the coast, alongside the sultanates of Raheita, Gobaad and Aussa. In the 19th century the sultanate came into contact with European powers interested in the Red Sea route: Tadjoura was among the first places in the region where France sought treaties, and in 1884 the sultan signed a treaty of friendship and protection with France, helping pave the way for the colony of French Somaliland.

Under French colonial rule the sultan retained a ceremonial and customary role, and the sultanate survived in traditional form after Djibouti's independence in 1977. The sultan of Tadjoura remains a respected customary authority among the Afar people of the region today, and the "Vizier" (Wazir) of Tadjoura has historically been an influential political figure in Djiboutian affairs.
