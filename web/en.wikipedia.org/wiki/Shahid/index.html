Shahid (Arabic: شهيد, romanized: shahīd, plural شهادة shuhadāʾ) is an Arabic word meaning "witness" that, in Islamic tradition, denotes a martyr — a person who dies in the cause of God or while defending the faith. The concept is closely related to that of martyrdom in other Abrahamic religions, and the term's semantic root — "to witness, to testify" — parallels the Greek martys (μάρτυς), from which the English word "martyr" derives.

Etymology

The word derives from the Arabic triliteral root sh-h-d (شهد), meaning "to witness", "to testify" or "to be present". The same root gives shahada, the Islamic declaration of faith, and shahid in its primary sense of one who bears witness. In the Quran the word is used both in the sense of "witness" and, in later interpretation, of one killed in God's path.

In the Quran and hadith

The Quran promises great reward to those killed "in the way of God" (fī sabīl Allāh). A well-known verse (3:169) states: "Do not think of those who are killed in the way of God as dead; rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision." Collections of hadith elaborate on the martyr's rewards, including the forgiveness of sins, a place in paradise, and intercession for relatives. In Islamic eschatology the shahid is traditionally exempted from the interrogation in the grave and is buried without the customary washing of the body, in the clothes in which he died.

Categories of martyrs

Classical jurists extended the title beyond battlefield death. Hadith literature lists several kinds of martyrs, including those who die of plague, of abdominal disease, by drowning, under a collapsed building, and women who die in childbirth. Jurists distinguished between the "martyr of this world and the next" — killed fighting unbelievers — and the "martyr of the next world only", who receives the otherworldly reward but is treated ritually like other deceased Muslims.

Historical development

During the early Islamic conquests, fallen fighters were commemorated as shuhada. The title acquired particular prominence in Shia Islam, where the martyrdom of Husayn ibn Ali at the Battle of Karbala in 680 CE became a central devotional paradigm, commemorated annually at Ashura. Sunni tradition likewise honours early martyrs such as Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib, called "chief of the martyrs".

Modern usage

In the modern period the term has been applied broadly: to soldiers killed in national wars (as in Iran during the Iran–Iraq War, in which the "Foundation of Martyrs" cares for their families), to Palestinian fighters, and — controversially — to suicide attackers, a usage rejected by many mainstream scholars who hold that suicide and the killing of non-combatants are prohibited. In South Asia the word (as shaheed) is used in Urdu, Hindi and Punjabi for national heroes of all faiths, such as Bhagat Singh.

Use in other traditions

In Sikhism, the cognate shahīd denotes martyrs such as Guru Arjan and Guru Tegh Bahadur. Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews have also used the term for their martyrs, and it appears in the names of churches dedicated to martyrs in the Levant.
