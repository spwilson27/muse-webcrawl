Rooftop farming


Part of: Urban agriculture
Methods: Soil beds, containers, hydroponics, greenhouses
Notable examples: Brooklyn Grange (New York), Lufa Farms (Montreal)

Rooftop farming is the practice of cultivating food crops on the roofs of buildings. It is a branch of urban agriculture in which otherwise unused roof space is converted to growing areas using soil-based beds, containers, hydroponic systems, or rooftop greenhouses. Rooftop farms range from small community gardens to commercial operations of more than a hectare.

Benefits

Rooftop farms produce fresh food close to consumers, reducing transport emissions and improving urban food security. Vegetated roofs insulate buildings, lowering heating and cooling costs; retain stormwater, easing pressure on drainage systems; mitigate the urban heat island effect; and provide habitat for pollinators. Community rooftop gardens also supply green space, education, and employment in dense neighbourhoods.

Challenges

Buildings must bear the substantial weight of saturated soil and water, requiring structural assessment and often reinforcement. Access for materials and harvests, wind exposure, irrigation supply, waterproofing integrity, zoning rules, and insurance all complicate projects. Commercial rooftop greenhouses — such as those of Lufa Farms in Montreal, which opened the world's first commercial rooftop greenhouse in 2011 — address climate control and year-round production but require high capital investment.

Examples and policy

Brooklyn Grange in New York operates some of the largest soil-based rooftop farms in the United States. Cities including Toronto (whose 2009 Green Roof Bylaw mandates green roofs on new buildings), Paris, and Singapore have adopted incentives or requirements encouraging productive and vegetated roofs. Rooftop farming is frequently combined with beekeeping, solar panels ("agrivoltaics"), and rainwater harvesting.
