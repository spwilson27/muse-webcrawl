Shukuba

Shukuba 宿場
CountryJapan
PeriodEdo period (1603–1868)
Main routesGokaidō — Tōkaidō, Nakasendō, Kōshū Kaidō, Ōshū Kaidō, Nikkō Kaidō

Shukuba (宿場) were post stations, or post towns, during the Edo period in Japan, generally located on one of the Edo Five Routes (the Gokaidō) or one of its subsidiary routes. They were developed for travellers' needs, providing inns (hatago), porters, pack horses, and facilities for the exchange of goods and relay of official communications, and they were a compulsory staging point in the travel of daimyō processions under the sankin-kōtai system.

Facilities and society

A shukuba typically contained one or two honjin — high-status inns reserved for daimyō and officials — together with a waki-honjin and dozens of ordinary hatago for common travellers, many employing serving girls (meshimori onna) who sometimes doubled as sex workers. Each station maintained a toiyaba, the transport office that fixed rates for porters and horses, and a notice board (kōsatsu) displaying the shogunate's edicts. Well-known post towns include Shinjuku, the 53 Stations of the Tōkaidō (immortalised by Hiroshige's woodblock prints) and the 69 Stations of the Nakasendō, among them Narai-juku, Tsumago-juku, and Magome-juku.

Decline and preservation

The shukuba declined rapidly with the coming of railways in the Meiji period. Those bypassed by major roads often preserved their historic streetscapes, and towns such as Tsumago-juku became pioneers of Japan's preservation movement; several are now designated Important Preservation Districts for Groups of Traditional Buildings.
