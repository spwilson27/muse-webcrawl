Energy landscape

A potential energy surface with minima, saddle points and barriers

An energy landscape is a mapping of the possible states of a system onto their energies, used across physics, chemistry and biology to visualise and reason about how systems evolve. Formally, the landscape assigns to every possible configuration of a system a value of a quantity such as potential energy, free energy or a cost function, producing a (possibly high-dimensional) surface whose low points correspond to stable states.

Concept

The landscape picture underlies the idea of a system relaxing downhill toward minima, being trapped in metastable states, and crossing barriers (saddle points) by thermal fluctuations or tunnelling. Reaction pathways in chemistry are pictured as valleys on a potential energy surface, with transition states at the passes between them.

Applications

  • Protein folding: the folding funnel picture describes a protein's free-energy landscape sloping toward the native state, resolving Levinthal's paradox by showing that folding need not be a random search.
  • Glasses and disordered materials: pioneered by Martin Goldstein and by Stillinger and Weber, the potential-energy landscape of a supercooled liquid — a vast set of basins ("inherent structures") separated by barriers — underpins modern theories of the glass transition and slow dynamics.
  • Spin glasses and optimisation: rugged landscapes with many local minima explain the difficulty of combinatorial optimisation and motivate algorithms such as simulated annealing, which is designed to escape local minima.
  • Machine learning: loss surfaces of neural networks are analysed as energy landscapes to understand training dynamics.
  • Ecology and evolution: fitness landscapes (Sewall Wright) are the direct analogue, with energy replaced by fitness.
