Fixed and floating cash flows exchanged between two counterparties

An interest rate swap is an derivative contract in which two counterparties exchange streams of interest payments on a notional principal amount for a specified period. In the most common ("plain vanilla") form, one party pays a fixed rate of interest and receives a floating rate (such as SOFR or EURIBOR), while the other party does the reverse. The principal itself is not exchanged; only the net difference between the two interest streams is paid at each settlement date.

Uses

Interest rate swaps are used by companies, banks and governments to manage exposure to fluctuations in interest rates. A borrower with a floating-rate loan who expects rates to rise may swap into fixed payments; an investor holding fixed-rate assets may swap into floating payments. Dealers also use swaps for speculation and arbitrage. They are the most widely traded type of interest rate derivative and among the largest derivatives markets in the world, with notional amounts outstanding in the hundreds of trillions of dollars.

Valuation

A swap is typically structured so that its value at inception is zero: the fixed rate (the swap rate) is set so that the present value of the fixed leg equals the present value of the expected floating leg, discounted using the relevant yield curve. As interest rates move, the swap gains value for one party and loses value for the other, creating counterparty credit exposure that is managed through collateral agreements and, increasingly, central clearing.

History

The first widely publicised interest rate swap was arranged in 1981 between IBM and the World Bank, exploiting differences between the US and Swiss/German bond markets. The market grew rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s with standardised documentation from the International Swaps and Derivatives Association (ISDA). After the 2008 financial crisis, reforms under the Dodd–Frank Act and EMIR pushed standardised swaps onto central clearing houses.

Types

  • Fixed-for-floating — the standard swap in a single currency.
  • Floating-for-floating (basis swap) — exchanging two different floating benchmarks.
  • Cross-currency swap — exchanging interest (and usually principal) in two different currencies.
  • Overnight indexed swap (OIS) — floating leg linked to an overnight rate such as SOFR or €STR.
