Mass timber

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mass timber is a category of engineered wood products used as a structural building material, consisting of large solid wood panels, columns and beams formed by laminating, nailing or dowelling smaller pieces of lumber together. Because the pieces are combined into thick, strong elements, mass timber can substitute for steel and concrete in the primary structure of buildings, including multi-storey and high-rise construction.

Products

The principal mass timber products include:

  • Cross-laminated timber (CLT) — layers of lumber boards stacked crosswise at 90-degree angles and glued under pressure, forming panels used for walls, floors and roofs.
  • Glued laminated timber (glulam) — laminations stacked in the same direction and glued, commonly formed into beams and columns, and capable of being curved.
  • Nail-laminated timber (NLT) — dimensional lumber stacked on edge and fastened together with nails or screws.
  • Dowel-laminated timber (DLT) — lumber joined with hardwood dowels rather than glue or metal fasteners.
  • Mass plywood panels (MPP) — large panels built up from veneer laminations.

Properties and advantages

Mass timber has a favourable strength-to-weight ratio compared with concrete, which reduces foundation requirements and speeds construction, since large prefabricated elements can be assembled quickly on site. Advocates emphasise its carbon profile: wood stores carbon sequestered during tree growth, and substituting mass timber for steel and concrete can reduce a building's embodied carbon emissions. The material also chars predictably in fire, forming an insulating char layer that protects the structural core, allowing large timber elements to achieve required fire-resistance ratings.

Concerns and criticism

Questions raised about mass timber include the sustainability of the forestry supplying it, the durability of connections and adhesives, moisture management during construction, acoustic performance, and the accuracy of carbon accounting when whole-life emissions, including end-of-life disposal, are considered. Building codes in many jurisdictions historically limited wood buildings by height, though codes have been progressively revised — for example, the 2021 International Building Code in the United States permits mass timber buildings up to 18 storeys under certain conditions.

Notable buildings

Prominent mass timber buildings include the T3 building in Minneapolis, Brock Commons Tallwood House at the University of British Columbia in Vancouver, Mjøstårnet in Norway (long cited among the world's tallest timber buildings), and Ascent in Milwaukee, Wisconsin.
