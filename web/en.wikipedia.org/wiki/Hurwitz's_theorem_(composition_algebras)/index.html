Hurwitz's theorem (composition algebras)

Hurwitz's theorem is a theorem of abstract algebra which classifies the finite-dimensional normed division algebras over the real numbers. It states that such an algebra must have dimension 1, 2, 4 or 8, and is isomorphic to one of: the real numbers R, the complex numbers C, the quaternions H, or the octonions O.

Statement

Let A be a finite-dimensional algebra over R equipped with a positive-definite quadratic form (a norm) q satisfying the composition law q(xy) = q(x)q(y) for all x, y in A, and possessing a multiplicative identity. Then dim A is 1, 2, 4 or 8, and A is isomorphic to R, C, H or O respectively.

History

The theorem was proved by Adolf Hurwitz in 1898. It is closely related to Hurwitz's theorem on sums of squares, which says that an identity expressing a product of sums of n squares as a sum of n squares exists only for n = 1, 2, 4, 8. The result explains why attempts to construct further "hypercomplex number" systems with a well-behaved norm must fail above dimension eight: the sedenions, obtained by one more application of the Cayley–Dickson construction, contain zero divisors and are not a division algebra.

Proof outline

Modern proofs typically proceed by showing that a composition algebra is alternative and satisfies strong identities obtained by polarising the composition law. One then shows the dimension must double at each step of the Cayley–Dickson process and that the process can yield a division algebra only up to the octonions; alternatively, arguments using Clifford algebras relate the dimension to the existence of certain representations.
