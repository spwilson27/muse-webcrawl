Permalloy

Permalloy is a nickel–iron magnetic alloy with very high magnetic permeability — roughly 80% nickel and 20% iron by content in the classic composition — making it a soft magnetic material that is easily magnetised and demagnetised. It was developed in the early twentieth century at the Western Electric / Bell Telephone Laboratories research establishment, principally by the physicist Gustav Elmen, in the search for a material to improve the loading coils and transmission characteristics of telegraph and telephone cables.

Properties

Permalloy's relative permeability can reach around 100,000 when properly annealed, far above that of ordinary iron, and its coercivity is very low. These properties follow from the near-zero magnetocrystalline anisotropy and magnetostriction of the nickel–iron system near the 80:20 composition. Its saturation magnetisation (about 0.8 tesla) is, however, lower than that of iron or silicon steel, limiting its use in high-flux applications.

Uses

Permalloy and related high-permeability alloys (including mu-metal, which contains additional molybdenum or copper) are used for magnetic shielding, transformer and inductor cores, magnetic recording heads, sensitive magnetometers and fluxgate sensors, and the yokes of cathode-ray tubes. Because its properties are sensitive to mechanical strain, finished parts are annealed after forming.
