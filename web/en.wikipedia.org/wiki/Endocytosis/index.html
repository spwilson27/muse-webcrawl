Endocytosis
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

Endocytosis is a cellular process in which substances are brought into the cell by invagination of the cell membrane, forming a vesicle that encloses the material. It is the counterpart of exocytosis and a form of active transport. Main types include phagocytosis ('cell eating', of particles and microbes, by macrophages), pinocytosis ('cell drinking', of fluids and solutes), and receptor-mediated endocytosis, in which specific molecules (e.g. LDL cholesterol particles, transferrin, many viruses hijacking the machinery) bind receptors concentrated in clathrin-coated pits.

Endocytosed vesicles typically fuse with endosomes, are sorted (cargo to lysosomes for degradation, receptors recycled to the surface), and are essential to nutrition, immune function (antigen presentation), synaptic transmission (recycling vesicles) and down-regulating signals. The clathrin mechanism was worked out by Barbara Pearse in the 1970s; failures of receptor-mediated uptake underlie familial hypercholesterolaemia (Brown and Goldstein's Nobel-winning work).
