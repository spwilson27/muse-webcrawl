Tandem wing

A tandem wing aircraft has two main wings, one located forward and the other aft, both providing lift. The arrangement differs from a biplane, in which the wings are stacked roughly one above the other, and from a canard, in which the forward surface is much smaller than the main wing and primarily a control surface. In a pure tandem design the two lifting surfaces are of broadly comparable size and the centre of gravity lies between them.

Characteristics

Because lift is shared between two wings of limited span, a tandem aircraft can be compact and structurally light, with low induced drag for its size when the wings are well separated vertically and longitudinally. The configuration can offer docile stall behaviour: the forward wing is usually set at a higher incidence so that it stalls first, lowering the nose before the rear wing stalls. Disadvantages include aerodynamic interference (downwash from the front wing reducing the effectiveness of the rear wing), sensitivity to centre-of-gravity position, and possible pitch instability if the design is not carefully balanced.

History and examples

Early experimenters included Samuel Langley, whose tandem-wing Aerodrome nearly flew in 1903. Henri Mignet's Pou-du-Ciel ("Flying Flea") of the 1930s popularised the tandem layout for homebuilders, though early accidents caused by incorrect rigging damaged its reputation. Later examples include Burt Rutan's Quickie series and the high-altitude Scaled Composites Proteus research aircraft.
