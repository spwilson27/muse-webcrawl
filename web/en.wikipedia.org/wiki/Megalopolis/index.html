Megalopolis

A megalopolis (from Greek: μεγαλόπολις, "great city") is a large, densely populated chain of adjoining metropolitan areas that have grown together into a more or less continuous urban region. The term in its modern sense was popularised by the French geographer Jean Gottmann in his 1961 study Megalopolis: The Urbanized Northeastern Seaboard of the United States, which described the Boston-to-Washington corridor as a single interdependent urban system.

Characteristics

Megalopolises typically contain tens of millions of inhabitants spread across multiple metropolitan areas, are highly integrated economically and by transport infrastructure, and often function as the political, financial and cultural core of their countries. Definitions vary, but populations above roughly 10 million across a polycentric urban chain are commonly taken as a threshold; a related concept is the megacity, a single metropolitan area of more than 10 million people.

Examples

United States

The original example is the Northeast megalopolis, often called BosWash, extending some 750 km from Boston through New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore to Washington, D.C., home to more than 50 million people. Other North American examples include the Great Lakes megalopolis (Chicago–Toronto corridor) and California's northern and southern urban complexes.

Asia

Japan's Taiheiyō Belt, running from Tokyo through Nagoya and Osaka to Fukuoka, is one of the world's most populous megalopolises. In China, the Yangtze River Delta, the Pearl River Delta (Greater Bay Area) and the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (Jing-Jin-Ji) region form enormous urban clusters; India's Delhi–Lahore and Indo-Gangetic corridors are also described in these terms.

Europe

The "Blue Banana" is a discontinuous corridor of urbanisation from North West England through the Benelux and the Rhine valley to northern Italy, sometimes counterposed with the "Golden Banana" along the Mediterranean coast.

History of the term

In antiquity, Megalopolis was a city founded in Arcadia, Greece, around 371 BC as a capital of the Arcadian League. The modern generic usage derives from Gottmann's work, although earlier writers, including Oswald Spengler, used the word for a late-stage world city.
