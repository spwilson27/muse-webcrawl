Speciation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Speciation is the evolutionary process by which populations evolve to become distinct species — groups whose members can (or could) interbreed but are reproductively isolated from other such groups. Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species took its title from the problem, although Darwin explained the branching pattern of life better than the splitting mechanism itself.

The modern account centres on reproductive isolation: when gene flow between two populations is interrupted or selected against, their gene pools diverge by mutation, selection and drift until interbreeding fails.

Modes

In allopatric speciation a barrier — a river, a mountain range, an ocean crossing — splits a population geographically. In peripatric speciation a small founder population at the edge of the range diverges. Parapatric speciation occurs along an environmental gradient, and sympatric speciation happens without geographic separation, often in plants via polyploidy (whole-genome doubling), which instantly isolates the new lineage.

Evidence

Island radiations — Galápagos finches, Hawaiian honeycreepers, cichlid fishes in African lakes — show speciation in progress and recently completed. Ring species, hybrid zones and laboratory selection experiments document intermediate stages. In the fossil record, speciation underlies the branching pattern of phylogenies used in taxonomy.
