The Paris Salon (French: le Salon), officially the Salon de peinture et de sculpture, was the official art exhibition of the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris. From the mid-18th century until about 1890 it was the greatest annual or biennial art event in the Western world, and acceptance by its jury was essential to an artist's career and commercial success in France.

The crowded Salon of the 19th century, with paintings hung frame-to-frame from floor to ceiling.

History

The Académie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture, founded in 1648, held its first exhibition in 1667. Exhibitions became public and regular from 1737, and took their name from the Salon Carré of the Louvre, where they were held from 1725. Denis Diderot's art criticism, written for the Salons of 1759–1781, effectively founded modern art reviewing. After the French Revolution the Salon was opened to all artists (1791), though juries were soon reinstated. In the 19th century the Salon grew enormous — thousands of works hung "skyed" from floor to ceiling — and entry became a matter of official patronage and market survival.

Rejection and the avant-garde

The Salon jury's conservatism provoked repeated crises. In 1863, after an exceptional number of rejections (including works by Manet and Whistler), Napoleon III authorised the Salon des Refusés, where Manet's Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe scandalised the public. The Impressionists, repeatedly refused or marginalised, mounted their own independent exhibitions from 1874. In 1881 the state withdrew and the Société des Artistes Français took over the Salon; in 1884 the jury-free Salon des Indépendants was founded, and in 1890 a schism created the Société Nationale des Beaux-Arts' rival Salon du Champ-de-Mars. By the early 20th century, with dealers and independent exhibitions dominant, the official Salon's centrality had ended.

Legacy

The Salon system shaped academic art training, the hierarchy of genres (with history painting at its apex), and the taste of the French state and bourgeoisie. Its name survives in successor exhibitions, including the Salon d'Automne (founded 1903) and the continuing Salon des Artistes Français.
