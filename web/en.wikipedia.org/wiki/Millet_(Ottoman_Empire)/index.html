Millet (Ottoman Empire)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The millets allowed religious communities in the Ottoman Empire a degree of self-government.

In the Ottoman Empire, a millet (Turkish: millet, from Arabic milla, "religious community") was a legally recognised, self-governing confessional community. Under the so-called millet system, non-Muslim religious groups — principally Orthodox Christians, Armenian Christians, and Jews — were organised into millets that administered their own personal-status law, education, and communal affairs under their religious leaders, who were responsible to the Sultan's government.

Organisation

Tradition attributes the system's foundation to Sultan Mehmed II after the conquest of Constantinople in 1453, when the Ecumenical Patriarch Gennadios II was recognised as milletbaşı (ethnarch) of the Orthodox Christians. The major recognised millets included the Rum milleti (Greek Orthodox, encompassing most Orthodox Christians of the empire), the Armenian millet, and the Jewish millet. Catholic and various Protestant millets were recognised later, particularly in the nineteenth century.

Millet leaders collected taxes from their communities, ran religious courts for matters such as marriage, divorce, and inheritance among their members, and oversaw schools and charitable foundations. Non-Muslims under the system were dhimmis — protected but subordinate subjects liable to a special tax (the jizya) and various legal restrictions relative to Muslims.

Development and decline

Historians debate how formal and systematic the arrangement was before the nineteenth century; the term "millet system" was largely standardised during the Tanzimat reform era, when the empire sought to regulate the millets through written constitutions. The reforms of 1839 and 1856 proclaimed the legal equality of all subjects, gradually undermining the millets' corporate privileges. With the rise of nationalism, the millets also became vehicles for national movements — the Bulgarian Exarchate of 1870, for example, effectively created a Bulgarian national millet. The system dissolved with the empire's collapse after the First World War, though millet-derived personal-status arrangements survive in some successor states, including Israel and Lebanon.
