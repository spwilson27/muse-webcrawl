The Palais-Royal (French pronunciation: [pa.lɛ ʁwa.jal]) is a former French royal palace located on Rue Saint-Honoré in the 1st arrondissement of Paris, directly across from the Louvre. The screened entrance court faces the Place du Palais-Royal, opposite the Louvre. Originally called the Palais-Cardinal, it was built for Cardinal Richelieu beginning in 1633 to designs by Jacques Lemercier. Richelieu bequeathed it to the Crown, and it took the name Palais-Royal.

History

The palace passed to the House of Orléans, who held it for most of its history. In the 1780s the Duke of Chartres (later Philippe Égalité) enclosed the garden with arcaded galleries containing shops, cafés and theatres—including the theatre that became the Comédie-Française—turning the garden into one of the liveliest public spaces in Paris. Because the gardens were the duke's private property, police could not enter during the Ancien Régime, and the arcades became a haunt of pamphleteers and revolutionaries; Camille Desmoulins harangued the crowd there on 12 July 1789, two days before the storming of the Bastille.

After the Revolution the palace housed various institutions; today it accommodates the Conseil d'État, the Constitutional Council and the Ministry of Culture. Its courtyard contains Daniel Buren's striped columns, Les Deux Plateaux (1986), and the garden remains a quiet public square lined with galleries.
