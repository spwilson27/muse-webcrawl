GWR 6000 Class

The Great Western Railway (GWR) 6000 Class, universally known as the King Class, is a class of thirty 4-6-0 steam locomotives designed by Charles Collett and built at Swindon Works between 1927 and 1930 for the Great Western Railway's heaviest express passenger work. When introduced, the Kings were the most powerful locomotives in Britain, and they remained the GWR's flagship class until the end of steam, with No. 6000 King George V as the class pioneer.

Background and design

By the mid-1920s the GWR's Castle Class was being stretched by ever-heavier West of England expresses. Collett developed the Castle design into a larger locomotive with a bigger boiler at 250 psi, 6 ft 6 in driving wheels, and a tractive effort exceeding 40,000 lbf — the highest of any British 4-6-0. To keep within the loading gauge and axle-load limits, the leading bogie used an unusual arrangement with outside bearings on the front axle. The class was named after British kings in reverse chronological order, starting with the reigning King George V.

Service

The Kings hauled premier expresses such as the Cornish Riviera Express between London Paddington and the West Country, though their weight restricted them to main lines built to the heaviest standard. In 1927 No. 6000 King George V was shipped to the United States for the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad's centenary celebrations, returning with a commemorative bell and plaque. After nationalisation in 1948 the class passed to British Railways' Western Region, gaining double chimneys and four-row superheaters in the 1950s that markedly improved performance. All were withdrawn in 1962, displaced by diesel-hydraulic locomotives.

Preservation

Three Kings survive: No. 6000 King George V (in the collection of the National Railway Museum), No. 6023 King Edward II, and No. 6024 King Edward I, the latter two returned to steam in preservation and noted for heavy main-line tour work.
