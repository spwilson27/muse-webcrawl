Peak car

Peak car (also peak car use) is a hypothesis that motor vehicle distance travelled per capita in many developed countries has peaked and will not return to its earlier growth trajectory, even as economies grow. The term was coined in 2010 by transport researchers including Phil Goodwin and Kurt Van Dender, by analogy with "peak oil".

Evidence

Studies of the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, Australia and other wealthy countries found that growth in car use per person slowed from the 1990s and in many places levelled off or declined in the 2000s, before and independently of the 2008 financial crisis. In the United States, vehicle miles travelled per capita peaked around 2004. Similar plateaus were observed in licensing rates among young people, who began obtaining driving licences later and in smaller proportions than previous generations.

Proposed causes

Explanations offered include saturation of car ownership and road capacity, rising fuel prices, urbanisation and the re-urbanisation of city centres, improved public transport, demographic change, the substitution of electronic communication for travel, changing cultural attitudes among younger cohorts, and road congestion itself. Debates continue over how much of the effect is a temporary response to economic conditions and fuel prices versus a durable structural shift.

Implications

If car travel no longer grows in step with GDP, forecasts used for road building, fuel tax revenue and emissions projections require revision. Peak car is a component of the wider "peak travel" discussion and of policies promoting sustainable transport and transit-oriented development.
