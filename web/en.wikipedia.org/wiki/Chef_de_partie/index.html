Chef de partie

A chef de partie, also known as a station chef or line cook, is a chef in charge of a particular area (station) of production in a large professional kitchen. In the classical brigade de cuisine system codified by Georges Auguste Escoffier, each chef de partie is responsible for one category of dishes and reports to the sous-chef and the chef de cuisine (executive chef). The chef de partie usually supervises a small team of commis chefs and apprentices (demi-chef de partie and commis).

Kitchen stations

The classical stations of the partie system include:

  • Saucier (sauté chef) — sauces and sautéed items; often the most prestigious station among the chefs de partie.
  • Poissonnier (fish chef) — fish dishes and their sauces.
  • Rôtisseur (roast chef) — roasted meats and their juices.
  • Grillardin (grill chef) — grilled items.
  • Friturier (fry chef) — fried items.
  • Entremétier — vegetables, soups and other entrées not involving meat or fish (sometimes split into potager for soups and légumier for vegetables).
  • Garde-manger — cold foods: salads, cold appetisers, pâtés and charcuterie.
  • Pâtissier (pastry chef) — pastries, desserts and baked goods.
  • Boucher (butcher) — butchery of meats and fish preparation.
  • Tournant (roundsman) — a swing cook who fills in at any station as needed.

Role

The chef de partie is responsible for the mise en place of the station, for cooking and plating its dishes to the standard set by the chef de cuisine, for training junior cooks, and for ordering and stock rotation within the station. Promotion to chef de partie normally follows several years of work as a commis and demi-chef, and the rank is a common step on the way to sous-chef.
