One-party state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A one-party state, single-party state, one-party system or single-party system is a type of state in which one political party has the legal right (de jure) or the effective monopoly (de facto) to form the government, with opposition parties banned or reduced to decorative satellites. In the de jure form, the ruling party — today principally communist parties in China, Cuba, Laos, North Korea and Vietnam — is constitutionally entrenched as the leading force of state and society.

Features and debates

One-party states fuse party and state bureaucracies, staff institutions by nomenklatura-style appointment, and control media and civil society to varying degrees; some (notably China since 1978) have combined single-party rule with market economies and rapid growth, generating a large literature on "authoritarian resilience", performance legitimacy and succession institutionalisation. De facto one-party or dominant-party systems — long governed by one party within formally competitive rules (Japan's LDP, Singapore's PAP, South Africa's ANC until 2024) — differ by permitting real, if uneven, electoral competition. Critics of the model cite the absence of peaceful alternation, accountability and free information; defenders historically argued development sequencing and stability, claims contested by comparative evidence.
