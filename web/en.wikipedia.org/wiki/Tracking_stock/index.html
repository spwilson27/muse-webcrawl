Tracking stock

A tracking stock, also known as a targeted stock or letter stock, is a specialized equity offering issued by a company that tracks the performance of a particular division or business unit of the company, rather than the company as a whole. Holders of a tracking stock have a claim on the earnings of the tracked unit but typically have limited or no voting rights in the parent company and no direct claim on the unit's assets in liquidation.

Purpose

Companies issue tracking stocks to unlock the perceived value of a high-growth division that might otherwise be hidden inside a diversified conglomerate, without spinning the division off entirely. The parent retains control, keeps the assets on its balance sheet, and can still benefit from tax consolidation and shared financing.

Examples

General Motors was a prominent early user, issuing Class E shares for Electronic Data Systems in 1984 and Class H shares for Hughes Electronics. AT&T issued a tracking stock for its wireless business in 2000. More recently, Liberty Media has repeatedly used tracking stocks (such as those for Sirius XM and the Atlanta Braves) to represent its varied holdings. Tracking stocks often trade at a discount to the estimated standalone value of the tracked business, because of their structural disadvantages and conflicts of interest between classes of shareholders.

Criticism

Critics note that tracking stocks create conflicts between the board's duties to different shareholder classes, that the tracked unit's results can be affected by allocations of shared costs and capital, and that investors lack a direct claim on assets. Many tracking stock structures have later been recombined or spun off entirely.
