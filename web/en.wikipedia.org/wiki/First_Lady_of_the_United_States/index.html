First Lady of the United States (FLOTUS) is the title typically held by the hostess of the White House, usually the wife of the president of the United States, concurrent with the president's term in office. Although the first lady's role has never been codified or officially defined, she figures prominently in the political and social life of the United States.

Role

The first lady serves as hostess of the White House, organises and attends official ceremonies, and commonly champions social causes. Since the 20th century the first lady has been assisted by a staff, including the White House social secretary and the Office of the First Lady, headquartered in the East Wing. Causes adopted by modern first ladies include literacy (Barbara Bush, Laura Bush), healthy eating and children's health (Michelle Obama's "Let's Move!"), and drug awareness (Nancy Reagan's "Just Say No").

History

Martha Washington set the precedent for the role, though the title "First Lady" only came into common use later in the 19th century. Eleanor Roosevelt transformed the position, holding press conferences, writing a newspaper column, and advocating civil rights and humanitarian causes. Jacqueline Kennedy's restoration of the White House and cultural patronage set a new standard in the 1960s. Hillary Clinton was the first first lady to hold elected office, becoming a U.S. Senator and later Secretary of State.

Non-spouse first ladies

When the president has been a widower or bachelor, the role has been filled by a relative or friend — for example Harriet Lane, niece of James Buchanan, and Martha Jefferson Randolph, daughter of Thomas Jefferson.
