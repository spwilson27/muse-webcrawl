H2 antagonist

H2 antagonists, sometimes referred to as H2 blockers, are a class of drugs that block the action of histamine at the histamine H2 receptors of the parietal cells of the stomach, thereby reducing the production of gastric acid. They are used in the treatment of peptic ulcer disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and dyspepsia.

History and members

The class was developed at Smith, Kline & French in the 1960s and 1970s under the direction of James Black, using histamine as a starting point for rational drug design; the result, cimetidine (Tagamet), was launched in 1976 and became the first blockbuster drug. Later members include ranitidine, famotidine, nizatidine and roxatidine. H2 antagonists were largely superseded for many indications by the more potent and longer-acting proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), but remain widely used, including over the counter. Ranitidine products were withdrawn from many markets in 2019–2020 over contamination with the probable carcinogen NDMA.

Pharmacology

Histamine released from enterochromaffin-like cells stimulates the H2 receptors on gastric parietal cells, raising intracellular cAMP and activating the proton pump. H2 antagonists competitively and reversibly block this step; they are particularly effective at suppressing nocturnal and basal acid secretion.
