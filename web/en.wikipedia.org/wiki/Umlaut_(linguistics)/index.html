Umlaut (linguistics)

In historical linguistics, umlaut (from German um- "around, changed" + Laut "sound") is a type of vowel harmony in which a vowel is assimilated to a vowel or glide in a following syllable. Its most famous manifestation is i-umlaut (also called i-mutation) in the Germanic languages, in which a back vowel was fronted when the next syllable contained *i or *j.

Germanic i-umlaut

In the early Germanic languages a stressed back vowel followed by a syllable containing *i or *j moved forward in the mouth: *u became y, *o became ø, *a became e (with variants by dialect and environment). When the triggering syllables were later reduced or lost, the alternation became morphologically significant and produced pairs such as English foot/feet, goose/geese, mouse/mice, man/men and strong-verb patterns. In German, where the conditioning environment was likewise lost, umlaut became a grammatical marker of plurals (Vater/Väter), diminutives and subjunctives, and is indicated in writing by the umlaut diacritic.

Other types

A-umlaut lowered *u to *o before a following *a in some Germanic dialects, and u-umlaut in Old Norse rounded vowels before a following *u, giving rise to the vowel ø in words such as jörð "earth". Analogous regressive assimilations are described as umlaut in the study of Celtic and other language families, though the Germanic i-umlaut remains the prototypical case. The term was coined by Jacob Grimm.

Effects and survival

Although umlaut began as automatic phonetics, in the modern languages its results are often morphologised or lexicalised: they survive as irregular plurals in English, as productive plural and derivation markers in German, and as pervasive vowel alternations in Scandinavian languages. English also preserves umlaut in pairs such as old/elder, strong/strength and full/fill.
