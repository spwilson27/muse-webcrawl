Conceptual blending

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conceptual blending, also called conceptual integration, is a theory of cognition developed by Gilles Fauconnier and Mark Turner. It proposes that elements from different mental spaces are selectively projected into a new "blended" space, where they combine to generate emergent structure and meaning not present in either input. Blending is claimed to be a basic, largely unconscious mental operation involved in metaphor, counterfactual reasoning, analogy, humour, ritual, and the construction of new ideas.

The network model

The basic model has four spaces: two input spaces containing the material to be combined; a generic space containing structure common to both inputs, which licenses cross-space mappings; and the blend, which receives selective projections from the inputs. In the blend, three operations generate new structure: composition (relations from separate inputs coexist), completion (background knowledge fills in patterns), and elaboration ("running the blend" — mentally simulating it).

Examples

Classic examples include the "debate with a philosopher" (imagining arguing with Kant, who answers back — impossible in either input alone), the desktop interface of computers (office objects blended with computation), the Grim Reaper (death blended with a harvester of crops), and counterfactuals such as "If I were king…". Fauconnier and Turner's book The Way We Think (2002) presents blending as the engine behind human creativity and the origin of language, art, and religion.

Principles and criticism

The theory specifies "optimality principles" or governing constraints — such as topology, pattern completion, and unpacking — that good blends tend to satisfy. Critics have questioned the theory's falsifiability and whether it predicts rather than post-hoc describes interpretations; supporters reply that it unifies phenomena treated separately by metaphor theory, mental-space semantics, and analogy research.

Relation to other theories

Conceptual blending builds on Fauconnier's mental spaces theory and extends conceptual metaphor theory by allowing multidirectional, many-space blends rather than only two-domain mappings. It has been applied in literary studies, linguistics, religious studies, and design research.
