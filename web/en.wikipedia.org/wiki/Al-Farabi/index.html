Abū Naṣr Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad al-Fārābī (c. 872 – c. 950), known in the Latin West as Alpharabius, was a philosopher and polymath of the Islamic Golden Age. Regarded in the Islamic tradition as "the Second Teacher" (al-Muʿallim al-Thānī) — second only to Aristotle, the First Teacher — he wrote influential works on logic, metaphysics, ethics, political philosophy, psychology and music, synthesising Greek philosophy, especially Aristotle and Plato, with Islamic thought.

Life

Little is known with certainty about al-Farabi's life. He was born around 872, probably in Farab (Otrar) in Transoxiana, of Turkic (or, on some accounts, Persian) descent. He studied in Baghdad under Christian scholars including the translator and logician Abu Bishr Matta and the Nestorian monk Yuhanna ibn Haylan, mastering the Aristotelian curriculum. He spent most of his career in Baghdad, later travelling to Aleppo, where he enjoyed the patronage of the Hamdanid emir Sayf al-Dawla, and to Damascus, where he died around 950.

Philosophy

Logic

Al-Farabi systematised Aristotelian logic for the Arabic-speaking world, commenting on the whole Organon and clarifying the roles of grammar, rhetoric and poetics within logic. His account of the syllogism and of demonstration shaped later Islamic and Jewish philosophy.

Metaphysics and the intellect

Following a Neoplatonic scheme, al-Farabi described the universe as emanating from the First Cause through a hierarchy of ten intellects, the last being the Active Intellect, which actualises human knowledge and prophecy. His essence–existence analysis of the Necessary Existent influenced Avicenna.

Political philosophy

In The Virtuous City (al-Madīna al-Fāḍila) and The Political Regime, he modelled the ideal state on Plato's Republic adapted to an Islamic framework: the perfect city is ruled by a philosopher-prophet-legislator whose soul is joined to the Active Intellect, and society is ordered like a healthy body, each part serving the whole.

Music

His Great Book of Music (Kitāb al-Mūsīqā al-Kabīr) is the most important medieval Arabic treatise on music theory, analysing melody, rhythm, instruments and the physics of sound.

Legacy

Al-Farabi's works deeply influenced Avicenna, Maimonides and, through Latin translations, medieval scholasticism. Modern Kazakhstan and Turkey both claim him as a national figure, and his image appears on Kazakhstani currency.
