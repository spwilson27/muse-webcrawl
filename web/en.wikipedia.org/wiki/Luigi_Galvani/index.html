Luigi Aloisio Galvani (Latin: Aloysius Galvanus; 9 September 1737 – 4 December 1798) was an Italian physician, physicist, biologist and philosopher, who studied animal electricity. In 1780, he and his wife Lucia Galeazzi Galvani discovered that the muscles of dead frogs' legs twitched when struck by an electrical spark. This was one of the first forays into the study of bioelectricity, a field that in modern terms would be called neurophysiology and electrophysiology. Galvani's name survives in terms such as galvanism, galvanometer, galvanisation and galvanic cell.

Life

Galvani was born in Bologna, then part of the Papal States. He initially considered the priesthood before turning to medicine, studying at the University of Bologna, where he became a lecturer in anatomy and professor of obstetrics. He married Lucia Galeazzi, daughter of his teacher Domenico Galeazzi, who assisted in his experiments. Late in life, when French revolutionary forces occupied Bologna, Galvani refused to swear allegiance to the new Cisalpine Republic and lost his academic positions; he died in poverty in 1798.

Animal electricity

According to the popular version of events, Galvani noticed that a scalpel touching a brass hook in a dissected frog's leg made the leg twitch when a nearby electric machine discharged. Experimenting through the 1780s, he concluded that animals possess an intrinsic "animal electricity" generated in the muscles and nerves. He published his findings in 1791 in De viribus electricitatis in motu musculari ("Commentary on the Effect of Electricity on Muscular Motion").

Alessandro Volta disputed Galvani's interpretation, arguing that the electricity arose from the contact of two different metals, not from the animal tissue. The controversy spurred Volta to invent the voltaic pile — the first electric battery — in 1800. Modern science regards both men as partly right: Volta identified contact electricity correctly, while Galvani had genuinely detected electrical activity in living tissue, the foundation of electrophysiology.

Legacy

Galvani's experiments inspired Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818), in which "galvanism" animates dead matter. The lunar crater Galvani and numerous electrical terms commemorate his name.
