Baguette

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Several golden baguettes in a Paris bakery basket]

A baguette is a long, thin loaf of French bread distinguished by its crisp, deeply golden crust and soft, open, irregular crumb. Made from a lean dough of flour, water, yeast and salt, a standard baguette is about 65 cm long and 5–6 cm wide, scored with diagonal slashes that open in the oven. It is an emblem of France: bought daily, eaten with every meal, and torn by hand as often as sliced.

History

Long loaves existed earlier, but the baguette's modern form is usually dated to early-20th-century Paris. Explanations compete: steam ovens introduced by Austrian bakers (who also brought the croissant's ancestors) made the crackling crust possible; labour laws restricting bakers' night work favoured a fast-baking thin loaf; and Napoleon-era stories about soldiers' trousers are likely legend. French law long regulated bread prices and weights, and the 1993 "décret pain" defined the traditional baguette (baguette de tradition) as made on the premises from just four ingredients, never frozen.

Making

The dough is mixed, fermented, divided, pre-shaped, rested, rolled into tapering batons, proofed in floured linen folds (couche), slashed, and baked briefly at high temperature with steam injected to gloss and crisp the crust. A baguette stales within hours and is traditionally bought twice a day; yesterday's baguette becomes toast or croutons.

Culture

The baguette anchors the French breakfast (torn and dunked, or as tartines with butter and jam) and the jambon-beurre sandwich, France's classic lunch. In 2022 UNESCO inscribed the artisanal know-how and culture of baguette bread on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
