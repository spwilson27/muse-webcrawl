Bumblebee

A bumblebee (genus Bombus) is a social bee characterised by black and yellow body hair, often in bands, giving it a fuzzy appearance. Over 250 species are recognised, found mainly in the Northern Hemisphere and in South America, and they have been introduced elsewhere for crop pollination. Bumblebees are important pollinators of both wild plants and agricultural crops, including "buzz pollination" (sonication) of flowers such as tomatoes, in which the bee vibrates its flight muscles to shake pollen loose.

Biology

Unlike honey bees, bumblebee colonies are usually annual. A mated queen overwinters alone, emerges in spring, founds a nest — often in an abandoned rodent burrow or tussock — and rears a first brood of workers. The colony grows through the summer to, depending on species, between 50 and 400 individuals, and produces new queens and males in late season. Only the young queens survive the winter. Bumblebees feed on nectar and pollen, have long tongues adapted to flowers of varying depth, and can thermoregulate, allowing them to fly in cool weather when other bees are inactive.

Cuckoo bumblebees

The subgenus Psithyrus comprises brood parasites: a cuckoo female invades an established nest, subdues or kills the host queen and uses the host workers to rear her own offspring, producing no worker caste of her own.

Flight

A persistent myth held that physics shows bumblebees cannot fly; high-speed photography and computational models show they fly by generating lift through unsteady aerodynamics, with wing beats of roughly 200 per second producing vortices that keep them aloft.

Conservation

Many species have declined through habitat loss, pesticide exposure and pathogens spread from commercially reared colonies; species such as the rusty patched bumble bee (Bombus affinis) have been listed as endangered in the United States. Commercial rearing of Bombus terrestris supports greenhouse tomato pollination worldwide.
