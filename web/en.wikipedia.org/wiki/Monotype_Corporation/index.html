The Monotype Corporation was a company specialising in typesetting and typeface design, founded on the inventions of Tolbert Lanston, whose Monotype system cast individual metal letters (in contrast to the Linotype's lines of type). Its British arm, the Lanston Monotype Corporation based at Salfords, Surrey, became famous for its typefaces, many designed under the direction of Stanley Morison, while the American company developed into a major supplier of fonts and imaging software.

The Monotype system

The Monotype machine consisted of a keyboard that punched a paper tape and a separate caster that read the tape and cast single types from matrices in a matrix case, allowing easy correction of individual letters. It was especially suited to complex and book work, and with attachments could cast display type and, later, produce film for phototypesetting.

Typefaces

Monotype's type programme produced or revived many influential faces, including Gill Sans (Eric Gill), Times New Roman (commissioned by The Times in 1931 under Morison), Perpetua, Albertus, Joanna and Plantin, as well as revivals of Baskerville, Bembo and Garamond. Its library became one of the foundations of digital typography.

Later history

As hot-metal typesetting declined, the company moved into phototypesetting (the Monophoto) and then digital fonts and printer technology. The business passed through several owners and names (including Monotype Imaging), was taken private in 2019, and continues as a major licenser of typefaces and font technology.
