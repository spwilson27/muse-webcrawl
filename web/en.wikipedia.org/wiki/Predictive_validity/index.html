Predictive validity

In psychometrics, predictive validity is the extent to which a score on a scale or test predicts scores on some criterion measure in the future. It is one form of criterion validity, alongside concurrent validity, in which the criterion is measured at the same time as the test.

Examples

A classic example is the predictive validity of university entrance examinations: the validity of the SAT or ACT is evaluated by how well scores predict first-year college grade point average. In personnel selection, cognitive ability tests and structured interviews show moderate predictive validity for job performance, with validity coefficients commonly in the range of 0.3 to 0.5. In clinical psychology, screening instruments are assessed by how well they predict later diagnosis or outcomes.

Measurement

Predictive validity is typically quantified as a correlation coefficient between the predictor and the criterion measured later in time. Its magnitude can be attenuated by restriction of range — for example, when only applicants who scored above a cutoff are admitted, the observed correlation in the admitted group underestimates validity in the full applicant pool. Corrections for range restriction and criterion unreliability are standard in validation research.

Relation to other validities

Predictive validity is a subtype of criterion validity within the broader framework of construct validity. Modern validity theory, following Samuel Messick, treats validity as a unitary concept concerning the adequacy of inferences drawn from test scores, of which predictive evidence is one source.
