Neoclassical synthesis

The neoclassical synthesis is the mid-20th-century reconciliation of Keynesian macroeconomics with neoclassical microeconomics that became the dominant framework of postwar economics, particularly in the United States. The term was introduced by Paul Samuelson in the third edition (1955) of his textbook Economics. Its core idea: with appropriate fiscal and monetary policy, an economy can be kept near full employment, in which case neoclassical principles — prices allocating resources, marginal analysis, and market clearing — apply; Keynesian tools of aggregate demand management handle short-run departures caused by sticky wages and prices.

Development

John Hicks's 1937 IS–LM model compressed Keynes's General Theory into a tractable system of simultaneous equilibrium in goods and money markets, and Franco Modigliani (1944) located involuntary unemployment in wage rigidity. Samuelson's textbook spread the synthesis worldwide; the IS–LM apparatus was extended by the Phillips curve (exploited as a policy menu in Samuelson and Solow's 1960 reading) and by neoclassical growth theory (Solow, 1956). By the 1960s the synthesis underpinned the "fine-tuning" ambitions of Kennedy-era economic policy under advisers such as Walter Heller.

Criticism and successors

Monetarists, notably Milton Friedman, attacked its neglect of money and expectations; the stagflation of the 1970s badly damaged the simple Phillips-curve version. The Lucas critique (1976) and new classical economics demanded microfoundations with rational expectations, while new Keynesian economists rebuilt Keynesian conclusions — sticky prices, effective demand management — on optimising foundations. The result, sometimes called the "new neoclassical synthesis", combines intertemporal optimisation and rational expectations with nominal rigidities, and informs modern DSGE models used by central banks.
