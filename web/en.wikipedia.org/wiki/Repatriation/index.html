Repatriation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The repatriation of servicemen after the Second World War was one of the largest such operations in history.

Repatriation is the return of a person to their place of origin or citizenship. The term covers the return of refugees and displaced persons, prisoners of war and other military personnel, expatriates returning home, the remains of the dead, and — by extension — the return of cultural property and human remains to their communities of origin. In international law, repatriation is one of the three traditional "durable solutions" for refugees, alongside local integration and resettlement in a third country, and UNHCR regards voluntary repatriation in safety and dignity as the preferred solution where conditions allow.

Refugees and displaced persons

Voluntary repatriation requires that return be genuinely voluntary, informed, and to conditions of physical, legal and material safety. The principle of non-refoulement prohibits returning people to a place where they face persecution, so forced repatriation of refugees can violate international law. Large repatriation programmes followed the end of conflicts in, for example, Mozambique (1992–1996, roughly 1.7 million returnees), Afghanistan after 2001 (millions returned from Pakistan and Iran), and Liberia and South Sudan in the 2000s–2010s.

Prisoners of war and the dead

The Geneva Conventions require that prisoners of war be repatriated without delay after the cessation of active hostilities (Third Convention, Article 118). Repatriation of the war dead and of missing servicemen — for example, continuing US efforts to recover personnel from the Korean and Vietnam wars — is a related, often diplomatically sensitive form of the practice.

Cultural property and human remains

Since the late 20th century, "repatriation" has also described the return of artefacts, sacred objects and ancestral human remains from museums and universities to indigenous peoples and source countries. In the United States, the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA, 1990) requires federally funded institutions to inventory and return Native American human remains and cultural items. Internationally, high-profile debates include the Parthenon Marbles, the Benin Bronzes — many of which Western museums and governments agreed in the 2020s to return to Nigeria — and looted colonial-era collections generally, under the framework of the 1970 UNESCO Convention.

Other uses

In business and finance, repatriation refers to bringing foreign-earned profits back to the home country, and to an expatriate employee's return at the end of a foreign assignment — a phase associated with "reverse culture shock" and supported by repatriation training in international human-resource management. In medicine, it means returning a patient to their home country or region for treatment.
