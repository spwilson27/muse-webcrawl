Nitrogenase

Nitrogenases are enzymes produced by certain bacteria, such as cyanobacteria and rhizobacteria, that catalyse biological nitrogen fixation: the reduction of atmospheric dinitrogen (N2) to ammonia (NH3). They are the only family of enzymes known to perform this reaction, which supplies most of the fixed nitrogen used by the biosphere and ultimately supports the production of all proteins and nucleic acids in nearly every ecosystem.

Structure

The canonical molybdenum nitrogenase consists of two proteins. The Fe protein, a homodimer containing a single Fe4S4 cluster, accepts electrons from ferredoxin or flavodoxin and delivers them one at a time to the catalytic MoFe protein, hydrolysing two ATP per electron. The MoFe protein is an α2β2 tetramer containing two remarkable metal clusters: the P-cluster (Fe8S7), which relays electrons, and the iron–molybdenum cofactor (FeMoco, MoFe7S9C), at which N2 binds and is reduced. Alternative nitrogenases replace molybdenum with vanadium or use iron alone, and are generally less efficient.

Mechanism and energetics

Breaking the N≡N triple bond is chemically demanding, and nitrogenase does so at ambient temperature and pressure — a feat industry achieves only with the high-temperature, high-pressure Haber–Bosch process. The biological reaction obligatorily produces one H2 molecule per N2 reduced and consumes at least 16 ATP, making fixation extremely expensive for the cell. Nitrogenase is irreversibly inactivated by oxygen, so diazotrophs protect it in various ways: legume root nodules use leghemoglobin to manage oxygen, cyanobacteria fix nitrogen in specialised heterocyst cells or at night, and some bacteria simply respire oxygen away rapidly.

Biological and applied significance

Symbiotic fixation by Rhizobium and related bacteria in legume root nodules underpins much of world agriculture's natural nitrogen supply, while free-living and associative diazotrophs fix nitrogen in oceans and soils. Introducing nitrogen fixation into cereals, or improving its efficiency, is a long-standing goal of agricultural biotechnology because synthetic nitrogen fertiliser production consumes a significant fraction of global energy.
