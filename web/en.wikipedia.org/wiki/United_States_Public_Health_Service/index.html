United States Public Health Service

The United States Public Health Service (USPHS or PHS) is the parent organisation of the public-health agencies of the US Department of Health and Human Services, and the home of the USPHS Commissioned Corps, one of the nation's eight uniformed services. The Service traces its origin to the Marine Hospital Service, founded in 1798 to care for sick and disabled merchant seamen.

History

Reorganised in 1870 under a Supervising Surgeon (later Surgeon General), the Marine Hospital Service took on quarantine, immigration medical inspection and epidemic-control duties. Renamed the Public Health and Marine-Hospital Service in 1902 and the Public Health Service in 1912, it expanded into disease research through the Hygienic Laboratory — ancestor of the National Institutes of Health. The Public Health Service Act of 1944 consolidated its authorities. The Service's structure changed with the creation of the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare (1953) and later HHS (1980).

Agencies

Agencies and offices within the PHS include the National Institutes of Health (NIH), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Indian Health Service, the Health Resources and Services Administration, the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality and the Administration for Strategic Preparedness and Response.

Commissioned Corps and legacy

The Commissioned Corps comprises more than 6,000 full-time uniformed health professionals — physicians, nurses, engineers, scientists, pharmacists and others — led by the Surgeon General, who deploy to public-health emergencies. The Service's history also includes notorious episodes, notably the Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972), in which treatment was withheld from African American men, an abuse that led to federal research-ethics reforms and a presidential apology in 1997.
