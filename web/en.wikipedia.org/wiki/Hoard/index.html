Hoard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hoard or treasure trove is a collection of valuable objects or artifacts, often purposely buried in the ground, that were deliberately concealed by their owner — usually with the intention of recovery at a later time, which for some reason never occurred. Hoards are a major source of archaeological information, especially for periods with few written records.

Classification

Archaeologists distinguish hoards by the presumed motive for deposition. Personal hoards contain an individual's valuables; merchant's hoards hold stock-in-trade; loot or emergency hoards were buried hastily in times of invasion or unrest; and votive hoards were offerings deliberately deposited in rivers, bogs, or sacred places with no intention of recovery. Scrap or founder's hoards contain broken metal objects gathered for recycling.

Famous hoards

Notable discoveries include the Staffordshire Hoard (found 2009), the largest known collection of Anglo-Saxon gold and silver metalwork; the Hoxne Hoard, a late Roman cache of gold and silver coins and jewelry buried in Suffolk; the Sutton Hoo ship burial (not strictly a hoard, but an associated treasure); and the Viking Cuerdale Hoard of silver. Bronze Age hoards of axes and ornaments are found across Europe.

Legal status

The ownership of found hoards varies by jurisdiction. In England and Wales, the Treasure Act 1996 requires reporting of qualifying finds, which may be acquired by museums with a reward to the finder and landowner. In many countries, antiquities found in the ground belong to the state.
