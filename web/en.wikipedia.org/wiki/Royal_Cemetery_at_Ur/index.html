Royal Cemetery at Ur

The Royal Cemetery at Ur is an archaeological site in the ancient Sumerian city of Ur (in present-day southern Iraq) containing about 2,000 burials dating to roughly 2600–2450 BC, in the Early Dynastic III period. Sixteen of the tombs, distinguished by stone chambers, vast wealth, and the bodies of ritually killed attendants, were designated "royal tombs" by their excavator, Leonard Woolley. The finds are among the most spectacular from ancient Mesopotamia.

Excavation

The cemetery was excavated between 1922 and 1934 by a joint expedition of the British Museum and the University of Pennsylvania Museum, directed by Sir Leonard Woolley. The discoveries rivalled Tutankhamun's tomb in public impact. Finds were divided among the Iraq Museum in Baghdad, the British Museum in London, and the Penn Museum in Philadelphia.

The royal tombs

The great tombs consisted of a vaulted or corbelled stone burial chamber at the bottom of a deep pit, reached by a sloping ramp. Along with the principal burial were the bodies of courtiers, soldiers, musicians, and drivers, together with ox-drawn wagons and sledges. Woolley concluded that the attendants had been sacrificed — in his reconstruction, they drank poison and lay down to die with their king or queen — though later CT-scan research on skulls suggests some victims were killed by blows to the head and preserved by heating and cinnabar treatment.

Named burials include King Meskalamdug, Akalamdug, and Queen Puabi, whose tomb (PG 800) was found largely intact. Puabi's grave goods included a magnificent headdress of gold leaves, carnelian and lapis lazuli beads, a gold lyre, and a cylinder seal naming her. Another tomb yielded the "Standard of Ur", a mosaic panel depicting war and feasting; the "Ram in a Thicket" figures of gold, silver, lapis, and shell; ornate lyres and harps with bulls' heads; and the Royal Game of Ur board found in one of the tombs.

Interpretation

The identity of the tomb occupants remains debated: some may have been kings and queens of the First Dynasty of Ur, others perhaps priestly or cultic figures. The rich imports — lapis lazuli from Afghanistan, carnelian from the Indus, gold from various sources — demonstrate the reach of Ur's trade networks. Human sacrifice on this scale is otherwise nearly unattested in Mesopotamia and appears to have been practised only briefly. The treasures of the Royal Cemetery remain key evidence for the wealth, artistry, and ideology of kingship in early Sumer.
