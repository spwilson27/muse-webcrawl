Egyptian chronology

The Turin King List, a key chronological source

Egyptian chronology is the system of dating the reigns of the pharaohs and dynasties of ancient Egypt, providing the chronological backbone for much of the ancient Near East. Egyptologists divide the timeline into the Old, Middle and New Kingdoms separated by Intermediate Periods, preceded by the Early Dynastic Period and followed by the Late Period.

Sources

Reconstruction rests on ancient king lists — the Palermo Stone, the Abydos and Saqqara lists, and the Ramesside Turin King List — and on Manetho's Aegyptiaca, which introduced the division into thirty dynasties still used today. Regnal years inscribed on monuments, genealogies, and records of astronomical events such as the heliacal rising of Sirius (the Sothic cycle) supply further anchor points.

Absolute dating

For the New Kingdom, synchronisms with Assyrian, Babylonian and biblical chronologies, plus astronomical observations, fix dates to within about a decade: the reign of Ramesses II is conventionally 1279–1213 BC. Earlier periods are less certain; the start of the First Dynasty is usually placed around 3100–2900 BC. Radiocarbon dating programmes, notably a large 2010 study of museum samples, have broadly confirmed the conventional framework while tightening some ranges. Alternative "low" and "high" chronologies differ mainly over the Middle Kingdom and Second Intermediate Period, and over the length of the Third Intermediate Period.
