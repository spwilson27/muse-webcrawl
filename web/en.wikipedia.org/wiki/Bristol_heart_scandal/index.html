Bristol heart scandal

The Bristol heart scandal concerned the unacceptably high mortality of babies and young children undergoing heart surgery at the Bristol Royal Infirmary in England between the late 1980s and mid-1990s. Revelations that death rates for open-heart surgery on infants were far worse than at comparable centres, and that warnings from insiders had been ignored, led to a major public inquiry whose 2001 report reshaped clinical governance in the National Health Service.

Background

Anaesthetist Stephen Bolsin collected data showing mortality for certain paediatric cardiac operations at Bristol roughly double the national average, and raised concerns from 1989 onward; his warnings were dismissed, and he eventually emigrated to Australia. In 1998 the General Medical Council found surgeons James Wisheart and Janardan Dhasmana and chief executive John Roylance guilty of serious professional misconduct; Wisheart and Roylance were struck off.

The Kennedy Inquiry

The public inquiry chaired by Professor (later Sir) Ian Kennedy reported in July 2001 with nearly 200 recommendations. It concluded that at least 30–35 more children died in Bristol between 1991 and 1995 than would have been expected at a typical unit, and diagnosed a "club culture" of hierarchy and secrecy in which patients' interests were subordinated. The inquiry drove reforms including routine publication of clinical outcome data, appraisal and revalidation of doctors, and stronger patient involvement — changes that profoundly influenced NHS governance and the development of clinical audit nationally.
