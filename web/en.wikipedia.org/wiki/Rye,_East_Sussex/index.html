Rye is a small town and civil parish in the Rother district of East Sussex, England, about two miles from the sea at the confluence of the rivers Rother, Tillingham, and Brede. A medieval town and former port, it is a popular tourist destination known for its well-preserved historic buildings and cobbled lanes.

History

Rye was originally built on a rocky hill overlooking a harbour on the English Channel. As a member of the Cinque Ports confederation, it provided ships and men to the Crown in return for privileges, and was an important port in the medieval period. French raids repeatedly attacked the town, most devastatingly in 1377 when it was almost entirely burned. Over the following centuries the sea retreated and the harbour silted up, stranding the town inland and ending its days as a major port, though fishing and a small harbour remain at Rye Harbour.

During the 18th century, Rye and the surrounding marshes were a centre for smuggling gangs, including the notorious Hawkhurst Gang, who used the Mermaid Inn in Mermaid Street as one of their haunts.

Landmarks

The town's historic core includes St Mary's Church, with one of the oldest working turret clocks in England; the Ypres Tower, built around 1249 as part of the town's defences and now a museum; the Landgate, the sole surviving medieval gate; and Lamb House, once home to the novelist Henry James and later to E. F. Benson, whose Mapp and Lucia novels fictionalised Rye as "Tilling." The cobbled Mermaid Street, lined with timber-framed houses, is among England's most photographed streets.

Transport

Rye railway station lies on the Marshlink line between Ashford International and Hastings. The A259 coast road passes through the town, and a small harbour serves fishing boats and leisure craft.
