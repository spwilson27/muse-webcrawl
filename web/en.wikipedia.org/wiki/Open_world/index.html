Open world

Type: Video game design concept

Related concepts: Sandbox game, nonlinear gameplay, emergent gameplay

Early examples: Elite (1984), The Legend of Zelda (1986)

An open world is a virtual world in video games where the player can approach objectives freely, as opposed to a world with more linear and structured gameplay. Notable examples of the genre include The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild (2017) and Rockstar's Grand Theft Auto series. Open world games often give the player a large environment to explore, with challenges and objectives distributed across the world that may be tackled in many different orders.

Gameplay and design

Open world design emphasizes player freedom: exploration, emergent gameplay, and nonlinear progression. Developers populate the world with side activities, dynamic systems (such as weather, day–night cycles, and NPC routines), and modes of traversal — on foot, by vehicle, or by fast travel — that make the world feel continuous. Because players can go almost anywhere, designers typically stream content in and out of memory around the player and use techniques such as level-of-detail scaling to render large environments.

History

Precursors appeared in the 1980s: the space trading game Elite (1984) offered a vast galaxy to explore, and The Legend of Zelda (1986) let players roam an overworld in flexible order. Shenmue (1999) modeled a detailed simulated town, while Grand Theft Auto III (2001) popularized the modern 3D open world city and became hugely influential. The genre subsequently expanded with series such as The Elder Scrolls, Fallout, Assassin's Creed, Red Dead Redemption, and The Witcher.

Reception and criticism

Open world games are among the best-selling in the industry: Grand Theft Auto V and Minecraft (whose procedural worlds are effectively open) rank among the best-selling video games of all time, and Breath of the Wild and Elden Ring won major Game of the Year awards. Critics sometimes fault the formula — particularly the checklists of map icons popularized by Ubisoft — for repetitive content, a critique described as "open world fatigue."
