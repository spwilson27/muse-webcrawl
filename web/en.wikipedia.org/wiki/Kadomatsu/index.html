Kadomatsu

A kadomatsu (門松, "gate pine") is a traditional Japanese decoration of the New Year, placed in pairs in front of homes and other buildings to welcome the ancestral spirits (kami) of the harvest. It is one of the best-known elements of Japanese New Year customs, together with the kagami mochi rice cakes and shimekazari rope decorations.

Construction and symbolism

A kadomatsu is traditionally made from three cut bamboo shoots of different lengths, set in a bundle with pine sprigs and bound with a straw mat and a newly woven straw rope. The bamboo, cut at an angle, represents prosperity and strength; the pine (matsu), an evergreen, represents longevity and steadfastness; and plum (ume) blossoms are sometimes added to represent purity and the renewal of life. The three bamboo shoots are commonly said to stand for heaven, earth, and humanity. The decoration serves as a yorishiro — a temporary dwelling or landing-place by which the toshigami, the New Year deity who brings good fortune and a bountiful harvest, can find and enter the home.

Placement and timing

Kadomatsu are set out after Christmas, generally around 26–28 December; placing them on 29 December is avoided in some regions because of wordplay associating the number with suffering ("double suffering"), and placing them on 31 December is considered disrespectfully last-minute (ikkiri kazari, "one-night decoration"). They are kept until mid-January — 7 January in the Kantō region and 15 January in Kansai — and are then burned at shrines in the dondoyaki fire festival to release the spirits.

History

The custom derives from a combination of Chinese influence — the pine as a symbol of longevity associated with New Year celebrations — and the Japanese Shinto practice of welcoming kami into cut evergreen branches. Kadomatsu became elaborate status symbols during the Edo period, when daimyo and wealthy merchants competed in the size and ornamentation of their gate pines. Today, real kadomatsu made by florists are displayed at train stations, offices and shrines, while many households use small printed or plastic versions.
