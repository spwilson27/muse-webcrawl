Morris Louis Bernstein (November 28, 1912 – September 7, 1962), known professionally as Morris Louis, was an American painter. During the 1950s he became one of the earliest exponents of Color Field painting. While living in Washington, D.C., Louis, along with Kenneth Noland and other Washington painters, formed an art movement known as the Washington Color School.

Life and work

Louis studied at the Maryland Institute College of Art from 1929 to 1933 and worked in various jobs, painting in the Federal Art Project during the Depression. From 1937 to 1940 he lived in New York, returning to Baltimore and then, in 1947, to Washington, D.C. Although somewhat separated from the New York art world, he absorbed the lessons of Jackson Pollock and Arshile Gorky, and after a pivotal 1953 visit with Kenneth Noland to Helen Frankenthaler's studio, where they saw her stain technique, he developed his signature method.

The Veils

Louis poured thinned acrylic resin paint (Magna) down unprimed, unstretched canvas so that pigment soaked into the weave rather than sitting on the surface. His first celebrated series, the Veils (1954, and 1958–60), layer translucent curtains of colour that billow and pool at the bottom edge. In the later Unfurled paintings (1960–61), rivers of colour stream in from the left and right edges, leaving a broad expanse of raw canvas in the centre; the final Stripe paintings (1961–62) arrange vertical bands of pure colour in parallel sequences.

Legacy

Louis died of lung cancer in 1962, at the height of his powers. The art critic Clement Greenberg championed his work as a bridge between Abstract Expressionism and the next generation, and Louis's stain paintings became a touchstone for Color Field painting. Major retrospectives followed at the Museum of Modern Art in 1963 and in Washington and Boston thereafter; a catalogue raisonné of his paintings was published in 1985.
