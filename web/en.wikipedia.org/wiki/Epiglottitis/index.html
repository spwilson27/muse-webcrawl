Epiglottitis

Epiglottitis is the inflammation of the epiglottis — the flap at the base of the tongue that prevents food entering the windpipe (trachea). Symptoms usually begin quickly and include difficulty swallowing, drooling, changes to the voice, fever and an increased breathing rate. Because the swollen epiglottis can obstruct the airway, the condition is a medical emergency that may require intubation.

Cause and diagnosis

The most common cause is infection with Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), though other bacteria including Streptococcus pneumoniae and group A streptococcus, viruses, and thermal or traumatic injury can also be responsible. The diagnosis can be confirmed by direct visualisation of a swollen, cherry-red epiglottis, often with a fibre-optic scope in a controlled setting; a lateral neck X-ray may show the "thumbprint sign." Examination of the throat with a tongue depressor is avoided in suspected cases because it can precipitate airway obstruction.

Treatment and epidemiology

Initial management focuses on securing the airway — frequently by endotracheal intubation in an operating theatre with anaesthetic and surgical support — followed by intravenous antibiotics such as a third-generation cephalosporin. Since the introduction of the Hib vaccine in the 1980s and 1990s, incidence in children has fallen dramatically; cases are now relatively more common in adults. George Washington is often said to have died of epiglottitis or a related upper-airway infection in 1799.
