Gene expression

[Image: Diagram of gene expression — DNA is transcribed to RNA, which is translated into protein]
Gene expression
StagesTranscription, RNA processing, translation, post-translational modification
FrameworkCentral dogma of molecular biology
Measured byRNA-seq, microarrays, qPCR, reporter genes

Gene expression is the process by which information from a gene is used to build a functional product, typically a protein. It proceeds through transcription — the synthesis of an RNA copy of the gene's DNA sequence by RNA polymerase — followed in eukaryotes by RNA processing (capping, splicing and polyadenylation), export of the messenger RNA to the cytoplasm, and translation of its codons into a chain of amino acids by the ribosome. Some genes are expressed as functional non-coding RNAs, such as transfer RNA, ribosomal RNA and microRNA, without being translated.

Regulation

Nearly every cell in a multicellular organism carries the same genome, so cell identity depends on which genes are expressed. Regulation occurs at many levels: transcription factors and enhancers control initiation; chromatin packaging and DNA methylation make genes accessible or silent; alternative splicing diversifies products from a single gene; RNA interference and RNA decay control message lifetime; and protein degradation controls the final product. Housekeeping genes are expressed constitutively, while others are induced by signals — the classic model being the lac operon of E. coli, described by Jacob and Monod in 1961.

Measurement and significance

Expression patterns are measured with DNA microarrays, quantitative PCR and, most comprehensively, RNA sequencing, which profiles the entire transcriptome. Aberrant gene expression underlies many diseases, including cancers driven by overactive oncogenes, and expression signatures are used clinically for diagnosis and prognosis.
