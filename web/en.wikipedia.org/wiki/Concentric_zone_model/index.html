Concentric zone model

The concentric zone model, also known as the Burgess model, is one of the earliest theoretical models to describe urban social structures. It was created by sociologist Ernest Burgess in 1925, based on observations of Chicago, and was a foundational product of the Chicago school of sociology.

The five zones of the Burgess concentric zone model.

The model

Burgess proposed that a city grows outward from its centre in a series of concentric rings, each with a characteristic land use and social composition:

Zone I is the central business district (CBD), the commercial and civic heart. Zone II, the zone in transition, contains industry and deteriorated housing occupied by recent immigrants and the poor. Zone III is the zone of working-class homes, settled by second-generation immigrants. Zone IV is the residential zone of middle-class houses and apartments. Zone V, the commuters' zone, lies beyond the city limits in suburbs and satellite towns.

Criticism and legacy

The model assumed a flat, featureless plain, a single centre, and early-20th-century transport conditions. Later models, such as Hoyt's sector model (1939) and the Harris–Ullman multiple nuclei model (1945), modified its assumptions. Despite its limitations, the concentric zone model remains a standard teaching tool in urban geography and sociology.
