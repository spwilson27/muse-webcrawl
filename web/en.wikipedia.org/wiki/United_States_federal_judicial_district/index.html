United States federal judicial district


Boundaries of the 94 U.S. federal judicial districts and the 13 circuits.

In the United States federal court system, a federal judicial district is a geographic unit served by a United States district court. There are 94 judicial districts: 89 within the fifty states, plus one each for the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and the three island territories with district courts (Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, and the U.S. Virgin Islands). The districts are defined by Congress in Title 28 of the United States Code, sections 81–131.

Structure

Every state has at least one judicial district. Single-district states include Alaska, Delaware, Hawaii, Montana, Wyoming, and others; populous states have several — California, New York, and Texas each have four (Northern, Southern, Eastern, and Western; Texas has Northern, Southern, Eastern, and Western as well). The largest districts by judgeships are the Southern District of New York and the Central District of California, each with 28 authorized judges. Many districts are further subdivided into divisions for venue and administrative purposes; court is held in specified cities within the district.

Functions within a district

Each district hosts not only the district court but also a United States Attorney's office (the federal prosecutor for the district), a United States Marshals Service district office, and a federal public defender organization in most districts. Bankruptcy courts operate as units of the district court. Venue in federal cases generally lies where defendants reside or where a substantial part of the events occurred.

Circuits

Districts are grouped into twelve regional circuits (plus the Federal Circuit) for appellate purposes; appeals from a district court go to the court of appeals for its circuit. The districts of a circuit are also the units used by the Judicial Conference of the United States for administration.
