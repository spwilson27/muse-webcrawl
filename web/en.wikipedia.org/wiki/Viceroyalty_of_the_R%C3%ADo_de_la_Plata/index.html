The Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata (Spanish: Virreinato del Río de la Plata) was the last of the four viceroyalties created by the Spanish Empire in the Americas. Established in 1776 by Charles III of Spain, it was carved out of the Viceroyalty of Peru and governed from Buenos Aires.

Territory and administration

The viceroyalty encompassed roughly the present-day states of Argentina, Bolivia, Paraguay and Uruguay, together with parts of southern Brazil and northern Chile. It included the wealthy silver-mining region of Upper Peru (Potosí), whose output was redirected through Buenos Aires, and was organised into intendancies under the Bourbon reforms. Buenos Aires grew rapidly as a legal Atlantic trading port, reducing the smuggling that had long flourished there.

End of the viceroyalty

The British invasions of the Río de la Plata in 1806–1807, repelled largely by local militias, weakened viceregal authority. In the May Revolution of 1810, Buenos Aires deposed Viceroy Baltasar Hidalgo de Cisneros and formed the Primera Junta, opening the Argentine War of Independence. Royalist control persisted longest in Upper Peru, and the viceroyalty disintegrated into separate independent states during the 1810s and 1820s.
