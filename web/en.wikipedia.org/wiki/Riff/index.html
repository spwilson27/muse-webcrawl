Riff

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A riff is a repeated chord progression or refrain in music (also known as an ostinato figure in the classical context); it is a pattern, or melody, often played by the rhythm section instruments or solo instrument, that forms the basis or accompaniment of a musical composition. Though riffs are most often found in rock music, heavy metal, Latin, funk and jazz, classical music is also sometimes based on a simple riff, such as Ravel's Bolero. Riffs can be as simple as a tenor saxophone honking a simple, catchy rhythmic figure, or as complex as the riff-based variations in the head arrangements played by the Count Basie Orchestra.

Usage

In popular music a riff is typically a short, memorable repeated phrase, usually played by guitar in rock music — famous examples include the opening figures of "Smoke on the Water" by Deep Purple, "Day Tripper" by The Beatles and "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction" by The Rolling Stones, whose riff originated as a Keith Richards idea recorded in the middle of the night. In jazz, a riff is a repeated melodic figure, often used as background for solos or as the head of a composition; big bands such as Count Basie's built whole arrangements from riff trading between brass and reed sections.

Etymology

The term riff entered musical slang in the 1920s. Suggested origins include a shortening of "rhythmic figure" or "refrain", or a borrowing from theatrical slang (a comic "riff" being a set piece of improvisation, related to "riffing" as unscripted elaboration).

Related concepts

A riff differs from a lick in that a lick is typically a one-off improvised phrase, while a riff is repeated and structural. A short riff used as an introduction or recurring motif may also be called a vamp when it is looped indefinitely to support improvisation.
