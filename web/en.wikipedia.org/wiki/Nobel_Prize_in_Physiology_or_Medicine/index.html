The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine is awarded yearly by the Nobel Assembly at the Karolinska Institute for outstanding discoveries in physiology or medicine. It is one of the five Nobel Prizes established by the 1895 will of Alfred Nobel — who is said to have been influenced by his own ill health and interest in experimental physiology — and it is traditionally the first prize announced each Nobel season, in early October.

History and selection

The first prize, in 1901, went to Emil von Behring for serum therapy against diphtheria. The prize has been awarded more than 110 times to over 220 laureates; it was not given in nine years, mostly during the two world wars. Up to three individuals may share the award, which can only go to the living — a rule that has caused notable omissions, as when Rosalind Franklin died before the DNA structure prize. Winners include Frederick Banting (still the youngest laureate, at 32, for insulin), Alexander Fleming (penicillin), Barbara McClintock (the only woman to win unshared, for transposons), and Tu Youyou (artemisinin).

Notable awards

Recent prizes have recognised mRNA vaccine technology (Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman, 2023), microRNA (Victor Ambros and Gary Ruvkun, 2024), the hepatitis C virus (2020), oxygen sensing (2019) and cancer immunotherapy (2018). The prize has also generated controversies, from the 1949 award for the lobotomy to disputes over credit allocation, but it remains medicine's most prestigious honour and carries a gold medal and a cash award funded by Nobel's estate.
