Supersonic transport

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A delta-wing supersonic airliner in flight above clouds]
A supersonic airliner concept.

Supersonic transport (SST) is civil air transport faster than the speed of sound. Only two SSTs ever entered service: the Soviet Tupolev Tu-144 (passenger service 1977–78, brief and troubled) and the Franco-British Concorde (1976–2003). Sonic booms restricted them mostly to overwater routes; high fuel burn, limited range and small cabins made them uneconomic for all but premium passengers. Renewed interest in the 2020s (Boom Supersonic\'s Overture, NASA\'s X-59 quiet-boom demonstrator) aims to reopen the market.

The 1960s race

Britain, France, the US (Boeing 2707, cancelled 1971) and the USSR raced for prestige and speed. Concorde\'s first flight (1969) preceded the Tu-144\'s (1968, the first SST to fly); Tupolev\'s crash at the 1973 Paris Air Show and a 1978 crash ended Soviet passenger ambitions.

Why SSTs failed commercially

A Concorde carried about 100 passengers burning several times a 747\'s fuel per seat; boom bans blocked lucrative overland routes; and the 2000 Paris crash plus post-9/11 economics finished the type in 2003. Subsonic speed, capacity and cost won the market.

Revival attempts

Boom Supersonic targets Overture service late this decade with sustainable fuels; NASA\'s X-59 (first flown 2024–25) gathers data to replace the boom ban with a noise standard; business-jet concepts persist. Sceptics cite the same physics and economics, plus climate concerns about high-altitude emissions.
