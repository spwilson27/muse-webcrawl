Vareniki (Ukrainian: вареники; Russian: вареники) are filled dumplings made by wrapping unleavened dough around a savoury or sweet filling and cooking in boiling water. They are a staple of Ukrainian cuisine and are widely eaten in Russia and other parts of the former Soviet Union, as well as in diaspora communities in North America.

Preparation

The dough — flour, water, egg and salt, sometimes enriched with sour cream or kefir — is rolled thin and cut into circles. A teaspoon of filling is placed in each, the dough folded into a half-moon and the edges pinched, often with a braided seal. Vareniki are boiled until they float, then tossed with butter and fried onions, and served with sour cream (smetana). Leftover boiled vareniki are often pan-fried the next day.

Fillings

  • Savoury: mashed potato (often with cheese or mushroom), farmer's cheese (tvorog), sauerkraut, cabbage, minced meat, liver, or mushroom.
  • Sweet: sour cherries, blueberries, strawberries, sweetened cottage cheese, or poppy seed — served with sugar and sour cream, as dessert.
  • Linyvi varenyky ("lazy vareniki") — the filling (usually cheese) is mixed directly into the dough, which is rolled into ropes, cut and boiled without shaping.

History and culture

Filled dumplings spread across Eurasia along trade routes — scholars connect vareniki's ancestry variously to Turkic dushvara and to dishes transmitted from China through Central Asia, akin to manti. In Ukraine the dish acquired deep cultural resonance: it features in Nikolai Gogol's story "The Night Before Christmas", in which the Cossack Patsyuk eats vareniki that fly into his mouth by themselves. Vareniki are a traditional part of the Ukrainian Christmas Eve supper (Sviata Vecheria) and of festive meals generally.

Related dishes

Vareniki overlap with Polish pierogi (the same word family, and in Ukrainian regions "pyrohy" is a common synonym), Russian Siberian pelmeni (smaller, always meat-filled and never sweet), and Georgian khinkali. In Canada and the United States, Ukrainian and Polish diaspora cooking has made the dumplings ubiquitous at church suppers and festivals.
