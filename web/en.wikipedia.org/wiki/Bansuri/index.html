The bansuri is a side-blown (transverse) bamboo flute of India, the principal flute of Hindustani classical music. Made from a single length of bamboo with six finger holes and one embouchure hole, it is intimately associated with the god Krishna, who is depicted playing it (as Venugopala), and with the pastoral culture of Braj; in Sanskrit sources it is called vaṃśa or veṇu, and bansuri derives from bans (bamboo) + sur (musical note).

Construction and technique

A bansuri is cut from one straight, knot-free section of mature bamboo, closed at one end by the natural node (or a cork), with the embouchure hole near the closed end. Concert instruments come in sizes keyed to different tonic pitches — large bass flutes for the lower tonic E are common — and players carry sets of flutes for different ragas. Half-holing and subtle embouchure and breath changes produce the microtonal inflections (shruti), glides (meend) and ornaments essential to raga performance.

Modern history

Long a folk and devotional instrument, the bansuri was raised to the classical concert stage in the 20th century by Pannalal Ghosh (1911–1960), who lengthened the instrument, added a seventh finger hole for the ma note, and adapted it to the gravity of khayal-style raga exposition. His disciple line and, above all, Hariprasad Chaurasia (born 1938) made the bansuri one of the most popular Hindustani instruments worldwide; other noted players include Raghunath Seth, Ronu Majumdar and Rakesh Chaurasia. The southern Carnatic counterpart is the eight-holed venu or pullanguzhal. Bansuri is also heard in Indian film music, devotional bhajan, and world-music collaborations (Chaurasia's Shakti-era and Making Music recordings among them).
