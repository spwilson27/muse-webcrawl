Seismic hazard

Seismic hazard maps underpin building-code design values worldwide

Seismic hazard is the probability that an earthquake will occur in a given geographic area, within a given window of time, and with ground motion intensity exceeding a given threshold. Seismic hazard analysis quantifies this hazard so that it can be combined with the vulnerability of buildings and infrastructure to estimate seismic risk.

Assessment methods

Two main approaches are used. Deterministic seismic hazard analysis postulates specific scenario earthquakes — typically the maximum credible event on each known fault — and computes the resulting ground motion at a site. Probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA), developed from the late 1960s, integrates over all possible earthquake sources, magnitudes and distances, using earthquake catalogues, fault slip rates and ground-motion models, to produce hazard curves and maps of ground motion with stated probabilities of exceedance, such as 10% in 50 years.

Inputs and outputs

Key inputs include the location and activity of faults and seismic source zones, magnitude-recurrence relations such as the Gutenberg–Richter law, ground-motion prediction equations, and local site conditions — soft soils, for example, can amplify shaking. Outputs are commonly expressed as peak ground acceleration or spectral accelerations at periods relevant to buildings, and are published as national seismic hazard maps.

Uses

Hazard maps directly determine the design earthquake loads in building codes, inform land-use planning and insurance pricing, and guide the siting of critical facilities such as hospitals, dams and nuclear power plants. Hazard is distinct from risk: hazard describes the natural phenomenon, while risk multiplies it by exposure and vulnerability to estimate expected losses.
