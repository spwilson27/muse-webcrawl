Scooter (motorcycle)

A scooter (motor scooter) is a motorcycle with an underbone or step-through frame, a seat, a transmission that shifts without the operator having to operate a clutch lever, a platform for the rider's feet, and a method of operation that emphasizes comfort and fuel economy. Elements of the scooter design were present in some of the earliest motorcycles, and motor scooters have been made since at least 1914.

Characteristics

The classic scooter design, popularised by the Italian Vespa (1946) and Lambretta (1947), encloses the engine and much of the mechanics within bodywork, places a leg shield in front of the rider, and uses small wheels with an easily exchanged spare. Modern scooters typically use a continuously variable transmission (CVT) with a centrifugal clutch, so the rider simply twists the throttle to go. Engines range from 50 cc mopeds — often legally restricted and rideable with a basic licence — to "maxi-scooters" of 600 cc or more with motorcycle-like performance.

Electric scooters have become increasingly common, particularly in sharing schemes and in East and South Asian cities.

History

Early motor scooters included the British ABC Skootamota and the American Autoped of the 1910s. The type came of age after the Second World War, when Italy's Piaggio and Innocenti marketed the Vespa and Lambretta as cheap transport for a rebuilding Europe. Scooters became style icons of the 1960s British mod subculture and the staple transport of dense Asian cities. Japanese and Taiwanese manufacturers (Honda, Yamaha, SYM, Kymco) later came to dominate world production; Honda's Super Cub, a related underbone design, is the best-selling motor vehicle in history.

Use

Scooters are prized as inexpensive urban transport: they are fuel-efficient, easy to park, and in many jurisdictions subject to lighter licensing at small displacements. Their low cost of ownership makes them the primary motorised transport for hundreds of millions of people in Asia.
