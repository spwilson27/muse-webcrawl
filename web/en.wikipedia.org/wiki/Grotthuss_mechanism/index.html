Grotthuss mechanism

The Grotthuss mechanism (also called proton hopping) is the process by which an excess proton diffuses through the hydrogen bond network of water or other hydrogen-bonded liquids, by the successive formation and cleavage of covalent bonds. It explains the anomalously high mobility of the hydrogen ion in water compared with other monovalent cations.

Mechanism

Rather than a single hydronium ion physically migrating through the liquid, charge is relayed along a chain of water molecules: a covalent O–H bond breaks and a new one forms on a neighbouring molecule, so the "excess proton" appears to move while individual atoms move only slightly. Proton transfer occurs through hydrogen bonds between hydronium and water, involving interconversion of Eigen (H₉O₄⁺) and Zundel (H₅O₂⁺) complexes.

History

The mechanism was first proposed by Theodor Grotthuss in 1806 to explain electrolysis. Modern understanding was developed through the twentieth century, with Manfred Eigen's work on proton transfer kinetics. Anomalous proton mobility is observed in water, liquid ammonia, hydrogen fluoride and phosphoric acid, and is important in acid–base chemistry, fuel-cell membranes and biological proton channels.
