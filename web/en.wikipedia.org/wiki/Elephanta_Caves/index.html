The Elephanta Caves are a network of sculpted cave temples on Elephanta Island (Gharapuri) in Mumbai Harbour, about 10 kilometres east of Mumbai, India. Carved from solid basalt rock, the caves are predominantly dedicated to the Hindu god Shiva, though the site also contains Buddhist caves. They were designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.

The Great Cave

The main cave is famous for its large high-relief sculptures narrating Shiva legends, including the wedding of Shiva and Parvati, Shiva as the androgynous Ardhanarishvara, and Ravana lifting Mount Kailasa. Its centrepiece is the colossal Trimurti, a roughly 6-metre (20 ft) three-headed sculpture representing Shiva as creator, preserver and destroyer.

History

The caves' patronage is debated; they are generally dated to the mid-first millennium CE, often attributed to the Kalachuri dynasty period. The island took its Portuguese name from a large stone elephant statue found there. Many sculptures were damaged during the Portuguese colonial period; conservation has been overseen since 1970 by the Archaeological Survey of India.
