Courtly love

Courtly love (Occitan: fin'amor; French: amour courtois) was a medieval European literary conception of love that emphasised nobility, chivalry, and ritualised, often adulterous devotion. It originated in the lyric poetry of the troubadours of Aquitaine and Provence in the late 11th century and spread across European courts, profoundly shaping Western literature's treatment of romantic love.

A knight pledging service to his lady, from a medieval manuscript.

Characteristics

In the troubadour tradition, the lover — typically a knight or poet — serves an unattainable, exalted lady (domna), usually married and of higher rank. His devotion is expressed through humility, courtesy, suffering, and feats performed in her honour; the love is ennobling whether or not it is ever fulfilled, and often deliberately secret and adulterous. Andreas Capellanus's treatise De amore (c. 1185) codified its "rules," though scholars debate how seriously they were meant.

Development and influence

The tradition flourished at the courts associated with Eleanor of Aquitaine and her daughter Marie de Champagne, and shaped works such as Chrétien de Troyes's romances of Lancelot, the Roman de la Rose, Dante's devotion to Beatrice, and Petrarch's sonnets to Laura. Through these it influenced the whole subsequent European tradition of romance.

Scholarship

The term "amour courtois" was coined by Gaston Paris in 1883. C. S. Lewis's The Allegory of Love (1936) popularised courtly love as a distinct historical phenomenon; later scholars have questioned whether it described real social practice or was chiefly a literary game, and whether it is a single coherent concept at all.
