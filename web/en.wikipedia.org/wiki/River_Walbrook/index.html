The River Walbrook is a subterranean river in the City of London that gives its name to the Walbrook ward and to Walbrook street, near the Bank of England. It ran from Shoreditch, outside the Roman city, southwards through the heart of Londinium to the River Thames at Dowgate. Though small, it was of great importance to Roman London: the city was founded between the Walbrook to the east and the River Fleet to the west, and the stream supplied water, powered mills, and received refuse for nearly two thousand years before being completely covered over.

Course

The Walbrook's several feeder streams rose in the marshy ground around modern Shoreditch and Hoxton, then met and crossed the line of the Roman wall near Moorgate. Within the walls it flowed south through what is now the Bloomberg building site and Bucklersbury, passing the Roman Temple of Mithras on its east bank, then by the Mansion House and along present-day Walbrook street to Cannon Street and Dowgate, entering the Thames near Cannon Street station, at a wharf known as Dowgate ("the water gate on the Dow"). The modern street named Walbrook follows the line of its lower valley, and the dips in Queen Victoria Street and Cannon Street still mark its course.

History

Roman Londoners used the Walbrook extensively: workshops, tanneries, and dwellings lined it, and the Temple of Mithras was built beside it in the 3rd century AD; the temple's remains, rediscovered in 1954, now sit in the "London Mithraeum" beneath the Bloomberg European headquarters, close to the former riverbank. The stream's muddy banks preserved extraordinary quantities of Roman organic material, including wooden writing tablets—among them the earliest written reference to London—and leather shoes, which archaeologists recovered in large numbers during the Bloomberg excavations of 2010–2014.

The name Walbrook is usually explained as Old English Weala broc, "brook of the Welsh/foreigners", or from wal, a wall, since it entered the city through the wall. By the 14th and 15th centuries the increasingly foul stream within the City was being arched over; a decree of 1462 lamented its obstruction. It was fully culverted by the late 16th century and now forms part of a combined sewer, entering the Thames only in storm discharges.
