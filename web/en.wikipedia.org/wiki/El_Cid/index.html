El Cid

Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar (c. 1043 – 10 July 1099) was a Castilian knight and ruler in medieval Spain. Fighting both with Christian and Muslim armies, his Arabic nickname was as-Sayyid ("the Lord" or "the Master"), which the Christians transcribed as El Cid, from the Old Spanish Mio Cid ("my lord"). He was born in Vivar, a village near the city of Burgos.

During his lifetime, El Cid was a highly successful military commander, serving first the Castilian king Sancho II and later Alfonso VI, before being exiled. In exile he offered his services to the Muslim taifa of Zaragoza and later carved out his own principality centred on Valencia, which he conquered in 1094 and ruled until his death.

Life

Rodrigo Díaz rose to prominence as the royal standard-bearer (alférez) of Sancho II of Castile. After Sancho's assassination in 1072, he served Alfonso VI but fell from favour and was exiled twice, in 1081 and again in 1089. In exile he fought for the Hudid rulers of Zaragoza against Christian and Muslim foes alike, winning a formidable reputation and the title El Campeador ("the Champion").

In 1094 he captured Valencia after a long siege, defeating the Almoravids at the Battle of Cuarte. He ruled Valencia as an autonomous principality. He died in 1099; his widow Jimena held the city until 1102, when it was evacuated before the Almoravids.

Legend

El Cid became the subject of the famous epic poem Cantar de mio Cid (The Song of My Cid), composed around 1200, the oldest preserved Spanish epic poem. In legend he became a national hero of Spain and of the Reconquista, and his sword Tizona and horse Babieca feature prominently in the tales. His tomb is in Burgos Cathedral.
