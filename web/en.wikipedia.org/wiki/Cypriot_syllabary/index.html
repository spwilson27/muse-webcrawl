The Cypriot or Cypro-Greek syllabary is a syllabic script used in Iron Age Cyprus from about the 11th to the 3rd century BC, replaced gradually by the Greek alphabet. It was used to write both the local Arcadocypriot dialect of Greek and the non-Greek Eteocypriot language.

Structure

The script has about 56 signs, each representing a syllable of the consonant–vowel (or vowel-only) type; all syllables are open, so consonant clusters and final consonants are spelled with "dummy" vowels, making the fit to Greek approximate. It derives from the undeciphered Cypro-Minoan script of the Bronze Age and is related to Linear B, the syllabary of Mycenaean Greek.

Decipherment

The syllabary was deciphered in the 19th century, following the discovery of bilingual inscriptions — most importantly the Phoenician–Cypriot bilingual from Tamassos — and the great bronze tablet of Idalion, now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. Decipherment revealed that most texts were Greek, the earliest substantial records of the Arcadocypriot dialect.

Decline

After the Hellenistic period, the Greek alphabet displaced the syllabary; the latest inscriptions date to the 3rd century BC.
