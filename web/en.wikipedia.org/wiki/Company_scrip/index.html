Company scrip is a form of private currency issued by an employer as an advance against wages, redeemable only at stores owned by the company. It was widely used in the 19th and early 20th centuries in isolated mining, lumber, and mill towns, particularly in the Appalachian coalfields of the United States.

History

Employers in remote company towns paid workers partly or wholly in scrip — metal tokens, paper notes, or punch-card credits. Because scrip could be spent only at the company store, where prices were often high, and could be exchanged for cash only at a discount, the system (known in Britain as the "truck system") bound workers to their employers and was widely criticised as exploitative.

Legislation

In Britain, the Truck Acts prohibited payment in kind for many workers. In the United States, state laws increasingly required wages to be paid in cash, and the practice declined after the 1930s, though scrip was still issued in some coal camps into the 1950s.

In popular culture

The system is immortalised in the 1946 song "Sixteen Tons" by Merle Travis, with the refrain "I owe my soul to the company store", later popularised by Tennessee Ernie Ford.
