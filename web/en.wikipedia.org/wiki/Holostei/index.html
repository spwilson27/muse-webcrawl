KingdomAnimalia
PhylumChordata
ClassActinopterygii
InfraclassHolostei
GroupsHalecomorphi (bowfin and relatives); Ginglymodi (gars and relatives)

Holostei is a group of ray-finned bony fish. It is divided into two major clades, the Halecomorphi, represented by the bowfin (Amia and the extinct ionoscopiforms), and the Ginglymodi, the most significant of which are the gars (Lepisosteiformes), represented by seven living species in two genera (Atractosteus and Lepisosteus). Holostei was once thought to be a grade of "primitive" fishes intermediate between the chondrosteans (sturgeons and paddlefish) and the teleosts, but modern phylogenetic analyses confirm it as a monophyletic group that is the sister group of Teleostei; together they form the Neopterygii.

Evolutionary history

Holosteans first appear in the fossil record in the Late Permian to Triassic, and they were diverse and abundant during the Mesozoic era, dominating many freshwater and shallow marine faunas. Fossil holosteans include the parasemionotiforms, ionoscopiforms, semionotiforms, and macrosemiids. Their diversity declined during the Cretaceous as teleosts radiated; only eight living species survive today, all in the fresh waters of North and Central America.

Characteristics

Holosteans share a number of features, including a slightly heterocercal tail fin, thick ganoid scales in gars (reduced in the bowfin), a shortened jaw joint arrangement, and a swim bladder that can function as a lung, allowing gars and bowfin to breathe air in oxygen-poor water. Because their genomes evolve slowly—most famously the spotted gar, whose genome has been used as a "bridge" for comparing teleost and tetrapod genomes—holosteans are of considerable interest to evolutionary biologists.
