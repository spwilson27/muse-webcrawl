Labarum

The labarum (Latin: labarum; Greek: λάβαρον, lábaron) was a vexillum (military standard) that displayed the Chi Rho symbol ☧, formed from the first two Greek letters of the word "Christ" (ΧΡΙΣΤΟΣ) — Chi (χ) and Rho (ρ). It was first adopted by the Roman emperor Constantine the Great. The labarum became the imperial battle standard of the Christian Roman emperors and one of the most recognizable emblems of early Christian Rome.

The labarum: a gilded spear with a crossbar, bearing the Chi Rho monogram within a wreath.

Constantine's vision

According to the historian Eusebius of Caesarea, in his Life of Constantine, the emperor saw a vision before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312 AD: a cross of light above the sun bearing the Greek words Ἐν Τούτῳ Νίκα — "In this sign, conquer" (in Latin rendering, in hoc signo vinces). That night, Eusebius reports, Christ appeared to Constantine in a dream and instructed him to make a likeness of the sign and use it as a safeguard in battle. The earlier account by Lactantius differs in detail, saying Constantine was told in a dream to mark the Chi Rho on his soldiers' shields.

Description

Eusebius describes the standard in detail: a long spear overlaid with gold, crossed by a horizontal bar to form the shape of a cross. At the top hung a wreath of gold and precious stones enclosing the Chi Rho monogram. From the crossbar hung a square purple cloth richly embroidered with gold and studded with gems, and below it were gold portraits of the emperor and his children. The standard was carried at the head of the army by a picked guard of fifty men, the quinquagenarii, chosen for their strength and piety.

Use after Constantine

The labarum remained the standard of the Roman and later Byzantine emperors for centuries, accompanying armies on campaign and appearing in imperial iconography and on coinage. The Chi Rho within a wreath became a standard motif of Christian imperial art. The etymology of the word "labarum" is uncertain; it may derive from a Celtic or Basque-related term, or from the Latin labare ("to totter, waver"), in reference to the waving of the banner.
