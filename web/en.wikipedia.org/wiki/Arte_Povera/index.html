Arte Povera
Years active1967 – early 1970s (movement)
LocationItaly (Turin, Rome, Genoa, Milan)
Key figureGermano Celant (critic/organiser)
ArtistsMichelangelo Pistoletto, Jannis Kounellis, Mario Merz, Giovanni Anselmo, Pino Pascali, Luciano Fabro, Giulio Paolini, Alighiero Boetti, Pier Paolo Calzolari, Giuseppe Penone

Arte Povera (Italian for "poor art") was an Italian art movement of the late 1960s and early 1970s whose artists used humble, everyday and natural materials — soil, rags, rope, wood, stones, plants, even live animals — in opposition to the polished products of industrial consumer society and the traditional precious materials of art.

History

The term was coined by the critic Germano Celant, who organised the exhibition Arte povera – Im spazio in Genoa in 1967 and published the manifesto "Arte Povera: Notes for a Guerrilla War" the same year. The name echoed the "poor theatre" of Jerzy Grotowski. Working mainly in Turin and Rome amid Italy's post-war industrial boom and the political turbulence leading to the Years of Lead, the artists attacked the commercial gallery system and sought to collapse the distinction between art and life, nature and culture.

Works and ideas

Signature works include Michelangelo Pistoletto's Venus of the Rags (1967), a classical statue facing a heap of discarded clothes; Jannis Kounellis's installation of twelve live horses in a Rome gallery (1969); Mario Merz's igloos and Fibonacci-sequence neon works; and Giuseppe Penone's tree-based pieces. Common themes were energy, process, entropy, and the confrontation of organic with manufactured matter.

Legacy

Arte Povera is now regarded as one of the most influential post-war European movements, a precursor to installation and land art practices, and its works command major museum retrospectives and high market prices — an irony often noted given its anti-commercial origins.
