O scale (or O gauge) is a scale commonly used for model trains. Introduced by German toy manufacturer Märklin around 1900, by the 1930s three-rail alternating current O gauge was the most common model railway scale in the United States, and remained so until the early 1960s. In Europe, its popularity declined before World War II due to the introduction of smaller scales.

Standards

O scale has several competing standards. In the United Kingdom, O gauge models are built to a scale of 7 mm to the foot (1:43.5) running on 32 mm gauge track. In the United States, the dominant scale is 1:48, with track gauge of 1.25 inches (32 mm), which is slightly narrow for the scale. Continental European practice generally uses 1:45 or 1:43.5. The term "O gauge" strictly refers to the track gauge rather than the scale of the models running on it.

History

The name derives from the earlier numbered gauges: after gauges 1, 2 and 3, the smaller size introduced by Märklin was designated "0" (zero), pronounced "oh". Early O gauge trains were largely tinplate toys; over the twentieth century the scale evolved toward highly detailed scale modelling, with two-rail direct current operation and fine-scale standards such as Proto:48 in North America.
