Early modern human (EMH), or anatomically modern human (AMH), refers to early members of Homo sapiens possessing anatomy consistent with the range of modern humans. The term distinguishes them from archaic humans such as the Neanderthals and Denisovans, with whom early modern humans coexisted and interbred. The oldest widely accepted fossils are from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, dated to about 300,000 years ago, and from Omo Kibish, Ethiopia, dated to about 233,000 years ago.

Anatomy

Anatomically modern humans are characterised by a globular braincase with a high, vertical forehead, reduced brow ridges, a retracted face tucked beneath the braincase rather than projecting forward, a true chin on the mandible, and a gracile (lightly built) skeleton with reduced robusticity compared with archaic humans. The pelvis and limb proportions are essentially modern. These traits appeared in mosaic fashion: the Jebel Irhoud fossils combine a modern-like face and teeth with a more elongated, archaic braincase, suggesting that the evolution of modern anatomy was a gradual, pan-African process rather than a single event in one region.

Fossil record

  • Jebel Irhoud, Morocco (c. 300,000 years ago) — faces and jaws with modern morphology, elongated braincase.
  • Omo Kibish, Ethiopia (c. 233,000 years ago) — Omo I shows a modern cranial shape and chin.
  • Herto, Ethiopia (c. 160,000 years ago) — near-modern skulls, sometimes assigned to Homo sapiens idaltu.
  • Skhul and Qafzeh, Israel (c. 90,000–120,000 years ago) — early excursions of modern humans into the Levant, with deliberate burials and ochre use.
  • Cro-Magnon, France (c. 28,000 years ago) — the classic early modern Europeans associated with the Upper Palaeolithic.

Dispersal and interbreeding

Genetic and archaeological evidence indicates a major successful dispersal of modern humans out of Africa beginning roughly 60,000–70,000 years ago, reaching Australia by about 65,000 years ago, Europe by about 45,000 years ago, and the Americas later in the Late Pleistocene. Earlier dispersals into the Levant and Arabia appear to have left little genetic legacy. Ancient DNA shows limited interbreeding with Neanderthals (contributing roughly 1–4% of non-African genomes) and with Denisovans (up to about 5% in some Oceanian populations), as well as evidence of "ghost" archaic admixture within Africa.

Behaviour

Early modern humans produced increasingly sophisticated stone tool industries (Middle Stone Age in Africa, Upper Palaeolithic elsewhere), bone tools, personal ornaments such as perforated shell beads, ochre pigments, and eventually figurative art — for example the cave paintings of Sulawesi (at least 45,000 years old) and Chauvet Cave in France (about 36,000 years old). Debates continue over whether "behavioural modernity" emerged gradually in Africa over tens of thousands of years or relatively rapidly; the current consensus favours gradual assembly of the modern behavioural package within Africa.
