In economics, capital is one of the four factors of production, alongside land, labour and entrepreneurship. Capital consists of durable produced goods that are in turn used as inputs for further production of goods and services. A typical example is the machinery used in a factory. At the macroeconomic level, "the nation's capital stock includes buildings, equipment, software, and inventories during a given year", as estimated by government statistical agencies.

Capital can be increased by investment: resources devoted to producing new capital goods rather than consumption. In national accounts, gross investment minus depreciation equals the net addition to the capital stock. Capital goods differ from consumer goods in that their benefit is indirect — they help produce other goods — though some goods, such as a car used both privately and commercially, can serve both roles.

Types of capital

Physical capital

Physical capital (or tangible capital) comprises produced goods such as plant, machinery, tools, vehicles, buildings and infrastructure. Economists often speak of the "capital stock" of a country as the accumulated aggregate of such goods, measured in money terms after accounting for depreciation.

Financial capital

Financial capital is money and other financial assets that can be used to purchase physical capital or fund production. In accounting, a firm's capital also refers to the funds contributed by its owners (share capital) plus retained earnings.

Human capital

Human capital is the stock of knowledge, skills, health and habits embodied in people that makes them productive. Investment in education and training increases human capital, an idea formalised by economists such as Gary Becker and Theodore Schultz.

Natural and social capital

Natural capital refers to stocks provided by nature — land, water, minerals and ecosystems — while social capital denotes networks, norms and trust that facilitate coordination within a society.

Capital in economic thought

The classical economists, including Adam Smith and David Ricardo, treated capital as accumulated stock used in production. In Karl Marx's critique, capital is not merely a thing but a social relation of production: money advanced to purchase labour-power and means of production in order to extract surplus value, a process analysed in Das Kapital (1867). The Cambridge capital controversy of the 1950s–60s disputed whether an aggregate "quantity of capital" could be measured independently of prices and distribution.

Measurement and depreciation

Statistical agencies such as the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis estimate capital stocks using the perpetual inventory method, cumulating investment flows and subtracting depreciation. Different assumptions about asset lives and depreciation profiles (geometric, straight-line) can materially change measured stocks and hence estimates of productivity growth.
