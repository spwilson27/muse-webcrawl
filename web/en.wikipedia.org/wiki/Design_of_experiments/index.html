The design of experiments (DOE), also known as experiment design or experimental design, is the design of any task that aims to describe and explain the variation of information under conditions that are hypothesized to reflect the variation. The term is generally associated with experiments in which the design introduces conditions that directly affect the variation, but may also refer to the design of quasi-experiments, in which natural conditions that influence the variation are selected for observation.

History

The statistical theory of experimental design was created by Ronald A. Fisher in the 1920s and 1930s at Rothamsted Experimental Station, where he analysed decades of agricultural field trials. His books Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925) and The Design of Experiments (1935) introduced factorial designs, the analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the principles of randomisation, replication and local control (blocking).

Principles

Fisher's three principles remain foundational. Randomisation — allocating treatments to experimental units by chance — validates statistical inference and guards against bias. Replication — repeating each treatment — allows estimation of experimental error. Blocking — grouping similar units and randomising within blocks — removes known sources of variation, such as fertility gradients across a field, from the error term.

Types of design

Standard designs include completely randomised designs, randomised block designs, Latin squares, factorial and fractional factorial designs (which vary several factors simultaneously to estimate main effects and interactions efficiently), and response surface designs for optimising a process. In medicine the randomised controlled trial is the canonical application; in industry, DOE underpins quality engineering methods such as those of Genichi Taguchi.
