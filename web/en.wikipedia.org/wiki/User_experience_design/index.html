User experience design

User experience design (often abbreviated UX design or UXD) is the process of defining the experience a user would go through when interacting with a company, its services, and its products. Design decisions in UX design are often driven by research, data analysis, and test results rather than aesthetic preferences and opinions alone. Unlike user interface design, which focuses on visual and interactive elements, user experience design encompasses all aspects of the end user's interaction, including usability, accessibility, information architecture, and emotional response.

Wireframing and journey mapping are core UX design activities.

History

The term "user experience" was coined by cognitive scientist Don Norman in the early 1990s while he was Vice President of the Advanced Technology Group at Apple. Norman later co-founded the Nielsen Norman Group with Jakob Nielsen. Precursors include industrial design, ergonomics, and human–computer interaction; Walt Disney has been described retrospectively as an early user-experience practitioner for his meticulous design of Disneyland.

Elements and process

Jesse James Garrett's influential model, The Elements of User Experience (2002), describes UX on five planes: strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, and surface. A typical UX process includes:

  • Research — interviews, surveys, analytics, personas, and journey maps.
  • Design — information architecture, wireframes, prototypes, and interaction design.
  • Testing — usability testing, A/B testing, and accessibility evaluation.
  • Iteration — refining designs based on evidence, often within agile development cycles.

Roles and deliverables

UX designers produce deliverables such as user flows, sitemaps, wireframes, interactive prototypes (in tools such as Figma), and design systems. Related roles include UX researcher, interaction designer, information architect, content designer, and product designer.

Relationship to UI design

User interface (UI) design is a subset concerned with the look, feel, and interactivity of specific screens; UX design is the broader, research-driven discipline that determines what those screens should do and why. In practice the roles frequently overlap.
