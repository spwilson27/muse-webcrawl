In mathematics, a 3-manifold is a topological space that locally looks like Euclidean three-dimensional space: every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open ball in R³. A 3-manifold is an important object of study in low-dimensional topology, knot theory, and geometry, and 3-manifolds can be regarded as candidate shapes for the spatial universe in cosmology. Everyday examples include the 3-sphere S³, the 3-torus (the product of three circles), and the complement of a knot in the 3-sphere.

Structure theory

The modern theory rests on decompositions: the Kneser–Milnor prime decomposition splits an orientable 3-manifold uniquely into connected sums of prime pieces, and the Jaco–Shalen–Johannson (JSJ) decomposition cuts along incompressible tori. In the late 1970s William Thurston proposed the geometrization conjecture: every closed orientable 3-manifold can be cut along spheres and tori into pieces each admitting one of eight homogeneous geometries (including hyperbolic, spherical, and Euclidean). Thurston proved the conjecture for large classes of manifolds, including knot complements via his hyperbolic Dehn surgery theorem — most knots in the 3-sphere have hyperbolic complements.

Proof and consequences

Grigori Perelman proved geometrization in 2002–2003 using Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow with surgery, thereby also proving the century-old Poincaré conjecture: every simply connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere. The result was confirmed by the mathematical community and is the only Millennium Prize Problem solved to date. Other central topics include Heegaard splittings, foliations, contact structures, Floer homology, and the study of hyperbolic volume — connecting 3-manifold topology to geometry, group theory, and mathematical physics.
