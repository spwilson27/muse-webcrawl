Automated fingerprint identification


Image placeholder: fingerprint minutiae map

Automated fingerprint identification is the process of using a computer to match fingerprints against a database of known and unknown prints. Systems implementing it are called automated fingerprint identification systems (AFIS). AFIS technology was developed from the 1960s onwards to relieve the burden of manual searching through paper fingerprint card collections maintained by police forces.

Operation

An AFIS encodes the ridge characteristics of a fingerprint — principally minutiae, the points where ridges end or bifurcate — together with pattern type and ridge counts. Searches compare the encoded features of a query print against millions of stored records and return a ranked candidate list, which a trained fingerprint examiner then verifies visually. Latent prints recovered from crime scenes can likewise be searched against tenprint databases.

Major systems

The FBI's Integrated Automated Fingerprint Identification System (IAFIS), launched in 1999 and since succeeded by the Next Generation Identification (NGI) system, holds fingerprint records for well over one hundred million subjects. In Europe, EURODAC stores fingerprints of asylum applicants and irregular border crossers. Live-scan devices capture fingerprints electronically at booking stations and border posts, replacing ink and card.

Uses and issues

Beyond criminal identification, AFIS supports background checks, border control and civil identity programmes such as India's Aadhaar. Accuracy depends on print quality and database design; false candidate matches require expert verification, and the forensic reliability of fingerprint evidence has been examined by bodies such as the US National Academy of Sciences (2009).
