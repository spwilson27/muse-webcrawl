Bourbon Democrat was a term used in the United States from the 1870s to the early 1900s for members of the Democratic Party who championed classical liberal policies: free markets and limited government, the gold standard, low tariffs, reduced federal spending, anti-imperialism, and opposition to high taxes. The name "Bourbon" — borrowed from the restored French royal house that, in Talleyrand's gibe, had "learned nothing and forgotten nothing" — was applied by critics, especially after 1876, to conservative Democrats accused of backward-looking politics; it was also used for the Redeemer regimes that overthrew Reconstruction in the South. The faction's supporters called themselves "Gold Democrats" or reformers.

History

The Bourbon Democrats rose in reaction to the corruption of the Grant era and the disruptions of Reconstruction. Their first presidential standard-bearer, Samuel J. Tilden of New York, won the popular vote in the disputed election of 1876 but lost the presidency in the Compromise of 1877. The faction reached its height under Grover Cleveland, the only Democrat elected president between 1861 and 1913, who served two non-consecutive terms (1885–89, 1893–97) and personified Bourbon principles: he vetoed hundreds of bills, defended the gold standard, and sought tariff reduction.

Cleveland's second term was overwhelmed by the Panic of 1893 and a severe depression. His repeal of the Sherman Silver Purchase Act and his use of federal troops in the Pullman Strike alienated labour and agrarian wings of the party. At the 1896 Democratic National Convention, William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech captured the nomination for the free-silver, populist wing, and the Bourbons bolted to form the National Democratic Party ("Gold Democrats"), nominating John M. Palmer. Their influence collapsed thereafter, though Alton B. Parker, a conservative in the Bourbon mould, won the Democratic nomination in 1904.

Policies

Bourbon Democrats advocated of "hard money" policies — redemption of currency in gold and opposition to free coinage of silver and to inflationary Greenback paper money. They supported tariff reduction, civil service reform in the Pendleton Act tradition, fiscal restraint, and a restrained federal role; most opposed overseas expansion, and many leading Bourbons were prominent in the Anti-Imperialist League after the Spanish–American War. In the South, Bourbon "Redeemer" governments also pursued low taxes and business promotion while entrenching white supremacy and disfranchisement — a central, sobering part of the faction's legacy.

Decline

After 1896 the Bourbon wing never regained control of the national party, which increasingly embraced Bryan's populism and later the Progressivism of Woodrow Wilson. The term "Bourbon" lingered as an epithet for conservative Southern Democrats well into the 20th century. Historians often draw a line from the Bourbons' economic liberalism to later conservative and libertarian currents, though their party allegiance differed.
