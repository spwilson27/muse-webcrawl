The Areopagus (Greek: Ἄρειος Πάγος, Áreios Págos, "Ares' Hill") is a prominent rock outcropping located northwest of the Acropolis in Athens, Greece. Its English name is the Late Latin composite form of the Greek name Areios Pagos, translated "Hill of Ares" (Ancient Greek: Ἀρήϊος Πάγος). In classical times, it was the site of a tribunal, also often called the Areopagus in English. The Council of the Areopagus was the oldest and most august judicial body in Athens, traditionally said to have been founded by the goddess Athena.

History

The origin of the tribunal is traced to the mythical trial of Ares, who was said to have been tried on the hill by the gods for the murder of Halirrhothius, son of Poseidon. According to another tradition, Orestes was tried there for matricide. The Areopagus was the chief homicide court of Athens in historical times.

In the pre-classical period (before the 5th century BC), the council of the Areopagus was the council of the city and filled with former archons, serving for life. It guarded the laws and exercised wide political influence. In the reforms of Ephialtes in 462/461 BC, the Areopagus was stripped of most of its political functions, retaining chiefly its jurisdiction over cases of homicide, wounding, arson, and certain religious offences.

Paul the Apostle

The Areopagus is associated with the sermon of Paul the Apostle described in Acts 17 of the New Testament, in which Paul addressed the Athenians after seeing an altar inscribed "to an unknown god". A bronze plaque commemorating the sermon stands at the foot of the hill.
