Operation Valkyrie (German: Unternehmen Walküre) was a Second World War German emergency plan for the territorial Reserve (Replacement) Army to execute and implement in the event of a general breakdown of civil order in the country. German Army officers, including Major-General Henning von Tresckow and Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg, modified the plan as part of a conspiracy to assassinate Adolf Hitler, remove the Nazi regime, and end the war.

The original plan

The Valkyrie plan, approved by Hitler himself, directed the Replacement Army under General Friedrich Fromm to seize key government installations and communications if slave labourers or others rose in revolt, or if civil government collapsed. Because the conspirators included the officers responsible for issuing Valkyrie orders, the plan gave them a ready-made mechanism for a coup: on the pretext that the SS had murdered Hitler, the Replacement Army could be turned against the Nazi apparatus.

20 July 1944

On 20 July 1944, Stauffenberg placed a briefcase bomb in the conference room at the Wolf's Lair in East Prussia and flew to Berlin to direct the coup. The bomb killed four men but Hitler survived, shielded by the heavy oak conference table. In Berlin, hesitation and delay — including General Olbricht's late activation of Valkyrie and the failure to seize radio stations — allowed the regime to counter the move: when Major Otto Ernst Remer heard Hitler's voice by telephone, his battalion switched sides, and by nightfall the coup had collapsed. Stauffenberg and Olbricht were shot that same night in the courtyard of the Bendlerblock.

Aftermath

In the ensuing purge, some 7,000 people were arrested and about 5,000 executed, including Field Marshal Erwin Rommel, who was forced to commit suicide. The failed plot — the 20 July plot — has since become the central symbol of German resistance to Nazism, commemorated at the Bendlerblock memorial in Berlin and dramatised in the 2008 film Valkyrie.
