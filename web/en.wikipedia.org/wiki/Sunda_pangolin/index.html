Sunda pangolin

The Sunda pangolin (Manis javanica) rolls into a ball when threatened

The Sunda pangolin (Manis javanica), also known as the Malayan or Javan pangolin, is a species of pangolin found throughout Southeast Asia, including Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, Vietnam, Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia and the southern Philippines. It is listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List, having suffered severe declines from hunting for its meat and scales and from habitat loss.

Description

The Sunda pangolin grows to a total length of roughly 80–120 cm including its thick tail, and weighs 4–7 kg. Its body is covered in overlapping keratin scales, brownish in colour, with softer skin on the face and underside. It has a small, elongated head, no teeth, and an extremely long sticky tongue — up to 40 cm — used to lap up ants and termites. Powerful curved claws on the forefeet break open insect nests and dig burrows. When threatened it rolls into an armoured ball.

Behaviour and habitat

Nocturnal and largely solitary, the Sunda pangolin inhabits primary and secondary forest, plantations and gardens. It is a capable climber, using its prehensile tail, and also digs sleeping burrows. Females give birth to a single pup after a gestation of about 70–90 days; the pup rides on the mother's tail until weaned at around three months.

Threats

Pangolins are among the most heavily trafficked mammals in the world. The Sunda pangolin's scales are used in traditional medicine and its meat is considered a delicacy and status food in parts of Asia. All pangolins were transferred to CITES Appendix I in 2016, banning commercial international trade, but poaching and large-scale seizures of scales and carcasses continue across its range.
