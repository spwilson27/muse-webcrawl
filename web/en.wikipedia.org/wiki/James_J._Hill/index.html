James Jerome Hill (September 16, 1838 – May 29, 1916) was a Canadian-American railroad director and builder. He was the chief executive officer of the Great Northern Railway, which he built into the dominant transcontinental line of the northern United States; his work earned him the nickname "The Empire Builder".

Early life

Hill was born in Upper Canada (now Ontario) and lost the sight in his right eye in a childhood accident. After his father's death he left school and worked as a clerk. In 1856 he moved to St. Paul, Minnesota, where he rose in the shipping and fuel businesses, gaining expertise in transportation on the Red River and the Mississippi.

Building the Great Northern

In 1878 Hill and partners including Norman Kittson, Donald Smith, and George Stephen acquired the bankrupt St. Paul and Pacific Railroad. Hill methodically extended the line westward across Minnesota, the Dakotas, and Montana, insisting on the lowest grades and shortest routes through the northern tier. In 1893 the road — renamed the Great Northern Railway — reached Seattle, completing a northern transcontinental route built largely without federal land grants.

Unlike many western railroads, the Great Northern prospered through the Panic of 1893. Hill promoted settlement and agriculture along his lines, believing the railroad's prosperity depended on the prosperity of the country it served. In 1901 he and J. P. Morgan gained control of the Northern Pacific and the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy railroads, forming the Northern Securities holding company, which the U.S. Supreme Court ordered dissolved in 1904 in a landmark antitrust decision.

Later life and legacy

Hill remained a dominant figure in northwestern business and philanthropy until his death in St. Paul in 1916. His great stone mansion on Summit Avenue in St. Paul is now a historic site, and the Amtrak train between Chicago and Seattle via St. Paul still carries the name Empire Builder.
