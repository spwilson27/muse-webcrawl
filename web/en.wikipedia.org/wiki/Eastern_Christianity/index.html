Eastern Christianity comprises the Christian traditions and church families that developed in the eastern Mediterranean, Eastern Europe, Asia Minor, the Middle East, and parts of Northeast Africa, in contrast to Western Christianity, which developed in Western Europe. Eastern Christians use distinctive approaches to theology and spirituality, worship in liturgies such as the Byzantine Rite, and preserve practices rooted in the early Church of the Greek-speaking Roman East.

Families of churches

Eastern Orthodox Church

The Eastern Orthodox Church is the second-largest Christian communion in the world, a fellowship of autocephalous (self-governing) churches — including the patriarchates of Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem, and national churches such as those of Russia, Greece, Serbia, Romania, and Bulgaria — united in doctrine and sacramental communion. It separated from Rome in the East–West Schism, conventionally dated to 1054.

Oriental Orthodox Churches

The Oriental Orthodox churches — among them the Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and Malankara churches — reject the christological definitions of the Council of Chalcedon (451) and form a separate communion with ancient roots in Egypt, Syria, and Armenia.

Church of the East

The Church of the East, historically centred in Persia and Mesopotamia, split from the imperial church after the Council of Ephesus (431). At its height its missionaries carried Christianity along the Silk Road to India, Central Asia, and China. Its modern heirs include the Assyrian Church of the East and the Ancient Church of the East.

Eastern Catholic Churches

Twenty-three Eastern Catholic Churches are in full communion with the Pope of Rome while retaining their own liturgical rites, theology, and canon law; examples include the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church, the Melkite Greek Catholic Church, and the Maronite Church.

Distinctive features

Eastern Christianity is characterised by the centrality of the Divine Liturgy, the veneration of icons, the Jesus Prayer and hesychast spirituality, a strong monastic tradition centred on places such as Mount Athos, married parish clergy, and an emphasis on theosis — the divinisation of the believer by divine grace — as the purpose of the Christian life. Its theology draws heavily on the Greek Church Fathers and the seven ecumenical councils accepted by the Orthodox Church.
