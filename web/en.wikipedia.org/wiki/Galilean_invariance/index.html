Galilean invariance, or Galilean relativity, is the principle that the laws of motion are the same in all inertial frames of reference. Galileo Galilei first described it in 1632 in his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, using the famous example of a ship sailing smoothly at constant speed: below decks, no experiment with falling objects, jumping, or flying insects can reveal whether the ship is moving or at rest.

Formal statement

Coordinates in one inertial frame (x, t) are related to those in another frame moving at constant velocity v along the x-axis by the Galilean transformations:

x' = x − vt
t' = t

Time is absolute — the same for all observers — and velocities simply add: an object moving at u in the first frame moves at u − v in the second. Newton's laws retain their form under these transformations, so no mechanical experiment can distinguish rest from uniform motion. The set of transformations, including rotations and translations, forms the Galilean group.

Application and limits

Galilean invariance underlies classical mechanics and remains an excellent approximation at speeds far below the speed of light. Its conflict with electromagnetism — Maxwell's equations are not Galilean invariant, and the measured speed of light refused to behave as simple velocity addition predicts — motivated first the ether theories of the nineteenth century and then Einstein's special relativity (1905), in which the Galilean transformations are replaced by the Lorentz transformations and absolute simultaneity is abandoned.
