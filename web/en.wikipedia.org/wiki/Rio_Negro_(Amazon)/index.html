The Rio Negro (Portuguese/Spanish for "Black River") is the largest left tributary of the Amazon River and the second largest tributary in the world by discharge. Rising as the Guainía River in the highlands of eastern Colombia near the Orinoco divide — to which it is linked by the natural Casiquiare canal — it flows about 2,250 km southeast through Venezuela and Brazil to join the Amazon at Manaus.

Blackwater character

The Rio Negro is the archetypal blackwater river: its waters are stained tea-brown by humic acids leached from the white-sand soils and flooded forests (igapó) of its nutrient-poor basin drained from the Guiana Shield. With very low sediment, nutrient content and pH as low as 3.8–4.9, the river supports few mosquitoes and little plankton, but distinctive adapted fish fauna, including many aquarium species such as cardinal tetras. At the confluence near Manaus, the famous "Meeting of the Waters" (Encontro das Águas) sees the dark Negro running side-by-side with the pale, sediment-laden Solimões (Amazon) for several kilometres without mixing, owing to differences in temperature, density and speed.

Geography and history

The river drains some 12% of the Amazon basin. The city of Manaus, founded in 1669 at its mouth, grew rich during the Amazon rubber boom, when the Rio Negro was a highway into the interior. The Anavilhanas Archipelago, one of the world's largest river island systems, lies in its lower course and is protected as a national park.
