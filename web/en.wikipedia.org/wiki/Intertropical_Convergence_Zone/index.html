Intertropical Convergence Zone

The Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ /ɪtʃ/), known by sailors as the doldrums or the calms, is the area surrounding Earth near the equator where the northeast and southeast trade winds converge. It encircles Earth near the thermal equator, though its specific position varies seasonally. When it lies near the geographic equator, it is called the near-equatorial trough.

Formation

The ITCZ forms where the trade winds of the Northern and Southern Hemispheres come together. Intense solar heating near the equator warms surface air, which rises as deep convection, drawing in the trade-wind flow. The rising air cools and its water vapour condenses, producing a band of heavy precipitation, frequent thunderstorms, and predominantly light, variable surface winds — the dreaded calms of sailing lore, punctuated by violent squalls.

Seasonal movement

The zone migrates north and south with the seasons, following the thermal equator with a lag of about one to two months behind the Sun's declination. Over the oceans its movement is modest, but over large landmasses such as Africa and Asia it can shift 20–30° of latitude, driving the monsoon cycle. Over the eastern Pacific and Atlantic it usually remains north of the equator.

Effects on weather and climate

The ITCZ is the engine of the tropical rain belt. Its seasonal shifts govern rainy and dry seasons across the tropics and modulate monsoon onset. Disturbances along the zone, including tropical waves, can seed tropical cyclones. Variations in its position are associated with droughts and floods in regions such as the Sahel, northeastern Brazil, and maritime Southeast Asia, and its behaviour during El Niño and La Niña events is a major research topic.

The doldrums

Sailing ships crossing the equator could be becalmed for days or weeks in the doldrums, a hazard that gave rise to the expression "in the doldrums" for a state of stagnation or depression. The band was also feared for its sudden, violent thunderstorms.
