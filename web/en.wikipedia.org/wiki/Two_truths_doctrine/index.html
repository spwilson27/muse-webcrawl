Two truths doctrine

The two truths doctrine (Sanskrit: dve satye; Tibetan: bden pa gnyis) is a central teaching of Mahayana Buddhism distinguishing between conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya) and ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya). According to the doctrine, the everyday world of persons, objects, and causal relations is conventionally real and can be spoken of truthfully, while ultimate analysis reveals that all phenomena are empty (śūnyatā) of inherent, independent existence.

The chariot analogy: conventionally real, but not findable under ultimate analysis.

Origins and development

Early roots of the distinction appear in the Prajñāpāramitā literature and in passages contrasting statements requiring interpretation (neyārtha) with those that are definitive (nītārtha). The doctrine received its classic philosophical form in the Madhyamaka school of Nāgārjuna (c. 2nd century CE), whose Mūlamadhyamakakārikā states that the Buddha's teaching rests on two truths, and that those who do not understand the distinction do not understand the profound truth of Buddhism. Crucially, Nāgārjuna held that emptiness itself is also empty, so the two truths are not two separate realms and the ultimate does not negate the conventional.

Candrakīrti and later Tibetan commentators elaborated the doctrine, debating how conventional truth is "truth" at all — for example, by analysing conventional reality in terms of what is accepted by ordinary worldly consensus without analysis, and its later subdivision into "correct" and "incorrect" conventional truth.

Function of the doctrine

The two truths serve several purposes. Pedagogically, they explain how the Buddha could both teach about persons, karma, and rebirth and also teach that no self or phenomenon exists inherently. Soteriologically, they steer practitioners between the extremes of eternalism (treating conventional things as ultimately real) and nihilism (denying conventional efficacy altogether) — the "middle way". They also structure Buddhist logic and debate, in which a claim may be accepted conventionally while rejected ultimately.

In other traditions

Comparable two-level schemes appear elsewhere in Indian thought: the Advaita Vedānta distinction between empirical (vyāvahārika) and absolute (pāramārthika) reality parallels the Buddhist scheme, though the traditions disagree on what ultimate analysis discloses.
