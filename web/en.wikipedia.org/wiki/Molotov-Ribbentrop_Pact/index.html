The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, officially the Treaty of Non-Aggression between Germany and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, and also known as the Hitler–Stalin Pact, was a non-aggression pact between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union signed on 23 August 1939 in Moscow by foreign ministers Joachim von Ribbentrop and Vyacheslav Molotov.

Molotov signing the pact in Moscow; Ribbentrop and Stalin stand behind him.

Terms

The public clauses of the ten-year pact pledged that neither party would attack the other, that neither would support a third party attacking the other, and that disputes would be settled by negotiation. The agreement was accompanied by a commercial agreement providing for Soviet deliveries of raw materials to Germany in exchange for German manufactured goods and technology.

Secret protocol

An additional secret protocol divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence. Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Bessarabia fell to the Soviet sphere; Lithuania was initially assigned to Germany but was transferred to the Soviet sphere by a follow-up agreement of 28 September 1939 (the German–Soviet Frontier Treaty) in exchange for a larger share of Polish territory. Poland was to be partitioned roughly along the line of the Narew, Vistula and San rivers. The Soviet Union denied the existence of the secret protocol for fifty years, acknowledging it only in 1989, when the Congress of People's Deputies declared it legally untenable from the moment of its signing.

Consequences

Eight days after the signature, on 1 September 1939, Germany invaded Poland, beginning the Second World War. The Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on 17 September, and the two powers partitioned the country. The USSR subsequently annexed the Baltic states and Bessarabia, and attacked Finland in the Winter War of 1939–40. The pact also underpinned large-scale economic cooperation, with Soviet trains delivering grain, oil and other raw materials to Germany until the eve of the German invasion.

The pact remained in force until 22 June 1941, when Germany launched Operation Barbarossa, the massive invasion of the Soviet Union, in breach of the treaty. The anniversary of the signing, 23 August, is now commemorated in several countries and by the European Parliament as a day of remembrance for the victims of Stalinism and Nazism.
