Feud

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A feud, referred to in more extreme cases as a blood feud, vendetta, faida, or private war, is a long-running argument or fight, often between social groups of people, especially families or clans. Feuds begin because one party perceives itself to have been attacked, insulted, injured or wronged by another, and are sustained by cycles of retaliatory violence in which revenge is regarded as a duty of honour.

Characteristics

Anthropologists and historians note that feuding flourishes where central state authority is weak or distrusted, leaving kin groups responsible for their own protection and redress. The logic of the feud is collective: an offence against one member is an offence against the group, and retaliation may fall on any member of the offending group, perpetuating the cycle across generations. Customary rules often regulate legitimate targets, truces, compensation payments (such as wergild or blood money) and mediation by respected third parties.

Historical examples

Feuds are prominent in medieval Icelandic sagas, in the Germanic Faida of the early Middle Ages, in the Albanian highlands under the Kanun (with the practice of gjakmarrja), in Corsican and Sicilian vendetta culture, and in Montenegro, the Scottish Highlands and the Anglo-Scottish border reivers. In the United States the Hatfield–McCoy feud (1878–1891) in the Tug Valley of West Virginia and Kentucky became the archetypal American family feud. In literature, the Montague–Capulet feud drives Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet.

Decline and modern usage

The growth of state monopolies on violence, courts and policing suppressed feuding in most societies, though feud-like cycles persist in some organised-crime and gang contexts. In modern colloquial usage "feud" also denotes any prolonged public quarrel, for example between celebrities, politicians or companies, without violence.
