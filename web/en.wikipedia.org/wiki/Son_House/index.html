Son House

Eddie James "Son" House Jr. (March 21, 1902 – October 19, 1988) was an American Delta blues singer and guitarist, noted for his highly emotional singing and vivid bottleneck slide guitar playing. A preacher in his youth who turned to blues in his mid-twenties, House was a central figure of the Mississippi Delta blues: he mentored and influenced both Robert Johnson and Muddy Waters. After recording a small but legendary body of work in 1930 and 1941–42, he disappeared from music for two decades before being rediscovered during the 1960s folk-blues revival.

Life

House was born in Lyon, Mississippi. Drawn to the church, he was preaching by his teens and initially regarded blues as sinful; he took up guitar at about 25, developing a driving slide style influenced by local players. In 1928 he shot a man — he claimed in self-defence — and served time at Parchman Farm (Mississippi State Penitentiary) until 1929. In 1930, performing alongside Charley Patton and Willie Brown, he recorded for Paramount Records in Grafton, Wisconsin, producing classics including "Preachin' the Blues", "My Black Mama", and "Dry Spell Blues". Alan Lomax recorded him for the Library of Congress in 1941 and 1942, sessions that yielded "Death Letter" precursors and some of his most intense performances. House then moved to Rochester, New York, worked as a railroad porter and cook, and gave up music.

Rediscovery

In 1964 young blues enthusiasts Nick Perls, Dick Waterman, and Phil Spiro tracked House down in Rochester. Re-taught his own songs from his old records (with help from guitarist Al Wilson of Canned Heat), House returned to performing, recording the album Father of Folk Blues (Columbia, 1965) and touring widely — including European tours — until ill health ended his career in 1974. He moved to Detroit in 1976 and died there in 1988. His percussive, passionate style influenced generations of blues and rock musicians, from Muddy Waters to the White Stripes, whose Jack White has cited "Grinnin' in Your Face" as his favourite song.
