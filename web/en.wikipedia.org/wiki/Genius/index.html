Genius

Genius is a characteristic of exceptional intellectual or creative power, marked by achievements that are original, transformative and far beyond ordinary levels of ability. The term derives from the Latin genius, a guardian spirit believed in Roman religion to accompany each person, and gradually came to denote innate talent and later extraordinary accomplishment itself.

History of the concept

Renaissance artists such as Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo were early exemplars of the modern idea. In the 18th and 19th centuries, philosophers including Immanuel Kant treated genius as the capacity to produce work that sets new standards, while the Romantic movement celebrated it as inspired, rule-breaking originality. Francis Galton's Hereditary Genius (1869) began the scientific — and controversial — study of eminence and inheritance.

Measurement and research

Psychology associates genius with very high intelligence, creativity, productivity and domain mastery, though no IQ threshold defines it. Studies of child prodigies, savants, and lifetime achievement suggest combinations of ability, motivation, and opportunity. The MacArthur Fellowship is popularly nicknamed the "genius grant," though the foundation avoids the term.

Criticism

Critics argue the label is socially constructed, historically biased toward men and particular cultures, and obscures the collaborative and cumulative nature of discovery — a point captured by Newton's remark about standing "on the shoulders of giants."
