The Landsat program is the longest-running enterprise for acquisition of satellite imagery of Earth. Jointly managed by NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey, it has provided a continuous global record of the planet's land surface since 1972, at resolutions of 15–120 metres.

History

Proposed in 1966 by USGS director William T. Pecora, the first satellite — launched as ERTS-1 and renamed Landsat 1 — carried a Multispectral Scanner that revolutionised land remote sensing. Successive satellites added the Thematic Mapper (Landsat 4/5), Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (Landsat 7), and the Operational Land Imager and Thermal Infrared Sensor on Landsat 8 (2013) and the near-identical Landsat 9 (2021). Landsat 5 operated for over 28 years, a Guinness world record for an Earth-observing satellite.

Data policy and uses

In 2008 the USGS made the entire Landsat archive free and open over the internet, triggering an explosion in applications: monitoring deforestation and agriculture, mapping urban growth and water bodies, tracking glacier retreat, disaster response, and providing the calibration reference for other sensors such as Europe's Sentinel-2. Landsat images underpin global land-cover datasets used in climate science and land management worldwide.
