Bartending terminology

ScopeTerms used in preparing and serving drinks
ExamplesNeat, on the rocks, straight up, dry, dash, float

Bartending terminology comprises the specialised vocabulary used by bartenders and customers when preparing, ordering, and describing drinks. Many terms specify how a drink is to be served (its temperature, dilution, and garnish), while others describe techniques, measures, and bar organisation.

Serving styles

A drink served neat is poured from the bottle into a glass at room temperature with nothing added. On the rocks means poured over ice cubes. Up or straight up means the drink is chilled — typically shaken or stirred with ice — and then strained into a stemmed glass without ice. A back is a small accompanying drink, usually water or beer, served alongside a spirit; a chaser is drunk immediately after a shot. A nightcap or an aperitif and digestif refer to drinks taken before or after a meal or at bedtime.

Recipe terms

Dry in a martini means less vermouth; dirty adds olive brine; perfect means equal parts sweet and dry vermouth. A dash, splash, and float (a small layer of spirit poured on top) indicate informal measures, while precise measures include the jigger (typically 1.5 US fluid ounces) and the pony (1 ounce). Muddling is pressing ingredients such as mint or fruit; bruising traditionally describes (disapprovingly) shaking a gin drink; building means assembling the drink directly in the serving glass.

Bar practice

To 86 an item is to declare it unavailable. A well drink uses the house (rail) spirits kept in the speed rack, as opposed to call and top-shelf brands requested by name. Free pouring measures by count rather than with a jigger. Orders may be virgin (non-alcoholic versions of cocktails) and garnishes include the twist of citrus peel and the flag (orange slice and cherry).
