Civil law (legal system)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Civil law is a legal system originating in Italy and France, which has been adopted in large parts of the world. It is characterised by comprehensive, continuously updated legal codes that form the core of the system and specify all matters capable of being brought before a court, the applicable procedure and appropriate sanctions. Civil law is the most widespread legal tradition, used in continental Europe, Latin America, much of Africa and parts of Asia, frequently in mixed systems with customary or religious law.

History

Its roots lie in Roman law, above all Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis (6th century AD), rediscovered and studied in medieval universities. Canon law and the ius commune developed the tradition; the great modern codifications — the French Civil Code (1804, the "Napoleonic Code"), the German Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (1900), and codes in Switzerland, Japan and Latin America — exported the model widely through empire and voluntary reception.

Operation

Judges in the classical account apply codes to facts, with scholarship (doctrine) highly influential and precedent formally less binding than in common law — though modern practice in both traditions has converged considerably. Criminal procedure historically follows an inquisitorial model, with an investigating judge directing the inquiry, contrasted with the adversarial common-law trial. Sub-fields are codified separately: civil codes govern persons, property, obligations and family matters; commercial, penal and administrative codes cover other domains.
