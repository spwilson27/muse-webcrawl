Unsafe at Any Speed: The Designed-In Dangers of the American Automobile is a nonfiction book by consumer advocate Ralph Nader, first published in 1965. It accused American automobile manufacturers of resisting the introduction of safety features, such as seat belts, and of spending more on styling than on protecting occupants. The book became a bestseller and is credited with helping to establish modern consumer protection and automobile safety regulation in the United States.

Contents

The book's famous first chapter, "The Sporty Corvair — the One-Car Accident," attacked the rear-engined Chevrolet Corvair for its swing-axle suspension, which Nader argued could cause the car to "tuck under" and roll over in hard cornering. Later chapters criticized Detroit's emphasis on horsepower and annual styling changes, the industry's dismissal of crashworthiness engineering, and the harm done by air pollution from automobiles.

Industry response and fallout

General Motors responded by attempting to discredit Nader, hiring private detectives to investigate his private life and trying to entrap him. When the campaign came to light, GM president James Roche apologized to Nader before a Senate subcommittee in 1966. Nader sued GM for invasion of privacy and won a settlement of $425,000, which he used to fund further consumer advocacy work.

Legacy

Publicity surrounding the book and the GM scandal contributed directly to the passage of the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act in 1966, which set federal safety standards for automobiles, and to the creation of the agency that became the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. The book launched Nader's career as America's most prominent consumer advocate.
