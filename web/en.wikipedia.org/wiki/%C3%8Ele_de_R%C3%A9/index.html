Île de Ré

Île de Ré is an island off the west coast of France near La Rochelle, in the Charente-Maritime department. About 30 kilometres long and 85 square kilometres in area, it has been joined to the mainland by the Île de Ré bridge since 1988. The island is a popular holiday destination known for its flat cycling terrain, salt marshes, oyster beds, whitewashed villages, and the fortified harbour town of Saint-Martin-de-Ré, part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing of Vauban's fortifications.

History

The island's salt pans and vineyards were worked from the Middle Ages, and Cistercian monks of the Abbaye des Châteliers were among its early developers. In 1627, during the Anglo-French War, English forces under the Duke of Buckingham landed on the island and besieged Saint-Martin-de-Ré in an attempt to relieve Huguenot La Rochelle; they were defeated, and Vauban later refortified the town. In the 19th and 20th centuries the island held a penal colony from which convicts, including Alfred Dreyfus at an earlier stage of his journey, embarked for French Guiana.

Economy and life

Salt production in the northern marshes, oyster farming, fishing, and potato growing (the early pomme de terre de l'île de Ré holds AOC status) continue alongside tourism. An extensive network of cycle paths criss-crosses the island, and its donkeys — traditionally dressed in culottes to protect them from marsh insects — are a local emblem.
