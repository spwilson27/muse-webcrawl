Spanish conquest of the Maya

The Spanish conquest of the Maya was a protracted campaign by Spanish conquistadores and their allies against the Late Postclassic Maya states and polities in the Yucatán Peninsula, Petén Basin, and the highlands of Guatemala and Honduras. Unlike the rapid collapse of the Aztec and Inca empires, the Maya region was politically fragmented into numerous competing provinces, and conquest took nearly two centuries, from first contact in 1511 until the fall of the last independent Maya kingdom, the Itza of Nojpetén, in 1697.

First contact

Europeans first encountered the Maya in 1502, when Christopher Columbus met a Maya trading canoe off the Bay Islands of Honduras. In 1511, survivors of a Spanish shipwreck, including Gerónimo de Aguilar and Gonzalo Guerrero, washed ashore in Yucatán. The expeditions of Francisco Hernández de Córdoba (1517), Juan de Grijalva (1518), and Hernán Cortés (1519) followed, charting the coast and clashing with Maya forces, notably at the Battle of Champotón, where Hernández de Córdoba was fatally wounded.

Conquest of Yucatán

Francisco de Montejo and his son launched campaigns into Yucatán beginning in 1527. Maya resistance, difficult terrain, and the absence of a single capital to capture made progress slow. Mérida was founded in 1542, but a great Maya revolt in 1546 had to be suppressed. Franciscan missionaries, including Diego de Landa, accompanied the conquest and destroyed many Maya codices and idols in campaigns against idolatry, an act Landa later described in his Relación de las cosas de Yucatán.

Guatemalan highlands

In the highlands, Pedro de Alvarado invaded in 1524, defeating the K'iche' Maya at Quetzaltenango and allying with the Kaqchikel against their rivals before turning on them as well. The Mam, Poqomam, and other peoples resisted for years; the last highland stronghold at Zaculeu fell in 1525, but remote regions remained restive.

The Petén and the last Itza kingdom

The dense forests of the Petén Basin sheltered independent Maya kingdoms long after the north was colonized. Cortés passed through in 1525, meeting the Itza king Ajaw Kan Ek'. Franciscan missionaries visited Nojpetén, the Itza island capital on Lake Petén Itzá, in the 17th century, but conversion failed. In 1697, Martín de Ursúa launched an expedition from Yucatán, built a road to the lake, and stormed Nojpetén on March 13, 1697 — the final conquest of the last independent Maya state, some 175 years after the fall of Tenochtitlan.
