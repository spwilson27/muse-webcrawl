Monohybrid cross

A monohybrid cross is a mating between two organisms that are heterozygous for a single trait — that is, a cross tracking the inheritance of one gene with two alleles. The classic examples come from Gregor Mendel's experiments with pea plants, such as crossing heterozygotes (Tt × Tt) for plant height.

In the first generation, crossing two homozygous parents (TT × tt) produces offspring that are all heterozygous and display the dominant trait. When those heterozygotes are self-crossed, the second generation shows a characteristic phenotypic ratio of approximately 3:1 (three showing the dominant trait to one showing the recessive trait), corresponding to a genotypic ratio of 1:2:1. These ratios follow from the segregation of alleles during meiosis and can be visualized with a Punnett square. A test cross, in which an individual showing the dominant phenotype is crossed with a homozygous recessive individual, can reveal the unknown genotype.
