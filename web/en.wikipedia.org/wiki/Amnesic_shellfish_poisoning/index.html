Amnesic shellfish poisoning

Amnesic shellfish poisoning (ASP) is a human illness caused by consuming shellfish contaminated with domoic acid, a neurotoxin produced by marine diatoms of the genus Pseudo-nitzschia. It is named for its most distinctive effect: potentially permanent loss of short-term memory. ASP is one of several shellfish poisoning syndromes, alongside paralytic, diarrhetic and neurotoxic shellfish poisoning.

The 1987 outbreak

ASP was first recognised in November–December 1987 on Prince Edward Island, Canada, where more than 100 people became ill after eating cultivated blue mussels. Three elderly patients died. Investigators traced the toxin to domoic acid produced by a bloom of the diatom Pseudo-nitzschia multiseries in the island's estuaries. Some survivors were left with lasting memory deficits resembling severe anterograde amnesia, together with damage to the hippocampus and amygdala found in follow-up studies.

Mechanism and symptoms

Domoic acid is a structural analogue of the neurotransmitter glutamate and acts as a potent agonist at kainate and AMPA receptors, over-exciting neurons — especially in the hippocampus — to the point of excitotoxic injury. Symptoms begin with gastrointestinal distress (vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal cramps) within 24 hours, followed in severe cases by headache, dizziness, seizures, coma and memory loss. There is no antidote; treatment is supportive.

Monitoring and wildlife effects

Following 1987, Canada and many other countries set a regulatory limit of 20 mg of domoic acid per kilogram of shellfish tissue and established monitoring programmes that close fisheries during Pseudo-nitzschia blooms. Domoic acid also periodically poisons marine wildlife: in 1991 it killed pelicans and cormorants in Monterey Bay, California (an event linked to the birds' behaviour that once inspired local unease), and in 1998 it caused the deaths of hundreds of California sea lions. Major blooms affected the US West Coast razor clam and Dungeness crab fisheries in 2015–16.
