The Marine Stewardship Council (MSC) is an international non-profit organisation that operates a certification and eco-labelling programme for wild-caught seafood. Fisheries that meet the MSC's standard for sustainable fishing may use the blue MSC label on their products, signalling to consumers that the seafood comes from a well-managed and sustainable source.

History

The MSC was founded in 1997 as a joint initiative between the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF) and Unilever, then the world's largest buyer of frozen fish. It became fully independent in 1999. The first fishery to be certified was the Western Australian rock lobster fishery in 2000, and the first labelled retail products appeared the same year. Over the following decades the programme expanded to hundreds of fisheries around the world, covering a significant share of the global wild marine catch.

The MSC standard

The MSC Fisheries Standard is built on three core principles: sustainable fish stocks, minimising environmental impact, and effective fisheries management. Fisheries are assessed by independent, accredited certification bodies against a set of performance indicators, and assessments are open to stakeholder consultation. Certification lasts five years, with annual surveillance audits, and certified fisheries may be required to complete improvement actions as conditions of certification.

A separate Chain of Custody standard ensures that MSC-labelled products can be traced through the supply chain from the certified fishery to the consumer.

Criticism

The programme has been criticised by some environmental groups and scientists who argue that certain certified fisheries do not deserve the label, that objection procedures are difficult to use, and that the organisation's reliance on certification fees creates conflicts of interest. The MSC responds that its standard is science-based, transparent and subject to continual review.
