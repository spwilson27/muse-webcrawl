Charles Vane (c. 1680 – 29 March 1721) was an English pirate who operated in the Caribbean during the final years of the Golden Age of Piracy. A former associate of Henry Jennings, Vane was based at Nassau in the Bahamas and became notorious for his refusal of the King's pardon and for his cruelty, including the torture of captured sailors. His quartermaster was John "Calico Jack" Rackham, who deposed him in 1718.

Early career

Vane began his piratical career under Henry Jennings, taking part in the 1716 raid on the camp of survivors of the Spanish treasure fleet wrecked off Florida. By 1717 he was captain of his own vessel, operating from the pirate republic at Nassau and plundering shipping off the North American coast and in the Caribbean.

Refusal of the pardon

When Governor Woodes Rogers arrived at Nassau in July 1718 with a royal pardon for pirates who surrendered, Vane refused. As Rogers's squadron blockaded the harbour, Vane fired a captured French ship as a fireship against the warships and escaped in his brigantine, defiantly shouting his refusal. He continued his depredations, at one point briefly retaking Nassau's fort with a smaller vessel.

Deposition and death

In November 1718 Vane's crew, angered by his refusal to attack a French warship, deposed him in favour of Calico Jack Rackham and set Vane adrift in a small sloop with his loyalists. He continued pirating but was wrecked by a storm in the Bay Islands off Honduras in 1719 and stranded for months. Rescued — and then recognised — by a passing ship whose captain had once been his prisoner, Vane was taken to Jamaica, tried at Port Royal, and hanged on 29 March 1721. His body was hung in chains at Gun Cay as a warning.
