Legionella pneumophila is a species of Gram-negative bacterium in the genus Legionella and the principal cause of Legionnaires' disease, a severe form of pneumonia. It accounts for roughly 90% of reported legionellosis cases, with serogroup 1 responsible for the large majority. The species name reflects its affinity for the lung (pneumophila, "air/lung-loving").

Characteristics

L. pneumophila is a thin, aerobic, flagellated, non-spore-forming rod. It is a facultative intracellular parasite with fastidious growth requirements — it needs L-cysteine and ferric iron and grows in the laboratory on buffered charcoal yeast extract (BCYE) agar at 35–37 °C, forming ground-glass colonies in 3–5 days. The genome of the type strains (e.g. the Philadelphia strain, ~3.4 Mb) was sequenced in 2004. Its lipopolysaccharide is a major virulence factor and the target of the widely used urinary antigen test, which detects serogroup 1.

Life cycle and virulence

In nature, L. pneumophila multiplies inside free-living amoebae such as Acanthamoeba and Vermamoeba and within biofilms in freshwater and engineered water systems. The bacterium has a biphasic life cycle alternating between a replicative form and a highly infectious, stress-resistant transmissive form. After inhalation in contaminated aerosols, it is phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages. Using the Dot/Icm type IV secretion system, it injects hundreds of effector proteins into the host cell, blocking fusion of the phagosome with lysosomes and building a ribosome-studded "Legionella-containing vacuole" in which it replicates until the cell lyses. Virulence factors include the Mip protein, the pore-forming toxin RtxA and its unusual LPS.

Clinical significance

Infection produces Legionnaires' disease (incubation 2–10 days; fever, cough, dyspnoea and often gastrointestinal and neurological symptoms, with case fatality around 10%, higher in nosocomial and immunocompromised cases) and the milder, non-pneumonic Pontiac fever. The species was identified in 1977 after the famous 1976 Philadelphia outbreak at an American Legion convention. Outbreaks have since been traced to cooling towers, hot tubs, hospital water systems, and fountains; notable incidents include Murcia, Spain (2001, one of the largest community outbreaks), Pas-de-Calais, France (2003–04) and the Bronx, New York (2015).

Treatment and prevention

Because the organism is intracellular, effective antibiotics are those that penetrate macrophages — a respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin) or a macrolide (azithromycin) is standard; beta-lactams are ineffective. Prevention is environmental: maintaining water systems to suppress growth (temperature control, disinfection, cooling-tower treatment) rather than vaccination, for which none exists.
