Acyl chloride

In organic chemistry, an acyl chloride (or acid chloride) is an organic compound with the functional group −C(=O)Cl, written as RCOCl, where R is an organic side chain. Acyl chlorides are among the most reactive derivatives of carboxylic acids and are widely used as reagents for introducing acyl groups into other molecules.

Preparation

Acyl chlorides are prepared from carboxylic acids by reaction with chlorinating agents such as thionyl chloride (SOCl2), phosphorus pentachloride (PCl5), phosphorus trichloride (PCl3), or oxalyl chloride. The simplest and most important examples include acetyl chloride, benzoyl chloride, and oxalyl chloride itself.

Reactions

The carbonyl carbon of an acyl chloride is strongly electrophilic, and chloride is an excellent leaving group, so acyl chlorides react readily with nucleophiles:

  • With water — hydrolysis to the carboxylic acid, often violently, releasing hydrogen chloride
  • With alcohols — forming esters
  • With ammonia and amines — forming amides
  • With aromatic compounds — Friedel–Crafts acylation in the presence of a Lewis acid such as AlCl3
  • With organometallic reagents — ketones or tertiary alcohols
  • Reduction — to aldehydes (Rosenmund reduction) or primary alcohols

Properties and hazards

Acyl chlorides are typically colorless, pungent liquids that fume in moist air as they hydrolyze to give hydrogen chloride; they are lachrymatory and corrosive and must be handled with care.
