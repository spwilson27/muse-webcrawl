Meditation music

[Image: Tibetan singing bowls, a staple sound of meditation recordings]
Singing bowls and drones characterise much meditation music

Meditation music is music intended to accompany meditation, yoga practice, relaxation and related contemplative disciplines. It is typically slow, quiet and repetitive, with long sustained tones, minimal melody and little rhythmic pulse, designed to support a calm, focused state of mind rather than to be actively listened to.

Characteristics and traditions

Sound has accompanied meditation for centuries: chanting in Hindu and Buddhist practice (mantras such as Om), Tibetan singing bowls and tingsha cymbals, Japanese shakuhachi honkyoku played by Zen monks, Indian classical ragas and drone instruments such as the tanpura, and Gregorian chant in Christian contemplative traditions. Modern commercial meditation music emerged in the 1970s as part of the New Age movement, drawing on these sources and on ambient music pioneered by Brian Eno, and typically features synthesiser pads, nature sounds (rain, ocean waves, birdsong), bells, flutes and slow piano.

Uses and research

Meditation music is used in meditation classes, yoga studios, spas, massage and complementary therapy, and — in the streaming era — as sleep aids and study or focus soundtracks, reaching very large audiences on video and music platforms. Studies of music and relaxation find modest effects on self-reported stress, heart rate and blood pressure, though researchers note that personal preference strongly influences outcomes; binaural-beat recordings are marketed with stronger claims for which evidence remains limited and contested.
