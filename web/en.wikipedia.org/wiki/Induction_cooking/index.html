Induction cooking

Induction cooking is a method of cooking in which a pan is heated directly by electromagnetic induction, rather than by heat transferred from a flame or an electric heating element. An induction cooktop contains a coil of copper wire beneath a ceramic-glass surface; a rapidly alternating electric current through the coil produces an oscillating magnetic field, which induces eddy currents in the cookware and heats it through resistive losses.

Only the pan is heated; the surface stays relatively cool.

How it works

For induction to work, cookware must be made of, or clad with, a ferromagnetic material such as cast iron or many stainless steels. The pan itself becomes the heating element, so energy transfer is fast and efficient — typically around 80–90% of the electrical energy is delivered to the pan, compared with roughly 70% for a conventional electric coil and under 40% for gas. Because the cooktop surface is heated only secondarily by the hot pan, it cools quickly once the pan is removed, and temperature control is rapid and precise.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include speed (water can boil faster than on most gas burners), safety, an easy-to-clean flat surface, and no indoor combustion pollutants of the kind produced by gas stoves. Disadvantages include the need for compatible cookware, a characteristic faint hum or clicking from cooling fans and pan vibration in some models, and higher initial cost in some markets. Induction cannot heat most glass, ceramic, aluminium or copper pans unless they incorporate a ferromagnetic base layer.

History

Patents for induction cooking appeared in the early 20th century, and demonstration units were exhibited in the 1930s and 1950s, but high electronics costs delayed widespread adoption. Modern units using inexpensive high-power semiconductors became common in Europe and Asia from the 1990s and 2000s onward, and have since gained popularity worldwide, helped by energy-efficiency regulations and concerns about gas cooking indoors.
