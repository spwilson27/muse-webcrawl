Fermat pseudoprime

In number theory, a Fermat pseudoprime is a composite number that passes Fermat's little theorem test for some base. Fermat's little theorem states that if p is prime and a is not divisible by p, then ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). A composite number n satisfying an−1 ≡ 1 (mod n) for a particular base a coprime to n is called a Fermat pseudoprime to base a.

Examples

The smallest Fermat pseudoprime to base 2 is 341 = 11 × 31: although composite, 2340 ≡ 1 (mod 341). Base-2 pseudoprimes (also called Poulet numbers when further conditions hold in some terminologies, and sequence A001567 in the OEIS) begin 341, 561, 645, 1105, 1387, 1729, 1905, … Pseudoprimes to base 2 were already known to Chinese mathematicians in antiquity in the mistaken conjecture that 2n−1 − 2 being divisible by n implies n is prime.

Carmichael numbers

Some composites pass the test for every base coprime to them: these are the Carmichael numbers, the smallest being 561 = 3 × 11 × 17. For them, no amount of Fermat testing with coprime bases can prove compositeness; stronger tests such as the Miller–Rabin primality test, which also detects Carmichael numbers as composite, are used instead.

Properties

There are infinitely many Fermat pseudoprimes to any given base (indeed infinitely many Carmichael numbers, as shown by Alford, Granville and Pomerance in 1994), yet they become proportionally rarer as numbers grow. The Fermat test remains the basis of practical probabilistic primality testing, refined by Miller–Rabin and Baillie–PSW.
