Hib vaccine

The Hib vaccine is a vaccine that protects against infection by Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), a bacterium that was once the leading cause of bacterial meningitis in children under five, as well as a cause of epiglottitis, pneumonia, sepsis and septic arthritis. Modern Hib vaccines are conjugate vaccines in which the bacterial capsular polysaccharide (polyribosylribitol phosphate, PRP) is chemically linked to a protein carrier such as tetanus toxoid, making it immunogenic in infants.

History

A plain polysaccharide vaccine was licensed in the United States in 1985 but was poorly immunogenic in the youngest children, who were at greatest risk. The first conjugate Hib vaccine was licensed in 1987, and improved conjugates followed by 1990. Routine infant immunisation caused invasive Hib disease in young children to fall by more than 99% in countries that adopted it.

Use

The World Health Organization recommends that Hib vaccine be included in all national infant immunisation programmes, usually given as part of a combination vaccine such as DTaP-IPV-Hib or the pentavalent/hexavalent combinations, in a series of two or three doses in the first year of life, sometimes with a booster in the second year.

Safety

Hib vaccine is considered very safe; reactions are generally limited to mild redness or swelling at the injection site and low-grade fever.
