Einstein notation

In mathematics, especially the usage of linear algebra in mathematical physics and differential geometry, Einstein notation (also known as the Einstein summation convention or Einstein summation notation) is a notational convention that implies summation over a set of indexed terms in a formula, thus achieving brevity. It was introduced by Albert Einstein in 1916 in his exposition of general relativity.

Repeated indices imply summation in Einstein notation.

The convention

According to the convention, when an index variable appears twice in a single term — once as a superscript (upper index) and once as a subscript (lower index) — it implies summation over all possible values of that index. For example, the expression y = cixi means y = Σi cixi. Repeated "dummy" indices are summed over, while "free" indices, occurring once, range independently and must match on both sides of an equation.

Vector and tensor operations

The convention compactly expresses common operations: a dot product a·b becomes aibi; the contraction of a matrix with a vector, (Ax)i = Aikxk; and the trace of a matrix becomes Aii. Upper and lower indices distinguish contravariant from covariant components, and indices are raised and lowered with the metric tensor, as in Tμ = gμνTν.

Applications

Einstein notation is standard in general relativity, continuum mechanics, electromagnetism in relativistic form and quantum field theory, where spacetime indices (often Greek letters μ, ν running over 0–3) pervade the equations.
