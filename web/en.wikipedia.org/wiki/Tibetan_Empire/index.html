Tibetan Empire

Tibetan Empire (བོད་ཆེན་པོ, Bod chen po)
Existence: 618–842 AD
Capital: Lhasa
Common language: Old Tibetan
Government: Monarchy (Tsenpo)
Greatest extent: approx. 7th–9th centuries

The Tibetan Empire was an empire centred on the Tibetan Plateau, formed in the 7th century under the Yarlung dynasty and lasting until a civil war and collapse in the 840s. Founded by Namri Songtsen and established by his son Songtsen Gampo (c. 618–650), who unified the plateau and chose Lhasa as his capital, at its height the empire controlled an area spanning parts of present-day China, Nepal, India, Pakistan, and Central Asia, and rivalled Tang-dynasty China for supremacy along the Silk Road.

The Tibetan Empire at its greatest extent, c. 790s, stretching from the Tarim Basin to Bengal.

Expansion

Songtsen Gampo's reign saw the creation of a Tibetan written script (traditionally credited to Thonmi Sambhota), the first codification of law, and marriage alliances with Nepal and China — Princess Wencheng's arrival in 641 bringing a famous statue of the Buddha to Lhasa. Under Trisong Detsen (755–797) the empire reached its zenith: Tibetan armies captured the Tang capital Chang'an in 763, controlled the Hexi Corridor and the Dunhuang region, and extended influence deep into Central Asia. Trisong Detsen also invited the Indian masters Śāntarakṣita and Padmasambhava, founding Samye monastery (c. 779) and establishing Buddhism as a state religion.

Treaties and decline

The Sino-Tibetan treaty of 821–823, inscribed on a pillar still standing in Lhasa, fixed the borders between the two empires in a famous declaration that Tibetans would be happy in Tibet and Chinese happy in China. After the assassination of the emperor Ralpachen and the anti-Buddhist backlash under Langdarma, whose assassination in 842 triggered civil war, the empire fragmented into competing principalities, ending the imperial period and ushering in Tibet's "Era of Fragmentation".

Legacy

The empire spread Tibetan language and culture across the plateau and into Central Asia, introduced and institutionalised Buddhism in Tibet, and left Old Tibetan documents — notably those recovered from Dunhuang — that form the earliest extensive records of Tibetan history.
