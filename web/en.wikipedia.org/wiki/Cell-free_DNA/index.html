Cell-free DNA

Cell-free DNA circulates as short fragments in blood plasma.

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is degraded DNA fragments released into the bloodstream and other body fluids. It is found in plasma, serum, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid, and in healthy individuals it derives mainly from the normal turnover of blood cells through apoptosis.

Characteristics

cfDNA fragments are typically short, with a characteristic peak around 166 base pairs, reflecting cleavage around nucleosomes during apoptosis. Concentrations are low in healthy people but can rise with tissue injury, inflammation, exercise, cancer, and other conditions.

Clinical applications

The best-known application is noninvasive prenatal testing: in 1997 Dennis Lo and colleagues demonstrated that fetal DNA circulates in maternal plasma, enabling screening for fetal chromosomal abnormalities such as trisomy 21 from a maternal blood sample. In oncology, tumour-derived cfDNA (circulating tumour DNA, ctDNA) is the basis of "liquid biopsy" tests used to detect mutations, guide targeted therapy, and monitor disease. cfDNA analysis is also used in transplant medicine, where donor-derived cfDNA can signal organ rejection.
