The spectacled caiman (Caiman crocodilus), also known as the common caiman, white caiman, or speckled caiman, is a crocodilian of the family Alligatoridae native to Central and South America. Its common name comes from a bony ridge between the eyes that resembles the bridge of a pair of spectacles.

Description

The spectacled caiman is a small to mid-sized crocodilian. Males typically grow to 1.8–2 m (5.9–6.6 ft) and females to about 1.2–1.4 m (3.9–4.6 ft); exceptional males may reach 2.5 m. Juveniles are yellowish with dark crossbands and spots that fade with age; adults are a dull olive-green. The snout is moderately broad and triangular, and the fourth mandibular tooth fits into a notch in the upper jaw, as in other caimans.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranges from southern Mexico through Central America and across northern South America to northern Argentina, inhabiting slow-moving rivers, lakes, wetlands, and flooded savannas. It has been introduced to Florida, Cuba, and Puerto Rico. It tolerates a degree of salinity and is the most widespread of the New World caimans.

Ecology

Spectacled caimans feed on fish, amphibians, snails, small mammals, birds, and other reptiles, shifting diet with size and season. Females build mound nests of vegetation and soil during the wet season, laying roughly 14–40 eggs; the female guards the nest and assists hatchlings to the water. The species is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN, though local populations have been affected by hunting for skins and the pet trade.
