Linguistic purism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Linguistic purism comprises the practice of defining or recognising one variety of a language as being purer or of intrinsically higher quality than others. It usually involves the proscription of certain words or grammatical forms that are regarded as corrupting influences — most commonly loanwords from other languages, but also neologisms, slang, archaisms or usages associated with particular social groups. Purism is closely connected with language planning and with national identity.

Forms and examples

Purist movements have often been institutionalised. The Accademia della Crusca in Florence (founded 1583) and the Académie française (1635) were established to regulate Italian and French respectively; the French academy and French law still campaign against anglicisms, promoting coinages such as courriel for email. Icelandic is known for systematic purism, coining words from native roots — sími ("thread") for telephone, tölva for computer. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Turkish language reform replaced many Arabic and Persian loanwords with Turkic coinages; Croatian, Tamil, Hebrew and Hindi have all had notable purist currents, while "Anglish" is a hobbyist project to purge English of its non-Germanic vocabulary.

Criticism

Descriptive linguists generally criticise purism as based on false premises: languages change continuously, borrowed words are a normal product of contact, and "purity" tends to reflect social or national ideology rather than linguistic fact. Purist interventions rarely stop change, though they can succeed in coining official terminology and in shaping registers of formal usage.
