Ukiyo-e (浮世絵, "pictures of the floating world") is a genre of Japanese art that flourished from the 17th through 19th centuries. Its artists produced woodblock prints and paintings of such subjects as female beauties, kabuki actors, sumo wrestlers, scenes from history and folk tales, travel scenes and landscapes, flora and fauna, and erotica (shunga). The term "floating world" refers to the licensed pleasure quarters and urban entertainments of Edo (modern Tokyo).

The Great Wave off Kanagawa (c. 1831) by Katsushika Hokusai, the most famous ukiyo-e print.

History

Ukiyo-e developed in Edo during the Tokugawa shogunate, as a merchant-class culture grew rich enough to patronise art. Hishikawa Moronobu is credited with the first prints in the 1670s; early prints were monotone or hand-coloured. Full-colour printing (nishiki-e) was introduced around 1765, associated with Suzuki Harunobu. Kitagawa Utamaro became famous for studies of beautiful women (bijin-ga), and Tōshūsai Sharaku for dramatic actor portraits.

In the 19th century, Katsushika Hokusai and Utagawa Hiroshige transformed the landscape print with series such as Hokusai's Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji and Hiroshige's The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō. Production involved a division of labour: the artist designed the image, a carver cut the wooden blocks (one per colour), a printer applied pigments, and a publisher financed and marketed the work.

Influence

After Japan opened to trade in the 1850s, ukiyo-e flooded into Europe and fuelled the craze of Japonisme, profoundly influencing Impressionist and Post-Impressionist painters such as Monet, Degas, Toulouse-Lautrec, and Van Gogh, as well as Art Nouveau design and modern graphic art.
