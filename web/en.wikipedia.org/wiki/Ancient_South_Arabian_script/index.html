The Ancient South Arabian script (also known as the musnad) is an abjad of 29 consonantal signs used in ancient Yemen and surrounding regions of the southern Arabian Peninsula from about the 9th century BC until the 6th century AD. It wrote the Old South Arabian languages — Sabaic, Minaic, Qatabanic and Hadramitic — the languages of the kingdoms of Saba, Ma'in, Qataban and Hadramawt.

Forms and use

The script is noted for its highly geometric, monumental letterforms, carved in formal inscriptions on stone and bronze recording royal building works, dedications to gods such as Almaqah, irrigation works, legal decrees and victories. A cursive everyday form (zabūr) was written with a stylus on wooden sticks and palm-leaf stalks; thousands of these mundane documents — letters, receipts and contracts from ancient Yemeni archives — have been recovered since the 1970s. Word division is marked by a vertical stroke, and texts run from right to left, though the earliest inscriptions may be boustrophedon.

History and descendants

The script derives ultimately from the Proto-Sinaitic/Canaanite alphabet tradition. Its use declined as Arabic spread and ended with the coming of Islam in the 7th century. The closely related alphabets carried across the Red Sea became the basis of the Ethiopic (Ge'ez) script; debate continues over the exact line of descent. The musnad was encoded in Unicode in 2009 as the Old South Arabian block.
