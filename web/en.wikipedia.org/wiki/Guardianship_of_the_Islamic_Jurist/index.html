Guardianship of the Islamic Jurist

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Guardianship of the Islamic Jurist (Persian: ولایت فقیه, Velâyat-e Faqîh; Arabic: ولاية الفقيه, Wilāyat al-Faqīh) is a doctrine in Twelver Shia Islam which holds that, during the occultation of the Twelfth Imam, Islamic jurists (fuqaha) hold religious and political authority over the community. In its most developed form it underpins the system of government of the Islamic Republic of Iran, where a senior jurist serves as Supreme Leader.

Development of the doctrine

Classical Shia jurisprudence gave jurists limited guardianship functions, such as administering the property of orphans. Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini expanded the concept in a series of lectures delivered in Najaf in 1970, published as Islamic Government (Hokumat-e Eslami), arguing that a just faqih should exercise the same governmental authority as the Prophet and the Imams, and that such rule is necessary to implement Islamic law.

In the Islamic Republic of Iran

After the Iranian Revolution of 1979, Khomeini's theory was written into the Constitution of Iran (Articles 5 and 107–112). The Vali-e Faqih, or Supreme Leader, is commander-in-chief of the armed forces, appoints the head of the judiciary and the commanders of the Revolutionary Guards, and supervises the general policies of the state. In 1988 Khomeini articulated the doctrine of "absolute guardianship" (velayat-e motlaqeh), asserting that the Islamic ruler may override even secondary religious ordinances when state interest requires.

Criticism

The doctrine has been criticised both by secular Iranians and by senior Shia clerics — including Grand Ayatollahs in Najaf and Qom — who argue that jurists' guardianship was never meant to constitute direct political rule, and that the theory conflates religious authority with state power.
