The Year of the Elephant (Arabic: عام الفيل, ʿĀm al-Fīl) is the traditional name for the year in which Abraha, the Aksumite ruler of Yemen, marched on Mecca with an army said to include war elephants, intending to destroy the Kaaba. The event is conventionally dated to about 570 CE and is remembered in Islamic tradition as the year of the birth of the prophet Muhammad.

The expedition

According to Islamic sources, Abraha — a Christian viceroy of the Kingdom of Aksum who had seized power in Yemen — built a great cathedral at Sanaa and sought to divert Arab pilgrimage from Mecca to his own shrine. When the Meccans resisted, he led an expedition northward with elephants, the mightiest of which was named Mahmud. As the army approached Mecca, tradition relates that the elephant refused to advance on the sanctuary, and that flocks of birds (ababil) pelted the soldiers with stones of baked clay, destroying the army. The episode is commemorated in the Quran's short chapter Surah Al-Fil, "The Elephant."

Dating

Historians debate the date. South Arabian inscriptions record Abraha's campaigns, and some scholars have proposed a date around 552 CE for the expedition, which would separate the event from the traditional year of Muhammad's birth. Others defend the conventional chronology of about 570 CE. Whatever its exact date, the failed expedition checked Aksumite expansion into Arabia shortly before the rise of Islam.
