Flaco Jiménez

Flaco Jiménez

Born: Leonardo Jiménez, 11 March 1939, San Antonio, Texas, U.S.

Instruments: Button accordion, vocals

Genres: Conjunto, Tejano, Tex-Mex, norteño, country, rock

Leonardo "Flaco" Jiménez (born 11 March 1939) is an American singer and accordionist from San Antonio, Texas. A master of the three-row button accordion, he is the best-known ambassador of conjunto and Tejano music, and over a career spanning more than seven decades he has carried the South Texas border sound into country, rock and pop through collaborations with artists in many genres.

Early life and career

Jiménez comes from a musical family: his father, Santiago Jiménez Sr., was a pioneering conjunto accordionist who adapted the German and Czech polka traditions of Texas immigrants to Spanish-language dance music, and his brother Santiago Jiménez Jr. also became a noted accordionist. "Flaco" — "Skinny" — was his father's nickname before it became his. He began performing as a teenager and recorded prolifically for local labels, developing a fluid, ornamented style that made him the standard against which conjunto accordionists are measured.

Collaborations and recognition

In the 1970s Jiménez reached wider audiences through work with Doug Sahm and Ry Cooder, whose 1976 album Chicken Skin Music introduced him to rock listeners. He later toured and recorded with the Texas Tornados — the Tex-Mex supergroup with Sahm, Freddy Fender and Augie Meyers — and played sessions for artists including Bob Dylan, the Rolling Stones, Dwight Yoakam, Buck Owens and Linda Ronstadt. He won multiple Grammy Awards, including a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 2015, and in 2012 received the National Heritage Fellowship from the National Endowment for the Arts. In 2022 he was among the inaugural inductees celebrated for conjunto music's place in American roots music.

Legacy

Jiménez is credited with preserving and modernising conjunto, a working-class Mexican-American music built on the diatonic accordion and the bajo sexto. His recordings — hundreds of singles and albums since the 1950s — remain central to the Tex-Mex repertoire in South Texas dance halls.
