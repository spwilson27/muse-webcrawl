The Latin Church (Latin: Ecclesia Latina) is by far the largest of the 24 autonomous (sui iuris) particular churches that together form the Catholic Church. Headed by the Pope as Bishop of Rome and Patriarch of the West in tradition, it comprises the great majority of the world's Catholics, mostly in Western Europe, the Americas, sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia.

History and character

The Latin Church developed in the Latin-speaking western Roman Empire, using Latin as its liturgical and scholarly language. Its law is codified in the 1983 Code of Canon Law, distinct from the code governing the 23 Eastern Catholic Churches. Its dominant liturgy is the Roman Rite, alongside smaller Latin liturgical rites such as the Ambrosian and Mozarabic rites. Clerical celibacy is the general discipline for its priests, in contrast to the Eastern churches.
