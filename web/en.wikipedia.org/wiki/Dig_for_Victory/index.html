Dig for Victory was a campaign launched by the British Ministry of Agriculture in October 1939, shortly after the outbreak of the Second World War, urging the population to grow their own food in gardens and allotments. With German U-boats blockading imports and merchant shipping under attack, Britain — which had imported roughly two-thirds of its food before the war — needed to maximise home production and free shipping capacity for war materials.

The campaign

The campaign's name was coined by the editor of the Daily Express, and its most famous image — a boot on a spade above the words "Dig for Victory" — became one of the best-known British wartime posters. The Ministry issued a stream of practical advice: monthly "Growmore" leaflets, crop plans, radio talks by celebrity gardeners such as C. H. Middleton, and demonstration plots. Public parks, railway embankments, sports grounds and even the moat of the Tower of London were turned over to vegetables; the lawns of Buckingham Palace were dug for crops.

Results

The number of allotments in England and Wales rose from about 815,000 in 1939 to roughly 1.75 million by 1943, and an estimated five million private gardeners also grew food. By 1945 domestic production had increased greatly: vegetable output roughly doubled, imports of food were cut by about half, and the campaign is credited with keeping the population fed under rationing. "Dig for Victory" also became a lasting cultural reference point for self-sufficiency and community gardening, revived during later periods of interest in local food.
