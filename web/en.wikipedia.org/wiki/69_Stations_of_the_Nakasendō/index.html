69 Stations of the Nakasendō

The 69 Stations of the Nakasendō were the rest stops along the Nakasendō, one of the five major routes of Edo-period Japan connecting Edo (modern Tokyo) with Kyoto through the mountainous interior of Honshu. The stations provided lodging, food, porters and horses for travellers, officials and the processions of daimyō travelling under the sankin-kōtai system.

The route

The Nakasendō began at Nihonbashi in Edo, shared its first stretch with the Kōshū Kaidō, and passed through the provinces of Musashi, Kōzuke, Shinano, Mino and Ōmi before ending at Kusatsu, where it joined the Tōkaidō for the final leg to Kyoto. Counting from Itabashi (the first station) to Moriyama (the last), there were 67 stations; including the starting and ending points the conventional count is 69.

Famous stations

Notable post towns include Narai-juku, with its long street of preserved wooden buildings; Magome-juku and Tsumago-juku in the Kiso Valley, now famous for the preserved trail between them; and the checkpoint towns of Fukushima and Usui. The challenging Usui Pass between Sakamoto and Karuizawa was one of the most difficult sections of the road.

In art

The stations were immortalised in the woodblock print series The Sixty-nine Stations of the Kiso Kaidō (1830s–1840s) by Utagawa Hiroshige and Keisai Eisen, a companion to Hiroshige's celebrated Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō.
