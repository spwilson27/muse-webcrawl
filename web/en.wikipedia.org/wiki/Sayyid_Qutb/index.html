Sayyid Quṭb (9 October 1906 – 29 August 1966) was an Egyptian author, educator, poet, and a leading member of the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood in the 1950s and 1960s. His writings, especially Ma'alim fi al-Tariq (Milestones, 1964) and his Qur'an commentary Fi Zilal al-Qur'an (In the Shade of the Qur'an), became foundational texts of modern Islamist thought.

Life

Qutb was born in the village of Musha in Upper Egypt and educated in Cairo, where he worked as a teacher and a literary critic. From 1948 to 1950 he studied in the United States, an experience he later described with strong hostility toward Western materialism and moral life in works such as "The America I Have Seen". On returning to Egypt he joined the Muslim Brotherhood, became editor of its newspaper, and headed its propaganda section.

After the Brotherhood was accused of involvement in an assassination attempt on President Gamal Abdel Nasser in 1954, Qutb spent most of the following decade in prison, where he wrote his major works. Released briefly in 1964, he was rearrested, tried for plotting against the state, and hanged on 29 August 1966.

Ideas

Qutb argued that the modern world — Muslim societies included — had lapsed into jahiliyyah, the pre-Islamic state of ignorance, because sovereignty (hakimiyya) had been usurped by human legislators instead of belonging to God alone. He called for a vanguard (tali'a) to revive society on Qur'anic principles. His ideas have been highly influential and highly controversial: reformist and mainstream scholars criticise his concept of takfir, while historians note his central role in the genealogy of later militant movements, sometimes termed "Qutbism".
