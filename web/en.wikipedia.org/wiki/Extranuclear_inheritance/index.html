Extranuclear inheritance

Organelle DNA passed to offspring through the egg cytoplasm.

Extranuclear inheritance, also called cytoplasmic inheritance, is the transmission of genes that occur outside the nucleus. It is found in most eukaryotes and is commonly known to occur in cytoplasmic organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts, or from cellular parasites such as viruses or bacteria. Traits determined by organelle DNA do not follow Mendelian patterns of inheritance, because the organelles are (usually) inherited from only one parent — typically the mother, whose egg contributes nearly all of the zygote's cytoplasm.

Organelle inheritance

Mitochondria have their own small circular genome (mtDNA in animals, about 16.6 kb in humans). Because sperm contribute essentially no mitochondria to the embryo, mitochondrial traits show maternal inheritance: an affected mother passes the trait to all of her children, while an affected father passes it to none. The classic early case of cytoplasmic inheritance was Carl Correns' 1909 description of variegated leaf colour in the four-o'clock plant (Mirabilis jalapa), determined by chloroplasts inherited from the maternal branch.

Medical significance

Mutations in human mitochondrial DNA cause a group of disorders — including Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy, MELAS, and MERRF — characterised by maternal transmission and variable severity due to heteroplasmy, the mixture of normal and mutant mitochondria within a cell. Mitochondrial DNA is also widely used in genealogy and evolutionary biology, since it mutates relatively quickly and is inherited along the maternal line.
