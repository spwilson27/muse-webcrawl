Tewodros II (Ge'ez: ዳግማዊ ቴዎድሮስ; born Kassa Hailegiorgis, c. 1818 – 13 April 1868) was Emperor of Ethiopia from 1855 until his death in 1868. His reign is generally taken to mark the end of the fragmented Zemene Mesafint ("Era of the Princes") and the beginning of modern Ethiopia, as he reunified the country's warring provinces under a centralised crown and attempted sweeping administrative, military, and social reforms.

Rise to power

Kassa was born in Qwara, in the north-west, of modest provincial nobility. After education in a monastery and a period as a shifta (rebel/outlaw), he rose through military talent and alliance-building, defeating the leading warlords of the era — Ras Ali II at Ayshal (1853) and Dejazmach Wube — and was crowned emperor at Derasge Maryam in 1855, taking the throne name Tewodros.

Reforms

Tewodros sought to create a standing salaried army, curb the power of regional lords and the church's vast landholdings, reform taxation and the judiciary, abolish the slave trade, and modernise the state with European technology; he employed foreign artisans at Gafat to cast cannon and mortars, including the giant mortar named Sebastopol. He envisioned a unified, modern Ethiopia and corresponded with European monarchs seeking alliances and technical help.

Conflict with Britain and death

Frustrated that a letter to Queen Victoria (1862) went unanswered and suspecting encroachment, Tewodros progressively detained European envoys, missionaries, and finally the British consul Charles Duncan Cameron and the emissary Hormuzd Rassam. Britain responded with the 1868 Expedition to Abyssinia under Sir Robert Napier — a major military-logistical operation that landed at Zula and marched to the emperor's mountain fortress of Magdala. On 13 April 1868, after the battle at Arogye and the storming of Magdala, Tewodros took his own life rather than surrender. The British freed the hostages, looted Magdala's treasures (many still in British museums, subject to restitution claims), and withdrew.

Legacy

Although his later reign was marked by harshness, rebellion, and personal decline, Tewodros is remembered in Ethiopia as a visionary unifier who laid the foundations of the modern Ethiopian state later consolidated by Yohannes IV and Menelik II.
