Loch Ness Monster

The Loch Ness Monster (Scottish Gaelic: Uilebheist Loch Nis), affectionately known as Nessie, is a rumoured creature said to inhabit Loch Ness in the Scottish Highlands. It is often described as large, long-necked, and humped. Evidence for its existence is anecdotal, with disputed photographic material and sonar readings; scientists generally regard reports as misidentifications of known animals, objects, or hoaxes.

History

Interest in the monster surged in 1933, after a road was completed along the loch's shore. In April 1933 Aldie Mackay reported a "whale-like fish" in the loch, and the Inverness Courier used the word "monster". George Spicer's July 1933 account of a long-necked animal crossing the road further fuelled publicity, as did the release of the film King Kong, which popularised prehistoric creatures in the public imagination.

The Surgeon's photograph

The most famous image, the "Surgeon's photograph" published in the Daily Mail on 21 April 1934 and attributed to gynaecologist Robert Kenneth Wilson, appeared to show a slender neck and head rising from the water. In 1994 it was revealed to be a hoax: the "monster" was a toy submarine fitted with a sculpted head, constructed by Marmaduke Wetherell and others.

Explanations

Proposed mundane explanations for sightings include floating logs, boat wakes, swimming deer and birds, sturgeon and other large fish, and optical effects from the loch's unusual water conditions. A 2019 environmental DNA survey of Loch Ness catalogued the loch's species and found no reptile DNA, though abundant eel DNA prompted speculation about oversized eels.

Cultural impact

Nessie is a major draw for Scottish tourism, with exhibitions at Drumnessie and Drumnadrochit, and appears frequently in fiction, film, and folklore. Despite extensive sonar sweeps such as Operation Deepscan (1987), no conclusive evidence of an unknown large animal has been found.
