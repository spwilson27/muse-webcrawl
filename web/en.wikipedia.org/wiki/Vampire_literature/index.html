Vampire literature


Classic works of vampire fiction

Vampire literature covers the spectrum of literary work concerned principally with the subject of vampires. The literary vampire first appeared in 18th-century poetry, before becoming one of the stock figures of Gothic fiction with the publication of John Polidori's The Vampyre (1819), which was inspired by the life and legend of Lord Byron. Influential works include the penny dreadful Varney the Vampire (1847), Sheridan Le Fanu's tale of lesbian vampirism Carmilla (1872), and the most famous work of the genre, Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897).

Origins

Vampire lore in Eastern Europe, reported in the early 18th century following vampire panics in Serbia and elsewhere, reached Western audiences through treatises and pamphlets, most famously Dom Augustin Calmet's Dissertations on the Apparitions of Angels, of Demons and of Ghosts, and concerning the Vampires of Hungary, Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia (1746). Poets took up the theme: Heinrich August Ossenfelder's "Der Vampir" (1748) is often cited as the first vampire poem, followed by works by Goethe ("The Bride of Corinth", 1797), Samuel Taylor Coleridge (Christabel, published 1816) and John Keats ("Lamia", 1820).

Nineteenth century

Polidori's The Vampyre, written during the "Year Without a Summer" gathering at the Villa Diodati that also produced Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, introduced the aristocratic, Byronic vampire in the figure of Lord Ruthven. James Malcolm Rymer's Varney the Vampire; or, the Feast of Blood (1845–47) popularized many conventions of the genre. Carmilla, predating Dracula by 25 years, established the female vampire as a central figure. Stoker's Dracula (1897) fixed the modern image of the vampire: a Transylvanian count with powers including shape-shifting, control of animals and the weather, superhuman strength, and vulnerability to garlic, sunlight and religious symbols.

Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Twentieth-century developments include Richard Matheson's I Am Legend (1954), which recast vampirism as a pandemic disease; Stephen King's 'Salem's Lot (1975); and Anne Rice's The Vampire Chronicles, beginning with Interview with the Vampire (1976), which popularized the sympathetic, introspective vampire as narrator and protagonist. Later bestsellers include Charlaine Harris's The Southern Vampire Mysteries and Stephenie Meyer's Twilight saga (2005–2008), which reframed the vampire for young-adult romance.

Themes

Scholars have read vampire literature as exploring sexuality, contagion and disease, aristocratic decadence, foreign invasion and xenophobia, addiction, and the fear and desire of death and immortality. The genre has continually reinvented the vampire to reflect the anxieties of each era.
