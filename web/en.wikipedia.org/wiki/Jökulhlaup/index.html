Jökulhlaup

A jökulhlaup (Icelandic pronunciation: [ˈjœːkʏl̥ˌl̥œyp], "glacial run") is a type of glacial outburst flood. It is an Icelandic term that has been adopted in glaciology, describing the sudden, sometimes catastrophic release of water from a glacier or an ice-dammed or subglacial lake.

Causes

Jökulhlaups may be triggered by several mechanisms:

  • Subglacial volcanic eruptions, in which geothermal heat rapidly melts ice and meltwater accumulates beneath the glacier until it lifts the ice dam and escapes — the classic Icelandic case.
  • Drainage of ice-dammed lakes, when water rises to near the flotation level of the damming ice.
  • Overtopping or failure of moraine dams, and progressive enlargement of subglacial drainage tunnels by frictional melting.

Notable events

The largest jökulhlaups in Iceland come from the Grímsvötn subglacial lake beneath Vatnajökull, drained down the Skeiðará river across the Skeiðarársandur outwash plain. In November 1996 an eruption under Vatnajökull produced a flood with a peak discharge estimated at 45,000–50,000 m³/s — briefly the second-largest river on Earth — destroying bridges and roads but causing no deaths thanks to monitoring and evacuation. The 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull also generated destructive jökulhlaups. Prehistoric megafloods from glacial Lake Missoula in North America, and the drainage of Lake Agassiz, are interpreted as jökulhlaup-scale events.

Hazards

Because discharge can rise from normal flow to peak within hours, jökulhlaups are among the most dangerous volcanic-glacial hazards; in Iceland they are closely monitored by the Icelandic Meteorological Office.
