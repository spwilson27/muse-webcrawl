Male gaze

FieldFeminist film theory
OriginatorLaura Mulvey (1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema")
Related conceptsFemale gaze, oppositional gaze, scopophilia, objectification

The concept of the male gaze describes the act of depicting women and the world, in the visual arts and in literature, from a masculine, heterosexual perspective that presents and represents women as sexual objects for the pleasure of the heterosexual male viewer. The term was coined by the British feminist film theorist Laura Mulvey in her influential 1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema".

Mulvey's theory

Drawing on psychoanalysis, Mulvey argued that mainstream narrative cinema structures looking into an active/male and passive/female split: the camera, the male characters and the spectator together occupy a "bearing of the look", while women function as image — objects of erotic contemplation (scopophilia) — whose presence tends to freeze narrative action. She called for alternative filmmaking that would break with these pleasures.

Development and criticism

The concept has been enormously influential in film studies, art history and media criticism, where the phrase predates Mulvey in art criticism — John Berger's Ways of Seeing (1972) had already observed that in European painting "women are depicted in a quite different way from men... because the 'ideal' spectator is always assumed to be male."

Later scholars extended and contested the idea: bell hooks proposed the "oppositional gaze" of Black women spectators who resist identification with mainstream images; others introduced the "female gaze" and criticised the theory for assuming a universal, heteronormative spectator and neglecting audience agency, race, class and sexuality.
