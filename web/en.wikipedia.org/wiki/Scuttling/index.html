Scuttling

Scuttling is the deliberate sinking of a ship by its own crew or owner. A ship is scuttled by opening seacocks or hatches to flood the hull, or by explosive charges, so that it sinks rather than falling into enemy hands, or to create an artificial reef, block a harbour entrance, or serve as a breakwater. The word may derive from the Spanish escotilla (hatchway) or from "scuttle", a coal hod with a hinged lid.

Famous scuttlings

Perhaps the most famous mass scuttling was that of the German High Seas Fleet at Scapa Flow on 21 June 1919, when Rear Admiral Ludwig von Reuter ordered 52 of the 74 interned German warships sunk to prevent their seizure by the Allies. Other celebrated cases include:

  • The Russian Black Sea Fleet scuttled at Sevastopol during the Crimean War (1854–55) to block the harbour.
  • The Spanish treasure galleons and numerous blockships sunk in harbour mouths during early modern warfare.
  • The French fleet scuttled at Toulon on 27 November 1942 — 77 vessels including three battleships — to prevent seizure by Nazi Germany after the occupation of Vichy France.
  • The German pocket battleship Admiral Graf Spee, scuttled off Montevideo in December 1939 after the Battle of the River Plate.
  • The surrendered Italian and Danish fleets were partly scuttled in 1943 (the Danish navy sank most of its ships in Copenhagen harbour rather than surrender them).
  • The wrecks at Bikini Atoll and the concrete "Mulberry" blockships of D-Day, where old ships were deliberately sunk as breakwaters off Normandy in 1944.

Modern uses

In peacetime, retired vessels are frequently scuttled, after removal of pollutants, to form artificial reefs that benefit marine life and recreational diving — examples include the aircraft carrier USS Oriskany sunk off Florida in 2006 and HMAS Adelaide off New South Wales. Scuttling a ship for insurance fraud is a crime, and deliberate sinking must comply with environmental law such as the London Convention on ocean dumping. Ships may also be scuttled as targets in weapons tests and exercises ("sinkex").
