Corvée

A corvée (French: [kɔʁve]) is a form of unpaid forced labour, which is applied in place of taxes or rent, in which a person is compelled to work for a superior (such as a lord, monarch or state) for a certain number of days per year. It is most associated with feudal Europe, where serfs owed labour on their lord's demesne, but analogous systems existed in ancient Egypt, China, Japan, and in colonial regimes.

History

In feudal Europe, corvée labour included services such as ploughing the lord's fields, harvesting, and maintaining roads and bridges ("week work" and boon work). In France, the royal corvée for road-building, greatly expanded under Louis XIV and his successors, became a hated burden borne only by peasants; attempts by Turgot to abolish it in 1776 failed, and the institution was swept away during the French Revolution. In the Habsburg lands, the robot was abolished after the revolutions of 1848. Corvée-like obligations were imposed in many colonies — for example in French West Africa until 1946, and in Egypt for the corvée that helped dig the Suez Canal before its abolition in 1882. Remnants survive in some countries' requirements for military or civic service by conscription.
