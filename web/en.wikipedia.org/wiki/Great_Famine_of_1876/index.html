The Great Famine of 1876–1878 was a famine in India under Crown rule that followed an intense drought linked to a strong El Niño event. It affected south and southwestern India — the presidencies of Madras and Bombay and the princely states of Mysore and Hyderabad — over two years. Excess mortality is estimated at between 5.5 and 10 million people.

Background

The failure of the monsoon in 1876 produced crop failure across the Deccan. Despite the famine, grain exports from India continued, and relief under Viceroy Lord Lytton was governed by strict principles of non-interference with trade and minimal state expenditure. Relief wages at government camps were set below subsistence levels, a policy later criticised by officials including William Digby and Florence Nightingale.

Aftermath

The famine led to the appointment of the first Indian Famine Commission under Sir Richard Strachey in 1880, which drafted the Provisional Famine Code of 1883. The catastrophe, and the government's response, fuelled early Indian nationalist criticism of colonial economic policy, including Dadabhai Naoroji's "drain of wealth" theory.
