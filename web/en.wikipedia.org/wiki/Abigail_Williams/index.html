Abigail Williams (born c. 1681) was one of the first accusers in the Salem witch trials of 1692–93 in colonial Massachusetts. Living in the household of her uncle, the minister Samuel Parris of Salem Village, the eleven- or twelve-year-old Abigail, together with her nine-year-old cousin Betty Parris, began in early 1692 to display the fits and contortions that launched the crisis, in which twenty people were executed.

Background

Abigail's parentage and later life are poorly documented; she appears to have been an orphan living as a dependent in the Parris household. In January 1692 she and Betty began behaving strangely — screaming, throwing objects, crawling under furniture and complaining of being pinched and bitten by invisible agents. A local doctor, William Griggs, concluded the girls were "under an evil hand".

Accusations

Under pressure, the girls named three women: Sarah Good, Sarah Osborne, and Tituba, the enslaved woman in the Parris household, whose confession on 1 March 1692 — describing pacts with the Devil and other witches — ignited the hunt. Abigail became one of the most active "afflicted" witnesses, testifying against numerous defendants, including Rebecca Nurse, John Proctor and George Burroughs, and her courtroom fits were treated as evidence of the accused's spectral torment.

Later life and legacy

Abigail's testimony ceases after June 1692 and she disappears from the historical record; nothing certain is known of her later life or death. She is a central character in Arthur Miller's 1953 play The Crucible, which portrays her — contrary to the record, as a seventeen-year-old former servant dismissed after an affair with John Proctor — as the trials' vindictive instigator; Miller's version has dominated her image in popular culture.
