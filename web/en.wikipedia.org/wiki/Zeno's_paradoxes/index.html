Zeno's paradoxes

Zeno's paradoxes are a set of philosophical problems devised by the Greek philosopher Zeno of Elea (c. 490-430 BCE) to support the doctrine of his teacher Parmenides that motion and plurality are illusions and reality is a single, unchanging whole. The paradoxes are known mainly through Aristotle's Physics and later commentators.

Achilles and the tortoise

In a race, the swift Achilles gives the tortoise a head start. By the time Achilles reaches the tortoise's starting point, the tortoise has moved on; when he reaches that new point, it has moved again, and so on infinitely. Thus, the argument claims, Achilles can never overtake the tortoise.

The dichotomy and the arrow

The dichotomy paradox argues that before a runner can reach a goal, he must reach the halfway point, before that the quarter point, and so on — so motion cannot even begin, since completing an infinite number of tasks seems impossible. The arrow paradox holds that at each instant a flying arrow occupies a space equal to itself and is therefore at rest; if time is composed of such instants, the arrow never moves. The stadium paradox concerns relative motion of rows of bodies passing one another.

Responses

Aristotle distinguished between potential and actual infinity in reply. Modern mathematics addresses the paradoxes of divisibility with the calculus and the theory of convergent series: the infinitely many partial distances in the Achilles paradox sum to a finite total, so the pursuer catches up in finite time. Philosophers continue to debate whether such mathematical resolutions fully dissolve the puzzles, and related thought experiments such as Thomson's lamp keep the problems alive.
