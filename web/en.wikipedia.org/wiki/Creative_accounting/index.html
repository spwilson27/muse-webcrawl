Creative accounting

Creative accounting exploits loopholes in accounting rules to present a desired financial picture.

Creative accounting is a euphemism referring to accounting practices that may follow the letter of the rules of standard accounting practices, but deviate from the spirit of those rules with questionable accounting ethics — specifically distorting results in favour of the "preparers", or the firm or persons that initiate the practice. Creative accounting is often used in the making of financial statements to make a company's performance and position appear better (or, occasionally, worse) than the underlying economic reality.

Motivations

Executives may engage in creative accounting to meet earnings expectations, secure bonuses tied to reported profits, support a share price ahead of a merger or flotation, satisfy loan covenants, or smooth income so that profits appear stable rather than volatile. Companies may also seek to reduce tax liabilities or to conceal deteriorating performance from investors and creditors.

Techniques

Common methods include recognising revenue prematurely, capitalising expenses that should be written off, using off-balance-sheet financing, manipulating provisions and reserves ("cookie jar accounting"), reclassifying debt, exploiting fair-value estimates, and timing asset sales to flatter a particular reporting period. Because many techniques rely on judgement within generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP), they can be difficult for auditors to challenge.

Notable scandals

Creative accounting shaded into outright fraud in cases such as Enron (2001), WorldCom (2002), Parmalat (2003) and Satyam (2009), each producing major reforms and prosecutions. In response, regulators tightened standards — for example through the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 in the United States — and standard-setters have worked to close loopholes, while critics note that incentives for manipulation remain.
