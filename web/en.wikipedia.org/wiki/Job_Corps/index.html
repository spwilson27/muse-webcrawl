Job Corps is a program administered by the United States Department of Labor that offers free education and vocational training to young men and women aged 16 to 24. Established by the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 as part of President Lyndon B. Johnson's War on Poverty, it is the largest nationwide residential career training program in the United States, modelled on the Depression-era Civilian Conservation Corps.

Program

Students live at Job Corps centers, most of them residential, where they can earn a high school diploma or GED, learn trades in fields such as construction, healthcare, automotive repair, culinary arts, information technology and manufacturing, and receive driver's education, healthcare, counselling and job placement assistance. A modest living allowance is paid, and graduates receive a transition allowance. Enrollment is voluntary and targeted at low-income youth. Most centers are operated by private contractors under federal oversight; a subset, the Civilian Conservation Centers, are run by the US Forest Service on public lands and include wildland firefighting training.

History and evaluation

The first centers opened in 1965. Job Corps survived attempts to close it in the 1960s and repeated budget debates, and was reauthorized under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act of 2014. Independent evaluations, notably a large random-assignment study published in the 2000s, found earnings gains for older participants (ages 20–24) and improvements in educational attainment, though the overall cost-effectiveness of the program has remained a subject of political debate.
