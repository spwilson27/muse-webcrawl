Acadian orogeny

The Acadian orogeny is a mountain-building event that took place starting in the Middle Devonian and reaching a climax in the Late Devonian, roughly 375 million years ago, along what is now the east coast of North America. It was the second of three major orogenies that built the Appalachian Mountains, following the Taconic orogeny and preceding the Alleghanian orogeny.

Causes

The orogeny resulted from the collision of the Avalonia terrane — a microcontinent that had rifted from Gondwana — with the eastern margin of Laurentia (ancestral North America) as the Rheic Ocean closed. Convergence produced intense deformation, regional metamorphism, and voluminous granitic plutonism from present-day Newfoundland and the Canadian Maritimes through New England. The name "Acadian" refers to Acadia, the French colonial name for the Maritime region where the deformed rocks were first studied in detail.

Effects

Acadian mountain building shed enormous volumes of sediment westward into the Appalachian foreland basin, forming the Devonian Catskill Delta complex in New York and Pennsylvania — the deposits that record the spread of early forests across the land. The orogeny is also associated with the New Hampshire plutonic suite and extensive Acadian metamorphism in the northern Appalachians. Contemporaneous deformation occurred across the Atlantic in the building of the Caledonian–Appalachian mountain system as Iapetus and Rheic oceans closed toward the assembly of Pangaea.
