Disposable camera

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A disposable camera, also called a single-use camera, is a simple box camera sold pre-loaded with film and intended to be used once. The whole camera is returned to the photofinisher for processing; the film is removed for development and prints, while the camera body is recycled or refurbished by the manufacturer and sometimes reloaded with new film and sold again.

Design

Most disposable cameras use a simple fixed-focus plastic meniscus lens, a simple mechanical shutter, and 135 film (35 mm) or, historically, 110 or APS film cartridges. Many models include a built-in electronic flash powered by a single AA battery. Variants have been marketed for specific purposes, including waterproof models for beach and snorkelling use, panoramic models, and cameras decorated for weddings and parties, where they are left on tables for guests to use.

History

Single-use cameras were developed in the mid-1980s. Fujifilm introduced its QuickSnap line (marketed in Japan as "Utsurun-desu", "it takes pictures") in 1986, and Kodak followed with the Fling and FunSaver cameras. The format became extremely popular in the 1990s and early 2000s as a cheap, convenient option for tourists and events. Sales declined sharply with the rise of camera phones, though the cameras have seen a modest revival among younger users attracted to the aesthetic of film photography.

Environmental aspects

Because the camera is returned whole, manufacturers operate extensive collection and recycling programmes; companies report that a high proportion of returned cameras are reused or recycled by weight. The built-in flash circuit and battery can be a hazard in mishandled units, since the flash capacitor can retain a charge.
