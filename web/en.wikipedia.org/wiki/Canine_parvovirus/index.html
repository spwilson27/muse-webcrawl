Canine parvovirus (also referred to as CPV, CPV2, or parvo) is a contagious virus mainly affecting dogs and other canids. CPV is highly contagious and is spread from dog to dog by direct or indirect contact with their feces. Vaccines can prevent this infection, but mortality can reach 91% in untreated cases, especially in puppies.

History

Canine parvovirus type 2 emerged suddenly around 1978 — likely derived from feline panleukopenia virus or a related virus of wild carnivores — and spread worldwide within about two years, causing an epidemic of myocarditis and gastroenteritis in dogs. It was soon replaced globally by variants CPV-2a and later 2b and 2c.

Signs and pathogenesis

The virus attacks rapidly dividing cells in the intestinal crypts, bone marrow and lymphoid tissue. Signs include lethargy, fever, vomiting, severe (often bloody) diarrhoea and dehydration; destruction of the intestinal barrier can lead to bacterial sepsis. In very young puppies, infection of the heart muscle can cause myocarditis.

Diagnosis, treatment and prevention

Diagnosis is usually by faecal antigen (ELISA/SNAP) tests. There is no specific antiviral cure; treatment is supportive — fluids, anti-emetics, antibiotics against secondary infection and nutritional support — while monoclonal antibody treatments have recently become available. Effective vaccines exist and form part of core puppy vaccination schedules; hygiene and isolation are essential in kennels and shelters because the virus is extremely hardy in the environment.
