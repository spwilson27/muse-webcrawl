Arresting gear

Arresting gear
TypeAircraft recovery system
Used onAircraft carriers; some military airfields
ComponentsArrestor wires (pendants), below-deck engines, tailhook

Arresting gear (also arrestor gear) is a mechanical system used to rapidly decelerate an aircraft as it lands. Most famously installed on the flight decks of aircraft carriers, it consists of a series of steel cables ("wires" or "pendants") stretched across the landing area, which the landing aircraft engages with a tailhook; the cables are connected below deck to engines that absorb the aircraft's kinetic energy, bringing it to a stop in roughly 100 metres (about 300 feet).

Operation

Modern carriers typically rig three or four wires. Pilots aim for the third wire ("the three wire") in US practice. As the hook snags a pendant, the cable pays out against hydraulic (in older systems) or water-twister resistance, decelerating the aircraft smoothly. The newest US system, the Advanced Arresting Gear (AAG) on the Gerald R. Ford class, uses turbo-electric machinery with digital control, allowing finer adjustment for aircraft of very different weights, including unmanned aircraft.

History

Early arresting systems of the 1910s–1920s used weights, sandbags and transverse wires with hooks and skids; Hugh Robinson and others pioneered practical gear for the first carrier landings. Hydraulic arresting engines became standard by the Second World War. Shore-based variants are fitted at some military airfields for emergency barrier engagements and for training.

Barricade

If an aircraft cannot use its hook — for example because of landing-gear or hook damage — a net-like barricade can be rigged across the deck to catch the aircraft by its wings and fuselage, at the cost of damage to the aircraft.
