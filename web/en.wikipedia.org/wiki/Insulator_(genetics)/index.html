Insulator (genetics)

An insulator is a type of cis-regulatory element known as a long-range regulatory element. Insulators are composed of specific transcription factor binding sites and are found in eukaryotes. They mediate two main functions:

  1. Enhancer-blocking: when positioned between an enhancer and a promoter, an insulator can prevent the enhancer from activating that promoter, while leaving the enhancer free to act on other promoters.
  2. Barrier activity: insulators can act as barriers that protect genes from the spreading of repressive heterochromatin, preventing position-effect silencing.

Mechanism

Insulator function depends on proteins that bind them. In vertebrates the key insulator protein is CTCF, an eleven-zinc-finger DNA-binding protein; in Drosophila, several insulator proteins are known, including Su(Hw), BEAF-32, GAGA factor and a fly CTCF homolog. Insulator proteins are thought to work largely by organising the three-dimensional structure of chromatin, contributing — together with cohesin — to the formation of loops that partition genomes into topologically associating domains (TADs), within which enhancer–promoter contacts are favoured and between which they are restricted.

Imprinting

The best-studied vertebrate insulator controls the imprinted Igf2/H19 locus. Between the two genes lies an imprinting control region with CTCF binding sites. On the maternal chromosome, which is unmethylated, CTCF binds and insulates Igf2 from enhancers beyond H19, so only H19 is expressed; on the paternal chromosome the region is methylated, CTCF cannot bind, and the enhancers activate Igf2 instead. Disruption of insulators has been implicated in developmental disorders and cancer, where loss of insulation can let enhancers abnormally activate neighbouring genes, including oncogenes.
