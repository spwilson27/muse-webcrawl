Hierapolis (Ancient Greek: Ἱεράπολις, "Holy City") was an ancient Greek and Roman city located on hot springs in classical Phrygia in southwestern Anatolia, at modern Pamukkale in Turkey. Its ruins lie adjacent to the famous white travertine terraces formed by the mineral-rich thermal waters.

History

The city was founded by the Attalid kings of Pergamon in the early 2nd century BC and passed to Rome in 133 BC. Thanks to its healing springs, Hierapolis became a fashionable spa and retirement centre; its large necropolis, one of the biggest in the ancient world, contains over 1,200 tombs of visitors who came for cures. An earthquake in AD 60 destroyed the city, which was rebuilt in Roman style with a theatre, baths, gymnasium, temples, and the great colonnaded main street (the Frontinus Street).

Hierapolis was also an early centre of Christianity: tradition holds that the apostle Philip was martyred here around AD 80, and a martyrium was built over his tomb. The city declined in late antiquity and was gradually abandoned after earthquakes in the 7th century.

Notable structures

  • The Roman theatre (capacity c. 12,000), exceptionally well preserved
  • The Plutonium (Ploutonion), a shrine to Pluto above a cave emitting toxic gases
  • The Nymphaeum and Temple of Apollo
  • The baths, later converted into a church
  • The extensive northern, southern and eastern necropoleis
