Xenarthra (from Greek xenos, "strange", + arthra, "joints") is a superorder of placental mammals endemic to the Americas, comprising the armadillos (order Cingulata) and the sloths and anteaters (order Pilosa). The group is named for the extra articulations (xenarthrous vertebrae) found between the lumbar vertebrae of its members, unique among mammals. Xenarthrans were one of the dominant mammal groups of Cenozoic South America during its long isolation, when they included giants such as Megatherium and the glyptodonts.

Characteristics

Xenarthrans share several peculiarities: accessory (xenarthral) vertebral articulations stiffening the back; simplified dentition, with cheek teeth reduced to peg-like forms lacking enamel (anteaters are entirely toothless); low body temperatures and slow metabolic rates compared with other mammals of similar size; and in armadillos, a carapace of dermal bone (osteoderms). Most have powerful foreclaws for digging or tearing ant and termite nests.

Classification

  • Order Cingulata: armadillos (one family, ~21 species, including the nine-banded armadillo).
  • Order Pilosa: sloths (two- and three-toed, ~6 species) and anteaters (giant anteater, silky anteater, tamanduas).

Molecular studies place Xenarthra, with Afrotheria, near the base of the placental mammal radiation.

Evolution

The group originated in South America by the early Paleogene and radiated during the continent's island-like isolation. After the Great American Interchange (~3 million years ago), several lineages moved north; the nine-banded armadillo continues to expand its range in the southern United States.

Extinct giants

Pleistocene xenarthrans included ground sloths the size of elephants (Megatherium), the tank-like glyptodonts (armoured relatives of armadillos, some as large as a small car), and pampatheres. Most vanished about 10,000 years ago, probably through a combination of climate change and human hunting.
