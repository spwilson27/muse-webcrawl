Anton Chekhov

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Anton Chekhov in 1889: the bearded young doctor-writer in a pince-nez]

Anton Pavlovich Chekhov (29 January 1860 – 15 July 1904) was a Russian playwright and short-story writer, considered one of the greatest writers of all time. A practising doctor who called medicine his lawful wife and literature his mistress, he reinvented both the short story — plotless, atmospheric, ending in resonance rather than resolution — and modern drama, with four plays produced by Stanislavski's Moscow Art Theatre that changed world theatre.

His plays are The Seagull (1896), Uncle Vanya (1897), Three Sisters (1901) and The Cherry Orchard (1904). Among hundreds of stories, the best known include 'The Lady with the Dog' (1899), 'Ward No. 6' (1892), 'The Darling' (1899) and 'The Steppe' (1888). His letters are a classic of the genre.

From comic sketches to mastery

Chekhov was born in Taganrog, grandson of a serf who bought his freedom. Supporting his family at medical school by writing comic sketches under pseudonyms, he graduated in 1884 and by 1888, with The Steppe and the Pushkin Prize, was Russia's rising writer. In 1890 he crossed Siberia to document the penal colony of Sakhalin Island, a journey that deepened the social conscience beneath his famous objectivity.

The plays and last years

The Seagull flopped in St Petersburg in 1896 and triumphed under Stanislavski in 1898 — the Art Theatre took the seagull as its emblem. Chekhov insisted The Cherry Orchard was a comedy; Stanislavski staged it as tragedy, and the argument has never closed. Tuberculosis drove him to Yalta and finally to the German spa of Badenweiler, where he died aged 44 in 1904, weeks after the Orchard's premiere.
