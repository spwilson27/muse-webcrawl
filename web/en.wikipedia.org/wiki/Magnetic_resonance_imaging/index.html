Magnetic resonance imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a medical imaging technique that uses strong magnetic fields, magnetic field gradients and radio waves to generate images of the organs and tissues inside the body. Unlike X-ray-based techniques such as CT, MRI does not use ionising radiation, and it provides excellent contrast between different soft tissues, making it especially valuable for imaging the brain, spinal cord, joints, heart and internal organs.

How it works

The scanner's powerful magnet aligns the magnetic moments of hydrogen nuclei (protons) in the body's water and fat. Pulses of radio-frequency energy tip these protons out of alignment; as they relax back, they emit signals whose timing (T1 and T2 relaxation) depends on the local tissue environment. Gradient coils vary the field slightly across the body so that signals can be located in space, and computers reconstruct two- or three-dimensional images from the data. Gadolinium-based contrast agents may be injected to highlight blood vessels and abnormal tissue.

History

MRI builds on nuclear magnetic resonance, discovered in the 1940s. Raymond Damadian proposed scanning the body for cancer detection in 1971; Paul Lauterbur published the first MR images in 1973, and Peter Mansfield developed fast imaging methods (echo-planar imaging). Lauterbur and Mansfield shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Clinical MRI spread rapidly through the 1980s.

Uses and safety

MRI is the investigation of choice for most neurological and musculoskeletal problems, and specialised forms include functional MRI (fMRI) for brain activity, MR angiography, diffusion imaging and cardiac MRI. Because of the strong magnet, patients with certain implants (some pacemakers, cochlear implants, metallic fragments) may be unable to undergo scanning, and loose ferromagnetic objects are dangerous in the scanner room. Claustrophobia, scan noise and long examination times are practical drawbacks.
