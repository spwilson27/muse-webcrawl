Language documentation

Language documentation (also called documentary linguistics) is a subfield of linguistics concerned with creating a lasting, multipurpose record of a language, typically through audio and video recordings of speech, their transcription, translation and annotation, together with dictionaries and grammars. It is closely related to, but distinct from, language description, whose primary goal is the scientific analysis of a language's structure.

Scope and methods

Documentary work aims to record a representative sample of a language in use: narratives, conversation, ritual speech, elicited paradigms and other genres, accompanied by metadata describing the speakers and circumstances. The resulting materials are deposited in digital archives—such as ELAR at SOAS, the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America (AILLA), and PARADISEC—where they can serve linguists, the speaker community, and future generations.

The modern field took shape in the 1990s, articulated notably by Nikolaus Himmelmann, at a time when the accelerating endangerment of the world's languages—many spoken by small communities and shifting to dominant languages—made durable records urgent. Practitioners emphasize collaboration with, and benefit to, speech communities, and documentation often supports language revitalization efforts.

Relation to language description

Whereas descriptive linguistics produces analyses (a grammar, a dictionary, a text collection) aimed at answering theoretical questions, documentary linguistics treats the record itself—the corpus of primary data with its annotations—as the primary output, designed to be usable for purposes not foreseeable at the time of recording. In practice the two activities overlap heavily, and most fieldwork combines them.

Technology

Digital recording, time-aligned transcription tools such as ELAN, lexical database software such as FLEx, and standardized metadata and file formats have transformed documentation practice, enabling long-term preservation and open access. Community-led projects increasingly use mobile apps and online dictionaries to make materials usable by speakers.
