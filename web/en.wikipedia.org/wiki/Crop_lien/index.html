Crop lien

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A crop lien is a legal claim against a farmer's future crop, given as security for credit advanced to plant and cultivate it. The crop-lien system was a credit system that became widely used by cotton farmers in the Southern United States in the decades following the American Civil War, from the 1860s through the 1930s.

Operation

Under the system, a farmer — often a sharecropper or tenant farmer with little cash — obtained supplies, seed, food, and equipment on credit from a local merchant (the "furnishing merchant") or landlord. In return, the farmer signed a lien giving the creditor a first claim on the crop at harvest. Because cotton was a dependable cash crop, merchants insisted that borrowers plant it, discouraging food crops and diversification.

Effects

Interest and credit prices charged by furnishing merchants were high — often effectively 50 percent or more per year — and farmers frequently ended the season still in debt, obliged to borrow again the following year. Historians have described the resulting cycle as a form of debt peonage that kept many Black and white farmers impoverished, locked the Southern economy into one-crop cotton production, depleted soils, and reinforced the sharecropping system. Merchants faced real risks of default, which they cited to justify the high charges, but their local monopolies on credit gave them substantial power.

Decline

The system declined with the mechanization of cotton production, New Deal agricultural programs and federal farm credit in the 1930s, and the Great Migration of Black Southerners to Northern cities, which together transformed Southern agriculture.
