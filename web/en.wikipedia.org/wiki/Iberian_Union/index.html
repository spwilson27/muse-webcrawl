Iberian Union

The Iberian Union is a historiographical term for the dynastic union of the crowns of Spain and Portugal from 1580 to 1640, during which the entire Iberian Peninsula and the two kingdoms' overseas possessions were ruled by the Spanish Habsburg monarchs Philip II, Philip III, and Philip IV of Spain (I, II, and III of Portugal).

The combined Spanish and Portuguese empires under the Iberian Union, around 1598.

Background

The Portuguese succession crisis of 1580 followed the death of the young King Sebastian at the Battle of Alcácer Quibir (1578) and of his successor, Cardinal-King Henry, in 1580. Philip II of Spain, a grandson of Manuel I of Portugal, pressed his claim against the Prior of Crato, António, and secured it by force: the Duke of Alba's army defeated António's forces at the Battle of Alcântara, and the Cortes of Tomar (1581) accepted Philip as King of Portugal on terms designed to preserve Portugal's separate laws, institutions, currency, and colonial administration.

The union

The union was a personal (dynastic) union, not an incorporation: Portugal remained formally a separate kingdom governed from Madrid through the Council of Portugal. The combined monarchy ruled the largest empire of the age, spanning the Americas, Africa, and Asia. Over time, Portuguese grievances grew — Portuguese colonies were attacked by Spain's enemies (notably the Dutch), taxation and appointments increasingly favoured Castile, and the terms of Tomar eroded under Philip IV and his minister Olivares.

Restoration

On 1 December 1640 a revolt in Lisbon proclaimed the Duke of Braganza as King John IV, beginning the Portuguese Restoration War. Spain, simultaneously facing the Catalan Revolt and war with France, was unable to reconquer Portugal, and finally recognised Portuguese independence in the Treaty of Lisbon (1668).
