Jettison (shipping)

Jettison, in maritime law and shipping, is the act of deliberately throwing cargo, stores or parts of a vessel's equipment overboard in order to lighten the ship and save it from peril, for example during a storm, after grounding, or to prevent capture. The word derives from the French jeter, "to throw", via Anglo-Norman; the goods so cast away are sometimes themselves called jetsam.

General average

Jettison is the classic example of a general average act — one of the oldest principles of maritime law, traceable to the ancient Rhodian sea law. When property is voluntarily and reasonably sacrificed in a time of peril for the common safety of the ship and the remaining cargo, the loss is not borne by the owner of the jettisoned goods alone: it is shared rateably among all the interests saved — shipowner and cargo owners alike. The rules governing this sharing are codified today in the York–Antwerp Rules, commonly incorporated into contracts of carriage, and a general average declaration by the master triggers a formal adjustment process in which cargo may be required to post security before delivery.

Practice and related concepts

For a sacrifice to qualify, the peril must be real and imminent, the act voluntary, and the sacrifice must succeed in preserving at least part of the venture. Deck cargo jettisoned in a storm, containers cut loose from a listing ship, and cargo thrown overboard to refloat a grounded vessel are typical instances. Goods involuntarily washed overboard are not the subject of jettison but form part of the law of wreck: floating wreckage cast up by the sea is jetsam when deliberately thrown overboard and flotsam when lost by accident — a distinction preserved in the phrase "flotsam and jetsam".

Jettison also has a military and historical dimension: stores and guns were thrown overboard to lighten a ship for escape or battle, and slave traders notoriously murdered enslaved people by throwing them overboard, as in the Zong massacre of 1781, where the ship's owners then claimed the loss on insurance as if the victims had been jettisoned cargo — a case that became a landmark scandal in the abolition movement.
