A lieutenant (UK: /lɛfˈtɛnənt/; US: /luːˈtɛnənt/) is a commissioned officer rank in the armed forces of many nations. The meaning of lieutenant differs in different militaries, but it is often subdivided into senior (first lieutenant) and junior (second lieutenant) ranks. The word derives from the French lieu tenant, meaning "place holder" — one who acts in place of a superior.

History

The term originated in the French army, where a lieutenant was the deputy of a captain, commanding a company in the captain's absence. In English use from the 14th century, the rank spread through European armies and navies. In early navies the lieutenant was the captain's deputy aboard ship; senior lieutenants at sea eventually became a stepping stone to the ranks of commander and captain.

Army usage

In most armies a lieutenant commands a platoon or troop, typically of 30 to 50 soldiers. In the British Army and Commonwealth forces the rank is the highest rank attainable by a new officer after second lieutenant. In the United States Army, Marine Corps and Air Force, second lieutenant (O-1) and first lieutenant (O-2) are the two grades.

Naval usage

In navies, a lieutenant is a more senior rank than in armies, equivalent to an army captain in NATO terms (OF-2). Naval lieutenants may serve as department heads aboard smaller vessels or as executive officers. The Royal Navy historically had a single lieutenant grade; the United States Navy lieutenant is pay grade O-3.

Other uses

The rank is also used in police forces, fire services and other uniformed organisations in some countries, and survives in titles such as lieutenant governor and Lord Lieutenant.
