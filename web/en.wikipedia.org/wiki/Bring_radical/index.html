Bring radical

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In algebra, the Bring radical, also called the Bring–Jerrard radical or ultraradical, is a mathematical function that inverts the quintic polynomial x5 + x. For a real number a, the Bring radical BR(a) is defined as the unique real root of the equation

x5 + x + a = 0.

For complex values of a, the "Bring radical" usually refers to a specific choice of root obtained by analytic continuation along a path in the complex plane, with branch cuts along the real axis. The function is named after the Swedish mathematician Erland Samuel Bring (1736–1798); the term ultraradical was introduced by George Jerrard (1804–1863).

Relation to the quintic equation

Just as the square root inverts the quadratic x2 and the cube root inverts higher symmetries of the cubic, the Bring radical inverts the simplest quintic. Any general quintic equation

x5 + px4 + qx3 + rx2 + sx + t = 0

can be reduced by Tschirnhaus transformations — which require solving only auxiliary equations of degree at most four — to the Bring–Jerrard normal form x5 + x + a = 0. Its roots are then given by −BR(a) and its conjugates. This is the closest analogue for degree five of the formulas available for quadratic, cubic and quartic equations; however, by the Abel–Ruffini theorem the reduction cannot be achieved using radicals alone, and the Bring radical is an essentially new function.

Properties and expansions

The Bring radical is a smooth, monotonically decreasing function of a, satisfying BR(−a) = −BR(a) and BR(0) = 0. Around a = 0 it has the convergent power series

BR(a) = −a + a5 − 5a9 + 35a13 − ⋯

with coefficients expressible in terms of generalized Catalan numbers. For large arguments the function can be evaluated by expansion in fractional powers of a. Charles Hermite showed in 1858 that the roots of the quintic in Bring–Jerrard form could also be expressed exactly using elliptic modular functions, an approach developed further by Felix Klein in his work on the icosahedron; the Bring radical can correspondingly be written in terms of generalized hypergeometric functions.

History

Bring's work, published in 1786, showed that the general quintic could be simplified to the one-parameter form that now bears his name, although his result was largely overlooked until Jerrard independently developed it in the 1830s and 1840s. Jerrard initially believed he had found a method for solving equations of all degrees, a hope ended by the impossibility results associated with Ruffini, Abel and Galois theory.
