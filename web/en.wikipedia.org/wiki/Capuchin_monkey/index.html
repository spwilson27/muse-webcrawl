The capuchin monkeys (or simply capuchins) are New World monkeys of the subfamily Cebinae. They are readily identified by their "cap" of dark fur on the head, resembling the cowl of a Capuchin friar, from which they take their name. Their range extends from Honduras in Central America through much of the Amazon basin and northern South America to northern Argentina.

Capuchins are considered among the most intelligent of the New World monkeys. They are famous for using tools — cracking nuts with stones, using sticks to probe for insects and syrup, and rubbing plants on their fur — behaviours studied intensively both in the wild and in captivity.

Taxonomy

Capuchins were traditionally all placed in the genus Cebus, but research since the 2000s has divided them into two genera: the gracile capuchins (Cebus), slender, lighter monkeys including the white-faced capuchin (Cebus capucinus), the tufted-brown-fronted Kaapori capuchin and the wedge-capped capuchin; and the robust capuchins (Sapajus), stockier monkeys with powerful jaws, including the tufted capuchin (Sapajus apella), the black-striped capuchin and the blond capuchin (Sapajus flavius), the last rediscovered in 2006 after being feared extinct. The two lineages diverged several million years ago, gracile forms spreading north and west and robust forms occupying the Amazon and Atlantic Forest.

Description

Capuchins are medium-sized monkeys, with a body length of 30 to 56 cm (12 to 22 in) and a tail of similar length that is semi-prehensile and often carried coiled. They weigh 3 to 5 kg in most species. Fur ranges from cream and white on the face and shoulders (as in the white-faced capuchin) to brown and black in the robust species. Their hands are dextrous, with a pseudo-opposable thumb, and their brains are large relative to body size.

Behaviour and ecology

Capuchins are diurnal and arboreal, though they descend to the ground to forage more than most New World monkeys. They live in social groups of typically 8 to 40 animals led by a dominant male and female, with complex social bonds, alliances, grooming networks and traditions that vary between groups — one of the strongest cases for culture in non-human primates. Their diet is highly omnivorous: fruit, leaves, insects, snails, small vertebrates, eggs and birds.

Tool use is most developed in the robust capuchins of dry habitats: bearded capuchins in Brazil's Serra da Capivara use heavy stones as hammers on stone anvils to crack palm nuts, select tools by weight and material, and probe with sticks — a tradition archaeologists have traced back at least 3,000 years in the monkey archaeological record. White-faced capuchins in Costa Rica rub themselves with citrus, millipedes and other substances, possibly as insect repellent or social bonding. Captive capuchins feature in classic experiments on cooperation, inequity aversion (famously rejecting cucumber when a partner receives grape) and economic behaviour.

Relationship with humans

The white-faced capuchin is the traditional "organ grinder" monkey, and capuchins appear frequently in film and television (for example in Pirates of the Caribbean and Night at the Museum). Trained capuchins have been used as helper animals for people with disabilities, though the practice is controversial and now restricted in many places. Capuchins are hunted for food in parts of their range, kept in the pet trade, and several species — notably the blond and Kaapori capuchins — are endangered through habitat loss.
