Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is a microscopy technique in which a beam of electrons is transmitted through a specimen to form an image. Because electrons have wavelengths far shorter than visible light, TEM achieves resolutions down to fractions of a nanometre — sufficient to image individual atoms — making it indispensable in materials science, nanotechnology and structural biology.

History

The first electron microscope was built in Berlin in 1931 by Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska; Ruska's 1933 instrument surpassed the resolution of the light microscope, and he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986. Commercial instruments from Siemens and others spread after the Second World War, and biologists adapted ultramicrotomy and heavy-metal staining to reveal cell ultrastructure — mitochondria, ribosomes, viruses — for the first time.

Operation

An electron gun (commonly a LaB6 or field-emission source) accelerates electrons to 100–300 kV. Magnetic lenses focus the beam through an ultrathin specimen (typically under ~100 nm), and the transmitted and scattered electrons form a magnified image on a detector. The whole column is under high vacuum. Variants include scanning TEM (STEM), high-resolution TEM for lattice imaging, electron diffraction, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) for chemical analysis.

Cryogenic TEM

In cryo-EM, biological specimens are flash-frozen in vitreous ice and imaged at low dose; averaging thousands of particle images reconstructs molecular structures at near-atomic resolution. The method's developers — Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson — won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and cryo-EM has since transformed structural biology, including rapid determination of viral spike-protein structures.
