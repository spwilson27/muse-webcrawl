Body armor, also known as personal armor, is protective clothing designed to absorb or deflect physical attacks. Historically used to protect military personnel, today it is also used by various types of police (riot police and SWAT in particular), private security guards and bodyguards, and occasionally ordinary civilians. Today there are two main types: regular non-plated body armor for moderate to substantial protection, and hard-plate reinforced body armor for maximum protection, used by combat soldiers.

History

Many factors have influenced the development of personal armor throughout human history. Significant factors in the development of armor include the economic and technological necessities of armor production. For instance, full plate armor first appeared in medieval Europe when water-powered trip hammers made the formation of plates faster and cheaper. Early modern armor often used chain mail, brigandines and cuirasses.

The introduction of firearms gradually reduced the effectiveness of heavy steel armor, and by the late 19th century most armor had disappeared from the battlefield. During the First World War, steel helmets returned, and flak jackets were developed for aircrews in the Second World War. The invention of synthetic fibres such as Kevlar by Stephanie Kwolek at DuPont in 1965 revolutionised soft body armor, allowing concealable vests capable of stopping handgun rounds.

Modern armor

Modern soft body armor is typically made from woven or laminated fibres such as aramids (Kevlar, Twaron) or ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (Dyneema, Spectra). Hard armor plates are made of ceramic (such as boron carbide or silicon carbide), steel, or polyethylene, and are inserted into carriers to defeat rifle rounds. In the United States, the National Institute of Justice (NIJ) standard 0101.06 classifies armor from Level IIA up to Level IV, the latter designed to stop armor-piercing rifle ammunition.
