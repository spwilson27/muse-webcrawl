Letters patent

Letters patent (always in the plural) are a type of legal instrument in the form of a published written order issued by a monarch, president, or other head of state, generally granting an office, right, monopoly, title, or status to a person or corporation. The term derives from the Latin litterae patentes, "open letters": unlike letters close, they are not sealed shut but issued open for all to read, authenticated by the affixing of the Great Seal.

Uses

Historically, letters patent were used to grant land, create peerages and corporations, confer monopolies, and appoint officials. The English Statute of Monopolies (1624) limited such grants for inventions, laying groundwork for modern patent law; indeed, the word "patent" for an invention right derives from this instrument. In the United Kingdom, peers are still created by letters patent, and royal warrants, coats of arms, and certain appointments take this form.

Modern practice

In Commonwealth realms, letters patent issued under the Great Seal constitute royal charters, appoint governors-general and judges, and create orders of chivalry. The Letters Patent, 1947 govern the exercise of the Canadian monarch's powers by the Governor General of Canada. In the United States, land patents and commissions of federal officers historically used the same form; the Department of State still issues certain instruments under seal.

Form

A traditional letters patent begins with the sovereign's name and titles, addresses "all to whom these presents shall come," and recites the grant. They are enrolled in the records of the issuing authority so that the grant is public — the essence of their "patent" (open) character.
