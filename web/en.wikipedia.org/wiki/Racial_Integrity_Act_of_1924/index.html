Racial Integrity Act of 1924

The Racial Integrity Act of 1924 was a law of the US state of Virginia that prohibited interracial marriage and codified the "one-drop rule", defining a white person as someone with no discernible trace of non-Caucasian ancestry. The act, passed on 20 March 1924 by the Virginia General Assembly, was part of a wave of American eugenic legislation and remained in force until the United States Supreme Court struck down Virginia's anti-miscegenation statutes in Loving v. Virginia (1967).

Provisions

The Act required all birth certificates and marriage certificates to record the race of the individual. It made interracial marriage a felony punishable by one to five years in prison, and it defined "white" strictly, with a narrow exception — the so-called "Pocahontas exception" — allowing persons with less than one-sixteenth Native American ancestry and no other non-white ancestry to be classified as white, a concession to prominent Virginia families who claimed descent from Pocahontas and John Rolfe.

Enforcement

The Act was aggressively enforced by Walter Plecker, registrar of the Virginia Bureau of Vital Statistics from 1912 to 1946. Plecker reclassified Virginia Indians as "colored" on official records, an administrative erasure later described as "paper genocide", pursued midwives and families to police racial classifications, and wrote to officials in other states urging similar measures. Virginia's eugenics programme, including the companion sterilisation statute upheld in Buck v. Bell (1927), was later cited by Nazi Germany's race-law drafters.

Repeal and legacy

In 1967 the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in Loving v. Virginia that Virginia's ban on interracial marriage violated the Fourteenth Amendment, invalidating the Racial Integrity Act's marriage provisions; the Act was formally repealed in 1975. Virginia has since apologised for its eugenics programme and compensated surviving sterilisation victims.
