Gnetum

Gnetum is a genus of gymnosperms, the sole genus in the family Gnetaceae and order Gnetales. It comprises roughly 40–50 species of evergreen trees, shrubs and woody vines (lianas) native to tropical regions of South America, Africa and Asia. Together with Ephedra and Welwitschia, Gnetum forms the gnetophytes, a small and unusual group of seed plants.

Description

Unlike most gymnosperms, which have needle- or scale-like leaves, most Gnetum species bear broad, oval leaves with net-like (reticulate) venation that closely resemble those of flowering plants. Their wood contains vessel elements, another angiosperm-like feature, and their reproductive structures are organised in cone-like spikes with whorls of small male or female units. Most species are dioecious, with male and female cones on separate plants. The seeds are often large and fleshy, coloured red or orange when ripe, and are dispersed by animals.

Evolutionary significance

The gnetophytes were once proposed, under the "anthophyte hypothesis", to be the closest living relatives of the flowering plants because of these shared features. Molecular phylogenetic studies instead place them within or beside the conifers (the "gnepine" hypothesis, allying them with pines). A form of double fertilisation also occurs in Gnetum, though it does not produce true endosperm as in angiosperms.

Uses

Several species are used as food. In Indonesia and Malaysia the young leaves, inflorescences and seeds of Gnetum gnemon (melinjo) are eaten as a vegetable and made into crackers (emping). The seeds of some African and Amazonian species are also consumed, and the fibrous bark has been used for cordage and fishing nets.
