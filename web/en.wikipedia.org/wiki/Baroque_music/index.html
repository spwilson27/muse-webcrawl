Baroque music (UK: /bəˈrɒk/, US: /bəˈroʊk/) is a period and style of Western classical music composed from approximately 1600 to 1750. It followed the Renaissance period and was succeeded by the Classical period. The word "baroque" comes from the Portuguese barroco, a misshapen pearl — originally a term of criticism for the style's perceived excess and ornamentation.

Characteristics

Baroque music is characterised by the systematic use of the basso continuo (figured bass), in which a bass line and improvised chords — usually played by harpsichord, organ, or lute together with a bass instrument — support the melody. This period consolidated major–minor tonality, replacing the older modal system, and developed functional harmony. Other hallmarks include dramatic contrast of volume and texture, elaborate ornamentation, the doctrine of the affections (music's power to arouse specific emotions), and a rhythmic vitality driven by regular metre.

Forms and genres

The Baroque era invented or transformed many of the central genres of Western music:

  • Opera — born in Florence around 1600 with the works of Peri and Monteverdi (L'Orfeo, 1607);
  • Oratorio and cantata — large-scale sacred vocal works (Handel's Messiah, Bach's church cantatas);
  • Concerto — the concerto grosso and solo concerto, perfected by Vivaldi;
  • Sonata, suite, fugue, and the French overture — instrumental forms cultivated by Corelli, Couperin, and J. S. Bach.

Periods and composers

Musicologists conventionally divide the era into early (1600–1650), middle (1650–1700), and late (1700–1750) Baroque. The late Baroque produced the era's most famous figures: Vivaldi in Venice, Handel in London, Bach in Leipzig, Telemann in Hamburg, and Rameau in Paris. The year 1750, the death of Bach, is conventionally taken as the era's end, as the galant and Classical styles displaced contrapuntal Baroque practice.

Legacy

Baroque music forms a core part of the classical repertoire today, and since the mid-twentieth century the historically informed performance movement has revived period instruments and performance practices, transforming how the music is played and heard.
