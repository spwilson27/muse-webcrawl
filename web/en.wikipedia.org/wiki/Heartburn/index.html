Heartburn, also called pyrosis or acid indigestion, is a burning sensation in the central chest or upper central abdomen, usually caused by regurgitation of gastric acid into the oesophagus (gastroesophageal reflux). Despite its name it has nothing to do with the heart, although its pain can mimic that of angina or a heart attack — a confusion that works in both directions and makes new or severe chest pain a reason for urgent medical assessment. Heartburn is a cardinal symptom of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GORD/GERD) and is experienced at least occasionally by a large proportion of adults.

Causes and mechanism

The lower oesophageal sphincter normally prevents stomach contents from flowing back. Transient relaxations of the sphincter, increased abdominal pressure (obesity, pregnancy, large meals, bending or lying flat), delayed gastric emptying and hiatal hernia all promote reflux. Common dietary triggers include fatty and spicy foods, chocolate, peppermint, alcohol, coffee and carbonic drinks; smoking and medicines such as calcium-channel blockers and some sedatives can worsen it.

Diagnosis and complications

Typical heartburn without alarm features is usually diagnosed clinically and treated empirically. Persistent symptoms warrant investigation for GORD complications: erosive oesophagitis, strictures, and Barrett's oesophagus, in which chronic acid exposure changes the lining of the lower oesophagus and slightly increases cancer risk. "Alarm" symptoms — difficulty swallowing, persistent vomiting, weight loss, anaemia or black stools — require prompt endoscopy.

Treatment

Management begins with lifestyle measures: weight loss, smaller evening meals, not lying down after eating, raising the head of the bed, and avoiding tobacco, alcohol and trigger foods. Medicines include antacids for quick relief, H2-receptor blockers, and proton pump inhibitors (such as omeprazole), which suppress acid production and heal oesophagitis. Severe or refractory cases may be treated surgically, most commonly by laparoscopic fundoplication, in which the top of the stomach is wrapped around the lower oesophagus to strengthen the barrier.
