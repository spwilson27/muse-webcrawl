Fence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fence is a structure that encloses an area, typically outdoors, and is usually constructed from posts that are connected by boards, wire, rails, or netting. Fences serve to mark boundaries, keep livestock in or predators out, provide privacy and security, and screen noise or wind. Unlike a wall, a fence is not usually a solid masonry structure, though the boundary between the two is sometimes blurred.

Types

  • Picket fence — evenly spaced vertical boards ("pickets") attached to horizontal rails; strongly associated with suburban homes, especially in the United States.
  • Chain-link fence — woven steel wire in a diamond pattern; cheap, durable, common around schools, sports courts, and industrial sites.
  • Barbed wire fence — strands of wire with sharp barbs; patented in the 1870s and transformative for enclosing the North American prairies.
  • Electric fence — delivers a short high-voltage pulse to deter animals or intruders from crossing.
  • Post-and-rail fence — horizontal rails mortised into posts; traditional for paddocks and rural properties.
  • Wattle — woven hazel or willow hurdles, one of the oldest fence forms.
  • Ha-ha — a sunken fence or ditch used in landscaped gardens to keep livestock away from the house without interrupting the view.
  • Snow fence — a porous barrier placed to control drifting snow.
  • Vinyl (PVC) fencing — low-maintenance panels imitating wood pickets or rails.

History

Fencing is as old as settled agriculture: farmers needed to protect crops from grazing animals and to confine stock. In England, the enclosure movement of the 16th to 19th centuries hedged and fenced common land into private fields, transforming rural society. In the American West, a shortage of timber made wire fencing essential; Joseph Glidden's 1874 barbed-wire patent triggered "fence wars" between ranchers and homesteaders before fencing became universal. The saying "good fences make good neighbours," popularized by Robert Frost's poem "Mending Wall" (1914), captures the fence's enduring role as a social as well as physical boundary.

Legal aspects

Fence law varies widely by jurisdiction. Boundary fences are often presumed to be jointly owned by the neighbours on either side, with shared duties to maintain them. Many places impose height limits on front-yard fences, special requirements for pool fencing as a child-safety measure, and "spite fence" doctrines prohibiting fences erected maliciously to annoy a neighbour. Agricultural districts may follow "fence-in" rules (livestock owners must fence animals in) or the older open-range "fence-out" rule (crop growers must fence animals out).

Construction

A typical fence is built from vertical posts set in the ground — often in concrete — connected by rails or panels. Corner and gate posts take the greatest strain and are braced. Materials include treated timber, vinyl, aluminium, steel, bamboo, and composite boards. Durability depends on resistance to rot, insects, and weather at the ground line, where posts most often fail.
