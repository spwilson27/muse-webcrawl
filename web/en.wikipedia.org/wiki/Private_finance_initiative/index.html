Private finance initiative

Hospitals, schools and other public buildings in the UK were built under PFI contracts.

The private finance initiative (PFI) was a United Kingdom government programme for procuring public infrastructure through public–private partnerships. Under PFI, a private-sector consortium designed, built, financed and operated a public facility — such as a hospital, school, prison or road — while the public sector paid an annual charge over a contract typically lasting 25 to 30 years.

History

PFI was launched in 1992 by the Conservative government of John Major and was greatly expanded under the Labour governments of Tony Blair and Gordon Brown after 1997. Hundreds of projects were signed, with a combined capital value of tens of billions of pounds, in sectors including health, education, defence and transport.

Criticism

PFI was controversial from the outset. Supporters argued that it delivered infrastructure on time and on budget and transferred construction and operating risk to the private sector. Critics — including the National Audit Office, parliamentary committees and trade unions — argued that private borrowing cost more than government borrowing, that the long contracts were inflexible, and that keeping the liabilities off the government's balance sheet was an accounting motive rather than good value for money. The collapse of the construction company Carillion in 2018, a major PFI contractor, renewed scrutiny of the model.

PF2 and abolition

In 2012 the government introduced a reformed model, PF2, intended to increase transparency and public-sector equity stakes. In the 2018 budget, the Chancellor announced that the government would no longer use PFI or PF2 for new projects, although existing contracts continue to run for decades. Variants of the PFI model were adopted in many other countries, including Australia, Canada and France.
