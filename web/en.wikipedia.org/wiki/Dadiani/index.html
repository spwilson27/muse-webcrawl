Dadiani

The Dadiani palace in Zugdidi, now a museum.

The House of Dadiani (Georgian: დადიანი) was a Georgian family of nobles, dukes (eristavi) and princes (mtavari) of the western Georgian province of Samegrelo (Mingrelia), which they ruled for centuries. The family rose to prominence in the 12th century and, from the 16th century, governed Mingrelia as effectively hereditary princes under nominal Georgian, and later Ottoman, Persian or Russian, suzerainty, until the principality was absorbed into the Russian Empire in 1867.

History

The Dadiani first appear as eristavis of Odishi (Mingrelia) in the records of the medieval Kingdom of Georgia, holding hereditary offices at court such as that of mandaturtukhutsesi (chief steward). After the fragmentation of the Georgian kingdom in the 15th century, the Dadiani princes — a leading figure of the era being Levan I Dadiani (r. 1533–1572) — consolidated their power in the west, frequently allying with or resisting the kings of Imereti and the Ottoman Empire. The 17th century saw the energetic rule of Levan II Dadiani, who extended Mingrelian influence and patronised the church.

Russian period and later lines

In 1803 Grigol Dadiani accepted Russian protection, and Mingrelia became an autonomous principality of the Russian Empire. The last ruling prince, Niko Dadiani, abdicated in 1867, after which Mingrelia was directly incorporated into the empire; the family retained the princely title, some members receiving the Russian style of Prince Dadian-Mingrelsky. Branches of the family produced notable figures in Russian service, literature and Georgian public life. The former princely palace at Zugdidi, built in the 19th century, now houses the Dadiani Palaces History and Architecture Museum, which preserves family relics including a famous death mask of Napoleon.
