Less eligibility

Less eligibility
OriginEnglish Poor Law, Poor Law Amendment Act 1834
ContextPoor relief; workhouses; social policy

Less eligibility was a principle of the English Poor Law system, the central idea of the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834. It held that the condition of a person receiving poor relief at public expense—an "able-bodied pauper" in the workhouse—must be kept less eligible, that is, less desirable, than the condition of the poorest independent labourer in paid employment. The object was to deter all but the truly destitute from seeking relief and to ensure that entering the workhouse was always a last resort.

Origins and implementation

The principle was articulated by the Royal Commission into the Operation of the Poor Laws (1832–1834), influenced by Edwin Chadwick and the economist Nassau Senior, who believed the older relief system—particularly wage subsidies such as the Speenhamland system—was demoralizing the labouring poor and exploding in cost. Under the 1834 Act, outdoor relief for the able-bodied was to be abolished and replaced by the "workhouse test": relief only inside workhouses run on deliberately austere regimes—plain food, hard labour such as oakum picking and stone breaking, uniform dress, separation of spouses, and strict discipline.

Criticism and legacy

Less eligibility was attacked from the start, by campaigners against the "Bastilles" of the New Poor Law and later by social reformers who argued it punished poverty rather than relieving it, and that the poorest independent labourer's condition was itself often so wretched that relief could scarcely be made worse without killing inmates. Charles Dickens satirized the system in Oliver Twist. Although the workhouse system was formally dismantled in the twentieth century (the boards of guardians were abolished in 1929), historians and social-policy writers note the persistence of less-eligibility thinking in modern welfare debates—for example, in rules that benefits must remain below the lowest wages, sanctions regimes, and deterrent approaches to asylum accommodation.
