The Port of Antwerp is a port in the city of Antwerp, Belgium, situated on the river Scheldt about 80 kilometres inland from the North Sea. It is one of Europe's largest seaports — second only to Rotterdam in total cargo volume for much of the modern era — and a world leader in breakbulk, steel, fruit, coffee, and petrochemicals. Since April 2022 the port has been operated jointly with the Port of Zeebrugge under the unified Port of Antwerp-Bruges authority.

History

Antwerp's golden age came in the first half of the 16th century, when it succeeded Bruges as northwestern Europe's chief trading and financial centre. The Dutch Revolt and the fall of Antwerp in 1585, followed by the closure of the Scheldt to navigation (confirmed by the Treaty of Münster in 1648), strangled the port for two centuries. Napoleon, who called Antwerp "a pistol aimed at the heart of England," began rebuilding its docks; the Scheldt tolls were finally bought off in 1863, and Belgian independence unleashed rapid expansion. The port recovered from both world wars — after 1944 it was a major Allied supply artery, enduring heavy V-weapon bombardment — and grew throughout the post-war decades with vast dock complexes on both banks of the Scheldt.

Facilities and cargo

The port extends over more than 12,000 hectares, with dock basins north of the city, the Deurganckdok tidal container dock on the left bank, and a petrochemical cluster that is the largest in Europe and the second largest in the world after Houston. The port is Europe's leading steel and breakbulk port and a major hub for containers, liquid bulk, cars, and project cargo. Locks including the Kieldrecht Lock (opened 2016, among the world's largest) link the tidal river to the dock system. Inland connections — the Albert Canal to Liège, dense rail links, and road and barge networks — serve a European hinterland of tens of millions of consumers.

Merger and recent developments

The 2022 merger with Zeebrugge created a single port authority intended to strengthen the combined ports' position in containers, roll-on/roll-off traffic, and the energy transition, with ambitions in hydrogen and carbon capture. The port also faces persistent challenges: disputes over nitrogen emissions and dock expansion, dredging of the Scheldt (deepening projects have required treaty negotiations with the Netherlands), and its notoriety as a principal European entry point for cocaine smuggling, which has driven major security investments.
