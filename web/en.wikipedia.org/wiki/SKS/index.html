SKS

Type: Semi-automatic carbine
Place of origin: Soviet Union
Designer: Sergei Gavrilovich Simonov
Designed: 1943–1945
Produced: 1945–present
Cartridge: 7.62×39mm
Feed system: 10-round fixed magazine, loaded by stripper clips

The SKS (Russian: Самозарядный карабин системы Симонова, Samozaryadny karabin sistemy Simonova, "Simonov's self-loading carbine") is a semi-automatic rifle designed by Soviet small arms designer Sergei Gavrilovich Simonov in the 1940s.

The SKS was first produced in the Soviet Union but was later widely exported and manufactured by various nations. Its distinguishing characteristics include a permanently attached folding bayonet and a hinged, fixed magazine. However, as the SKS lacked select-fire capability and its magazine was limited to ten rounds, it was rendered obsolete in the Soviet Armed Forces by the introduction of the AK-47 in the 1950s. Nevertheless, SKS carbines continued to see service with Soviet border guards, internal troops, and second-line and reserve units for decades.

History

The SKS was designed alongside the new 7.62×39mm intermediate cartridge, first used in the RPD light machine gun. Field trials of early carbines took place in 1944–1945, and full production began in 1949 at Tula and later at Izhevsk. It served briefly as a front-line infantry arm before being superseded by the AK-47.

Variants and production abroad

The SKS was manufactured under license or copied in China (Type 56 carbine), Yugoslavia (M59/66), Romania, Albania, East Germany, North Korea, and Vietnam. Total worldwide production ran into the millions. Chinese Type 56 carbines were widely exported and saw extensive use in the Vietnam War.

Civilian use

Large numbers of surplus SKS rifles were exported to Western civilian markets, where they became popular inexpensive sporting and hunting rifles. Many have been modified with aftermarket stocks, detachable magazines, and scope mounts, though such conversions are regulated in some jurisdictions.
