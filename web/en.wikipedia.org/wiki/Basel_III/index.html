Basel III is a set of international banking regulations developed by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision at the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, Switzerland, in response to the deficiencies in financial regulation revealed by the 2008 financial crisis and the subprime mortgage crisis. It strengthened bank capital requirements, introduced leverage and liquidity standards, and aimed to reduce the risk of bank runs and systemic failure.

Key requirements

  • Capital: minimum Common Equity Tier 1 of 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, Tier 1 capital of 6%, total capital of 8%, plus a capital conservation buffer of 2.5% and possible countercyclical and systemic (G-SIB) surcharges.
  • Leverage ratio: a non-risk-based Tier 1 leverage ratio of at least 3%.
  • Liquidity: the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR), requiring enough high-quality liquid assets for 30 days of stressed outflows, and the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) over one year.

Implementation

Phase-in began in 2013 and ran through the decade, with the 2017 finalisation — which constrains internal risk models via an output floor — often called the "Basel III endgame" or Basel IV. Implementation timelines vary by jurisdiction; banks such as Goldman Sachs and other global systemically important banks hold additional buffers.

Criticism

Critics argue the rules remain complex and model-dependent, may constrain lending, and pushed risk toward shadow banking; supporters credit them with much larger, higher-quality bank capital since 2008.
