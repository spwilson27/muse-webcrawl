Scopolamine

Scopolamine, also known as hyoscine, is a naturally occurring tropane alkaloid and anticholinergic drug found in plants of the nightshade family (Solanaceae), including henbane (Hyoscyamus niger), jimsonweed (Datura stramonium), and the corkwood tree (Duboisia). It is used medically to prevent nausea and vomiting caused by motion sickness and to reduce saliva production before surgery. Scopolamine works by blocking muscarinic acetylcholine receptors.

Medical uses

Scopolamine is a mainstay treatment for motion sickness, most often delivered as a transdermal patch worn behind the ear that releases the drug over about three days. It is also used to prevent nausea after anaesthesia and surgery, and in palliative care to dry excessive respiratory secretions. In ophthalmology it has been used to dilate the pupil. Historically it was combined with morphine to produce "twilight sleep" in childbirth.

Side effects and toxicity

Common side effects include dry mouth, drowsiness, blurred vision and dilated pupils. At higher doses scopolamine can cause confusion, agitation, hallucinations and amnesia; it readily crosses the blood–brain barrier and has stronger central effects than its relative atropine. Because of its amnesic and sedative properties at toxic doses, scopolamine acquired a sinister popular reputation — it was investigated as a supposed "truth serum" in the early twentieth century, and in Colombia a crude preparation from Brugmansia trees known as burundanga has been linked to criminal poisonings, though many dramatic claims about it are exaggerated.

Chemistry and biology

Scopolamine is biosynthesised in plants from the amino acid ornithine via tropinone, sharing a pathway with atropine and cocaine. It differs from atropine (hyoscyamine) by an epoxide bridge on the tropane ring, which increases its central nervous system activity. World supply for pharmaceutical use is derived mainly from cultivated Duboisia in Australia.
