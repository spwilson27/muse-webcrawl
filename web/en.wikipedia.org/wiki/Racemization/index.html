Racemization

In chemistry, racemization is a conversion, by heat or by chemical reaction, of an optically active compound into a racemic (optically inactive) form, in which half of the optically active substance becomes its mirror image (enantiomer). If the conversion results in exactly equal quantities of dextrorotating and levorotating enantiomers, the mixture is called a racemate.

Mechanism

Racemization commonly occurs when a chiral centre — for example the α-carbon of an amino acid or a stereogenic carbon adjacent to a carbonyl — passes through a planar intermediate, such as a carbanion, carbocation or enol, and is then re-formed from either face with equal probability. The rate depends on temperature, pH and the stability of the intermediate.

Applications

Because L-amino acids in living tissue slowly convert to D-amino acids, amino acid racemization dating estimates the age of fossils and archaeological materials such as bone, teeth and shells. In pharmacology racemization matters for chiral drugs whose enantiomers differ in activity: thalidomide, for example, racemizes in the body, so administering a single enantiomer cannot avoid the teratogenic form. Racemization can also be exploited in dynamic kinetic resolution to convert an unwanted enantiomer back into useful material.
