A restriction enzyme, restriction endonuclease, or restrictase is an enzyme that cleaves DNA into fragments at or near specific recognition sites known as restriction sites. Bacteria and archaea use restriction enzymes, together with modification (methylation) systems, as a defence against invading viruses (bacteriophages): the host's own DNA is methylated and protected, while foreign DNA is cut. Restriction enzymes are foundational tools of molecular cloning and recombinant DNA technology.

Recognition sites and cleavage

Most commonly used restriction enzymes recognise short, often palindromic, sequences of four to eight base pairs. Cleavage may leave single-stranded overhangs ("sticky ends") — for example EcoRI cuts G|AATTC — or flush "blunt ends", as with SmaI. Sticky ends from different DNA molecules cut with the same enzyme can anneal and be joined by DNA ligase, enabling recombinant DNA construction.

Classes

Restriction enzymes are classified into types I–IV based on composition and mechanism. Type II enzymes, which cut at defined positions within or near their recognition sites and require only Mg2+ as a cofactor, are the workhorses of the laboratory; more than 3,000 have been characterised and several hundred are commercially available. Type I enzymes cut at random sites far from their recognition sequence, while type III enzymes cut at short distances offset from it. Type IIS enzymes such as FokI recognise asymmetric sequences and cut outside them, a property exploited in Golden Gate cloning.

Nomenclature

Enzyme names derive from the producing organism: the first letter from the genus, the next two from the species, followed optionally by a strain designation and a Roman numeral for the order of discovery — hence EcoRI from Escherichia coli strain RY13, first enzyme identified.

Applications

Restriction enzymes are used in molecular cloning, restriction mapping, DNA fingerprinting (historically via RFLP analysis), library construction, and diagnostics. Their discoverers Werner Arber, Hamilton O. Smith and Daniel Nathans shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
