Tugboat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A harbour tugboat spraying water jets beside a large container ship]
A tug assisting a big ship.

A tugboat (tug) is a powerful small vessel that manoeuvres other ships by pushing or towing them, especially in harbours, canals and narrow waters where large ships cannot use their own propulsion effectively. Despite their size, harbour tugs may generate 50–100 tonnes of bollard pull using azimuth thrusters or Voith Schneider propellers, and ocean-going tugs tow rigs, barges and casualties across seas.

Work

Ship-assist tugs shepherd tankers and container ships onto berths; escort tugs guard loaded tankers through confined channels; anchor-handling tugs service offshore platforms; and salvage tugs (often state-chartered emergency towing vessels) stand by to prevent groundings. River tugs push lashed barge tows of 15,000+ tons on the Mississippi.

Design

Modern ASD (azimuthing stern drive) tugs can direct thrust in any direction and work as readily astern as ahead. Firefighting (FiFi) capability, heavy towing hooks with quick release, and low freeboard aft for handling towlines are standard. Hybrid and fully electric tugs (first in New Zealand and the Netherlands in the 2020s) are entering service.

History

The first practical tug, Charlotte Dundas (1802), was a steam pioneer on the Forth and Clyde Canal. Paddle tugs gave way to screw tugs; famous firms such as Smit and Svitzer globalised towage, and wartime rescue tugs saved countless ships in the Atlantic convoys.
