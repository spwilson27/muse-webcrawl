Collateral consequences of criminal conviction

Collateral consequences of criminal conviction are the legal and regulatory sanctions and restrictions that are imposed on a person as a result of a criminal conviction, in addition to the sentence handed down by the court itself. Unlike the direct consequences of conviction — imprisonment, fines, probation or community service — collateral consequences are generally classified in law as civil or regulatory disabilities rather than as punishment, even though they may persist for life and may be more burdensome than the sentence itself.

Collateral consequences apply in many jurisdictions, but the term is most commonly used in the law of the United States, where the American Bar Association's National Inventory of the Collateral Consequences of Conviction has catalogued tens of thousands of such provisions in federal and state law — more than 44,000 in total, roughly half of which concern employment and occupational licensing.

Types of collateral consequence

Civil and political rights

A conviction may result in the loss of the right to vote (felon disenfranchisement, which varies widely by U.S. state and by country), the loss of the right to own or possess firearms, ineligibility for jury service, and ineligibility to hold public office.

Employment and occupational licensing

Many statutes bar people with specified convictions from holding professional or occupational licences, working in fields such as healthcare, education, childcare, law enforcement and financial services, or obtaining government contracts. Background-check regimes can make these bars effectively permanent even where no statute formally imposes them.

Immigration

For non-citizens, a conviction — including for many offences classed as "aggravated felonies" under U.S. immigration law, a category far broader than its name suggests — can lead to deportation, exclusion from admission, and ineligibility for asylum or naturalisation. In Padilla v. Kentucky (2010), the U.S. Supreme Court held that defence counsel must advise a non-citizen client of the deportation risks of a guilty plea.

Public benefits, housing and education

Convictions, particularly drug convictions, have historically restricted access to federal student aid, public housing, food assistance and cash welfare. Many of these restrictions have been relaxed or repealed since the 1990s, but some remain, and public housing authorities retain broad discretion to exclude applicants with criminal records.

Registration and notification

People convicted of sex offences are typically required to register with local authorities, often for life, and their presence may be publicly notified to the community. Similar, less extensive regimes exist in some places for other categories of offence.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue that collateral consequences are frequently invisible at the time of plea or sentence, are imposed automatically without judicial consideration of proportionality, and frustrate rehabilitation and reintegration, increasing recidivism. Reform efforts include "ban the box" rules delaying criminal-history questions in hiring, expungement and record-sealing statutes, restoration-of-rights procedures, and uniform laws encouraging consideration of whether a conviction is directly related to the licence or benefit sought. The Uniform Law Commission promulgated the Uniform Collateral Consequences of Conviction Act in 2010 to systematise notice and relief.
