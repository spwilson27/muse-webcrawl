Sienna

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sienna (from Italian: terra di Siena, "earth of Siena") is an earth pigment containing iron oxide and manganese oxide. In its natural state it is yellow-brown and is called raw sienna; when heated it becomes a reddish brown and is called burnt sienna. It takes its name from the Italian city-state of Siena, in whose surrounding hills it was produced during the Renaissance.

History and use

Sienna is among the oldest pigments used by humans, appearing in prehistoric cave paintings. Together with ochre and umber it forms the family of natural earth colours. It was a staple of Renaissance and Baroque palettes — used by artists such as Caravaggio and Rembrandt — and remains popular in oil, watercolour and acrylic painting. In the Colour Index it is designated PBr7 (when containing manganese) or PY43 for the yellow oxide.

Properties

Sienna is valued for its transparency, which makes it useful for glazing, and for its permanence and low toxicity. Raw sienna is a dull yellow-brown; burnt sienna is warmer and more red, widely used for underpainting ("burnt sienna imprimatura") and for rendering flesh tones and autumnal landscapes.
