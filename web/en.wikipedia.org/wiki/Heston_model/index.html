Heston model

The Heston model is a mathematical model that describes the evolution of the volatility of an underlying asset. It is a stochastic volatility model: such a model assumes that the volatility of the asset is not constant, nor even deterministic, but follows a random process.

The model was proposed by Steven Heston in his 1993 paper "A Closed-Form Solution for Options with Stochastic Volatility with Applications to Bond and Currency Options". It is widely used in the pricing of financial derivatives, particularly European options.

Basic framework

In the Heston model the asset price St and its instantaneous variance vt follow the coupled stochastic differential equations

dSt = μ St dt + √vt St dWtS
dvt = κ(θ − vt) dt + ξ √vt dWtv

where κ is the rate of mean reversion of the variance, θ is the long-run variance, ξ is the volatility of volatility, and the two Wiener processes are correlated with correlation parameter ρ. The variance process is a square-root (CIR-type) diffusion, so the variance is always non-negative; the condition 2κθ ≥ ξ², known as the Feller condition, ensures it stays strictly positive.

Valuation

The key advantage of the Heston model over the Black–Scholes model is that it admits a semi-analytic solution for the price of a European call option, expressed through Fourier inversion of a known characteristic function. This makes calibration to market option prices fast and practical. The model can reproduce the volatility smile and skew observed in equity option markets, in particular through a negative correlation ρ between asset returns and volatility changes.

Variants and extensions

Extensions include the double Heston model, in which two variance factors are used, and models combining stochastic volatility with jumps in the asset price.
