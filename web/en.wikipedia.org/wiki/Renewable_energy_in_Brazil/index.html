Renewable energy in Brazil accounts for an unusually large share of the country's energy supply by global standards. Brazil generates most of its electricity from hydropower and has, since the 2000s, rapidly expanded wind and solar generation and electricity from biomass, mainly sugarcane bagasse. Around 45–50% of Brazil's total primary energy supply is renewable — roughly three times the world average — thanks to hydroelectricity and the widespread use of sugarcane ethanol as a transport fuel.

Hydropower

Brazil's great river systems made hydro the backbone of the grid. The binational Itaipu Dam on the Paraná River, with 14,000 MW of installed capacity, is one of the largest power plants in the world and long held the record for annual generation. Other giants include Belo Monte (11,233 MW) on the Xingu River, Tucuruí, and Santo Antônio and Jirau on the Madeira. Hydro's dominance brings vulnerability to drought, as in the 2001 rationing crisis and the 2014–2015 and 2021 dry spells, which pushed Brazil to diversify into wind, solar and thermal backup.

Wind and solar

Wind power grew explosively after the PROINFA incentive programme and competitive energy auctions in the 2000s–2010s, concentrated in the north-eastern states of Bahia, Rio Grande do Norte, Piauí and Ceará, whose wind regime conveniently peaks in the dry season when reservoirs fall. By the early 2020s Brazil had over 25 GW of installed wind capacity, among the world's top markets. Solar power, historically negligible, expanded later but steeply: utility-scale plants in the sunny north-east and a boom in distributed rooftop generation under net-metering rules pushed total solar capacity past wind in the mid-2020s.

Biofuels

Brazil is the world's second-largest producer of ethanol and its pioneer. The military government launched the Proálcool programme in 1975 in response to the oil shocks, mandating blending of sugarcane ethanol into gasoline; anhydrous ethanol blending has since ranged around 20–27%. Flex-fuel vehicles, introduced in 2003, let drivers choose hydrous ethanol (E100) or gasoline at the pump, and most new cars sold in Brazil are flex-fuel. Sugar mills also burn bagasse to generate electricity, making biomass the third leg of the renewable system. Biodiesel, mainly from soy oil, is blended into diesel under a rising mandate. The RenovaBio policy (2017) created decarbonisation credits (CBIOs) to support the sector.

Challenges

Challenges include drought risk to hydro, deforestation and land-use emissions associated with Amazon dams and agricultural expansion, transmission bottlenecks between the north-east's wind and solar resources and southern demand centres, and curtailment of renewables as wind and solar growth outpaces grid flexibility.
