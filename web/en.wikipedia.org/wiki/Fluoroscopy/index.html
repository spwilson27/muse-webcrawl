Fluoroscopy is an imaging technique that uses X-rays to obtain real-time moving images of the interior of an object. In its primary application of medical imaging, a fluoroscope allows a physician to see the internal structure and function of a patient, so that the pumping action of the heart or the motion of swallowing, for example, can be watched. This is useful for both diagnosis and therapy and occurs in general radiology, interventional radiology, and image-guided surgery.

In its simplest form, a fluoroscope consists of an X-ray source and a fluorescent screen, between which a patient is placed. Since the 1950s most fluoroscopes have included X-ray image intensifiers and cameras, allowing the images to be recorded and displayed on a monitor. Fluoroscopy is similar to radiography and X-ray computed tomography (CT) in that it generates images using X-rays.

History

Immediately after Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays in 1895, researchers began experimenting with fluorescent screens to view the transmitted radiation directly. Thomas Edison investigated fluorescent materials and invented an early fluoroscope using calcium tungstate screens, though he abandoned the work after his assistant Clarence Dally suffered severe radiation burns and died. Early fluoroscopists worked in darkened rooms, dark-adapting their eyes or wearing red adaptation goggles, and received substantial radiation doses; long-term effects included dermatitis, cataracts, and cancers among pioneers.

The invention of the X-ray image intensifier in the late 1940s and 1950s, combined with closed-circuit television cameras, greatly brightened the image, reduced dose, and allowed viewing in normal room light. By the 1960s fluoroscopy had largely moved from direct viewing to remote monitor display. In the 2000s, flat-panel detectors began replacing image intensifiers, offering higher resolution, greater dynamic range, and a square field of view without geometric distortion.

Operation

Fluoroscopy produces a continuous X-ray beam that is passed through the body part being examined and is transmitted to a monitor so the body part and its motion can be seen in detail. The X-ray tube generates a beam that is attenuated as it passes through tissues of differing density; the transmitted beam strikes a detector (historically a fluorescent screen, later an image intensifier, now usually a flat-panel detector), which converts it into a visible or electronic image displayed in real time, typically at up to 25–30 frames per second. Pulsed fluoroscopy, in which the beam is emitted in short bursts, reduces dose at the cost of temporal resolution.

Medical uses

  • Barium studies of the gastrointestinal tract, such as the barium swallow and barium enema, where motion of contrast through the gut is observed directly
  • Cardiac catheterization and angiography, guiding catheters and visualizing coronary arteries
  • Orthopedic surgery, to guide fracture reduction and placement of implants and screws
  • Placement of catheters, stents, and drainage tubes in interventional radiology
  • Hysterosalpingography and voiding cystourethrography
  • Swallowing studies in speech and language pathology (modified barium swallow)
  • Guide injections in pain management procedures

Risks

Fluoroscopy involves exposure to ionizing radiation, and because examinations can be prolonged, the radiation dose to the patient can be substantial by radiological standards. Absorbed doses can approach thresholds for deterministic skin effects such as erythema and epilation during lengthy interventional procedures, and there is a stochastic risk of cancer induction. Dose is managed by pulsed operation, collimation, last-image-hold features, magnification restraint, and monitoring of reference air kerma and kerma-area product. Operators protect themselves with lead aprons, thyroid shields, leaded eyewear, and distance from the source, since scatter from the patient is their main exposure.

Non-medical uses

Fluoroscopy is used in security screening of baggage and cargo, in industrial inspection of castings and welds (a form of industrial radiography), and in the study of animal locomotion through X-ray motion analysis (XROMM). From the 1920s to the 1950s, shoe-fitting fluoroscopes were common in shoe stores, allowing customers to see the bones of their feet inside shoes; they fell out of use as the radiation hazard became better appreciated.
