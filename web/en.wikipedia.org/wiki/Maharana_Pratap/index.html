Maharana Pratap Singh I (9 May 1540 – 19 January 1597), popularly known as Maharana Pratap, was the 13th king of Mewar, a region in present-day Rajasthan, India. A Rajput ruler of the Sisodia clan, he is celebrated in Indian history and folklore for his prolonged resistance to the expansion of the Mughal Empire under the emperor Akbar, most famously at the Battle of Haldighati in 1576. Unlike most other Rajput rulers of his era, Pratap refused to submit to Mughal suzerainty or enter a matrimonial alliance with the imperial house, and he spent much of his reign waging guerrilla war from the Aravalli hills.

Early life and accession

Pratap was the eldest son of Maharana Udai Singh II, founder of Udaipur, and Rani Jaiwanta Bai. He was born at Kumbhalgarh fort in 1540. After Akbar captured the great fortress of Chittorgarh in 1568 — during which thousands of Rajputs and civilians died and the royal women performed jauhar (self-immolation) — the Mewar court withdrew to the hills. On Udai Singh's death in 1572, Pratap was crowned at Gogunda, though his accession was briefly contested by a faction supporting his younger brother Jagmal.

Conflict with Akbar

Akbar repeatedly sent envoys — including the Kachhwaha prince Man Singh of Amber and the poet-diplomat Abd al-Qadir Badayuni's missions — offering Pratap rank and honours in exchange for submission. Pratap received the embassies courteously but refused to attend the Mughal court in person or accept subordination.

Battle of Haldighati

On 18 June 1576, Pratap's army of roughly 3,000 cavalry and 400 Bhil archers met a much larger Mughal force led by Man Singh at the narrow pass of Haldighati, near Gogunda. The battle was fierce and bloody. Pratap's famed horse Chetak, wounded in the fighting, is said in legend to have carried him to safety before dying. The Mughals held the field and the victory was theirs in tactical terms, but they failed to capture Pratap or destroy Mewar's resistance, and Haldighati became, in Rajput memory, a byword for heroic defiance against overwhelming odds.

Guerrilla war and recovery

For the next two decades Pratap waged guerrilla warfare from the Aravalli forests, supported by Bhil tribal allies and financed in part by his minister Bhama Shah, who donated his fortune to the cause. He harassed Mughal supply lines, avoided pitched battle, and gradually recovered much of western Mewar; his victory over a Mughal force at the Battle of Dewair in 1582 became known in popular tradition as the "Marathon of Mewar". By the time of his death he had regained most of his kingdom except Chittorgarh and Mandalgarh. He established a new capital at Chavand, where he died on 19 January 1597, reportedly from injuries sustained while hunting. He was succeeded by his son Amar Singh I, who eventually concluded a peace with Akbar's successor Jahangir in 1615.

Legacy

Maharana Pratap is one of the most venerated figures in Rajasthan and in India generally, a symbol of courage, honour and resistance to foreign domination. Statues of him astride Chetak stand in cities across India, his birth anniversary is a public holiday in Rajasthan, and his life has been retold in ballads, novels, films and television series.
