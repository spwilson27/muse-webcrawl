Steglich esterification

The Steglich esterification is a mild method of forming esters from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol using a carbodiimide coupling reagent — typically N,N′-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) — and a catalytic amount of 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) as an acyl-transfer catalyst. The method was reported by Wolfgang Steglich and co-workers in 1978 and is also known as the Steglich–Höfle esterification, after Gerhard Höfle's earlier work on DMAP catalysis.

Reaction

The reaction is typically run in dichloromethane at room temperature. The carbodiimide first reacts with the carboxylic acid to form an O-acylisourea; DMAP intercepts this intermediate to form a highly reactive N-acylpyridinium species, suppressing the competing rearrangement to an unreactive N-acylurea, and the alcohol then attacks to give the ester, regenerating DMAP. The by-product dicyclohexylurea (DCU) precipitates and is removed by filtration. Water-soluble carbodiimides such as EDC are often substituted for DCC to simplify purification, and DMAP derivatives or additives such as HOBt may be used.

Applications

Because it proceeds under neutral, room-temperature conditions and tolerates acid- and base-sensitive functionality and sterically hindered alcohols, the Steglich esterification is a standard method in complex natural product synthesis and macrolactonization, complementing the classical acid-catalysed Fischer esterification, which requires strongly acidic conditions and is unsuitable for sensitive substrates.
