Lambton Worm

The Lambton Worm is a legend from County Durham in north-east England. It tells of a monstrous serpent-like creature caught by the young heir of Lambton Castle that grew to terrorize the countryside, and of the curse laid on the Lambton family for nine generations.

The legend

John Lambton, a dissolute young heir, skipped church one Sunday to fish in the River Wear and caught only a small, strange eel-like creature with a salamander's head. Disgusted, he threw it into a well. Years later, after Lambton had gone to the Holy Land to atone for his sins, the creature grew into an enormous worm that coiled around a local hill (Worm Hill, near Fatfield) and devoured livestock and children.

The slaying and the curse

On his return, Lambton was advised by a witch to wear armour studded with spearheads and to fight the worm in the river, but first to vow to kill the first living thing he met after his victory. The worm, cut in two, was swept away by the current and could not rejoin itself. Lambton blew his horn as a signal, but his father, forgetting the plan, ran out first; Lambton could not kill his father, and a servant was sacrificed instead. For breaking the vow, the witch cursed the Lambtons: no lord of Lambton would die in his bed for nine generations.

In popular culture

The story is celebrated in a well-known nineteenth-century music-hall song, "The Lambton Worm", written in 1867 by C. M. Leumane, and is commemorated by the Penshaw Monument overlooking the Wear.
