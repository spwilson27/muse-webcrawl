Albrecht Dürer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Melencolia I (1514), Dürer's master engraving: a winged figure brooding amid the instruments of geometry and craft, a magic square on the wall]

Albrecht Dürer (21 May 1471 – 6 April 1528) was a German painter, printmaker and theorist from Nuremberg, the towering figure of the Northern Renaissance. His woodcuts and engravings — reproduced and sold across Europe — made him the first artist to build an international reputation largely through prints, while his writings on geometry, proportion and perspective laid theoretical foundations for art in the North.

His most famous works include the Apocalypse woodcut series (1498), the engravings Knight, Death and the Devil (1513), St Jerome in His Study and Melencolia I (both 1514), the watercolour Young Hare (1502), and the Four Apostles (1526), his gift to the city of Nuremberg.

Printmaker and entrepreneur

Apprenticed first to his goldsmith father and then to the painter Michael Wolgemut, Dürer travelled to Italy twice, absorbing Renaissance perspective and anatomy. Back in Nuremberg he ran a businesslike workshop, employed agents to sell his prints, protected his monogram in one of history's first copyright disputes (against Marcantonio Raimondi's copies), and worked for the Emperor Maximilian I on monumental printed propaganda such as the Triumphal Arch.

Theory and last years

Dürer's published treatises — on measurement and geometry (1525), fortification (1527) and human proportion (1528, posthumous) — brought Italian theory north in German. His journey to the Netherlands in 1520–21, recorded in a surviving diary, shows him lionised by fellow artists and anxiously following the fate of Martin Luther. He died in Nuremberg in 1528, aged 56.
