Congenital syphilis is syphilis that occurs when a mother with untreated syphilis passes the infection to her baby during pregnancy or at birth. It is caused by the bacterium Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum, which crosses the placenta, typically after 16–18 weeks of gestation. Congenital syphilis can cause stillbirth, neonatal death, prematurity and severe disease in the infant, but it is entirely preventable by screening pregnant women and treating infection with penicillin. A resurgence since the 2010s in the United States and other countries has made it a renewed public-health concern; US cases rose roughly tenfold between 2012 and 2022.

Signs and symptoms

Clinical presentation is divided into early congenital syphilis (presenting before age 2) and late congenital syphilis (after age 2). Many infected newborns are asymptomatic at birth.

Early signs include a characteristic rash (including on the palms and soles), persistent rhinitis ("snuffles"), hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, anaemia, lymphadenopathy, and bone abnormalities such as periostitis and osteochondritis, which may cause pseudoparalysis of a limb (Parrot's pseudoparalysis).

Late signs reflect chronic inflammation and scarring: Hutchinson's teeth (notched incisors) and mulberry molars, interstitial keratitis, eighth-nerve deafness — together forming Hutchinson's triad — plus saddle nose, saber shins, frontal bossing, Clutton's joints and rhagades (scarring around the mouth).

Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines maternal serology with evaluation of the infant: quantitative non-treponemal tests (VDRL or RPR) on infant serum compared with maternal titres, examination of the placenta and umbilical cord, dark-field microscopy or PCR of lesions, long-bone radiographs, and cerebrospinal fluid examination. Because maternal antibodies cross the placenta, interpreting infant serology requires care.

Treatment and prevention

The treatment of choice is intravenous aqueous crystalline penicillin G for 10 days; procaine penicillin is an alternative in some guidelines. Prevention rests on routine serological screening of all pregnant women at the first prenatal visit (with repeat testing later in pregnancy for those at high risk) and prompt penicillin treatment of infected mothers and their partners. Adequate maternal treatment more than 30 days before delivery usually prevents congenital disease.
