Electric organ

An electric organ is an organ that an electrically active fish can use to generate an electric field. Electric organs are derived from modified muscle (in most cases) or in some cases nerve tissue, called electrocytes. These organs can be used to help the fish navigate, communicate, mate, locate prey, and sometimes to attack and kill prey.

Structure

The electric organ is composed of stacks of flat, coin-shaped cells called electrocytes, arranged in columns. Each electrocyte can generate a small voltage when activated by the nervous system; because the cells are arranged in series like the cells of a battery, their voltages sum. In the electric eel, thousands of electrocytes in the main organ, Hunter's organ, and Sachs' organ can produce discharges of up to 600–860 volts.

Types of discharge

Strongly electric fish, such as electric eels, torpedo rays, and electric catfish, produce high-voltage discharges used to stun prey or deter predators. Weakly electric fish, such as the gymnotiform knifefish of South America and the mormyriform fish of Africa, generate low-voltage fields used for electrolocation and communication.

Evolution

Electric organs have evolved independently at least six times among fishes, a celebrated example of convergent evolution. Research on electric organs informed Alessandro Volta's design of the first electric battery, the voltaic pile, which he explicitly modelled on the torpedo ray's organ.
