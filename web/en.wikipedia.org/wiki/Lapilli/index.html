Lapilli (singular: lapillus, Latin for "little stones") are pyroclasts — fragments of molten or solid volcanic rock ejected during an eruption — with diameters between 2 and 64 millimetres. In the standard classification of tephra by size, lapilli fall between fine volcanic ash (under 2 mm) and volcanic bombs and blocks (over 64 mm).

Formation and types

Lapilli form when magma is fragmented in an eruption column or lava fountain and the droplets cool in flight, landing as rounded or angular cinders and scoria — for example around the cinder cones of Hawaii, Iceland, and the Eifel. Accretionary lapilli are spherical pellets formed when moist ash aggregates around a nucleus in an eruption plume, often falling like hail from phreatomagmatic eruptions; they are a useful field indicator of wet eruption conditions. Armoured lapilli have a core of rock or crystal coated in fine ash.

Deposits

Accumulations of lapilli consolidate into lapillistone or lapilli tuff. Because lapilli are too heavy to travel far, their deposits are concentrated near the vent, building cinder cones and mantling nearby slopes; layers of lapilli in the geological record (tephrochronology) can be used to date and correlate eruptions. On the flanks of Vesuvius, the lapilli of the 79 CE eruption buried Pompeii several metres deep before the later pyroclastic surges.
