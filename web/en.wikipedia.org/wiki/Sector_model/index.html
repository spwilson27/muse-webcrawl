Sector model

The sector model, also known as the Hoyt model, is a model of urban land use proposed by the economist Homer Hoyt in 1939. It explains the internal structure of cities by the ways different social groups and land uses arrange themselves along transportation routes radiating from the city centre.

Description

Hoyt, studying rent patterns in 142 American cities for the Federal Housing Administration, found that residential areas of different quality tended to grow outward from the central business district (CBD) in wedge-shaped sectors rather than in the concentric rings described by Ernest Burgess's concentric zone model. High-rent districts located in one sector — often along a fast transport route, toward open country or high ground — and migrated outward in the same sector as the city grew. Low-rent housing clustered near industry and rail lines in other sectors, while middle-rent housing filled between them.

Factors and criticism

Key influences on sector formation include transport corridors, which lower commuting costs along their length; environmental amenities or disamenities; and the tendency of similar activities and social groups to cluster for convenience and status. The model improved on the concentric theory by recognising transport and direction of growth, but it has been criticised for underplaying the automobile era, suburbanisation and multiple business centres (addressed by the multiple nuclei model of Harris and Ullman, 1945), and for fitting the pre-war American cities it was derived from better than cities elsewhere.
