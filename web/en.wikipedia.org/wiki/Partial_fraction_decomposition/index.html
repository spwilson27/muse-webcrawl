Partial fraction decomposition

In algebra, the partial fraction decomposition or partial fraction expansion of a rational fraction (a fraction such that the numerator and the denominator are both polynomials) is an operation that expresses the fraction as a sum of a polynomial (possibly zero) and one or several fractions with a simpler denominator.

The importance of the partial fraction decomposition lies in the fact that it provides algorithms for various computations with rational functions, including the explicit computation of antiderivatives, series expansions, inverse Z-transforms, and inverse Laplace transforms.

Basic procedure

Given a rational function whose denominator factorises into distinct linear factors, one writes:

f(x) = P(x)/[(x − a)(x − b)] = A/(x − a) + B/(x − b)

The constants A and B are found by clearing denominators and equating coefficients, or by the Heaviside cover-up method: multiply through by (x − a) and substitute x = a to obtain A directly, and similarly for B. Repeated factors require terms with each power of the factor up to the multiplicity, and irreducible quadratic factors require linear numerators.

Example

(5x − 4)/(x2 − x − 2) = 2/(x + 1) + 3/(x − 2)

because x2 − x − 2 = (x + 1)(x − 2), and solving 5x − 4 = A(x − 2) + B(x + 1) gives A = 2 and B = 3.

Applications

The symbolic integration of rational functions reduces, after partial fraction decomposition, to integrating terms whose antiderivatives are rational functions, logarithms and arctangents. In engineering, the decomposition is applied to transfer functions so that time responses can be read from tables of Laplace transform pairs. The method is attributed in part to Johann Bernoulli and was developed in the early calculus of Leibniz's school.
