Malthusianism

Malthusianism is the doctrine derived from the writings of the English cleric and economist Thomas Robert Malthus (1766–1834), especially his Essay on the Principle of Population (1798, greatly expanded 1803). Its central claim is that population, when unchecked, grows geometrically (exponentially), while the means of subsistence grow at best arithmetically (linearly), so that population tends always to press against the limits of food supply.

Checks on population

Malthus argued that equilibrium is maintained by preventive checks that lower the birth rate — principally "moral restraint," late marriage, and celibacy — and by positive checks that raise the death rate: famine, disease, and war. Without moral restraint, he contended, poverty and misery are the inevitable lot of the labouring classes, and schemes of social perfection such as those of Godwin and Condorcet, against whom the Essay was aimed, were utopian.

Influence and criticism

The Essay profoundly influenced 19th-century political economy, the reform of the English Poor Laws in 1834, and the thinking of Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace, for whom the "struggle for existence" suggested natural selection. Critics from Marx onward attacked Malthus for blaming poverty on the poor; later critics pointed to the demographic transition, agricultural productivity growth, and contraception as refuting his fixed ratios. In the 20th century, "neo-Malthusianism" described movements advocating contraception and family planning, and Malthusian arguments resurfaced in debates over Paul Ehrlich's The Population Bomb (1968), the Club of Rome's Limits to Growth (1972), and modern concerns about resource scarcity and climate change.

The so-called "Malthusian trap" describes the pre-industrial pattern in which gains in productivity were absorbed by population growth, leaving living standards stagnant for millennia.
