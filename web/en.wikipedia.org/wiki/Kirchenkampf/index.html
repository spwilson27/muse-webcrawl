Kirchenkampf

Kirchenkampf Meaning: "church struggle"
Period: 1933–1945
Setting: Nazi Germany
Protestant opponents: Confessing Church
Catholic context: Reichskonkordat (1933), Mit brennender Sorge (1937)

The Kirchenkampf (German for "church struggle") is the term for the conflict between Christian churches and the Nazi government in Germany between 1933 and 1945. The regime sought to bring the churches under state control (Gleichschaltung), while clergy and laypeople who resisted formed opposition movements, most notably the Protestant Confessing Church.

Protestant churches

In 1933 the Nazi-aligned "German Christians" movement won the church elections and installed Ludwig Müller as Reich Bishop of a unified German Evangelical Church. Opponents responded with the Pastors' Emergency League and, in May 1934, the Barmen Declaration, drafted chiefly by Karl Barth, which rejected the subordination of the church to Nazi ideology and founded the Confessing Church. Leading figures included Martin Niemöller, who was imprisoned in concentration camps from 1937, and Dietrich Bonhoeffer, executed in 1945 for his involvement in the resistance.

The regime answered with arrests, censorship of sermons and the creation of a Reich Ministry for Church Affairs under Hanns Kerrl. Hundreds of pastors were imprisoned, though the state never fully subordinated the regional churches.

Catholic Church

The Holy See concluded the Reichskonkordat with the regime in July 1933, hoping to protect Catholic institutions; the regime repeatedly violated its terms, dissolving Catholic organisations and harassing clergy. In 1937 Pope Pius XI issued the encyclical Mit brennender Sorge ("With burning concern"), smuggled into Germany and read from pulpits, condemning Nazi racial ideology and breaches of the concordat. Bishop Clemens August Graf von Galen's sermons of 1941 publicly denouncing the euthanasia programme are credited with helping force its official suspension.
