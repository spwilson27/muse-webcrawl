Louis-Antoine, Comte de Bougainville (12 November 1729 – 31 August 1811) was a French admiral, mathematician and explorer. He is best remembered for leading the first French circumnavigation of the world (1766–1769), a voyage of scientific exploration that visited Tahiti, Samoa, the New Hebrides and the coast of New Guinea, and for his part in the French and Indian War in Canada and the American Revolutionary War at sea. The island of Bougainville in Papua New Guinea and the flowering vine Bougainvillea are named after him.

Early career

Born in Paris, the son of a notary, Bougainville first distinguished himself in mathematics, publishing a treatise on integral calculus in his twenties and being elected a Fellow of the Royal Society of London. He instead chose a military career, served as aide-de-camp to the Marquis de Montcalm in Canada during the Seven Years' War, fought at the capture of Louisbourg and in the defence of Quebec, and after the fall of New France negotiated in Europe. In 1763–1765 he founded a French colony in the Falkland Islands (Îles Malouines), which he later reluctantly handed over to Spain.

Circumnavigation, 1766–1769

Commissioned by Louis XV to circle the globe, Bougainville sailed from Nantes in November 1766 with the frigate Boudeuse and the storeship Étoile. After delivering the Falklands to Spain and passing the Strait of Magellan, he crossed the Pacific, reaching Tahiti in April 1768 — only months after Samuel Wallis — and claimed it for France as "Nouvelle Cythère". He went on to visit Samoa (which he called the Navigator Islands), the New Hebrides (his "Grandes Cyclades"), the Louisiade Archipelago and the Solomon Islands, including the island that now bears his name, and returned to Saint-Malo in March 1769 with the loss of only a handful of men.

The expedition carried the naturalist Philibert Commerçon, who named Bougainvillea for its commander, and the astronomer Pierre-Antoine Véron; it also carried Jeanne Baret, Commerçon's assistant, disguised as a man, who thereby became the first woman known to have circumnavigated the globe. Bougainville's account, Voyage autour du monde (1771), was a bestseller and, with its idyllic description of Tahiti, deeply influenced European ideas of the "noble savage" — Denis Diderot wrote a famous Supplement to it.

Later life

Bougainville commanded French naval forces in support of American independence, notably at the Battle of the Chesapeake (1781) and the Battle of the Saintes (1782). Surviving the Revolution despite his royal service — he was briefly imprisoned during the Terror — he was made a senator, a count of the Empire and a grand officer of the Legion of Honour by Napoleon. He died in Paris in 1811 and was buried in the Panthéon.
