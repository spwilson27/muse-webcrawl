Plimsoll line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Plimsoll line (also called the Plimsoll mark, load line or waterline mark) is a reference mark painted on the hull of a merchant ship amidships indicating the maximum depth to which the vessel may be legally and safely loaded. The mark consists of a circle with a horizontal line through it — the deck line standard — together with a "grid" of additional lines for loading in fresh water and in tropical, summer and winter conditions, since buoyancy varies with water density and temperature.

History

The device is named after Samuel Plimsoll, a British Member of Parliament who campaigned against the practice of sending overloaded, heavily insured "coffin ships" to sea. His book Our Seamen (1873) publicised the scandal; after public pressure and an embarrassing outburst by Plimsoll in the Commons, the government passed the Merchant Shipping Act 1876 requiring a load mark on British ships. Initially the shipowners could position the line themselves; authority was soon transferred to the Board of Trade and classification societies.

The marks

Letters beside the lines indicate the applicable conditions: TF (tropical fresh), F (fresh), T (tropical), S (summer), W (winter) and WNA (winter North Atlantic). The circle is crossed by the summer load line, and the vertical bar gives allowances for different water densities. Survey under the 1966 International Convention on Load Lines, administered by the International Maritime Organization, standardised the marks worldwide; the older name "Plimsoll line" remains in common use.
