Lotka–Volterra equations

The Lotka–Volterra equations, also known as the predator–prey equations, are a pair of first-order nonlinear differential equations frequently used to describe the dynamics of biological systems in which two species interact, one as a predator and the other as prey. They were proposed independently by Alfred J. Lotka in 1925 and Vito Volterra in 1926.

The equations

dx/dt = αx − βxy
dy/dt = δxy − γy

Here x is the prey population and y the predator population; α is the prey growth rate, β the predation rate, δ the rate at which predators increase by consuming prey, and γ the predator mortality rate. The model assumes prey grow exponentially when predators are absent, that predation is proportional to the encounter rate, and that predator growth depends entirely on the food supply.

Behaviour and applications

The solutions are periodic: the two populations oscillate out of phase, with predator numbers lagging behind prey numbers in a recurring cycle around an equilibrium point. Volterra developed the model to explain fluctuations in fish catches in the Adriatic Sea, and it has since become a foundational model in mathematical ecology, though its simplicity — ignoring carrying capacities, age structure and environmental variation — limits its realism. Extensions such as the Lotka–Volterra competition equations apply the same framework to competing species. See also Alfred J. Lotka and Vito Volterra.
