Richard Evans Schultes (12 January 1915 – 10 April 2001) was an American biologist, taxonomist and writer. He is widely regarded as the father of modern ethnobotany — the study of the relationships between peoples and plants — for his fifteen years of field research in the Amazon rainforest, where he documented the plant knowledge of Indigenous peoples and collected tens of thousands of specimens.

Early life and education

Schultes was born in Boston to working-class parents of German and English descent. A bedridden childhood illness led him to read accounts of Amazonian explorers, and he won a scholarship to Harvard, where a term paper on peyote redirected him toward ethnobotany. His undergraduate research took him to Oklahoma to study peyote use among the Kiowa, and his doctoral work in Mexico identified the Aztec hallucinogen teonanácatl as a Psilocybe mushroom and the morning glory ololiuqui as a source of ergoline alkaloids.

Amazon years

In 1941 Schultes travelled to the Colombian Amazon on a fellowship to study arrow poisons (curare). When the United States entered the Second World War, the U.S. government redirected him to survey wild rubber (Hevea) as part of the strategic effort to secure natural rubber supplies after the loss of Southeast Asian plantations. He remained in the Amazon until 1953, travelling some 30,000 miles by canoe, living among peoples including the Witoto, Makuna, Cubeo and Tanimuka, and collecting over 24,000 plant specimens — including hundreds of species new to science and some 1,500 with documented medicinal or ritual uses. He was among the first scientists to alert the wider world to the destruction of the rainforest and its peoples.

Harvard career and legacy

Schultes joined the Harvard faculty in 1953, becoming curator of the Orchid Herbarium, director of the Botanical Museum, and eventually Edward C. Jeffrey Professor of Biology. His courses were legendary, and his students included Wade Davis, Mark Plotkin and Michael Balick, who carried ethnobotany into conservation. With Albert Hofmann he wrote Plants of the Gods (1979), the classic survey of hallucinogenic plants. The Colombian government honoured him by naming a vast protected area, and a genus of orchids, as well as numerous species, bear his name. He campaigned until his death for Amazonian conservation and for respect toward Indigenous knowledge.
