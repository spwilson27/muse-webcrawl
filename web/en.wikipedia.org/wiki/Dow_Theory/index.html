Dow Theory

Dow Theory
OriginatorCharles H. Dow (editorials, 1900–1902)
Developed byWilliam Peter Hamilton, Robert Rhea, E. George Schaefer
FieldTechnical analysis of stock market trends

Dow Theory is a method of analysing stock market movements derived from the editorials of Charles H. Dow, co-founder of Dow Jones & Company and The Wall Street Journal. Dow never wrote a book setting out a formal theory; his ideas were organised and named by later writers, principally William Peter Hamilton in The Stock Market Barometer (1922) and Robert Rhea in The Dow Theory (1932). It is regarded as the foundation of modern technical analysis.

Basic tenets

  • The averages discount everything — market prices reflect all available information and the hopes and fears of participants.
  • The market has three trends — a long-term primary trend, medium-term secondary reactions against it, and minor day-to-day fluctuations.
  • Primary trends have three phases — in a bull market: accumulation, public participation and excess; in a bear market: distribution, public participation and panic.
  • The averages must confirm each other — the Industrial and Transportation averages should both reach new highs or lows to confirm a trend.
  • Volume must confirm the trend — trading activity should expand in the direction of the primary trend.
  • A trend remains in effect until it gives definite signals that it has reversed.

Criticism

Followers credit Dow Theory with calling major turning points, but academic studies inspired by the efficient-market hypothesis have questioned whether its signals, often recognised only after the fact, provide a systematic advantage over buy-and-hold investing.
