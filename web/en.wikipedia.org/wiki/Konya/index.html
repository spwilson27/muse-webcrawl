Konya

Konya is a major city in central Anatolia, Turkey, on the southwestern edge of the Central Anatolian Plateau. With a population of over two million in its metropolitan province — the largest Turkish province by area — it is one of the country's principal agricultural and industrial centres, and one of its most historically conservative and religious cities.

History

The site is ancient: it is identified with Iconium, a Phrygian and later Roman city visited by Saint Paul in the New Testament. The nearby Neolithic settlement of Çatalhöyük, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, dates human settlement in the plain back some 9,000 years.

Konya reached its golden age as the capital of the Sultanate of Rum, the Seljuk Turkish state in Anatolia, from the late 11th to the early 14th century. Under sultans such as Alaeddin Kayqubad I it was adorned with madrasas, mosques, and caravanserais, including the slender-minareted İnce Minareli Medrese and the Karatay Madrasa.

The city is inseparable from the Persian-language poet and mystic Jalal al-Din Muhammad Rumi (Mevlana), who settled in Konya and died there in 1273. His tomb, beneath the green conical dome of the Mevlana Museum, is one of Turkey's most visited pilgrimage sites, and the Sufi order he inspired — the Mevlevi, famous for the whirling dervish ceremony (sema) — remains emblematic of the city.

Modern Konya

An economic hub for the surrounding wheat-growing plain, Konya is known for machinery, food processing (including the traditional candy and the dish etli ekmek), and religious tourism centred on the December Şeb-i Arus festival commemorating Rumi's death. The city is served by a high-speed rail line to Ankara and Istanbul.
