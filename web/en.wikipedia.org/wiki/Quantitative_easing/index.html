Quantitative easing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Quantitative easing (QE) is a form of unconventional monetary policy in which a central bank purchases securities — government bonds and, in some programmes, corporate bonds and mortgage-backed securities — from the open market in order to lower long-term interest rates, increase the money supply and encourage lending and investment when short-term rates are already at or near zero. Announced quantities are large: the Federal Reserve's balance sheet grew from under US$1 trillion in 2007 to a peak of about US$9 trillion in 2022 across successive programmes (QE1–QE3, 2008–14, and the pandemic purchases of 2020–22).

The term was applied first to the Bank of Japan's policy of 2001–06. Mechanisms claimed include portfolio-balance effects (pushing investors into riskier assets), signalling of future policy, and liquidity provision; measured effects on yields are robust (event studies find large drops on announcement), while effects on inflation and output are debated, as are side effects — asset-price inflation and inequality, fiscal-dominance pressure (central banks holding large shares of government debt), zombie-firm survival and difficult exits (quantitative tightening, runoff or sales, with the 2013 “taper tantrum” as the cautionary episode). The ECB (from 2015) and the Bank of England (from 2009) ran their own large programmes, making QE a standard, if contested, tool of twenty-first-century central banking.
