Early-warning satellite

PurposeDetection of ballistic missile launches
SensorsInfrared telescopes
OrbitsGeostationary; highly elliptical (Molniya)
OperatorsUnited States, Russia, China

An early-warning satellite is a satellite designed to detect the launch of ballistic missiles from space, providing early warning of a possible nuclear attack so that political and military leadership can assess the threat and, if necessary, order a retaliatory strike before incoming warheads arrive. Such satellites form the space-based layer of early-warning systems, complementing ground-based early-warning radars.

Operation

Early-warning satellites carry infrared sensors that detect the intense heat of a missile's exhaust plume against the colder background of the Earth. Satellites in geostationary orbit can watch a whole hemisphere continuously, while satellites in highly elliptical Molniya orbits provide coverage of high northern latitudes where missile fields and flight paths are concentrated. Data are relayed to ground stations within seconds of a launch.

History

The United States pioneered the concept with the MIDAS programme of the 1960s, followed by the long-running Defense Support Program (DSP), whose satellites first launched in 1970 and famously detected Scud launches during the 1991 Gulf War. DSP is being succeeded by the Space-Based Infrared System (SBIRS) and the Next Generation Overhead Persistent Infrared (OPIR) satellites. The Soviet Union and Russia operated the Oko system and its successors, using a mix of highly elliptical and geostationary satellites; the newer Tundra (EKS) satellites began launching in 2015. China has also developed missile-warning satellites.

Role in strategy

Satellite warning typically gives 15–30 minutes' notice of an intercontinental missile attack — roughly twice the warning from ground radars alone — and is a cornerstone of nuclear deterrence and launch-on-warning postures. False alarms have occurred, such as the 1983 incident assessed by Soviet officer Stanislav Petrov, in which a Soviet satellite reported apparent US missile launches that were in fact sunlight reflections from clouds.
