1925 serum run to Nome
Date27 January – 2 February 1925
RouteNenana to Nome, Alaska (about 674 miles / 1,085 km)
Participants20 mushers and about 150 sled dogs in relay
Cargo300,576 units of diphtheria antitoxin
Duration5½ days (a record for the route)

The 1925 serum run to Nome, also known as the Great Race of Mercy, was the transport of diphtheria antitoxin by dog sled relay across the U.S. Territory of Alaska to save the town of Nome from a developing diphtheria epidemic. Twenty mushers and about 150 sled dogs relayed the serum 674 miles (1,085 km) in just over five days, in extreme cold and blizzard conditions. The event captured worldwide attention and is commemorated annually by the Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race.

The epidemic

In the winter of 1924–25, Dr Curtis Welch, the only physician in Nome, diagnosed cases of diphtheria among children in the town of about 1,400 people, including nearby Alaska Native communities. Nome's supply of antitoxin had expired, and the port was icebound, so no ship could deliver more until spring. An airlift was considered too dangerous in the extreme cold with the unreliable open-cockpit aircraft of the day. The decision was made to move a supply of 300,576 units of antitoxin from Anchorage by rail to Nenana, and from there by dog sled relay along the mail trail to Nome.

The relay

The serum left Nenana on 27 January 1925. Teams of experienced mushers — many of them mail drivers and Alaska Native and Norwegian settlers — passed the 20-pound cylinder of serum from team to team day and night through temperatures that fell below −50 °F (−46 °C), with wind chills far lower, across frozen rivers, the sea ice of Norton Sound, and over the Topkok mountains. Leonhard Seppala, with his lead dog Togo, covered the longest and most hazardous leg, including a shortcut across the breaking ice of Norton Sound; Togo's leg is now regarded by many historians as the outstanding achievement of the run. The final leg into Nome was driven by Gunnar Kaasen, whose lead dog Balto became the most famous dog of the event when the serum arrived at 5:30 a.m. on 2 February.

Outcome and commemoration

The antitoxin arrived in time to halt the epidemic; official deaths in Nome were kept to a small number, though the toll in surrounding Native villages was likely higher. Mushers and dogs were celebrated across the United States; a statue of Balto was erected in Central Park, New York, in 1925, and Balto himself became a national celebrity. Since 1973 the Iditarod has commemorated the run along much of the same trail, and Togo has received growing recognition, including a statue in New York and the 2019 film Togo.
