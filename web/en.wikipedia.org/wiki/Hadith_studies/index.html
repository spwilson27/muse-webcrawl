Hadith studies

Hadith studies is the academic study of hadith — the body of traditions reporting the words, actions, and tacit approvals of the Islamic prophet Muhammad — and of the methods traditionally and academically used to evaluate their authenticity, transmission, and meaning. In classical Islamic scholarship the field is known as ʿilm al-ḥadīth ("the science of hadith") and includes disciplines such as the criticism of narrators (ʿilm al-rijāl), the study of chains of transmission (isnād), and the analysis of the transmitted text (matn).

History

The systematic study of hadith developed over the first centuries of Islam as collections grew and scholars sought criteria for distinguishing reliable reports from fabricated ones. Major collections, such as the Ṣaḥīḥayn of al-Bukhārī and Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj, were compiled in the 9th century CE. Classical critics such as Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī codified terminology for grading reports as sound (ṣaḥīḥ), good (ḥasan), or weak (ḍaʿīf).

Western academic study

Modern Western scholarship on hadith began with figures such as Ignaz Goldziher, whose Muhammedanische Studien (1889–90) argued that many hadith reflect the concerns of later generations rather than the lifetime of Muhammad. Joseph Schacht extended this scepticism in The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence (1950), developing the idea that isnāds tended to "grow backwards". Later scholars, including Harald Motzki, have applied isnād-cum-matn analysis to date some traditions earlier than Schacht allowed, and the field remains an active area of debate.

Methods

Traditional hadith criticism evaluates both the chain of narrators — checking their biographies, reliability, memory, and contemporaneity — and the text itself for internal consistency and agreement with the Qur'an and better-attested reports. Academic methods additionally include form criticism, historical-critical analysis, and statistical study of transmission networks.
