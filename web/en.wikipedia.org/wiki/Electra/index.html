Electra (Ancient Greek: Ἠλέκτρα, Ēléktra, "amber") is a princess of Argos in Greek mythology, the daughter of King Agamemnon and Queen Clytemnestra. She is one of the central figures of the tragedies of the House of Atreus: after Clytemnestra and her lover Aegisthus murder Agamemnon on his return from the Trojan War, Electra saves her young brother Orestes and, years later, helps him avenge their father by killing their mother and Aegisthus.

In tragedy

All three great Athenian tragedians dramatised her story: Aeschylus in the Libation Bearers (458 BC), and Sophocles and Euripides each in a play titled Electra. In Sophocles' version she is an uncompromising figure of grief and resolve, kept unmarried and degraded in her own palace while awaiting Orestes' return; the famous recognition scene turns on a lock of hair and footprints at Agamemnon's tomb. Euripides gives a darker, more psychologically raw portrait, marrying her to a poor farmer and making her a driving force of the matricide. Her name was borrowed by Carl Jung and others for the "Electra complex", a proposed female counterpart to Freud's Oedipus complex.

Later adaptations

The story has been retold in works including Hugo von Hofmannsthal's play Elektra and Richard Strauss's opera of the same name (1909), Eugene O'Neill's Mourning Becomes Electra (1931), Jean Giraudoux's Électre (1937) and Michael Cacoyannis's film Electra (1962). The name is also borne by figures including one of the Pleiades and an Oceanid, and has been given to stars, aircraft and the Lockheed Electra airliners.
