Doñana

LocationHuelva, Seville and Cádiz provinces, Andalusia, Spain

Doñana is a region and protected natural area in southwestern Spain, at the delta of the Guadalquivir River where it meets the Atlantic Ocean, spanning the provinces of Huelva, Seville and Cádiz in Andalusia. It comprises Doñana National Park (created in 1969) and the surrounding Doñana Natural Park, together one of Europe's most important wetland reserves.

Ecology

The park protects a mosaic of marshes (marismas), shallow streams, sand dunes, scrubland and pine forest covering more than 54,000 hectares. Its wetlands flood in winter and dry out in summer, hosting enormous numbers of migratory birds — some six million birds pass through annually — including greater flamingos, spoonbills, storks and wintering geese. Doñana is a stronghold of the endangered Iberian lynx and one of the last refuges of the Spanish imperial eagle, and it is home to free-roaming horses and cattle of ancient local breeds.

History and threats

The area takes its name from Doña Ana de Silva y Mendoza, wife of the 7th Duke of Medina Sidonia, whose family used the estate as a hunting ground for centuries. Conservationists including José Antonio Valverde campaigned for its protection in the 1950s and 1960s, leading to the park's creation with support from the World Wildlife Fund. In 1998 the Aznalcóllar mine disaster sent millions of cubic metres of toxic sludge toward the park, narrowly averted at its boundary. In recent decades the wetlands have suffered from drought and from illegal water extraction for surrounding strawberry farms, prompting intervention by Spanish courts and the European Union, and measures to buy out farms and restore the aquifer.

Doñana was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994 and is also a Ramsar wetland of international importance and a Biosphere Reserve. The village of El Rocío, on the park's edge, hosts one of Spain's largest pilgrimages each Pentecost.
