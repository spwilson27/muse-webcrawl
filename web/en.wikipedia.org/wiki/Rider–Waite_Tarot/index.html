The Rider–Waite Tarot is a widely popular deck for tarot card reading. It was first published by the publisher Rider & Co. in 1909, conceived by the academic and mystic Arthur Edward Waite and illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith, both members of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. Also known as the Waite–Smith or Rider–Waite–Smith deck, it was the first mass-market tarot to feature fully illustrated scenes on all 56 Minor Arcana cards, and it remains one of the best-selling and most influential tarot decks in the world.

Overview

Earlier tarot decks, such as the Tarot de Marseilles, depicted the suit ("pip") cards with simple arrangements of suit symbols. Waite and Smith instead filled every card with symbolic narrative scenes, allowing readers to interpret images intuitively without memorising abstract meanings. Waite accompanied the deck with his guidebook The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1910).

Composition

The deck follows the structure Waite inherited from the Golden Dawn: 22 Major Arcana trumps and 56 Minor Arcana in four suits — Wands, Cups, Swords, and Pentacles — each with court cards (King, Queen, Knight, Page). Waite made notable changes to tradition: he swapped the numbering of Strength (VIII) and Justice (XI) to match the Golden Dawn's astrological correspondences, and he redesigned The Lovers to depict Adam and Eve rather than a man choosing between two women. Smith's artwork drew on sources including the late-15th-century Sola Busca tarot, the first known deck with illustrated pips.

Publication history and influence

The deck first appeared in December 1909 in a limited edition printed from stone lithographs; subsequent printings through the 20th century made it the standard deck for tarot in the English-speaking world. U.S. Games Systems acquired publishing rights in 1971 and issued the deck as the Rider Tarot, keeping it continuously in print. The imagery of Smith's cards — including the famous back design of roses and lilies — has permeated popular culture and shaped nearly every modern tarot deck that followed. The deck entered the public domain progressively as copyrights expired (2012 in the United Kingdom, seventy years after Smith's death, and 2021 in many other jurisdictions counting from Waite's death in 1942).
