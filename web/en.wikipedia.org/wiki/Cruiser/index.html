A cruiser is a type of warship. The term, which has been in use for several hundred years, has changed substantially over time: in the age of sail it described fast vessels used for independent scouting and raiding ("cruising"), and with steam and steel it became a distinct class of medium-sized warship — larger and more powerful than destroyers and frigates, smaller than battleships — designed for long-range, independent operations. Modern cruisers are large multi-mission surface combatants, principally equipped with guided missiles for air defence and land-attack strike.

A modern guided-missile cruiser, the largest class of surface combatant in most navies.

History

Early cruisers

The steam-era cruiser family began with unarmoured and "protected" cruisers (with an armoured deck) in the 1880s, joined by armoured cruisers carrying a side armour belt. The battlecruiser, introduced by Admiral Fisher with HMS Invincible (1908), traded armour for battleship guns and high speed. The Washington Naval Treaty (1922) formalised the distinction between heavy cruisers (guns up to 8-inch/203 mm) and light cruisers (guns of 6.1-inch/155 mm or less), categories central to the fleets of the Second World War.

Second World War and after

Cruisers fought as commerce raiders, fleet scouts, anti-aircraft escorts and shore-bombardment ships in every theatre. After 1945 the gun cruiser gave way to the guided-missile cruiser (CG), built around surface-to-air missiles, anti-submarine weapons and later land-attack cruise missiles; the Soviet Union and Russia built very large missile cruisers (the Kirov class, sometimes called battlecruisers) armed for anti-ship strike. The US Navy's Ticonderoga class, with the Aegis combat system and Mk 41 vertical launching system, typifies the modern type. In most contemporary navies the cruiser/destroyer distinction has blurred, as large destroyers match earlier cruisers in size and capability.
