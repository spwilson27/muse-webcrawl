Peru–Chile Trench

LocationEastern Pacific Ocean, off Peru and Chile
LengthAbout 5,900 km (3,670 mi)
Maximum depthAbout 8,065 m (Richards Deep)

The Peru–Chile Trench, also known as the Atacama Trench, is an oceanic trench in the eastern Pacific Ocean, about 160 km off the coast of Peru and Chile. It delineates the boundary between the subducting Nazca Plate (and, in the far south, the Antarctic Plate) and the overriding South American Plate.

Geology

The trench is approximately 5,900 km long and reaches a maximum depth of about 8,065 metres below sea level at Richards Deep. Subduction along this margin, proceeding at several centimetres per year, built the Andes Mountains and drives the volcanism of the Andean Volcanic Belt. Where the trench crosses the hyper-arid Atacama Desert offshore, sediment supply is unusually low, so the trench is less filled with sediment than most.

Seismicity and biology

The subduction zone has produced some of the largest earthquakes ever recorded, including the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile (magnitude 9.5, the strongest measured) and the 1868 Arica and 1877 Iquique events, which generated destructive Pacific-wide tsunamis. In 2018, an expedition to the trench documented hadal snailfish and other fauna at depths below 7,500 metres, extending knowledge of life in the hadal zone.
