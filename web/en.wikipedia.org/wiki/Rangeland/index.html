Rangeland

Rangelands are grasslands, shrublands, woodlands, wetlands and deserts that are grazed by domestic livestock or wild animals. They are ecosystems whose native vegetation consists predominantly of grasses, grass-like plants, forbs or shrubs, and that are managed as natural ecosystems rather than as planted pasture. Rangelands occupy roughly half of the world's land surface — some estimates place them at 47–54% — and support a large share of global livestock production and wildlife habitat.

Types and distribution

Rangeland types include tallgrass and shortgrass prairies, steppes, savannas, desert shrublands, tundra, alpine meadows and marshes. Major rangeland regions include the Great Plains of North America, the Eurasian steppe, the African savannas, the Australian outback and the pampas of South America. Unlike cropland, rangeland is usually unsuitable for cultivation because of low or erratic rainfall, rough terrain or poor soils.

Uses and management

Rangelands provide forage for livestock, wildlife habitat, water storage and filtration, carbon sequestration, recreation and open space. Range management applies ecological principles to grazing: controlling stocking rates, rotating animals among pastures, managing fire (including prescribed burning) and controlling invasive species and brush encroachment. Degradation through overgrazing, erosion and desertification is a major concern, particularly in arid regions under pressure from population growth and climate change. In the United States, much rangeland is publicly owned and administered by the Bureau of Land Management and the Forest Service under multiple-use mandates, a system that has generated long-running debates over grazing fees, wildlife and conservation.
