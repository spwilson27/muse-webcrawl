Liu Yu (363–422), courtesy name Dexing, posthumously Emperor Wu of Song, was the founding emperor of the Liu Song dynasty of China. Rising from impoverished commoner origins through the ranks of the Eastern Jin army, he became the dominant military and political figure of his age, campaigned successfully against the northern states, and in 420 took the throne, ending the Eastern Jin.

Early life and rise

Liu Yu was born in Pengcheng (modern Xuzhou) to a family that had fallen into poverty; he worked as a farmer, woodcutter and shoemaker before enlisting in the elite Beifu Army. He distinguished himself in the suppression of the rebel Sun En, and in 404 marched on the capital and overthrew the usurper Huan Xuan, restoring Emperor An of Jin. Thereafter Liu steadily concentrated power in his own hands, eliminating rivals such as Liu Yi and the regent Sima Xiuzhi.

Northern expeditions

In 409–410 Liu conquered the Southern Yan state, and in 416–417 he mounted his greatest campaign, recovering Luoyang and Chang'an from Later Qin — the furthest north a southern regime would reach. News of Emperor An's death and domestic intrigue forced him to withdraw, leaving his generals to hold the gains; Chang'an was soon lost again, though Henan was partly retained.

Reign

Having had Emperor An and his successor Emperor Gong killed, Liu Yu accepted Gong's abdication in 420 and proclaimed the Song dynasty (to distinguish it from the later Song dynasty of 960, called Liu Song). His short reign emphasised frugality, the promotion of merit over aristocratic birth, curbs on great clans' land and retainers, and continued pressure on the north. He died in 422 after falling ill, and was succeeded by his eldest son Liu Yifu (Emperor Shao), whom regents deposed within two years.

Assessment

Historians rank Liu Yu among the most capable military commanders of the age of division. His rise from commoner to emperor — unusual in an era dominated by great aristocratic families — and his campaigns set the pattern of the north–south confrontation for the next century and a half.
