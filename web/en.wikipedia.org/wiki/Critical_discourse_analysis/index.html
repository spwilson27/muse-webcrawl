Critical discourse analysis

CDA studies how language in media and politics reproduces power and ideology.

Critical discourse analysis (CDA) is an interdisciplinary approach to the study of discourse that views language as a form of social practice and analyses how texts and talk in social and political contexts enact, reproduce and resist power, dominance and inequality. The approach emerged from critical linguistics in the late 1970s and 1980s, in the work of scholars such as Roger Fowler, Norman Fairclough, Ruth Wodak and Teun van Dijk.

Principles

Fairclough's framework analyses any communicative event at three levels: the text itself, the discourse practice (production and interpretation of texts), and the wider sociocultural practice. Core concepts include ideology, hegemony, intertextuality and the "order of discourse". CDA is explicitly committed rather than neutral: its practitioners argue that analysis should expose how discourse sustains unequal power relations — for instance in political speeches, news coverage of immigration, educational policy and corporate communication.

Major schools

Prominent variants include Fairclough's dialectical-relational approach, Wodak's discourse-historical approach, and van Dijk's socio-cognitive approach. CDA has been applied to studies of racism in the press, populism, gender, globalisation and climate change discourse.

Criticism

Critics such as Henry Widdowson and Michael Stubbs have charged some CDA work with reading political conclusions into selective textual details, cherry-picking data, and blurring the line between analysis and advocacy. Practitioners reply that all analysis is positioned, and that methodological rigour and triangulation can mitigate bias.
