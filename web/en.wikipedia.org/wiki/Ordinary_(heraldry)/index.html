In heraldry, an ordinary (also called a charge of the ordinary class, or an "honourable ordinary") is a simple geometric shape used on a coat of arms, usually occupying a substantial portion of the shield and extending across it. Ordinaries are among the oldest heraldic devices, dating to the earliest days of heraldry in the 12th century, when bold, simple designs were needed for recognition on the battlefield.

The honourable ordinaries

Authorities differ on the exact list, but the principal ordinaries are generally:

  • Chief — a band across the top of the shield.
  • Pale — a vertical band down the centre.
  • Bend — a diagonal band from upper dexter to lower sinister.
  • Fess — a horizontal band across the centre.
  • Chevron — an inverted V-shape.
  • Cross — the upright cross formed by pale and fess combined.
  • Saltire — a diagonal cross (St Andrew's cross).
  • Pall — a Y-shape.
  • Pile — a triangle issuing from the chief.

Subordinaries and diminutives

Smaller geometric charges — the bordure, canton, inescutcheon, gyron, fret, flaunch, and lozenge among them — are often classed as subordinaries. Most ordinaries have diminutive forms with narrower width and distinctive names, such as the bendlet (half a bend), the cotise, the pallet (half a pale), and the bar (a narrow fess, used in pairs or multiples).

Lines of division

The edges of ordinaries may be straight or patterned with the heraldic lines of division — engrailed, invected, embattled, wavy (undy), nebuly, dancetty, raguly, and others — lending wide variety to simple designs.
