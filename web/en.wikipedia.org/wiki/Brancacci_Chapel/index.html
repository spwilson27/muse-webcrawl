The Brancacci Chapel (Italian: Cappella Brancacci) is a chapel in the church of Santa Maria del Carmine in Florence, central Italy. Its fresco cycle depicting the life of St Peter, painted in the 1420s by Masolino da Panicale and his younger collaborator Masaccio, is one of the founding monuments of Early Renaissance art. Left unfinished when Masaccio died in 1428, the cycle was completed in the 1480s by Filippino Lippi. The chapel is sometimes called "the Sistine Chapel of the early Renaissance" for its influence on generations of artists.

History

The chapel was acquired in the late 14th century by Pietro Brancacci and endowed by his descendant Felice Brancacci, a wealthy Florentine silk merchant and diplomat, who commissioned the frescoes around 1424. Masolino and Masaccio began work together, dividing scenes between them; Masaccio's contributions display a revolutionary grasp of linear perspective, anatomy, and light. Work stopped when Masolino left for Hungary and Masaccio departed for Rome, where he died in 1428 aged only 26. After Felice Brancacci was exiled as an enemy of the Medici in 1436, the chapel was rededicated and portraits of the Brancacci were effaced. Filippino Lippi completed the cycle in the 1480s, carefully harmonising his scenes with the earlier work. The frescoes survived a fire that gutted the church in 1771, and a major restoration in the 1980s removed centuries of grime and overpainting, recovering the brilliance of Masaccio's colouring.

The frescoes

The cycle narrates episodes from the life of St Peter, framed by two scenes from Genesis. Masaccio's Expulsion from the Garden of Eden, on the upper left, is celebrated for its anguished, fully embodied Adam and Eve driven from Paradise—Eve's cry modelled on classical sculpture. His Tribute Money combines three moments of the Gospel story in a single perspectival space, with Christ at the vanishing point; the figures cast consistent shadows from a single light source corresponding to the chapel's actual window. Other celebrated Masaccio scenes include the Baptism of the Neophytes, whose shivering convert is a landmark of Renaissance naturalism, St Peter Healing the Sick with His Shadow, and the Distribution of Alms and Death of Ananias. Masolino's more lyrical, International Gothic-inflected scenes include the Temptation of Adam and Eve and the Healing of the Cripple and Raising of Tabitha. Filippino Lippi completed the lower register, including the Disputation with Simon Magus and the Crucifixion of St Peter.

Influence

Throughout the 15th and 16th centuries the chapel served as a school for Florentine painters: according to Vasari, artists including Fra Angelico, Leonardo da Vinci, and Michelangelo studied and copied Masaccio's frescoes there; Michelangelo is said to have had his nose broken in a fight with a fellow student during these studies. The cycle's synthesis of perspective, classical form, and human emotion set the course for Renaissance painting.
