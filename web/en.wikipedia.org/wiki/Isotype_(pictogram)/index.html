Isotype (pictogram)

ISOTYPE (the International System of Typographic Picture Education) is a method of showing social, technological, biological and historical information in pictorial form. It was developed in Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s by the Austrian sociologist and philosopher Otto Neurath, working with his wife Marie Neurath (née Reidemeister) and the artist Gerd Arntz, who designed most of the system's pictograms.

Principles

Neurath, a key figure of "Red Vienna" and director of the Museum of Society and Economy (Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum) from 1925, believed statistics should be understandable to ordinary citizens and schoolchildren without specialist training. His famous motto — "words divide, pictures unite" — captured the ambition of a universal visual language.

The system's core rule is that quantities are shown by repeating a symbol a fixed number of times, never by enlarging it: each pictogram stands for a constant number of units, so comparisons remain honest. Pictograms are flat, simplified silhouettes designable without a ruler, following consistent visual grammar — a man, a factory, a ship — and charts avoid perspective, decoration and three-dimensional effects. Only a small stock of standardised "signatures" is used, combined according to set rules, in a style Neurath compared to a pictorial "international language" related to his interest in Basic English and logical positivism (he was a member of the Vienna Circle).

Spread and legacy

After the suppression of the museum in 1934, Neurath fled Austria; the team re-formed in The Hague and, after 1940, in Oxford, where Marie Neurath founded the Isotype Institute in 1942 and the method was applied to wartime and postwar public information in Britain. Isotype shaped modern information design, pictorial statistics and wayfinding symbols; its descendants include Olympic pictograms, airport signage and the pictogram charts of modern infographics and data journalism.
