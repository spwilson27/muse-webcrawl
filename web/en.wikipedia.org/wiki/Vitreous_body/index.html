Vitreous body

The vitreous body (or vitreous humour) is the clear gel that fills the space between the lens and the retina of the eyeball. It makes up about four-fifths of the volume of the eye.

Composition and function

The vitreous is about 98–99% water, with a network of collagen fibres and hyaluronic acid giving it a gel-like consistency, together with salts, sugars and cells called hyalocytes. It maintains the shape of the globe, holds the retina in place, and transmits light to the retina. Unlike aqueous humour, it is not continuously replenished: the vitreous present at birth stays for life.

Disorders

With age the gel liquefies (syneresis) and may detach from the retina — posterior vitreous detachment — producing floaters. Vitreous detachment or traction can contribute to retinal tears and detachment; surgical removal of the vitreous is called vitrectomy.
