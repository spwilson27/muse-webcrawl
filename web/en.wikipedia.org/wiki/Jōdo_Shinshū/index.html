Jōdo Shinshū (浄土真宗, "The True Essence of the Pure Land Teaching"), also known as Shin Buddhism or True Pure Land Buddhism, is a school of Pure Land Buddhism founded by the Japanese monk Shinran (1173–1263), a disciple of Hōnen. It is the most widely practised branch of Buddhism in Japan, and its teaching centres on absolute reliance on the saving power of Amida Buddha (tariki, "other-power") rather than on any effort or merit of the practitioner. History

Shinran, a former Tendai monk of Mount Hiei, concluded after twenty years of monastic practice that he could not achieve enlightenment by his own power (jiriki) in the degenerate age of the Dharma (mappō). Under Hōnen he embraced exclusive practice of the nembutsu — recitation of Amida's name, Namu Amida Butsu — as the sole means of birth in the Pure Land. Exiled to Echigo in 1207 in a suppression of the nembutsu movement, Shinran married and lived as "neither monk nor layman", teaching farmers, fishermen, and other ordinary people in the Kantō region. His masterwork, the Kyōgyōshinshō, systematised the teaching.

The community Shinran founded was organised by his daughter Kakushinni and later consolidated around the Hongan-ji temple in Kyoto. Under the eighth abbot Rennyo (1415–1499), the school expanded dramatically through pastoral letters (ofumi) and leagues of adherents (ikkō-ikki) powerful enough to govern Kaga province for a century. Oda Nobunaga's ten-year war against the Ishiyama Hongan-ji ended in 1580, and Tokugawa Ieyasu later split the Hongan-ji into eastern (Higashi) and western (Nishi) institutions to limit its power.

Teaching

Jōdo Shinshū teaches that Amida Buddha's Primal Vow guarantees birth in the Pure Land to all who entrust themselves to it. The nembutsu is therefore not a practice by which one earns salvation but an expression of gratitude for salvation already assured through shinjin (entrusting faith), itself a gift of Amida. Shinran famously taught that "even the good person attains birth in the Pure Land; how much more so the evil person" — a doctrine intended to underscore that self-power calculations of any kind are beside the point. Shin priests may marry and eat meat, and the school's clergy and temple families form a hereditary tradition.

Branches and temples

The two largest branches are the Honganji-ha (Nishi Hongan-ji) and the Ōtani-ha (Higashi Hongan-ji), each administering some ten thousand temples in Japan, alongside eight smaller branches of the ten traditional ha. The twin Kyoto head temples, with buildings such as the Goeidō and Amidado halls, are among the largest wooden structures in Japan.

In the West

Japanese emigration carried Shin Buddhism to Hawaii and North America in the late 19th century; the Buddhist Churches of America (founded 1899) is the oldest Buddhist organisation on the US mainland. Shin temples in Canada, South America, Europe, and Australia continue to serve both ethnic Japanese and other congregations.
