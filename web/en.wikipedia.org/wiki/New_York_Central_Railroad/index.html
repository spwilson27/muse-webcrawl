The New York Central Railroad (reporting mark NYC) was one of the largest railroads in the United States, operating primarily in the Great Lakes and Mid-Atlantic regions. It was formed in 1853 by the consolidation of ten railroads stretching from Albany to Buffalo, New York, and under Cornelius Vanderbilt's control in the 1860s it grew into a system linking New York City with Chicago and St. Louis along its celebrated low-grade "Water Level Route" beside the Hudson River, Lake Erie, and Lake Michigan.

History

Vanderbilt combined the New York Central with his Hudson River Railroad in 1869 and extended control to Chicago via the Lake Shore and Michigan Southern Railway. The railroad's flagship passenger train, the 20th Century Limited, inaugurated in 1902 between New York and Chicago, became one of the most famous trains in the world, renowned for its red-carpet treatment at Grand Central Terminal.

The Central built the present Grand Central Terminal, opened in 1913, and electrified its New York-area lines. Its four-track main line and enormous freight business made it the great rival of the Pennsylvania Railroad.

Decline and merger

After World War II, passenger traffic collapsed under competition from automobiles and airlines, and freight revenues stagnated. Under president Alfred E. Perlman from 1954, the Central cut costs and modernized, but the northeastern railroad industry continued to decline. On 1 February 1968 the New York Central merged with its arch-rival the Pennsylvania Railroad to form the Penn Central Transportation Company, which declared bankruptcy in 1970 — then the largest corporate failure in American history. Much of the former Central system passed to Conrail in 1976 and, in 1999, was divided mainly between CSX Transportation and Norfolk Southern.
