Iliopsoas

The iliopsoas muscle (from Latin ilio-, relating to the ilium, and Greek psoa, "loins") is a paired muscle group of the posterior abdominal wall and pelvis, formed by the union of the psoas major and the iliacus. It is the strongest flexor of the hip.

Structure

The psoas major originates from the transverse processes and bodies of the lumbar vertebrae (T12–L4) and descends through the pelvis, where it is joined by the iliacus from the iliac fossa. The combined muscle passes beneath the inguinal ligament and inserts into the lesser trochanter of the femur. The psoas minor, a slender muscle absent in many people, lies on the front of the psoas major.

Function

The iliopsoas flexes the hip (raising the thigh toward the trunk) and, with the femur fixed, flexes the trunk forward; it also contributes to lateral flexion of the lumbar spine and postural stabilisation. It is continuously active in standing and walking.

Clinical significance

The iliopsoas may be involved in snapping hip syndrome ("dancer's hip"), iliopsoas bursitis, and haemorrhage — iliopsoas haematoma is a recognised complication of anticoagulant therapy and haemophilia. An abscess of the psoas historically arose from spinal tuberculosis (Pott disease). Pain on passive hip extension (the psoas sign) is a classical indicator of appendicitis.
