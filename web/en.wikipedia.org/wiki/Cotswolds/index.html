The Cotswolds are a range of rolling hills in south-central England, running roughly 145 km (90 miles) from near Bath in the south-west to Stratford-upon-Avon in the north-east, across the counties of Gloucestershire, Oxfordshire, Warwickshire, Wiltshire and Worcestershire. The region is famed for its honey-coloured oolitic limestone — used for the characteristic stone cottages, dry-stone walls and churches of its villages — and was designated an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty (now a National Landscape) in 1966.

History

The Cotswolds' prosperity rests on the medieval wool trade: Cotswold sheep, a large breed nicknamed the "Cotswold Lion", produced a heavy, high-quality fleece exported across Europe. Wealthy wool merchants and landowners built the great "wool churches" — such as those at Northleach, Chipping Campden and Fairford — and fine manor houses. The region later became known for market towns including Cirencester (Roman Corinium), Stow-on-the-Wold, Moreton-in-Marsh, Broadway and Burford, and for villages celebrated as quintessentially English, notably Bibury, Bourton-on-the-Water and Lower Slaughter.

Geography and culture

The hills are an escarpment of Jurassic limestone; Cleeve Hill near Cheltenham, at 330 m, is the highest point. The Cotswold Way, a 164 km National Trail, follows the escarpment from Chipping Campden to Bath. The area is also associated with the Arts and Crafts movement — William Morris lived at Kelmscott Manor — and with attractions such as Blenheim Palace at its eastern edge, Hidcote Manor Garden and the Roman Baths at Bath. Agriculture, tourism and commuting to London and Oxford now dominate the economy, and house prices are among the highest in rural England.
