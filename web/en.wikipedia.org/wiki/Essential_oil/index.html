Essential oil

An essential oil is a concentrated hydrophobic liquid containing volatile chemical compounds from plants. Essential oils are also known as volatile oils, ethereal oils, or simply as the oil of the plant from which they were extracted, such as oil of clove. An oil is "essential" in the sense that it contains the "essence of" the plant's fragrance — not in the sense that it is nutritionally indispensable. Typical constituents include monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes and their oxygenated derivatives.

Production

Most essential oils are produced by steam distillation, in which steam passes through plant material and carries volatile compounds into a condenser. Citrus oils are commonly obtained by cold expression (mechanical pressing of the peel), while delicate floral materials may be extracted with solvents or by enfleurage, yielding concretes and absolutes. Yields are often low — many kilograms of plant material may be needed for a small amount of oil — which accounts for the high price of oils such as rose and sandalwood.

Uses

Essential oils are widely used in perfumery, cosmetics, soaps, food and drink flavouring (e.g., peppermint, citrus), and household products. They are central to aromatherapy, a complementary practice in which inhaled or diluted oils are used for relaxation or mood effects; clinical evidence for specific medical benefits is limited and mixed. Some oils (e.g., tea tree, oregano) show antimicrobial activity in laboratory settings.

Safety

Undiluted essential oils can irritate or sensitize skin and mucous membranes, and several are toxic if ingested, particularly to children and pets. Some (such as bergamot) are phototoxic. Quality varies, and adulteration with synthetic compounds is common in the commercial market.
