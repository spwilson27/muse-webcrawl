Naval Combat Demolition Unit

The Naval Combat Demolition Units (NCDUs) were United States Navy units established during the Second World War to clear beach obstacles and prepare landing zones ahead of amphibious invasions. Alongside the later Underwater Demolition Teams (UDTs), they are regarded as direct forerunners of the modern Navy SEALs.

Origins

The programme grew out of the Navy's bomb and mine disposal school and the experience of landings in North Africa, where obstacles and mines threatened landing craft. In 1943 a training programme was established at Fort Pierce, Florida, under officers including Lieutenant Commander Draper Kauffman, drawing many volunteers from the Seabees (Naval Construction Battalions) who already knew explosives and construction.

Normandy and the Pacific

NCDUs were typically small teams — an officer and five enlisted men — assigned to specific beach sectors. At Normandy on D-Day, 6 June 1944, gap assault teams of NCDU and Army engineer personnel landed in the first waves at Omaha and Utah beaches to blow gaps in the steel and concrete obstacles. Casualties at Omaha were extremely heavy — over half in some teams — yet enough gaps were cleared for follow-on forces. In the Pacific, NCDU personnel were progressively folded into the larger Underwater Demolition Teams, which conducted pre-invasion reconnaissance and demolition at Saipan, Guam, Iwo Jima, Okinawa, and elsewhere.

Legacy

After the war the NCDUs were disbanded, but their training culture — including the gruelling "Hell Week" instituted at Fort Pierce — passed through the UDTs to the SEAL teams created in 1962.
