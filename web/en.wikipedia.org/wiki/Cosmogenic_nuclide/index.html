Cosmogenic nuclide

[Image: Cosmogenic nuclide]

Cosmogenic nuclides (or cosmogenic isotopes) are rare nuclides created when a high-energy cosmic ray interacts with the nucleus of an atom. These nuclides are produced within the Earth's atmosphere and in material at the Earth's surface, including soils and rocks, as well as in extraterrestrial material such as meteorites.

Production

Primary cosmic rays, mostly protons and alpha particles from outside the Solar System, collide with nuclei in the upper atmosphere and generate showers of secondary particles, including neutrons and muons. These secondary particles then react with target nuclei — for example nitrogen and oxygen in the atmosphere, or silicon, aluminium and calcium in rocks — producing cosmogenic nuclides through spallation and other reactions.

Important nuclides

Widely used cosmogenic nuclides include carbon-14, beryllium-10, aluminium-26, chlorine-36, calcium-41, and the noble gases helium-3, neon-21 and argon-38 produced in surface rocks. Carbon-14 produced in the atmosphere underpins radiocarbon dating.

Applications

Measurement of cosmogenic nuclides by accelerator mass spectrometry allows scientists to date the exposure of rock surfaces to cosmic rays, a technique known as surface exposure dating. It is used to date glacial moraines, fault scarps, lava flows and erosion rates, and to determine the exposure ages and terrestrial ages of meteorites.
