Sex verification in sports

Sex verification in sports refers to the various tests and policies used to confirm the eligibility of athletes competing in women's sporting events. Governing bodies have justified such verification as preventing men from entering women's competition, but the methods employed — visual inspection, chromosome testing and testosterone measurement — have repeatedly misclassified women with differences of sex development (DSDs) and intersex traits.

History

Early verification took the form of physical examinations and "nude parades" in the 1960s, which were replaced by Barr body (sex chromatin) testing from 1968. Chromosome testing wrongly excluded women such as the Polish sprinter Ewa Kłobukowska and the Spanish hurdler María José Martínez-Patiño, who successfully challenged her ban in the 1980s. The International Olympic Committee abandoned blanket genetic screening in 1999, moving to case-by-case investigation.

Testosterone regulations

Attention shifted to endogenous testosterone. The case of South African runner Caster Semenya after the 2009 World Championships led World Athletics to adopt hyperandrogenism regulations in 2011 and, in 2018, DSD regulations requiring athletes with certain DSDs to reduce their testosterone below 10 nmol/L (later 2.5 nmol/L for some events) to compete in restricted women's events. Indian sprinter Dutee Chand's appeal to the Court of Arbitration for Sport suspended an earlier version of the rules in 2015, but the 2018 regulations were upheld. In 2023 World Athletics barred athletes who had gone through male puberty from women's events. The debate continues, balancing claims of fairness against athletes' rights and privacy.
