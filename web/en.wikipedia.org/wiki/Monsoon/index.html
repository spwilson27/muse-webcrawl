Monsoon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of prevailing winds that brings a dramatic change in rainfall. The word derives from Arabic mawsim ("season"), via the winds that sailors used to time voyages across the Indian Ocean. The best-known is the South Asian monsoon: in summer, the Asian landmass heats faster than the ocean, creating low pressure that draws moist air inland from the Indian Ocean; the air rises over the Western Ghats and the Himalayas, dumping torrential rain from about June to September. In winter the circulation reverses, bringing dry northeasterly winds (with some rain to India's southeast coast).

The Indian monsoon

The southwest monsoon reaches Kerala around 1 June and covers India by mid-July, delivering roughly 70–75% of the country's annual rainfall — the lifeblood of farming for over a billion people and a central subject of national economics and poetry. Its strength varies with El Niño (which tends to weaken it) and the Indian Ocean Dipole; failed monsoons historically meant famine, while excessive rain brings floods to cities like Mumbai and to the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta. The monsoon's behaviour under climate change — overall rainfall possibly increasing while becoming more erratic — is a major research concern for the World Health Organization's climate-and-health work and for agriculture.

Other monsoons

The East Asian monsoon brings the meiyu/baiu rains to China, Korea and Japan. The West African monsoon feeds the Sahel's short rainy season. A weaker North American monsoon brings summer thunderstorms to Arizona and northwest Mexico, and northern Australia has a monsoon season (the "Wet") from about December to March.
