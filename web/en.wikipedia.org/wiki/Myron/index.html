Myron

Myron of Eleutherae (c. 480 – c. 440 BC) was an Athenian sculptor of the mid-5th century BC. He was one of the best-known and most influential artists of the Early Classical period of ancient Greek sculpture, celebrated especially for his ability to capture movement and athletic tension in bronze.

Works

Myron's most famous work is the Discobolus (Discus Thrower), which depicts an athlete at the moment of maximum tension before releasing the discus. The original bronze is lost, but the composition survives in numerous Roman marble copies, including the Discobolus Palombino in Rome. He also made statues of the heroes Perseus and Hercules, a celebrated bronze cow on the Acropolis said by ancient writers to seem almost alive, and paired statues of Marsyas and Athena.

Style and reception

Ancient critics praised Myron for rhythm and symmetry and for revolutionising the representation of the body in motion, though some noted that his figures could appear emotionally restrained. Working almost exclusively in bronze, he stood between the Severe Style of the early 5th century and the High Classical art of Phidias and Polykleitos, by whom he was influenced and whom he in turn influenced.
