The Ser–His–Asp catalytic triad in the active site of a serine protease.

Serine proteases (or serine endopeptidases) are enzymes that cleave peptide bonds in proteins, in which a serine residue serves as the nucleophilic amino acid at the active site. They are among the most abundant and widespread proteases: roughly one third of all proteases belong to this class, and they occur in virtually all organisms, from bacteria to humans.

Mechanism

The classic serine proteases use a catalytic triad of serine, histidine, and aspartate. The histidine, polarised by the aspartate, deprotonates the serine hydroxyl, making it a powerful nucleophile that attacks the carbonyl carbon of the scissile peptide bond. This forms a tetrahedral intermediate stabilised by an "oxyanion hole", then an acyl-enzyme intermediate with release of the first product; hydrolysis of the acyl-enzyme releases the second product and regenerates the enzyme.

Families and functions

The best-studied family, the chymotrypsin-like (PA clan) proteases, includes digestive enzymes (trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase), the clotting factors of the blood coagulation cascade (thrombin, factor Xa), fibrinolytic enzymes (plasmin, tPA), complement proteases, and granzymes used by cytotoxic lymphocytes. The subtilisin-like (SB clan) proteases evolved the same triad independently—an example of convergent evolution. Specificity is largely determined by the shape of the substrate-binding pocket adjacent to the catalytic serine (e.g. trypsin prefers basic residues, chymotrypsin large hydrophobic ones).

Regulation and inhibition

Serine proteases are commonly secreted as inactive zymogens activated by proteolytic cleavage, and their activity is checked by inhibitors, notably the serpins—a superfamily of suicide-substrate inhibitor proteins. Synthetic inhibitors such as PMSF and AEBSF are standard laboratory reagents; clinical serine protease inhibitors include anticoagulants targeting factor Xa and thrombin.
