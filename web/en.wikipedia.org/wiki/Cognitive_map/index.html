Cognitive map

A cognitive map is a type of mental representation which serves an individual to acquire, code, store, recall, and decode information about the relative locations and attributes of phenomena in their everyday or metaphorical spatial environment. The concept was introduced by Edward Tolman in 1948.

History

Tolman proposed the idea in his 1948 paper "Cognitive maps in rats and men", arguing that rats navigating mazes formed internal representations of the layout rather than merely learning chains of stimulus-response associations. Kevin Lynch extended the concept to urban design in The Image of the City (1960), identifying paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks as the elements of people's mental images of cities.

Neuroscience

The discovery of place cells in the hippocampus by John O'Keefe in the 1970s, and of grid cells in the entorhinal cortex by May-Britt and Edvard Moser, provided a neural basis for cognitive maps. O'Keefe and the Mosers shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.

Types of spatial knowledge

Researchers commonly distinguish landmark knowledge, route knowledge and survey (configurational) knowledge, which develop in roughly that order as an environment is learned.
