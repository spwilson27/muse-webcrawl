Haleakalā

Elevation3,055 m (10,023 ft)
TypeShield volcano
LocationMaui, Hawaii, United States
Last eruptionBetween 1480 and 1600 (approx.)
Protected areaHaleakalā National Park (est. 1916, separate park 1976)

Haleakalā ("house of the sun" in Hawaiian) is a massive shield volcano that forms more than 75% of the Hawaiian island of Maui. Its summit, Puʻu ʻUlaʻula, rises 3,055 metres (10,023 ft) above sea level and more than 8,500 metres from the ocean floor. The mountain's vast summit depression — often called a crater, though formed largely by erosion — is about 11 km long and 3.2 km wide, studded with cinder cones.

Geology

Haleakalā is the younger and larger of the two volcanoes that built Maui. It began growing above sea level roughly a million years ago from the Hawaiian hotspot and has erupted repeatedly into historic time estimates; its most recent eruption, on the southwest rift zone, occurred sometime between about 1480 and 1600. Although dormant, it is considered capable of erupting again.

Mythology

In Hawaiian tradition, Haleakalā is where the demigod Māui snared the sun with a rope woven from coconut fibre, forcing it to slow its journey across the sky so that his mother's kapa cloth could dry — the origin of the mountain's name, "house of the sun."

National park and ecology

Haleakalā National Park protects the summit area and the lush Kīpahulu Valley on the windward coast. The park is a stronghold of endemic Hawaiian species, including the Haleakalā silversword (Argyroxiphium sandwicense subsp. macrocephalum), which blooms once after decades of growth, and the nēnē (Hawaiian goose). The summit's clear, dry, stable air hosts major observatories, including the Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope, the largest solar telescope in the world.

Tourism

Sunrise viewing from the summit is one of Maui's most popular activities; commercial bicycle tours famously descend the winding park road after dawn. The Sliding Sands (Keoneheʻeheʻe) Trail crosses the crater floor past coloured cinder cones.
