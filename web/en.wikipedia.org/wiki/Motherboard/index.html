Motherboard

TypePrinted circuit board
Also calledMainboard, system board, logic board
ComponentsCPU socket, chipset, memory slots, expansion slots
Form factorsATX, Micro-ATX, Mini-ITX

A motherboard, also called a mainboard or system board, is the main printed circuit board in a general-purpose computer and other expandable systems. It holds and allows communication between many of the crucial electronic components of a system, such as the central processing unit (CPU) and memory, and provides connectors for other peripherals. Typically the motherboard contains significant sub-systems such as the chipset, and slots for expansion cards and storage devices.

Components

The motherboard provides the CPU socket, slots for random-access memory (usually DIMM slots), and expansion slots such as PCI Express for graphics cards and other add-in boards. It carries connectors for storage (SATA and M.2), USB ports, networking, audio and power, as well as firmware stored in a flash chip that runs the BIOS or UEFI initialisation at start-up.

The chipset manages data flow between the processor, memory and peripherals; in modern designs many of its historical functions have moved onto the CPU die itself.

Form factors

Motherboards are produced in standardised form factors that specify dimensions and mounting-hole positions, ensuring compatibility with cases and power supplies. The ATX specification, introduced by Intel in 1995, and its smaller Micro-ATX and Mini-ITX derivatives remain the most common standards for desktop computers.

Laptop and mobile motherboards, often called logic boards, are custom-shaped for each machine and integrate most components permanently, trading expandability for compactness.

History

Early microcomputers placed the processor and support circuitry on a single board with expansion slots; the IBM Personal Computer of 1981 popularised the term motherboard for the main board into which daughterboards and cards were plugged. Successive generations integrated functions once handled by separate cards — disk controllers, graphics, sound and networking — onto the motherboard itself.
