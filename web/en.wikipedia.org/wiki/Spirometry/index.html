Spirometry

Spirometry
PurposeMeasure lung volumes and airflow to diagnose and monitor respiratory disease
Key outputsFVC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC ratio, PEF

Spirometry is the most common of the pulmonary function tests. It measures how much air a person can inhale and exhale and how quickly the air can be moved, using an instrument called a spirometer. Spirometry is central to diagnosing and monitoring asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and other conditions affecting breathing.

Procedure

The subject breathes through a mouthpiece connected to the spirometer, with the nose clipped shut. After breathing normally, the person inhales maximally and then exhales as hard, fast, and completely as possible (a forced vital capacity manoeuvre), usually repeating the effort at least three times for consistency. Modern devices plot volume against time and flow against volume (the flow–volume loop). Tests may be repeated after an inhaled bronchodilator to assess reversibility.

Principal measurements

ParameterMeaning
FVCForced vital capacity — total volume forcibly exhaled after maximal inhalation
FEV1Volume exhaled in the first second of the forced manoeuvre
FEV1/FVCRatio distinguishing obstructive from restrictive patterns
PEFPeak expiratory flow — maximum speed of exhalation
FEF25–75%Mean flow over the middle half of the exhalation

Interpretation

In obstructive diseases such as asthma and COPD, airflow is limited, so FEV1 falls more than FVC and the FEV1/FVC ratio drops (below about 0.70 in COPD criteria). In restrictive diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis, lung volumes shrink: FVC falls while the ratio is normal or raised. Results are compared with predicted values for a person's age, sex, height, and ancestry, and standardised interpretation guidelines are issued jointly by the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society.

History

John Hutchinson invented the water spirometer and coined the term "vital capacity" in the 1840s, systematically measuring thousands of subjects. Twentieth-century refinements added timed volumes and, later, electronic flow sensors and computerised analysis.
