Winter of Discontent

Uncollected rubbish piling up in London during the strikes of 1979

The Winter of Discontent was the period between November 1978 and February 1979 in the United Kingdom characterised by widespread strikes by local authority trade unions demanding larger pay rises than had been offered by the Labour government of James Callaghan, then in power, in its attempt to control inflation. The term, coined by journalists, derives from the opening line of Shakespeare's Richard III: "Now is the winter of our discontent."

Background

Callaghan's government had pursued an incomes policy to restrain wage growth and bring down inflation, which had peaked at over 25 per cent in 1975. By 1978 the policy, agreed with the Trades Union Congress, had largely succeeded in reducing inflation, but the government sought to impose a limit of 5 per cent on pay rises for 1978–79 ("Phase IV"), well below the rate unions expected after years of restraint. At the Labour Party conference in October 1978, a motion rejecting the 5 per cent limit was carried against the wishes of the leadership, and the TUC also rejected the limit.

The strikes

The wave of industrial action began with a strike by Ford workers in the autumn of 1978, which ended with a settlement of around 17 per cent. In January 1979, a road haulage strike disrupted supplies, and on 22 January a strike of public sector workers brought out more than a million employees in the largest stoppage since the General Strike of 1926. Ambulance drivers, hospital staff, refuse collectors and gravediggers joined strikes in the following weeks. In Liverpool, striking gravediggers left bodies unburied, while mountains of uncollected rubbish accumulated in London squares, and in some areas rats were reported among the refuse.

Political consequences

The crisis gravely damaged the Labour government. Callaghan's return from a summit in Guadeloupe in January 1979, and his perceived complacency in response to press questions — summarised by The Sun in the famous headline "Crisis? What Crisis?" although he never used those exact words — came to symbolise a government losing control. The government lost a vote of no confidence in March 1979 and was defeated in the general election of May 1979, which brought Margaret Thatcher and the Conservatives to power. The Winter of Discontent is often cited as a turning point that ended the postwar consensus and enabled Thatcher's program of trade union reform.
