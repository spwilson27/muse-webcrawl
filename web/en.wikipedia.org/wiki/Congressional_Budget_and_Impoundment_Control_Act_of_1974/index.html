Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974

The Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974 (Pub. L. 93-344, often called the Congressional Budget Act) is a United States federal law that fundamentally restructured the role of Congress in the federal budget process. Enacted in response to President Richard Nixon's extensive use of impoundment — refusing to spend funds Congress had appropriated — and to Congress's own fragmented, piecemeal budgeting, it was signed into law on 12 July 1974.

Provisions

The Act created the House and Senate Budget Committees and the nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office (CBO), which provides Congress with independent budgetary and economic analysis. It established a timetable for an annual budget resolution — a concurrent resolution setting overall levels of spending, revenue, and the deficit, and allocating spending among committees — and created the reconciliation process, an expedited procedure for bringing existing law into conformity with the budget resolution; reconciliation bills are protected from filibuster in the Senate and have become the vehicle for much major tax and entitlement legislation. The Act also changed the start of the fiscal year from 1 July to 1 October.

Impoundment control

Title X, the Impoundment Control Act, regulates presidential withholding of appropriated funds. Rescissions — permanent cancellations of budget authority proposed by the President — take effect only if Congress affirmatively approves them within 45 days of continuous session. Deferrals — temporary delays — must be reported to Congress and (as originally enacted) could be overturned by either house; after later litigation and amendment, deferrals are permitted only for limited, non-policy reasons. The Government Accountability Office is empowered to monitor and report unreported impoundments and to sue for release of unlawfully withheld funds.

Legacy

The Budget Act gave Congress its own budget, its own scorekeeper, and its own timetable for the first time, permanently shifting the balance of fiscal power toward the legislature. In practice, budget resolutions are frequently not adopted at all, deadlines are routinely missed, and much spending is enacted through omnibus measures; nevertheless, the institutions and procedures the Act created — CBO scores, budget committees, and reconciliation — remain the framework of U.S. federal budgeting.
