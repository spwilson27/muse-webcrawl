Hemolytic uremic syndrome


Hemolytic uremic syndrome
Specialty: Nephrology, haematology
Triad: Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury

Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) is a group of blood disorders characterized by low red blood cells (microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia), acute kidney failure, and low platelets. Initial symptoms typically include bloody diarrhea, fever, vomiting, and weakness, followed as the diarrhea improves by kidney problems and the risk of low urine output. While most common in children, it does occur in adults, and complications may include neurological problems and heart failure.

Causes

The most common cause (about 90% of cases) is infection with Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC), especially serotype O157:H7, often acquired from undercooked meat, unpasteurised products or contaminated produce; Shigella dysenteriae can cause a similar picture. Shiga toxin damages vascular endothelium, especially in the kidneys, triggering platelet activation and thrombotic microangiopathy. Atypical HUS results from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, often due to mutations in complement regulatory genes such as factor H; it may be triggered by pregnancy or illness and treated with the complement inhibitor eculizumab. A large outbreak in northern Germany in 2011 was caused by the enteroaggregative Shiga-toxigenic strain O104:H4.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical triad with supporting laboratory findings: schistocytes on a blood film, raised lactate dehydrogenase, low haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia and rising creatinine. Treatment is mainly supportive — careful fluid management, transfusion and, in many cases, dialysis; antibiotics are generally avoided in STEC-HUS because they may increase toxin release. Prognosis in childhood typical HUS is generally good with modern supportive care, though a minority develop chronic kidney disease.
