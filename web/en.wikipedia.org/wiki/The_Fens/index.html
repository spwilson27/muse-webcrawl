The Fens

The Fens, also known as the Fenlands, is a coastal plain in eastern England, covering parts of Lincolnshire, Cambridgeshire, Norfolk and Suffolk. Historically a vast marshy wetland of peat and silt fens around the Wash, it was progressively drained from the 17th century onward and is now one of the most productive agricultural regions in Britain, much of it lying at or below sea level and protected by embankments, pumps and drainage channels.

Geography

The Fenland basin is drained by the rivers Witham, Welland, Nene and Great Ouse, which flow into the Wash. The land is exceptionally flat; the lowest point in Britain, at Holme Fen, lies about 2.75 metres below sea level. The drained peat soils are among the richest arable land in the country, though the peat shrinks as it dries and oxidises, requiring ever-deeper drainage.

Drainage

Systematic drainage began in the 1630s under the Earl of Bedford, employing the Dutch engineer Cornelius Vermuyden, who cut channels such as the Old and New Bedford Rivers. Local resistance from "Fen Tigers", who made their living from fishing and wildfowling, sometimes turned violent. Wind pumps were later replaced by steam and then diesel and electric pumping stations.

Wildlife

Remnant wetlands at Wicken Fen and Woodwalton Fen — among Britain's oldest nature reserves — preserve something of the original fen ecosystem, and recent projects aim to rewet large areas for conservation.
