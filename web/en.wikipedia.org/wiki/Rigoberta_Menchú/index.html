Rigoberta Menchú Tum (Spanish pronunciation: [riɣoˈβeɾta menˈtʃu]; born 9 January 1959) is a K'iche' Guatemalan human rights activist and Nobel Peace Prize laureate. She has dedicated her life to publicizing the rights of Guatemala's Indigenous peoples during and after the country's civil war (1960–1996) and to promoting Indigenous rights internationally.

Life and activism

Menchú was born into a poor peasant family in the El Quiché highlands. Her parents and younger brother were killed by the Guatemalan military during its counterinsurgency campaign — her mother was tortured and murdered in 1980 and her father died in the burning of the Spanish Embassy in Guatemala City the same year. Forced into exile in Mexico, she helped found opposition groups and told her life story to anthropologist Elisabeth Burgos-Debray, published in 1983 as Me llamo Rigoberta Menchú y así me nació la conciencia ("I, Rigoberta Menchú"). In 1992, aged 33, she became the first Indigenous person — and at the time the youngest person — to receive the Nobel Peace Prize, recognized in the year of the Columbus quincentenary. The book's factual accuracy was later disputed by anthropologist David Stoll (1998), prompting a wide debate about testimonio literature; the Nobel Committee declined to revisit the award, and Menchú remains a prominent global voice for Indigenous peoples.

Political career

After the 1996 peace accords Menchú campaigned for the prosecution of former military leaders and ran for President of Guatemala in 2007 and 2011 with the Winaq party she founded — the first Indigenous presidential candidate of a major party — receiving a small share of the vote. Her foundation works on education, health and political participation for Indigenous communities.
