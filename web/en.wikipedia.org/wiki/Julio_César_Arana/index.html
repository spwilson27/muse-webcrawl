Julio César Arana del Águila (1864–1952) was a Peruvian entrepreneur and politician. A major figure of the Amazon rubber boom, he dominated the rubber trade in the Putumayo region of the Amazon basin and was a senator for Loreto. His Peruvian Amazon Company, registered in London in 1907, became internationally notorious after reports by the American engineer Walter Hardenburg and the British consul Roger Casement exposed systematic atrocities — including enslavement, torture, and mass killings — committed against the Indigenous peoples of the Putumayo under the company's regime. Early life and rubber business

Arana was born in Rioja, in the department of San Martín, Peru. He began as a travelling merchant selling hats in Yurimaguas before entering the rubber trade in the 1880s, during the height of the Amazon rubber boom. Operating from Iquitos, he built a network of river stations and trading posts that expanded into the Putumayo basin, a territory disputed between Peru and Colombia. Through debt peonage — the habilitación system — his agents bound Indigenous workers, principally of the Huitoto (Witoto), Bora, Andoque, and Ocaina peoples, to collect wild rubber in exchange for goods advanced at inflated prices.

By 1907 Arana's interests were consolidated as the Peruvian Amazon Company, a firm listed on the London Stock Exchange with British directors, giving him access to London capital and making the enterprise a matter of British public concern when abuses surfaced.

The Putumayo scandal

In 1909 the American railway engineer Walter Ernest Hardenburg, who had travelled through the region, published accounts of atrocities in the London magazine Truth, describing floggings, stocks, starvation, rape, and murder of Indigenous rubber gatherers who failed to meet quotas. The British government, because the company was British-registered and employed Barbadian overseers who were British subjects, commissioned the consul Roger Casement — already famous for his 1904 report on abuses in the Congo Free State — to investigate. Casement's 1911 report documented a catastrophic decline in the Indigenous population of the Putumayo, estimated in the tens of thousands over roughly a decade, and confirmed Hardenburg's charges.

A parliamentary select committee examined the company in 1913, and it was wound up the same year. Despite warrants issued in Peru, Arana was never brought to trial for the abuses; he denied personal responsibility and blamed subordinates.

Later life and politics

Arana remained a powerful figure in the Peruvian Amazon, serving as a senator for the department of Loreto and continuing in business in Iquitos. He defended his record for the rest of his life and died in Lima in 1952. The Putumayo atrocities have been compared to the contemporaneous abuses of the Congo rubber regime and remain a byword for the violence of the rubber boom; they formed part of the background to Mario Vargas Llosa's 2010 novel The Dream of the Celt, centred on Casement.
