Strategic Defense Initiative

The Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) was a proposed missile defence system announced by U.S. President Ronald Reagan on March 23, 1983, intended to protect the United States from attack by Soviet intercontinental ballistic missiles using ground- and space-based weapons. Critics dubbed it "Star Wars", after the film franchise, and the name stuck in public debate.

Strategic Defense Initiative
Announced: March 23, 1983, by President Ronald Reagan
Administered by: SDI Organization (from 1984)
Purpose: Defence against strategic ballistic missiles
Successor focus: Ballistic Missile Defense Organization (1993)

Programme

Reagan's speech called on American scientists to render nuclear weapons "impotent and obsolete" by intercepting missiles in flight. The SDI Organization, created in 1984 under Lt. Gen. James Abrahamson, funded research into directed-energy weapons (the free-electron and chemical lasers, and the nuclear-pumped X-ray laser of Project Excalibur), kinetic-energy interceptors and "Brilliant Pebbles" space-based interceptors, advanced sensors, and battle-management computing. Ground-based elements included the ERIS and HEDI interceptor programmes, ancestors of today's ground-based midcourse defence.

Criticism and controversy

Physicists such as Hans Bethe and Richard Garwin argued that a leak-proof shield was technically infeasible, easily defeated by countermeasures and decoys, and destabilising because it threatened the doctrine of mutual assured destruction. The American Physical Society's 1987 directed-energy study concluded that a decade or more of research would be needed before feasibility could even be judged. The programme was also controversial under the 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty.

Legacy

Total SDI spending reached roughly $30 billion by the early 1990s. With the Cold War over, President Clinton in 1993 renamed the organisation the Ballistic Missile Defense Organization and refocused it on theatre and limited national missile defence; the effort continues in today's Missile Defense Agency. Historians still debate SDI's role in pressuring the Soviet Union during the Cold War's final years.
