Taft equation

FieldPhysical organic chemistry
Developed byRobert W. Taft (1952–1953)
Relates toLinear free-energy relationships; Hammett equation

The Taft equation is a linear free-energy relationship in physical organic chemistry, developed by Robert W. Taft in the early 1950s, that separates the polar (electronic), steric, and resonance effects of substituents on the rates and equilibria of organic reactions — particularly for aliphatic systems, where the earlier Hammett equation does not apply.

Formulation

The equation takes the form log(k/k0) = ρ*σ* + δEs, where σ* is Taft's polar substituent constant, Es is the steric substituent constant, and ρ* and δ are reaction constants expressing the sensitivity of the reaction to each effect. The parameters were derived from the acid- and base-catalysed hydrolysis of esters: because acid-catalysed hydrolysis is relatively insensitive to polar effects, it furnished the steric scale Es, while comparison with base hydrolysis yielded the polar constants σ*.

Significance

The Taft treatment was a landmark in quantitative structure–reactivity analysis, allowing chemists to dissect substituent effects into independent contributions. Its σ* and Es scales were later extended and complemented by other parameter sets, such as the Charton and Sterimol parameters, and remain standard tools in mechanistic and medicinal chemistry.
