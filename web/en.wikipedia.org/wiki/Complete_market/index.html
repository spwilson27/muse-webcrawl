Complete market

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, a complete market (also called an Arrow–Debreu market) is a market in which agents can trade claims to payoffs in every possible state of the world. Formally, a market is complete when every contingent claim — every payoff pattern across states — can be replicated by a portfolio of traded securities. Completeness is a central assumption of the Arrow–Debreu model of general equilibrium and underlies much of modern financial economics.

Definition

Suppose there are S possible states of the world and N traded securities, each paying a vector of returns across states. The market is complete if the payoff matrix of the securities has rank S — that is, the securities span the whole space of state-contingent payoffs — so that any desired consumption or payoff in any state can be achieved by some portfolio. In continuous models, the analogous condition is that the volatility matrix of asset prices is invertible.

Arrow securities

The canonical securities of a complete market are Arrow securities (Arrow–Debreu securities): pure claims that pay one unit if a particular state occurs and nothing otherwise. If a full set of Arrow securities trades, any contingent claim can be constructed as a portfolio of them, and their prices give the state prices used in valuation.

Complete and incomplete markets

Real markets are generally incomplete: there are more states of the world than independent securities, so some risks cannot be hedged or insured. Sources of incompleteness include transaction costs, asymmetric information, non-traded assets such as human capital, and restrictions on short selling. In an incomplete market, equilibrium need not be unique, derivative prices need not be uniquely determined by no-arbitrage, and welfare conclusions of the complete-market model may fail.

Dynamic completeness

A market can be dynamically complete even with few securities if continuous rebalancing of portfolios replicates the payoffs of all contingent claims. The Black–Scholes model is the standard example: with one risky asset following geometric Brownian motion and a risk-free bond, continuous trading makes the market complete, which is why option prices are uniquely determined in that model.

Implications

In a complete market with no arbitrage there exists a unique stochastic discount factor (pricing kernel) consistent with all asset prices, and the fundamental theorems of asset pricing take their cleanest form. Risk can be fully shared, so competitive equilibrium allocations are Pareto efficient under the usual assumptions.
