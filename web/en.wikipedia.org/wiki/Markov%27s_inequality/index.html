Markov's inequality

In probability theory, Markov's inequality gives an upper bound on the probability that a non-negative random variable is greater than or equal to some positive threshold, in terms of its expected value.

Statement

If X is a nonnegative random variable and a > 0, then P(X ≥ a) ≤ E(X) / a. The inequality requires nothing about the distribution of X beyond nonnegativity and the existence of the mean, which makes it universally applicable but often loose.

Proof and corollaries

The proof follows by noting that E(X) ≥ E(X·1{X≥a}) ≥ a·P(X ≥ a). Applying Markov's inequality to (X − E X)2 yields Chebyshev's inequality, and applying it to etX yields the Chernoff bound. The inequality is named after Andrey Markov, and it is sharp in the sense that equality can be attained by suitable two-point distributions.

Measure-theoretic form

More generally, for a measurable function f on a measure space, μ({x : |f(x)| ≥ t}) ≤ (1/tp) ∫ |f|p dμ, a form used throughout analysis.
