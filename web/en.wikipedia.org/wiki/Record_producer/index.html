Record producer

A record producer is a music recording project's creative and technical leader, commanding studio time and coaching artists, musicians and sound engineers. The role is analogous to that of a film director: the producer oversees the recording process, shapes the sound and style of a record, selects songs and personnel, manages the budget, and is ultimately responsible for delivering a finished recording to the record label. Producers are typically paid a fee and/or "points" (a percentage of royalties), and many are themselves musicians, songwriters, arrangers or engineers.

Roles and responsibilities

  • Pre-production — choosing or developing material, arrangements, keys and tempos; hiring session musicians; booking studios.
  • Recording — directing performances in the studio, deciding takes, and supervising the recording engineer.
  • Post-production — overseeing editing, mixing and mastering.
  • Administration — controlling the recording budget and schedule on behalf of the label or artist.

History

The producer's creative role emerged in the 1960s. George Martin, working with the Beatles at Abbey Road, expanded the studio itself into a compositional tool, while Phil Spector's "Wall of Sound" made the producer an auteur whose name could sell records. Brian Wilson, Quincy Jones and later figures such as Rick Rubin, Dr. Dre, Max Martin, Mark Ronson and Jack Antonoff further raised the profile of the role; in hip hop and electronic music, the "producer" is often the composer of the underlying beat or track itself, a distinct but overlapping usage.

Producer versus engineer

Traditionally, the producer made artistic decisions while the audio engineer operated the equipment; in practice the boundaries blur, and many individuals work as producer-engineers. An executive producer, by contrast, is usually a financier or label executive with overall responsibility for a project rather than hands-on studio involvement.
