Legal Tender Act

[Image: A Civil War-era greenback]

The Legal Tender Act of 1862 was an act of the United States Congress, enacted on 25 February 1862 during the American Civil War, that authorised the issuance of United States Notes — paper currency not backed by gold or silver, popularly known as "greenbacks" — and declared them legal tender for the payment of debts. It was sponsored by Representative Elbridge G. Spaulding and supported by Treasury Secretary Salmon P. Chase as a means of financing the Union war effort.

Provisions

The Act authorised the issue of $150 million in United States Notes, receivable for most payments to and from the federal government (though customs duties and interest on the public debt were still to be paid in coin), and declared them lawful money and legal tender in payment of all debts, public and private. It also authorised a large federal bond issue. Two further authorisations, in July 1862 and March 1863, brought the total greenbacks authorised to $450 million.

Constitutional controversy

The legal tender quality of paper money was challenged after the war in the Legal Tender Cases. In Hepburn v. Griswold (1870), the Supreme Court initially held the Act unconstitutional as applied to debts contracted before its passage — an opinion written, ironically, by Chief Justice Chase, who had championed the measure as Treasury Secretary. The following year, in Knox v. Lee and Parker v. Davis (1871), a reconstituted Court reversed course and upheld Congress's power to make paper notes legal tender. Juilliard v. Greenman (1884) extended the doctrine to peacetime issuance.

Legacy

The greenbacks remained in circulation for decades, and United States Notes survived, in vestigial form, into the twentieth century. The Act is regarded as a landmark in the development of federal monetary power and of fiat currency in the United States.
