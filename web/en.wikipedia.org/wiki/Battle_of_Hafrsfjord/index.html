The Battle of Hafrsfjord was a naval battle fought in the Hafrsfjord, near present-day Stavanger in southwestern Norway, traditionally dated to 872. The forces of Harald Fairhair defeated a coalition of rival petty kings, and the battle has traditionally been regarded as the decisive event in the unification of Norway into a single kingdom.

Sources and dating

The battle is described in later saga literature, including Heimskringla, and commemorated in the skaldic poem Hrafnsmál (also called Haraldskvæði), attributed to Harald's court poet Þórbjǫrn Hornklofi, which describes berserkers and shield-maidens among the fighting. Modern historians question the traditional date of 872, derived from later Icelandic chronology, and place the battle later in Harald's reign, perhaps in the 880s or around 900; some regard the unification as a longer process rather than the result of a single battle.

The battle

According to the sagas, Harald met a coalition led by kings from Hordaland, Rogaland, Agder, and Telemark, including Eirik of Hordaland and Kjotve the Rich. The fighting was hard and long, with the opposing fleet lashed together in the fjord, but Harald's forces prevailed; the enemy kings were killed or fled, some overseas to Iceland and the Atlantic settlements.

Significance

In Norwegian national tradition, Hafrsfjord made Harald the first king of a united Norway, and the great monument Sverd i fjell ("Swords in Rock") near Stavanger, erected in 1983, commemorates the battle. The emigration of defeated chieftains to Iceland is also woven into accounts of the Norse settlement of that island, though historians now treat Harald's rule as more limited and regional than the sagas suggest.
