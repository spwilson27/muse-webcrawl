The Battle of Brunanburh was fought in 937 between Æthelstan, King of the English, and an alliance of Olaf Guthfrithson, King of Dublin; Constantine II, King of Alba (Scotland); and Owen I, King of Strathclyde. The battle, in which Æthelstan and his brother Edmund won a decisive victory, is often called the "founding battle" of England, confirming the unification of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms achieved a decade earlier.

Background

After Æthelstan's annexation of Viking York in 927, his overlordship was challenged by a coalition of the Norse kingdom of Dublin and the northern British kings, who sought to restore Norse rule in York. In 937 Olaf sailed from Ireland with a large fleet — later sources say 615 ships — and joined his allies in northern England.

The battle

The main source is the Old English poem "The Battle of Brunanburh" preserved in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, one of the most famous heroic poems in Old English. It describes a day-long slaughter in which the West Saxons and Mercians pressed the shield-wall, hewed the enemy with swords, and drove the survivors to their ships. Constantine escaped to Scotland, leaving his son dead on the field; Olaf fled by sea to Dublin. The poem boasts that never since the Angles and Saxons first came to Britain had so great a slaughter been made.

Location

The site of Brunanburh is unidentified. The strongest candidate, based on the place-name evidence (Brunanburh, "stronghold of Bruna") and the logistics of the campaign, is Bromborough on the Wirral Peninsula in Merseyside; other proposals across northern England and southern Scotland have their advocates, and the question remains open.

Legacy

Brunanburh secured Æthelstan's kingdom, although York returned to Norse hands after his death in 939 and was only finally incorporated into England in 954. The battle has a large place in English national memory: the poem was translated by Tennyson, and Jorge Luis Borges wrote a sonnet on the battle.
