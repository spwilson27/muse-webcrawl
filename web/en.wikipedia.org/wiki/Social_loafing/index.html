Social loafing

Social loafing is the tendency for individuals to exert less effort when working in a group than when working alone. It is considered one reason groups are sometimes less productive than the combined output of their members working separately.

Research

The phenomenon was first observed by French agricultural engineer Max Ringelmann, whose rope-pulling experiments (reported in 1913) found that groups pulled with less than the sum of individual efforts — the Ringelmann effect. Alan Ingham and colleagues (1974) showed the loss was partly coordination and partly genuine motivation loss, by having participants believe others were pulling when they were not. Bibb Latané, Kipling Williams and Stephen Harkins coined the term "social loafing" (1979) using clapping and shouting experiments, and proposed social impact theory as an explanation.

Causes and remedies

Loafing increases when individual contributions are unidentifiable or unrewarded, when the task seems unimportant, and when members expect others to compensate. It is reduced by making contributions identifiable, setting group goals, keeping groups small, increasing task meaningfulness and cohesion, and evaluating individual performance. The effect varies by culture and gender, being generally weaker in collectivist cultures and among women.
