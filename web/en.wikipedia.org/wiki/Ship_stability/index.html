Ship stability

Ship stability is the ability of a ship to resist capsizing and to return to an upright position after being heeled by wind, waves, turning, or shifts of weight. It is a central concern of naval architecture and of safe ship operation.

Principles

A floating ship experiences gravity acting downward through its centre of gravity (G) and buoyancy acting upward through the centre of buoyancy (B). When the ship heels, the shape of the submerged hull changes, B moves, and the line of buoyancy crosses the ship's centreline at the metacentre (M). If M lies above G — a positive metacentric height (GM) — the resulting righting lever (GZ) pushes the ship back upright. Excessively large GM makes a ship "stiff" with violent rolling; too small a GM makes her "tender" and at risk of capsizing.

Factors and regulation

Stability is affected by loading, free surface of liquids in partly filled tanks, icing, cargo shifting, and damage that floods compartments. International rules, principally the IMO Intact Stability Code and SOLAS damage-stability requirements, set minimum criteria. Famous stability failures — from the Vasa (1628) and Mary Rose (1545) to modern ro-ro ferries — have repeatedly driven improvements in design and regulation.
