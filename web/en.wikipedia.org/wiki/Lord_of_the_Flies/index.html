Lord of the Flies

Lord of the Flies is a 1954 novel by the British author William Golding. The plot concerns a group of British boys who are stranded on an uninhabited island and their disastrous attempts to govern themselves. The novel explores themes of human nature, civilisation and savagery, and has become one of the most widely studied works of twentieth-century English literature.

Plot

During a wartime evacuation, a plane carrying schoolboys crashes on a remote tropical island. With no adults surviving, the boys elect the charismatic Ralph as chief, advised by the intelligent, overweight Piggy, whose glasses are used to start signal fires. Order gradually collapses as Jack Merridew, leader of the choirboys turned hunters, draws the others into hunting, ritual and violence. Simon, a sensitive boy, discovers that the feared "beast" is merely a dead parachutist, but he is killed by the frenzied group before he can tell them. Piggy is later murdered, and Ralph is hunted across the island until a naval officer arrives, drawn by the fire the hunters set.

Themes and title

The title is a literal translation of Beelzebub, a name associated with a demon. In the novel the "Lord of the Flies" is a pig's head impaled on a stick, which Simon hallucinates as speaking to him, symbolising the evil inherent within human beings. Golding described the theme as "an attempt to trace the defects of society back to the defects of human nature."

Reception and adaptations

Initially a modest seller, the book grew in reputation and became a staple of school curricula. It was adapted into films in 1963, directed by Peter Brook, and in 1990. Golding received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1983. See also William Golding.
