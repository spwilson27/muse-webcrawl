Thought reform in China

Thought reform (Chinese: 思想改造; pinyin: sīxiǎng gǎizào, "ideological remoulding") was a campaign of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) in the People's Republic of China, launched after 1949 and especially prominent in 1951–1952, aimed at reshaping the thinking of Chinese citizens — above all intellectuals, academics, and former students and employees of Western institutions — to conform to Marxism–Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought. The closely related practice of reform through labour and "re-education" of prisoners and dissenters developed from it, and the term became well known in the West through Robert Jay Lifton's study of "brainwashing".

Origins

The roots of thought reform lie in the Yan'an Rectification Movement of 1942–1945, in which Mao consolidated the party's ideology through intensive study, criticism and self-criticism. After the founding of the PRC in 1949, the CCP extended these techniques nationwide. The Thought Reform Movement of 1951–52 targeted universities and intellectuals first: under the slogan of "wash the heart and cleanse the mind", professors and students were organised into study groups, required to write confessional autobiographies, to denounce "imperialist" and "feudal" influences in their thinking, and to subject one another to criticism and "struggle" sessions.

Methods

Techniques included compulsory study of Marxist classics and Mao's writings; small-group criticism and self-criticism (批评与自我批评); public self-examinations (检讨) in which individuals confessed ideological faults; mutual denunciation; and social isolation of those who resisted. In prisons and camps holding Korean War POWs and Chinese prisoners, thought reform was combined with exhausting routines, manipulation of guilt and group pressure — practices that UN forces and journalists described as "brainwashing", from the Chinese xǐnǎo ("wash brain").

Western studies

Psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton interviewed Westerners and Chinese intellectuals who had been subjected to thought reform in Hong Kong and published his findings in Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism (1961), which identified eight criteria of ideological totalism — milieu control, mystical manipulation, demands for purity, cult of confession, sacred science, loaded language, doctrine over person, and the dispensing of existence. Edgar Schein's Coercive Persuasion (1961) offered a parallel analysis based on repatriated prisoners of war.

Later phases and legacy

Thought reform techniques recurred through subsequent campaigns — the Anti-Rightist Movement (1957), the Socialist Education Movement, and the Cultural Revolution — and in the system of reform through labour (laogai) and re-education through labour (laojiao). Historians regard thought reform as a central instrument by which the early PRC sought to remake society and the individual, and its vocabulary and methods continue to shape debate about political education in China.
