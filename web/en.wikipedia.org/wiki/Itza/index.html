The Itza (Spanish: Itzá) are a Maya ethnic group of the Petén Basin in northern Guatemala and the Yucatán Peninsula. Historically they are known as the builders associated with Chichen Itza in Yucatán — whose name means "at the mouth of the well of the Itza" — and as the rulers of the last independent Maya kingdom, centred on the island capital of Nojpetén (also called Tayasal) in Lake Petén Itzá, which did not fall to Spain until 1697, more than 170 years after the first Spanish expeditions to the region.

History

The origins of the Itza are debated. Colonial Maya chronicles such as the Books of Chilam Balam describe the Itza as migrants who arrived in Yucatán and dominated Chichen Itza during the Terminal Classic and Early Postclassic periods, before being expelled, according to the chronicles, in the 13th century and migrating south to the Petén lakes. There, around Lake Petén Itzá, they built a confederated kingdom ruled from Nojpetén by a king known by the title Ajaw Kan Ek' ("Canek").

Contact and conquest

Hernán Cortés visited Nojpetén in 1525 on his march to Honduras and left a lame horse that the Itza venerated after its death — an episode recorded by later missionaries. Franciscan friars visited the kingdom in the early 17th century, but Itza resistance and the remoteness of Petén preserved independence while Spanish pressure grew from Yucatán and Belize. In 1696–97 the Spanish built a road from Yucatán; after negotiations failed, Martín de Ursúa y Arismendi launched an assault, and Nojpetén fell on 13 March 1697 after a battle on the lake. The last king, Kan Ek', was captured and spent the rest of his life under house arrest in Guatemala City.

Language and modern Itza

The Itza language belongs to the Yucatecan branch of Mayan languages. Once spoken around the lake, it is now critically endangered, with only a handful of elderly fluent speakers remaining in and around San José on the lake's north shore; community revitalisation efforts have been under way since the 1990s. Modern Itza communities farm maize and other crops around Lake Petén Itzá and maintain elements of traditional ceremonial practice.
