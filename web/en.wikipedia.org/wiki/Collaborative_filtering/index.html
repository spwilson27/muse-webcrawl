Collaborative filtering

Collaborative filtering (CF) is a technique used by recommender systems to make predictions about a user's interests by collecting preferences and behavior from many users. The underlying assumption is that if two people agreed in their evaluations in the past, they are more likely to agree again in the future — so a user can be recommended items liked by others with similar tastes. The term was coined in connection with the Tapestry system at Xerox PARC in the early 1990s, one of the first systems to filter email based on annotations by other readers.

Approaches

Memory-based (neighborhood) methods compute similarity directly over the user–item ratings matrix. In user-based filtering, a target user's rating for an item is predicted from the ratings of the most similar users; in item-based filtering — popularized by Amazon.com — predictions are built from similarities between items instead, which scale better and are more stable. Similarity is usually measured with Pearson correlation or cosine similarity.

Model-based methods instead learn a compact model of the data. Matrix factorization techniques, which represent users and items as vectors of latent factors and were refined dramatically during the Netflix Prize competition (2006–2009), became the state of the art, and were later extended by neural-network models. Collaborative filtering contrasts with content-based filtering, which recommends items similar in attributes to those a user already liked, and most production systems are hybrids of the two.

Problems

Well-known challenges include the cold start problem (new users or items have no ratings), data sparsity, popularity bias, and vulnerability to "shilling" attacks in which fake profiles manipulate recommendations. Implicit-feedback data such as clicks and purchases require adaptations of the classic rating-based methods.
