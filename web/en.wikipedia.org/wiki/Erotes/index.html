Erotes

The Erotes are a collective of winged gods of love and desire in Greek mythology, attendants of Aphrodite, including Eros, Anteros, Himeros and Pothos.

The Erotes (Ancient Greek: ἔρωτες, singular ἔρως) are a group of winged gods associated with love and sexual desire in Greek mythology. They form the retinue of Aphrodite, goddess of love, and are the plural counterparts of Eros, who in later tradition was multiplied into a playful company of boyish love-gods. In Roman art their equivalents are the Cupids or Amores.

Members

Individual Erotes acquired distinct personalities and domains. Eros is love and desire itself. Anteros, his brother, is requited love and the avenger of unrequited love. Himeros personifies impetuous longing and is named with Eros among Aphrodite's attendants at her birth in Hesiod's Theogony. Pothos is yearning for what is absent or unattainable, a favourite subject of the sculptor Scopas. Hedylogos represents sweet talk and flattery, Hermaphroditus the child of Aphrodite and Hermes, and in some lists Hymen, god of marriage ceremonies, joins the company.

Depiction and influence

In classical art the Erotes appear as winged youths; in Hellenistic and Roman art they shrink into chubby infants — the putti and cherubs of later Western art — engaged in hunting, fishing, athletics, harvesting and mischief. They crowd sarcophagi, mosaics, gems and wall paintings, often enacting scenes of the seasons or the vintage. Through Roman Cupids and Renaissance putti, the Erotes became one of the most durable motifs in European art, embodying desire in its playful, multiplied forms rather than as a single great god.
