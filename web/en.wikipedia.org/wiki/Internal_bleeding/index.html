Internal bleeding

SpecialtyEmergency medicine
SymptomsPain, dizziness, pallor, rapid pulse, low blood pressure
CausesTrauma, ulcers, aneurysm rupture, anticoagulants

Internal bleeding (also called internal haemorrhage) is a loss of blood from a blood vessel that collects inside the body. Internal bleeding is usually not visible from outside the body and can be a serious medical emergency, because substantial blood loss may occur before symptoms become obvious, and because accumulated blood can compress or damage organs. Its severity depends on the rate of bleeding and the location of the bleed.

Causes

Internal bleeding may result from trauma — for example, blunt abdominal injury damaging the liver or spleen, pelvic fractures, or penetrating wounds — or from non-traumatic causes. Common non-traumatic sources include bleeding peptic ulcers and varices in the gastrointestinal tract, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy, intracranial haemorrhage after head injury or from a ruptured cerebral aneurysm, and bleeding into muscles or joints in people with haemophilia. Medicines that impair clotting, such as warfarin and other anticoagulants, increase the risk.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on where the blood collects. Bleeding into the abdomen may cause abdominal pain, distension and bruising; bleeding into the skull raises intracranial pressure and can cause headache, vomiting and reduced consciousness; gastrointestinal bleeding may present as vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools. As blood loss increases, general signs of hypovolaemic shock appear: rapid weak pulse, low blood pressure, pale clammy skin, thirst, confusion and eventually collapse.

Diagnosis

Because the bleeding is hidden, diagnosis relies on clinical assessment supported by imaging and laboratory tests. Ultrasound (such as a FAST scan in trauma), CT scanning with contrast, and endoscopy are commonly used to locate the source. Blood tests may show a falling haemoglobin, although in acute bleeding the haemoglobin may initially be normal.

Treatment

Initial management follows the principles of resuscitation: securing the airway and breathing, establishing intravenous access, giving fluids and blood products, and treating shock. Definitive treatment aims to stop the bleeding, which may require endoscopic therapy, interventional radiology (such as embolisation), or surgery. Correcting clotting abnormalities and reversing anticoagulants are also important. Uncontrolled internal bleeding is life-threatening and requires immediate emergency care.
