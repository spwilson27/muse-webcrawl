Assisted suicide

Assisted dying laws vary widely around the world.
Assisted suicide
Other namesAssisted dying, physician-assisted suicide (when a doctor is involved)
FieldMedical ethics, law, palliative care
Related conceptsEuthanasia, right to die, advance directive, hospice

Assisted suicide, sometimes called assisted dying, is the practice of ending one's own life with the help of another person, typically by self-administering a lethal medication that has been prescribed or supplied for that purpose. It is distinguished from euthanasia, in which another person directly administers the lethal agent. The practice is one of the most debated topics in medical ethics and law.

Terminology

Supporters often prefer the term "assisted dying" or "medical aid in dying," arguing that "suicide" carries connotations of mental illness and impulsivity that do not apply to a terminally ill person making a considered decision. Opponents argue that the plainer term is more accurate and that euphemisms obscure the moral reality. When a physician prescribes the medication, the practice is called physician-assisted suicide or physician-assisted dying.

Legal status

Assisted suicide is legal under regulated conditions in a number of jurisdictions, including Switzerland (where it has been tolerated since the 1940s provided the assistant has no selfish motive), the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Canada (as medical assistance in dying, MAiD), Spain, New Zealand, several Australian states, and a number of US states including Oregon — whose Death with Dignity Act, in force since 1997, was the first such law in the United States — Washington, California, Colorado, Vermont, Hawaii, Maine, New Jersey, New Mexico and the District of Columbia. Most laws restrict eligibility to adults with decision-making capacity who are terminally ill, and impose safeguards such as waiting periods, multiple requests and independent medical assessments.

Arguments

Supporters cite personal autonomy, relief of unbearable suffering, and dignity in dying. Opponents, including many disability-rights advocates and religious organisations, warn of a "slippery slope," pressure on vulnerable patients, and the risk that inadequate palliative care drives requests. Empirical studies from Oregon and elsewhere are frequently cited by both sides.
