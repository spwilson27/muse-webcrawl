Busy waiting

FieldComputer science, concurrent computing
Also calledSpinning
Implemented bySpinlocks
AlternativeBlocking / sleeping waits

In busy waiting, a process repeatedly checks a condition without yielding the processor.

Busy waiting, also called spinning, is a technique in which a process repeatedly checks to see whether a condition is true, such as whether a lock has been released or input is available, without relinquishing the processor. The waiting process consumes CPU cycles while doing no useful work.

Uses

Busy waiting is appropriate when the expected wait is very short — shorter than the cost of a context switch — as in spinlocks on multiprocessor systems, where another processor is expected to release the lock almost immediately. It is also used in low-level device drivers polling hardware status registers, and in precise timing loops in embedded systems where no operating system scheduler exists.

Drawbacks

On a single processor, busy waiting wastes cycles that the process being awaited could have used, and can even cause deadlock-like stalls if priorities are mismatched. It increases power consumption and heat, a serious concern in laptops and data centres. Most programming environments therefore provide blocking primitives — sleeps, condition variables, and interrupts — and modern spinlocks are often "spin-then-block" hybrids or include processor hints such as the x86 PAUSE instruction to reduce contention and power draw.
