Robert Allen Bjork (born May 25, 1939) is an American cognitive psychologist and Distinguished Research Professor of Psychology at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA). His research focuses on human learning and memory, particularly the relationship between how information is encoded, stored, and retrieved, and the implications of that relationship for education and training.

Bjork is best known for the concept of desirable difficulties—conditions of learning that make acquisition appear slower or harder, such as spacing, interleaving, testing, and varying practice conditions, but that produce stronger long-term retention and transfer. With his wife and frequent collaborator Elizabeth Ligon Bjork, he founded the Bjork Learning and Forgetting Lab at UCLA. His other contributions include the New Theory of Disuse (with Elizabeth Bjork), which distinguishes storage strength from retrieval strength, and research on inhibitory processes in memory, including retrieval-induced forgetting and directed forgetting.

Education and career

Bjork earned his B.A. in mathematics from the University of Minnesota and his Ph.D. in psychology from Stanford University. Before joining UCLA, he taught at the University of Michigan. He has served as editor of Psychological Review and as president of the Association for Psychological Science, and has received awards for research and for the application of psychological science to education.

Desirable difficulties

The desirable difficulties framework explains why learners' intuitions about effective study are often wrong: massed re-reading feels productive but yields rapid forgetting, while effortful retrieval practice, spaced study, and interleaved problem types feel harder but enhance durable learning. The Bjorks' work has influenced evidence-based study strategies and instructional design. A difficulty counts as desirable only if the learner can overcome it and it engages processes that support later retrieval.
