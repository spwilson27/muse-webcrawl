Driftless Area
LocationSouthwestern Wisconsin, northwestern Illinois, northeastern Iowa, southeastern Minnesota, US
Area~62,000 km² (24,000 sq mi)
Defining featureUnglaciated during the last Ice Age; no glacial drift

The Driftless Area, also known as the Paleozoic Plateau, is a region of the Midwestern United States — chiefly southwestern Wisconsin with portions of southeastern Minnesota, northeastern Iowa and northwestern Illinois — that escaped glaciation during the last Ice Age. It takes its name from the absence of drift, the gravel, sand and boulders deposited by glaciers over the surrounding landscape. Surrounded on all sides by glaciated terrain, the Driftless Area is an island of much older, deeply eroded topography.

Landscape

Because glaciers never planed it flat or filled its valleys, the region retains steep, rugged hills, sandstone and limestone bluffs, narrow stream valleys (locally called coulees), caves, sinkholes and cold-water spring-fed trout streams — scenery strikingly unlike the flat farmland around it. The Mississippi River, which forms the region's western edge, flows between towering bluffs here. Goatsuckers aside, notable features include the effigy mounds preserved at Effigy Mounds National Monument and the lead-mining country around Galena, Illinois.

Ecology and history

The Driftless Area's microclimates shelter unusual "algific talus slopes" — cold-air-producing scree slopes that harbour relict northern species left behind since the Ice Age, including the endangered Iowa Pleistocene snail. The region supported the mound-building cultures of the Woodland period and was heavily settled in the 19th century by lead miners and immigrant farmers, including Norwegian, German and Swiss communities. Today it is noted for small-scale and organic farming (the headquarters of Organic Valley is at La Farge, Wisconsin), trout fishing, and tourism in towns such as Decorah, Iowa, and Viroqua and Prairie du Chien, Wisconsin.
