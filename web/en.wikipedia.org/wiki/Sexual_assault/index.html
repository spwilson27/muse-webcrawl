Sexual assault

Sexual assault is an act in which one intentionally sexually touches another person without that person's consent, or coerces or physically forces a person to engage in a sexual act against their will. It is a form of sexual violence, and encompasses a range of non-consensual sexual behaviours, including rape, groping, child sexual abuse, attempted rape and non-consensual sexual contact. Because definitions and recording practices vary between jurisdictions, reported rates of sexual assault differ widely around the world, and researchers generally agree that official statistics substantially understate its true prevalence.

Definition

Sexual assault is generally defined as any sexual act or attempt to obtain a sexual act by violence or coercion, or in which the other person does not or cannot consent. Consent must be freely given, informed and revocable; it cannot be given by a person who is unconscious, intoxicated beyond the capacity to decide, asleep, or below the legal age of consent. The World Health Organization defines sexual violence as "any sexual act, attempt to obtain a sexual act, unwanted sexual comments or advances, or acts to traffic, or otherwise directed, against a person's sexuality using coercion, by any person regardless of their relationship to the victim, in any setting".

Types

  • Rape — non-consensual sexual penetration, however slight.
  • Child sexual abuse — sexual contact with, or exploitation of, a minor.
  • Groping — fondling or touching another person in a sexual way without consent.
  • Sexual coercion — pressuring or manipulating someone into sexual activity, including through abuse of authority.
  • Drug-facilitated sexual assault — assault committed when the victim is incapacitated by alcohol or drugs, sometimes administered surreptitiously.
  • Marital or intimate partner sexual assault — assault by a spouse or partner; historically exempted in some legal systems, it is now criminalised in most jurisdictions.

Prevalence

Survey data suggest sexual assault is common worldwide. In the United States, the National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey has found that roughly one in five women and a smaller but substantial share of men report experiencing attempted or completed rape during their lifetimes, with much larger numbers reporting other forms of non-consensual sexual contact. Under-reporting is widespread: victims often cite fear of retaliation, shame, self-blame, distrust of police, or the belief that nothing will be done. Most assaults are committed by someone known to the victim, such as an acquaintance, partner, colleague or family member, rather than by a stranger.

Effects on survivors

Survivors may experience a range of physical and psychological effects, including injuries, sexually transmitted infections, unwanted pregnancy, post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance and substance misuse. Support services — including crisis hotlines, rape crisis centres, medical forensic examinations and trauma-informed counselling — can mitigate long-term harm. Advocacy movements such as the Me Too movement, which went viral in 2017, drew global attention to the prevalence of sexual assault and harassment.

Law

Most legal systems criminalise sexual assault, with penalties graded by severity, the victim's age and the degree of force or incapacity involved. Legal reforms over the past half-century have included the abolition of marital-rape exemptions, the introduction of rape shield laws limiting evidence about a complainant's sexual history, and the adoption of consent-based definitions of rape in a growing number of jurisdictions. Sexual assault in armed conflict may be prosecuted as a war crime or crime against humanity. Civil remedies and campus disciplinary proceedings (for example under Title IX in the United States) provide additional, distinct avenues of redress.

Prevention

Evidence-based prevention strategies include consent education in schools, bystander intervention training, programmes challenging norms that condone violence, alcohol policies in high-risk settings, and prompt, consistent institutional responses to reports. Public health approaches treat sexual violence as preventable, emphasising primary prevention aimed at potential perpetrators and community conditions rather than solely on victim behaviour.
