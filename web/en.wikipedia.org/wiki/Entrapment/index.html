Entrapment

Entrapment is a practice in which a law enforcement agent or agent of the state induces a person to commit a crime that the person would not have otherwise been likely or willing to commit. It is a defence to criminal liability in many jurisdictions, most prominently in the United States, where it rests on the idea that the government may not originate a criminal design and implant the disposition to commit it in an innocent person's mind.

United States

Federal courts apply a subjective test, established in Sorrells v. United States and Sherman v. United States: the defence succeeds if government agents induced the crime and the defendant was not predisposed to commit it. Many states instead, or additionally, apply an objective test focusing on whether police conduct would induce a normally law-abiding person. In Jacobson v. United States (1992), the Supreme Court reversed a conviction where agents spent 26 months persuading the defendant to order illegal material.

Other jurisdictions

England and Wales have no substantive entrapment defence, but judges may stay proceedings for abuse of process or exclude evidence obtained by state-created crime (R v Looseley, 2001). Canada recognises entrapment as a basis for a stay where police provide an opportunity without reasonable suspicion or induce the offence. The European Court of Human Rights has held that incitement by undercover agents can violate the right to a fair trial under Article 6.

Distinguishing opportunity

Merely providing an opportunity to commit a crime — a decoy, an undercover buyer, a bait car — is generally not entrapment. The line is between detecting existing criminal activity and manufacturing crime by persuasion, fraud, threats or persistent solicitation.
