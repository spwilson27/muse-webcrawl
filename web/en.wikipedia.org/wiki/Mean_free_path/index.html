Mean free path

Mean free path
A particle's random path between collisions; λ is the average step length
Common symbolλ
SI unitmetre (m)
FieldKinetic theory, statistical mechanics

In physics, the mean free path is the average distance travelled by a moving particle (such as an atom, molecule or photon) between successive impacts (collisions) that change its direction or energy. It is a central concept of the kinetic theory of gases, first quantified in the nineteenth century by Rudolf Clausius and James Clerk Maxwell, and it underlies the elementary theory of transport properties such as viscosity, thermal conductivity and diffusion.

Kinetic theory of gases

For gas molecules of diameter d moving among identical molecules at number density n, the mean free path is

λ = 1 / (√2 · n · σ),    with σ = πd²

where σ is the collision cross-section. Using the ideal gas law, n = p / (kBT), this becomes

λ = kBT / (√2 π d² p)

so λ increases with temperature and decreases with pressure. For air at standard temperature and pressure the mean free path is of the order of 68 nanometres — roughly a hundred molecular diameters. The concept explains why gases diffuse slowly despite molecular speeds of hundreds of metres per second: each molecule's progress is a random walk of tiny, frequently interrupted steps.

Other contexts

  • Radiative transfer: the mean free path of a photon in an absorbing medium is the inverse of the absorption coefficient; in stellar interiors it can be a fraction of a millimetre, so energy from the Sun's core takes tens of thousands of years to random-walk outward.
  • Electron transport: in metals and semiconductors, the mean free path of conduction electrons (limited by scattering from phonons and impurities) determines electrical resistivity; in modern nanoscale devices it may exceed the device dimensions, giving ballistic transport.
  • Vacuum technology: when the mean free path becomes comparable to the size of a vessel, gas flow enters the molecular (Knudsen) regime; the ratio of mean free path to a characteristic length is the Knudsen number.
  • Nuclear physics: the analogous quantity for neutrons is used in reactor design.
