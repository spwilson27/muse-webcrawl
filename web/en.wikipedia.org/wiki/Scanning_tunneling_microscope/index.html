Scanning tunneling microscope
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

A scanning tunneling microscope (STM) is an instrument for imaging surfaces at the atomic level. Invented in 1981 by Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM Zürich (Nobel Prize in Physics 1986), it scans a sharp conducting tip a few ångströms above a surface: a quantum-tunnelling current between tip and surface varies exponentially with distance, so feedback holding the current constant traces atomic corrugations.

The STM launched scanning probe microscopy (including the atomic force microscope, AFM, which works on insulators too) and nanotechnology: in 1989 Don Eigler's team at IBM arranged 35 xenon atoms to spell 'IBM' — the iconic demonstration that individual atoms could be picked up and placed. STMs work best in ultra-high vacuum at low temperatures, on conducting samples.
