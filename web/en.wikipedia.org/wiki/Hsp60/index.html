Hsp60

The chaperonin barrel formed by Hsp60 subunits.

Hsp60, also known as heat shock protein 60, is a member of the chaperonin family of molecular chaperones. Chaperonins are large, barrel-shaped double-ring complexes that help other proteins fold into their correct three-dimensional shapes in an ATP-dependent manner. The bacterial Hsp60 homologue is GroEL, which works with the co-chaperonin GroES; the human protein is encoded by the HSPD1 gene and operates mainly inside mitochondria, assisted by Hsp10.

Mechanism

An unfolded or partially folded substrate protein enters the central cavity of one ring of the chaperonin. Binding of ATP and the co-chaperonin cap (GroES or Hsp10) seals the cavity, creating an isolated chamber in which the substrate can fold without aggregating with other proteins. Hydrolysis of ATP and binding to the opposite ring then release the cap and the folded — or partly folded — substrate. The cycle repeats until folding is complete.

Distribution and clinical relevance

Hsp60 homologues occur in bacteria, in the mitochondria and chloroplasts of eukaryotes (a legacy of their endosymbiotic origins), and in the eukaryotic cytosol as the related CCT/TRiC complex. Although named for the heat shock response, Hsp60 is expressed constitutively because protein folding is a constant housekeeping need. Mutations in human HSPD1 cause hereditary spastic paraplegia and a hypomyelinating leukodystrophy. Extracellular Hsp60 can also act as a signalling molecule and has been implicated in autoimmune and inflammatory processes, in part because of its similarity to bacterial counterparts.
