Pitrs

Sanskritपितृ (pitṛ), plural पितरः (pitaraḥ)
Meaning"Fathers" — the spirits of departed ancestors
AbodePitṛloka, the realm of the ancestors
Associated deityYama, lord of the dead; Aryaman, chief of the Pitrs

The Pitrs (Sanskrit: पितरः, Pitaraḥ, "the fathers") are the spirits of the departed ancestors in Hinduism, Vedic religion, and related Indic traditions. They are regarded as benevolent beings who dwell in Pitṛloka, the realm of the ancestors, presided over in many accounts by Yama, the god of death, and led by the Āditya Aryaman. The living owe the Pitrs one of the great debts of human life (pitṛ-ṛṇa), discharged through offerings and through the continuation of the family line.

In the Vedas

The Rigveda contains hymns inviting the Pitrs to the sacrifice to accept offerings of soma, barley, and clarified butter, and to grant their descendants long life, wealth, and protection. The Pitrs were believed to have found the path to the world of the dead, defeating darkness, and to sit with Yama at the feast beneath the spreading tree of the afterlife. Later texts distinguish classes of Pitrs, including the Agniṣvāttas and Barhiṣads, and identify whole categories of celestial beings — gods, sages, and ancestral orders — as descended from or included among them.

Rites for the ancestors

The central rite for the Pitrs is śrāddha, performed at prescribed times — notably during Pitru Paksha, the dark fortnight of the month of Bhādrapada/Ashvin — in which the eldest son or another male descendant offers piṇḍas (balls of cooked rice and barley flour, often with sesame seeds) and water libations (tarpaṇa) to three generations of paternal and maternal ancestors. The offerings are believed to nourish the ancestors and help them attain and sustain their place in Pitṛloka. Failure to perform śrāddha is traditionally said to leave the ancestor as a restless preta (hungry ghost). Sacred places for these rites include Gaya in Bihar, where the Viṣṇupada temple marks the footprint of Vishnu upon the demon Gayāsura.

Relationship with the gods

In the ritual order, offerings go first to the gods (devas), then to the Pitrs, then to humans — a sequence embedded in the daily pañca-mahāyajña (five great sacrifices). The Pitrs are thus intermediate beings, more approachable than the high gods, linking the household to the cosmic order (ṛta).
