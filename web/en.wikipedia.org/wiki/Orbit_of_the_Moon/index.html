Orbit of the Moon

The Moon orbits Earth in the prograde direction, completing one revolution relative to the stars in approximately 27.32 days (a sidereal month) and one revolution relative to the Sun in about 29.53 days (a synodic month, the period of the lunar phases). The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, so the same side always faces the planet.

Characteristics

The orbit is elliptical with an eccentricity of about 0.0549; the Moon's distance varies between roughly 356,500 km at perigee and 406,700 km at apogee. The orbital plane is inclined about 5.145° to the ecliptic, so eclipses occur only when the Moon is near one of its nodes at new or full moon. The line of nodes regresses with a period of about 18.6 years, and the line of apsides precesses in about 8.85 years.

Tidal evolution

Tidal friction transfers angular momentum from Earth's rotation to the Moon's orbit: the Moon recedes from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year, measured precisely by lunar laser ranging to retroreflectors left by Apollo missions, while Earth's day gradually lengthens. In the remote past the Moon was much closer and the day much shorter, as shown by tidal rhythmites and fossil coral growth records.

Perturbations

The Sun's gravity strongly perturbs the lunar orbit, producing periodic variations such as evection and variation known since antiquity. Precise prediction of the Moon's position required the development of elaborate lunar theories, from Newton's gravitational analysis through the analytical theories of Hill and Brown to modern numerical ephemerides maintained by observatories and space agencies.
