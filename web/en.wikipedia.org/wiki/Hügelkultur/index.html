Hügelkultur (German pronunciation: [ˈhyːɡl̩kʊlˌtuːɐ̯], roughly "hill culture" or "mound culture") is a horticultural technique in which a mound is constructed from decaying wood debris and other compostable plant material, and then planted as a raised bed. As the buried wood decomposes, it acts as a sponge that retains water, slowly releases nutrients, and generates a small amount of heat, which advocates claim improves soil fertility and moisture retention over many years. The technique has old roots in German and Eastern European gardening and was popularised internationally by the Austrian farmer Sepp Holzer and the permaculture movement.

Construction

A typical hügelkultur bed is built as follows:

  1. A trench is dug, often 30–70 cm deep, and the turf and topsoil set aside.
  2. Logs, branches and woody debris are laid in the trench — larger logs at the bottom. Hardwoods are usually preferred; freshly cut wood and certain species such as black walnut (which releases the allelopathic compound juglone) or black locust and cedar (which rot very slowly) are often avoided.
  3. The wood is packed with leaves, grass clippings and other nitrogen-rich green material to help balance the high carbon content of the wood.
  4. The turf is replaced upside down over the mound, and the whole is covered with the excavated topsoil, compost and mulch, forming a mound that may be a metre or more high when fresh.
  5. The bed is planted immediately; it settles as the wood decays.

Beds are often built on contour on slopes, where they also serve to slow and capture rainwater runoff.

Claimed benefits

Supporters point to several effects. Decaying wood holds many times its weight in water, so mature beds may need little irrigation after the first year. Decomposition releases nutrients gradually and builds soil organic matter and fungal networks. The mound increases planting area, warms earlier in spring because of its slope and exposure, and can extend the growing season slightly. The method also provides a use for woody waste that might otherwise be burned, and sequesters carbon in the ground for the decades the wood takes to rot.

Criticism and limitations

Hügelkultur requires large amounts of wood and considerable labour, and the beds subside substantially as they decompose. In the first year or two, decomposing wood can temporarily immobilise soil nitrogen ("nitrogen drawdown"), starving plants unless extra nitrogen is added. Research comparing hügelkultur with conventional raised beds is limited, and results are mixed; some gardeners report problems with rodents nesting in the wood, dry conditions in the first season before the wood has saturated, and difficulties in arid climates. The technique is also unsuitable where termites are a major hazard near buildings.
