Surgical suture

A surgical suture is a medical device used to hold body tissues together after an injury or surgery. Application generally involves using a needle with an attached length of thread, drawn through the wound edges and secured with surgical knots. Suturing is one of the oldest surgical techniques, described in ancient Egyptian and Indian texts, and remains fundamental to surgery alongside staples, adhesive strips, and tissue glues.

Materials

Suture materials are classified as absorbable or non-absorbable, and as monofilament or multifilament (braided). Absorbable sutures — catgut (from sheep or cattle intestine), and synthetics such as polyglycolic acid, polydioxanone (PDS), and poliglecaprone (Monocryl) — are broken down by the body over days to months and need not be removed, making them suitable for internal tissues. Non-absorbable sutures include silk, nylon, polypropylene (Prolene), and stainless steel; used in skin they are removed after healing. Size is denoted by the USP system, from heavy 0 sizes to hair-fine 11-0 threads used in ophthalmic and microsurgery.

Needles and technique

Suture needles are usually curved and come in cutting (triangular cross-section, for tough tissue like skin), reverse-cutting, and tapered (round-bodied, for soft tissue) forms; most are "swaged" — permanently attached to the thread — for atraumatic passage. Common stitch patterns include simple interrupted sutures, continuous (running) sutures, mattress sutures that evert or relieve tension, and buried subcuticular sutures for cosmetically sensitive closures. Barbed sutures, which hold without knots, are a more recent innovation.

Considerations

Choice of suture balances tensile strength, tissue reactivity, infection risk, and handling. Braided materials handle and knot well but can harbour bacteria in their interstices; monofilaments pass through tissue with less drag and lower infection risk but have more "memory" and require more knots. Sutures are supplied sterile, and skin sutures are typically removed after 5–14 days depending on the site.
