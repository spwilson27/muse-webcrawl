Alois Hába (21 June 1893 – 18 November 1973) was a Czech composer, music theorist, and teacher, best known as a pioneering advocate of microtonal music, especially compositions in the quarter-tone and sixth-tone systems. Drawing partly on the microtonal inflections of Moravian folk song, he built a complete theoretical and instrumental apparatus for music dividing the octave far more finely than the conventional twelve semitones.

Life

Hába was born in Vizovice, Moravia, into a musical family of ten children. He studied in Vienna with Franz Schreker, where his fellow students included future microtonalist Ivan Wyschnegradsky, and later worked in Berlin, associating with Ferruccio Busoni, whose own speculative writings on third-tones encouraged him. From the 1920s he taught at the Prague Conservatory, where a special quarter-tone department was established in 1934. After the Second World War he continued composing and teaching in Prague, though official cultural policy in communist Czechoslovakia was often hostile to his work.

Microtonal system and instruments

Hába argued that the twelve-note chromatic scale was a historical convention, not a natural limit, and that finer divisions — quarter tones (24 per octave), sixth tones (36 per octave), and even twelfth tones — offered new melodic and harmonic resources. At his instigation, firms including Förster and August established workshops building quarter-tone pianos (including a three-manual instrument), a quarter-tone harmonium, specially keyed clarinets and trumpets, and a quarter-tone guitar.

Works

His output of around one hundred works includes sixteen string quartets, many in quarter tones, the quarter-tone opera Matka ("The Mother", premiered Munich 1931), set in Moravian village life, and the sixth-tone opera Nová země. His theoretical treatise Neue Harmonielehre (1927) sets out his harmonic system, which extended beyond microtones to embrace athematic construction and free combinations of all tones within his chosen division.

Legacy

Though his instruments and notation remained a specialism, Hába's advocacy helped legitimise microtonality within European modernism, and his Prague teaching influenced generations of Czech composers. Interest in his music revived with the broader late-20th-century exploration of alternative tunings.
