Transport phenomena

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In engineering, physics, and chemistry, the study of transport phenomena describes the exchange of mass, energy, charge, momentum, and angular momentum between observed and studied systems. It draws from continuum mechanics and thermodynamics and treats the transport of different quantities in a unified mathematical framework.

The three modes of transport

Transport phenomena encompass three closely related fields. Momentum transfer is the subject of fluid dynamics and is governed, for a Newtonian fluid, by Newton's law of viscosity. Heat transfer concerns the movement of thermal energy, described by Fourier's law of conduction and Newton's law of cooling. Mass transfer concerns the mixing and diffusion of chemical species, described by Fick's laws of diffusion. The striking structural similarity of these laws is commonly shown in the form:

Flux = − (diffusivity) × (gradient of the transported quantity)

History and teaching

The unified discipline was shaped above all by the textbook Transport Phenomena (1960) by R. Byron Bird, Warren E. Stewart, and Edwin N. Lightfoot, which became a fixture of chemical engineering education. Balance equations for mass, momentum, and energy — derived over microscopic, macroscopic, and molecular scales — provide the governing equations used to design heat exchangers, reactors, separation processes, and biological systems.
