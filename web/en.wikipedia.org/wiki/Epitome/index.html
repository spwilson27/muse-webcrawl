Epitome

An epitome (from the Greek ἐπιτομή, epitomí, meaning "a summary") is a summary or miniature form; it is a short, concise version of a larger work or a representative example of something. Epitomes were especially important in the ancient and Byzantine worlds, where the abridgements of epitomators often survive while the full originals they summarised have been lost.

Historical epitomes

Many classical works are known wholly or partly through epitomes. The Epitome of Pompeius Trogus's Historiae Philippicae by Justin is the principal surviving form of that history. Books of Livy's history of Rome that are otherwise lost are known through brief summaries, the Periochae. The mathematician and astronomer Proclus and the Byzantine scholar Photius produced important summaries of earlier literature in his Bibliotheca. Abridgements of this kind shaped what of classical literature was transmitted to later ages.

Modern usage

In current English, "epitome" usually denotes a person or thing that is a perfect example of a quality or type — "the epitome of elegance" — a sense developed from the idea of a form that embodies the essence of the whole. The pronunciation varies: the formal four-syllable rendering (eh-PIT-oh-mee) contrasts with a common mistaken reading as "EP-i-tome."
