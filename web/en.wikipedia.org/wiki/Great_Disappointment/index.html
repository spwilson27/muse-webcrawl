The Great Disappointment in the Millerite movement was the reaction that followed Baptist preacher William Miller's proclamation that Jesus Christ would return to the Earth by 1844, which he named the Second Advent. His study of the Daniel 8 prophecy during the Second Great Awakening led him to conclude that the cleansing of the sanctuary prophesied in Daniel 8:14 would be the Second Coming of Christ, and that it would occur around 1843–44.

Background

Miller began preaching publicly in 1831 and gained tens of thousands of followers, spread by the publications of Joshua Vaughan Himes. When 21 March 1844 passed without incident, Miller accepted a short delay; a recalculated date of 18 April 1844 also passed. In August 1844, at a camp meeting in Exeter, New Hampshire, Samuel S. Snow's "seventh-month message" fixed the date precisely at 22 October 1844, identified with the tenth day of the seventh month — the Day of Atonement — in the Karaite Jewish calendar. Many believers sold or gave away property and settled their affairs in expectation.

22 October 1844

The day passed without Christ's return. Miller wrote of his bitter disappointment, and the movement fragmented amid public ridicule — newspapers mocked believers who supposedly waited on rooftops in "ascension robes," stories largely exaggerated or invented in later retellings. Most Millerites drifted back to their former churches or abandoned the faith.

Aftermath

A minority concluded that the date had been correct but the event misunderstood: on 23 October 1844, Hiram Edson reported a vision that Christ had in 1844 entered the Most Holy Place of the heavenly sanctuary — the doctrine of the investigative judgment. This group, joined by Ellen G. White and Joseph Bates, developed into the Seventh-day Adventist Church (organised 1863). Other fragments became the Advent Christian Church and groups whose lines led to the Bible Student movement of Charles Taze Russell. The Great Disappointment is a classic case study in the psychology of failed prophecy, notably in the theory of cognitive dissonance popularised by Leon Festinger's When Prophecy Fails (1956), which studied a similar later group.
