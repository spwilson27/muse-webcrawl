Islamism, also often called political Islam, is a broad set of religious and political ideologies that hold that Islam should guide social and political life as well as personal life. Islamist movements generally favour the implementation of Islamic law (sharia) and the establishment of political orders grounded in Islamic principles, though they differ widely in strategy, from electoral participation to revolutionary violence.

Terminology

The term has been used since the late twentieth century by scholars and journalists, and is contested: some movements described as Islamist accept the label, while others prefer descriptions such as "Islamic movement". Scholars distinguish Islamism—the political project—from the broader religious tradition of Islam.

History

Modern Islamism emerged in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries in response to colonialism and the decline of the Ottoman Empire. The founding of the Muslim Brotherhood in Egypt in 1928 by Hasan al-Banna is conventionally treated as a landmark. Thinkers such as Abul A'la Maududi in South Asia and Sayyid Qutb in Egypt developed influential doctrines emphasising divine sovereignty (hakimiyya). The Iranian Revolution of 1979, led by Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, established a state explicitly governed by Islamic jurists and inspired movements across the Muslim world.

Varieties

Islamist organisations span a wide spectrum. Mainstream parties have contested elections in countries such as Tunisia, Morocco, Jordan and Turkey. Salafi movements emphasise a literal return to the practices of early Islam. Militant groups including al-Qaeda and the Islamic State have pursued transnational jihad, and are distinguished in most scholarship from electoral Islamist parties, which have often condemned them.

Criticism and debate

Critics, including many Muslim scholars and activists, argue that Islamist projects conflate a diverse faith with particular political programmes. Supporters respond that Islam has always contained a public and legal dimension. Academic debate continues over the coherence of the category and over whether participation in democratic politics moderates Islamist movements.
