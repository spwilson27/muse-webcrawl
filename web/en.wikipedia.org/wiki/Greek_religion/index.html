Greek religion

Greek religion refers to the body of myths, rituals, and religious practices of the ancient Greeks. In the broadest sense it encompasses the religious life of the Greek-speaking world from the Bronze Age through the Hellenistic period and into the Roman era, until its suppression under Christian emperors in late antiquity. It had no centralised priesthood, no sacred scripture in the manner of later revealed religions, and no concept of orthodoxy; its unity lay in shared myths, festivals, and sanctuaries.

Origins

The religion of archaic and classical Greece developed from the practices of the Mycenaean civilisation, itself influenced by Minoan Crete, and absorbed elements from the Near East and Egypt. The names of several classical gods, including Zeus, Poseidon, and Dionysus, already appear in the Linear B tablets of the Mycenaean palaces.

The gods

The central figures of the Greek pantheon were the twelve Olympians, said to dwell on Mount Olympus: Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Hermes, and either Hestia or Dionysus. Beneath them stood a host of lesser deities, nature spirits, heroes, and the deified dead. Greek religion was polytheistic and anthropomorphic: the gods were imagined in human form, with human passions, though immortal and vastly more powerful than mortals.

Practice

The central act of worship was sacrifice, usually of domestic animals, performed at an altar in the open air before a temple that housed the god's cult image. Prayer, libations, votive offerings, hymns, and processions accompanied the sacrifice. Cities organised their calendars around festivals such as the Panathenaea at Athens and the games at Olympia, Delphi, Nemea, and Isthmia, which combined athletic and musical competition with religious observance.

Oracles and mysteries

Greeks consulted oracles, most famously the Pythia of Apollo at Delphi, for divine guidance. Mystery cults, including the Eleusinian Mysteries and the cults of Dionysus and Orpheus, offered initiates promises concerning the afterlife that mainstream civic cult did not emphasise.

Later history

Under the Hellenistic kingdoms and the Roman Empire, Greek religion interacted with Egyptian, Syrian, and other traditions, producing syncretic cults such as that of Isis and Serapis. The conversion of the Roman Empire to Christianity in the fourth century CE led to the closure of temples and the abolition of sacrifice under Theodosius I, though isolated pockets of pagan practice survived for centuries.
