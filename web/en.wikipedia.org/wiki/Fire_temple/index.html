Fire temple

Fire temple Zoroastrian terms: Agiary (Gujarati), Atashkadeh (Persian), Dar-e Mehr
Grades of fire: Atash Dadgah, Atash Adaran, Atash Behram
Oldest continuously burning: Yazd Atash Behram (fire said to date to 470 CE)

A fire temple in Zoroastrianism is the place of worship for Zoroastrians, housing a sacred fire (atar) that is kept continually burning as a symbol of purity and of the divine light of Ahura Mazda. In India they are known as agiaries; in Iran as atashkadeh.

Grades of fire

Three grades of sacred fire are recognised. The Atash Dadgah is the lowest grade, maintainable in homes and small shrines. The Atash Adaran, the "fire of fires," combines fire from households of different occupational classes. The Atash Behram, the "victorious fire," is the highest grade, consecrated from sixteen fires—including fire from a cremation pyre and lightning—and requires lengthy ritual purification; only a handful exist, mostly in India and one in Yazd, Iran.

Ritual and architecture

The fire burns in a metal urn (afarganyu) on a stone platform in a chamber of the temple, tended by priests who wear a mouth covering (padan) so as not to pollute the flame with breath. Worshippers offer sandalwood and may apply ash to the forehead. Non-Zoroastrians are traditionally not admitted to Parsi fire temples in India.

History

Fire altars appear in Achaemenid-era Iran, and temple cults developed under later dynasties. After the Islamic conquest of Persia, many Zoroastrians migrated to Gujarat in India (the Parsis), where temples such as the Iranshah Atash Behram in Udvada, established in the eighteenth century, remain centres of pilgrimage.
