External combustion engine

Image placeholder: Model Stirling engine, an external combustion design

An external combustion engine (EC engine) is a reciprocating heat engine where a working fluid, contained internally, is heated by combustion in an external source, through the engine wall or a heat exchanger. The fluid then, by expanding and acting on the mechanism of the engine, produces motion and usable work. The fluid is then dumped (open cycle), or cooled, compressed and reused (closed cycle).

Comparison with internal combustion

In an internal combustion engine, fuel and oxidiser burn directly inside the working cylinder, and the combustion products themselves act on the piston. In external combustion, the combustion products never mix with the working fluid, which is heated through a solid wall. This permits the use of almost any fuel or heat source — coal, biomass, solar heat, geothermal heat, or nuclear heat — and allows combustion to be continuous and optimised for low emissions, at the cost of larger size and slower response to load changes.

Examples

The steam engine and steam turbine, historically the dominant prime movers of the Industrial Revolution, are external combustion engines in most of their applications. The Stirling engine is a closed-cycle external combustion (or external heat) engine that can operate on any temperature difference. Steam locomotives, steamships, and most thermal power stations generate power by this principle.
