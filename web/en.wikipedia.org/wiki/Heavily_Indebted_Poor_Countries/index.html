Heavily Indebted Poor Countries

Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) is a group of developing countries with high levels of poverty and debt overhang, eligible for special assistance from the IMF and World Bank.

Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) is the group of countries eligible for the debt relief initiative launched by the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank in 1996, one of the first comprehensive efforts to reduce the external debts of the world's poorest countries to sustainable levels. An enhanced version of the initiative followed in 1999, linking relief more closely to poverty-reduction strategies.

Mechanism

Countries qualify by facing an unsustainable debt burden, being eligible for concessional IDA and IMF lending, and establishing a track record of reform. At the "decision point" interim relief begins; at the "completion point", reached after implementing agreed reforms and a Poverty Reduction Strategy Paper, creditors deliver irrevocable debt reduction. The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative of 2005 went further, cancelling 100% of eligible debts owed to the IMF, IDA and African Development Fund by completion-point countries.

Results

Thirty-nine countries were identified as potentially eligible, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa; by the 2020s, 37 had reached completion point, receiving relief packages nominally exceeding $100 billion including MDRI cancellations. Critics have argued that eligibility thresholds were too strict, relief too slow, and that freed resources still left countries vulnerable to new debt distress — as later debt crises in Zambia, Ghana and Ethiopia illustrated — while supporters note substantial increases in poverty-reducing spending in beneficiary countries.
