Seduction of the Innocent is a book by German-American psychiatrist Fredric Wertham, published in 1954, that warned that comic books were a negative form of popular literature and a serious cause of juvenile delinquency. The book was a minor bestseller and a landmark of the postwar moral panic over comics, contributing directly to congressional hearings and to the comics industry's adoption of self-censorship through the Comics Code Authority.

Arguments

Wertham, a senior psychiatrist who ran a clinic serving juvenile offenders in Harlem, drew on his clinical case material to argue that crime and horror comics normalised violence, sadism and criminality, and that even superhero comics were harmful: he claimed Superman embodied fascist and sadistic fantasies, that Batman and Robin's domestic life together represented a "wish dream of two homosexuals living together", and that Wonder Woman's independence and bondage imagery made her a disturbing ideal for girls. He also attacked the comics' advertising, their poor print quality (which he said strained children's eyes), and their role in impairing reading skills.

Impact

Excerpts and discussion of the book fuelled parent-teacher campaigns, comic-book burnings, and local ordinances against crime comics in the United States, Canada and Britain. Wertham testified before the U.S. Senate Subcommittee on Juvenile Delinquency in April 1954, where publisher William Gaines of EC Comics famously defended a horror cover as being in "good taste" for its genre. Facing boycotts and probable legislation, the industry formed the Comics Magazine Association of America and its Comics Code Authority, whose restrictive seal drove EC and many crime and horror titles out of business and dominated American mainstream comics content for decades.

Reassessment

Later scholars have criticised Wertham's methods: research by Carol Tilley, published in 2012 from Wertham's own papers, documented that he manipulated, overstated, and in some cases fabricated evidence, combined case histories, and misrepresented the ages and statements of patients. Historians note, however, that Wertham was not a simple censor — he opposed outright legal bans, was sceptical of the comics industry's commercial exploitation of children, and his clinic did pioneering work for poor and minority youth. The book remains the most famous attack on a mass medium in twentieth-century America and a standard case study in moral-panic literature.
