PRISM

PRISM is a code name for a program under which the United States National Security Agency (NSA) collects internet communications from various US internet companies. The program is also known by the SIGAD US-984XN. PRISM collects stored internet communications based on demands made to internet companies such as Google and Facebook under Section 702 of the FISA Amendments Act of 2008 to turn over any data that match court-approved search terms.

Disclosure

The existence of PRISM was leaked by NSA contractor Edward Snowden and reported by The Washington Post and The Guardian in June 2013, based on a classified PowerPoint presentation. The slides indicated that the program began in 2007 and that participating companies included Microsoft, Yahoo, Google, Facebook, PalTalk, YouTube, Skype, AOL, and Apple. The companies stated that they provided data only in response to lawful government requests and denied giving the NSA direct, unfettered access to their servers.

Scope and operation

According to official statements, PRISM targets the communications of non-US persons reasonably believed to be located outside the United States, under the oversight of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court. Data obtained included email content, search histories, file transfers, video and voice chat, and stored documents. Critics argued that the collection inevitably swept in large volumes of Americans' communications incidentally.

Reactions and aftermath

The disclosures provoked a worldwide debate about mass surveillance, privacy, and the balance between security and civil liberties, and strained relations with governments whose citizens were affected. In the United States, the USA FREEDOM Act of 2015 ended the separate bulk telephony metadata program and added transparency measures. Section 702 was subsequently reauthorized, most recently in 2024, with continuing controversy over its scope.
