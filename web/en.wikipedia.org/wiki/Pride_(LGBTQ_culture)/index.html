In LGBTQ culture, Pride is the promotion of self-affirmation, dignity, equality and increased visibility of lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and queer people as a social group. Pride, as opposed to shame and social stigma, is the predominant outlook that bolsters most LGBTQ rights movements. It is expressed publicly through Pride parades and festivals, the rainbow flag and other symbols, and is most commonly celebrated during Pride Month in June.

Origins

The modern Pride movement traces its origins to the Stonewall riots of June 1969 in New York City, when patrons of the Stonewall Inn resisted a police raid. The first anniversary marches in 1970 — Christopher Street Liberation Day in New York, and parallel events in Los Angeles, Chicago and San Francisco — established the tradition of annual June commemorations. Early organisers, including Brenda Howard, known as the "Mother of Pride", helped coordinate the first march and the idea of a Pride week.

Symbols

The rainbow flag, designed by Gilbert Baker in 1978, is the best-known symbol of LGBTQ pride; the original eight-stripe version was simplified to six stripes. Later variants, including the Progress Pride flag, add stripes representing transgender people and LGBTQ people of colour. The word "pride" itself was popularised by early activists — one account credits the acronym "Personal Rights in Defense and Education" (PRIDE), a 1966 Los Angeles organisation.

Events and criticism

Pride events now take place in hundreds of cities worldwide, ranging from small community gatherings to São Paulo's parade, among the world's largest public gatherings. Debates continue within the movement about commercialisation and corporate sponsorship, the role of police in parades, and whether Pride should retain an explicitly political, protest character alongside celebration. In many countries, Pride marches remain banned or face official hostility, underscoring the movement's continuing activist dimension.
