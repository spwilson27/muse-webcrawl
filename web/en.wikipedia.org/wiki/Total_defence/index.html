Total defence

Total defence is a national defence strategy in which all sectors of society — military, civil government, economy, infrastructure and citizens — are organised to resist aggression and sustain the country in war or crisis. The concept developed in the neutral states of Cold-War Europe and remains central to the security doctrines of Sweden, Finland, Norway, Switzerland and Singapore.

Components

Typical pillars include military defence (often conscription-based), civil defence and emergency preparedness (shelters, stockpiles, continuity of government), economic and infrastructure resilience, psychological defence against disinformation, and, in Singapore's formulation (adopted 1984), social and digital defence as well. Households may be expected to survive for days or a week without state support, as in Sweden's booklet If Crisis or War Comes.

Rationale

Small states unable to match a great-power aggressor seek deterrence by denial: raising the cost of occupation through whole-of-society resistance, militia and guerrilla capacity, and rapid mobilisation. Finland's system, rooted in the Winter War experience, maintains a large wartime reserve; Sweden reactivated its total defence planning in 2015 after a post-Cold War drawdown.
