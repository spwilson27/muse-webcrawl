Multiculturalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Multiculturalism is the view, and the set of policies, that the different cultures in a society should be maintained, protected and given public recognition — as opposed to assimilation into a single national culture or to laissez-faire coexistence without policy. As official policy it was pioneered by Canada (1971, enacted in the Multiculturalism Act 1988) and Australia in the 1970s, and influenced Britain, the Netherlands, Sweden and others, with theorists (Kymlicka's liberal multiculturalism, Taylor's politics of recognition, Parekh) distinguishing group-differentiated rights for national minorities, indigenous peoples and immigrant communities.

Backlash and reassessment

From the 2000s, European leaders across the spectrum pronounced multiculturalism failed (Merkel 2010, Cameron and Sarkozy 2011), citing segregated neighbourhoods, illiberal practices sheltered by deference (forced marriage, parallel legal norms), and terrorist radicalisation; majorities in policy shifted toward "interculturalism", civic-integration requirements and national-values tests. Defenders answer that the obituaries misdescribe the policies (recognition never licensed rights violations), that Canada's version correlates with comparatively successful integration, and that the underlying pluralism — demography — does not retire on announcement. The concept's fortunes track the politics of immigration itself, which is why its scholarly refinements keep being overtaken by events.
