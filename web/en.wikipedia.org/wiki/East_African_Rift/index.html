East African Rift

The East African Rift (EAR) or East African Rift System (EARS) is an active continental rift zone in East Africa. The EAR began developing around the onset of the Miocene, some 22–25 million years ago. In the past it was considered to be part of a larger Great Rift Valley that extended north to Asia Minor.

A narrow zone, the rift is a developing divergent tectonic plate boundary where the African Plate is in the process of splitting into two tectonic plates, called the Somali Plate and the Nubian Plate, at a rate of 6–7 mm (0.24–0.28 in) per year. The rift system consists of three microplates: the Victoria microplate to the north, and the Rovuma and Lwandle microplates to the south. The two plates meet at the junction in the Afar region of Ethiopia, often called the Afar Triangle, one of the most geologically active areas on Earth.

Extent

The East African Rift System extends from the Afar Triple Junction in the Afar Depression southward through eastern Africa. It is believed to have formed as a result of the upwelling of one or more mantle plumes beneath the region, which produced the Ethiopian and Kenyan domes and weakened the continental lithosphere.

Branches

The rift splits into two main branches. The Eastern Rift Valley (also called the Gregory Rift) includes the Main Ethiopian Rift and runs from the Red Sea southward through Ethiopia and Kenya to northern Tanzania. The Western Rift Valley (also called the Albertine Rift) runs from Lake Albert in Uganda southward through the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Burundi, Rwanda and Tanzania, and contains a chain of deep lakes including Lake Tanganyika, the world's second deepest freshwater lake.

Volcanism and seismicity

The rift is associated with extensive volcanism, including the great shield volcanoes Mount Kilimanjaro and Mount Kenya, and the continuously active lava lakes of Erta Ale and Nyiragongo. Earthquakes are frequent, and in 2005 a major dyke intrusion event near Dabbahu in Ethiopia opened a fissure tens of kilometres long within days.

Human origins

The rift valley is of immense importance to palaeoanthropology. Its sediments preserve fossils of early hominins, including "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis), discovered at Hadar, Ethiopia, in 1974, and the Laetoli footprints in Tanzania. For this reason the region around the Olduvai Gorge is often called the "Cradle of Humankind".
