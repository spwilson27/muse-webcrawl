Fall line

A fall line (or fall zone) is the area where an upland region and a coastal plain meet, characterised by waterfalls and rapids where rivers cross it. Technically it is an unconformity: a geological discontinuity between hard, erosion-resistant metamorphic rock of the upland (such as the Piedmont) and the softer sediments of the coastal plain.

The Atlantic fall line

The most famous example runs along the east coast of the United States, where rivers drop from the Appalachian Piedmont to the Atlantic coastal plain. The falls marked the head of navigation — the furthest point that ships could travel upstream — and provided water power for mills. As a result, many major cities were founded at fall-line points, including Philadelphia on the Delaware, Baltimore on the Patapsco, Washington, D.C. on the Potomac (Georgetown/Great Falls), Richmond on the James, Columbia on the Congaree, Augusta on the Savannah, and Macon on the Ocmulgee. Fall-line cities grew as transshipment points where goods moved between river boats and land transport, and as manufacturing centres powered by the falling water.

Other fall lines

Fall lines occur worldwide wherever rivers cross from resistant upland rock to soft lowland sediment; the concept also describes the line of rapids marking boundaries of navigability on African and South American rivers.
