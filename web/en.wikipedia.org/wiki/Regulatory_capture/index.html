Regulatory capture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Regulatory capture is a theory of government failure in which a regulatory agency, created to act in the public interest, instead advances the commercial or political interests of the industry it is charged with regulating. George Stigler's “economic theory of regulation” (1971) argued that regulation is “acquired” by industries, which have concentrated stakes in its content, while consumers' interests are diffuse; Marver Bernstein (1955) had earlier described an agency life-cycle ending in identification with the regulated.

Mechanisms include the revolving door of personnel, industry dominance of the technical information agencies need, campaign and social networks, and “cognitive capture” — regulators absorbing industry's worldview. Classic alleged cases include US railroads and the ICC, aviation before deregulation, finance before the 2008 crisis, and pharmaceutical review; the diagnosis drives institutional remedies (adversarial procedures, sunset review, cooling-off periods, independence with accountability). Capture theory is contested — agencies also act against industry interests, and the theory's predictive edges blur — but it permanently changed how economists and political scientists analyse regulation.
