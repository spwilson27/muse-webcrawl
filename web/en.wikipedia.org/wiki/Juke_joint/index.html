Juke joint

A juke joint (also jook joint, jook, or juke) is an informal establishment featuring music, dancing, gambling, and drinking, primarily operated by African Americans in the southeastern United States. The term began as a Black slang usage and eventually entered the wider American vocabulary.

Etymology

The word "juke" is believed to derive from the Gullah word joog or jug, meaning disorderly or wicked, itself possibly from the Wolof word dzug, "to misconduct oneself". Writer Zora Neale Hurston documented the "jook" as a centre of Black folk culture in Florida.

History

Juke joints emerged after emancipation, when freedpeople working on plantations and in turpentine and lumber camps sought leisure spaces outside the control of employers and the segregation laws of the Jim Crow South. Typically set at rural crossroads in a converted house or a purpose-built shack, a juke joint offered fried fish and barbecue, moonshine and beer, dice and card games, and dancing to blues, ragtime, boogie-woogie and later rhythm and blues.

Music was provided by itinerant bluesmen playing guitar or piano, and later by jukeboxes — from which the establishments increasingly took their name. Juke joints were foundational venues for the Delta blues: musicians such as Robert Johnson, Charley Patton, Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf performed in them. Poet Langston Hughes and folklorists John and Alan Lomax documented the culture surrounding them.

Decline and legacy

The Great Migration, the rise of urban clubs, and changing economies eroded the rural juke joint from the mid-twentieth century, though a small number of celebrated examples — such as Po' Monkey's Lounge in Mississippi — survived into the twenty-first century. The juke joint remains a potent symbol of African American cultural resilience and a cradle of American popular music.
