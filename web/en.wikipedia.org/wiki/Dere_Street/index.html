Dere Street

Dere Street or Deere Street is a modern name for a Roman road that ran north from Eboracum (York) to the Antonine Wall in what is now Scotland. It formed the principal route between northern England and Scotland for nearly two thousand years, and substantial parts of its alignment are still followed by modern roads, including sections of the A1, the A68 and the A697.

Route

From York, Dere Street ran north-west to the fort and town at Catterick (Cataractonium), then crossed the River Tees near Piercebridge. It continued through Bishop Auckland to the forts at Binchester (Vinovia), Lanchester (Longovicium) and Ebchester (Vindomora), before reaching Corbridge (Coria) on the River Tyne, an important supply base and junction with the Stanegate road serving Hadrian's Wall.

North of Corbridge the road crossed Hadrian's Wall near Portgate and climbed through Redesdale, where a series of forts and marching camps guarded the route. It passed the fort at Risingham (Habitancum) and High Rochester (Bremenium), crossed the Cheviot Hills, and descended to the Tweed valley near Newstead (Trimontium), a major fort beneath the Eildon Hills. From there it ran north to Inveresk and Cramond on the Firth of Forth, and onward to the Antonine Wall.

History

The road was first constructed during the campaigns of Gnaeus Julius Agricola in the late 1st century AD, when Roman forces advanced into Scotland. It was maintained and rebuilt throughout the Roman occupation and remained a major artery after the Roman period: armies, cattle drovers and travellers used it through the medieval and early modern eras. The name "Dere Street" is first recorded in the medieval period and is thought to derive from the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Deira, through which its southern section passed.

Surviving remains

Long stretches of the road survive as earthworks, hollow ways and straight alignments of modern roads and field boundaries. Some sections, such as those crossing the Cheviots, are protected as scheduled monuments, and a number of Roman forts, camps and signal stations along its line can still be visited.
