The Tortricidae are a family of moths, commonly known as tortrix moths or leafroller moths, in the order Lepidoptera. This large family has over 11,000 species described, placed in over 1,000 genera, and is the sole member of the superfamily Tortricoidea. Many of the economically most important pests in agriculture, forestry and horticulture belong to this family.

Description

Tortricid moths are generally small, with wingspans mostly under 2.5 cm. At rest, the wings are held folded flat or in a characteristic bell-like profile over the body, the forewings often appearing squared-off or bell-shaped. Colouration is typically mottled browns, greys and ochres providing camouflage on bark. Larvae of many species roll or fold leaves with silk to make shelters — hence "leafrollers" — while others bore into buds, shoots, fruit, seeds or stems.

Subfamilies

  • Chlidanotinae – a small, mostly tropical group.
  • Tortricinae – includes many leafrollers of trees and shrubs.
  • Olethreutinae – includes many fruit-boring species such as the codling moth.

Economic importance

Notable pest species include the codling moth (Cydia pomonella), the classic "worm in the apple"; the oriental fruit moth (Grapholita molesta); the spruce budworms (Choristoneura spp.), major defoliators of North American conifer forests; the grape berry moth; the light brown apple moth (Epiphyas postvittana); and the false codling moth. Tortricid pests are managed with monitoring pheromone traps, mating disruption, biological control and selective insecticides.
