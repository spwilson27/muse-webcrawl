Herm (French: Her) is one of the Channel Islands and part of the Bailiwick of Guernsey. About 2.4 km long and less than 800 m wide, it is the smallest of the Channel Islands open to the public. Cars and bicycles are banned, and the island — leased from the States of Guernsey — is managed as a single estate centred on tourism, with a resident population of around 60 people.

Geography

Herm has two contrasting halves. The south is a granite plateau of common land covered in gorse and bracken, centred on the island's highest point (106 ft); the low northern half is a sandy isthmus ending in Shell Beach, named for the tiny shells washed up by Atlantic currents, and Belvoir Bay on its sheltered east side. At low tide the northern common connects toward the islet of Jethou's tidal neighbours and extensive rock pools are exposed.

History

Neolithic tombs dot the northern common, and the island later had a monastic presence; its chapel of St Tugual dates in part to the 11th century. In the 19th century Herm's granite quarries fed construction in London, and the island passed through various tenants, including Prince Blücher von Wahlstatt before the First World War. During the Second World War Herm, like the other Channel Islands, was occupied by German forces. From 1949 the tenant Major Peter Wood developed Herm as a holiday island; the lease later passed to a charitable trust, and since 2008 the island has been leased to Herm Island Limited, run by the Starboard Hotels group on behalf of the island trust.

Life on Herm

The island has one hotel (the White House Hotel, famously with no televisions, clocks or telephones in its rooms), a pub — the Mermaid Tavern — a handful of holiday cottages and farmhouses, a campsite, a small primary school, and a shop. There is no resident doctor and no church incumbent; the island's economy is almost entirely seasonal tourism, supplied by ferry from St Peter Port in Guernsey, a 20-minute crossing.
