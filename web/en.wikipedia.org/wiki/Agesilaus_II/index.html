Agesilaus II (Greek: Ἀγησίλαος, Agēsilaos; c. 444 – 360 BC) was king of the ancient Greek city-state of Sparta from the Eurypontid dynasty, reigning from about 400 BC until his death. He was one of the most prominent figures of the Greek world in the early fourth century BC, leading Spartan armies in Asia Minor, mainland Greece, and finally Egypt, and he dominated Spartan politics for nearly forty years.

Agesilaus was the son of King Archidamus II and the half-brother of King Agis II. Although lame in one leg, he received the traditional Spartan upbringing, the agoge, because he was not originally expected to succeed. On the death of Agis, Agesilaus, supported by the powerful admiral Lysander, successfully claimed the throne against his nephew Leotychidas.

Campaign in Asia

In 396 BC Agesilaus crossed into Asia Minor to defend the Greek cities against the Persian Empire, presenting himself, in conscious imitation of Agamemnon, as the champion of Greece against Persia. He won notable victories, defeating the satrap Tissaphernes near Sardis in 395 BC, and advanced into Phrygia and Paphlagonia. He was preparing a deeper invasion when he was recalled to Greece by the outbreak of the Corinthian War.

Corinthian War and after

Returning by land through Thrace and Thessaly, Agesilaus defeated a Thessalian force and fought the inconclusive battle of Coronea (394 BC) against a coalition of Thebes, Athens, Corinth, and Argos backed by Persian money. In the following years he campaigned repeatedly against Acarnania and in defence of Sparta's allies. The war ended with the King's Peace of 387/386 BC, dictated by Persia, which Agesilaus enforced to Sparta's advantage, dissolving the Boeotian League and punishing recalcitrant allies.

Later years

After Sparta's catastrophic defeat by the Thebans under Epaminondas at Leuctra in 371 BC — a battle Agesilaus did not attend — Spartan power declined sharply. Agesilaus nevertheless defended Sparta itself against Epaminondas's invasions of Laconia and fought a successful defensive action near Mantinea in 362 BC. In his eighties he went to Egypt as a mercenary commander in support of the pharaoh Tachos and then of Nectanebo II. He died on the return voyage in Cyrenaica in 360 BC, and his body, preserved in honey or wax, was carried back to Sparta.

Sources

The principal ancient sources are Xenophon, who served under Agesilaus and wrote an encomium of him, and Plutarch, whose Life of Agesilaus draws on a wider range of material.
