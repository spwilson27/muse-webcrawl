The Abel Prize (Norwegian: Abelprisen) is a Norwegian international award presented annually by the King of Norway to one or more outstanding mathematicians. Named after the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel, it was established by the Norwegian government in 2002, on the bicentenary of Abel's birth, and is modelled on the Nobel Prizes — mathematics famously lacking a Nobel of its own. It is widely regarded, alongside the Fields Medal, as the most prestigious honour in mathematics, differing from the Fields Medal in having no age limit and being awarded annually for lifetime achievement.

History

An Abel prize was first proposed in 1899 by Sophus Lie when it became clear Alfred Nobel's prizes would exclude mathematics, but the plan lapsed. The modern prize was proposed in 2001 by Prime Minister Jens Stoltenberg's government, funded through a memorial fund initially of 200 million kroner, and first awarded in 2003 to Jean-Pierre Serre. The recipient is selected by a committee of five appointed by the Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters, and the award ceremony takes place in the Aula of the University of Oslo, the former venue of the Nobel Peace Prize.

Laureates

Laureates include Michael Atiyah and Isadore Singer (2004, for the index theorem), Peter Lax (2005), Lennart Carleson (2006), John G. Thompson and Jacques Tits (2008), Mikhail Gromov (2009), John Tate (2010), John Milnor (2011), Endre Szemerédi (2012), Pierre Deligne (2013), Yakov Sinai (2014), John Nash and Louis Nirenberg (2015), Andrew Wiles (2016, for Fermat's Last Theorem), Yves Meyer (2017), Robert Langlands (2018), Karen Uhlenbeck (2019, the first woman laureate), Hillel Furstenberg and Gregory Margulis (2020), László Lovász and Avi Wigderson (2021), Dennis Sullivan (2022), Luis Caffarelli (2023), Michel Talagrand (2024) and Masaki Kashiwara (2025).
