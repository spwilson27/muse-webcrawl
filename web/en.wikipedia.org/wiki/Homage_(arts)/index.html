Homage (arts)

In the arts, an homage (from French hommage) is a deliberate, respectful allusion by one artist to another artist, work or style. Unlike plagiarism, an homage is meant to be recognised by the audience: the borrower's work acknowledges a debt and invites comparison with its source. Homages appear in painting, literature, music, theatre and especially cinema, where directors frequently quote shots, scenes or scores from earlier films.

Characteristics

An homage typically reproduces a distinctive element — a composition, a melodic figure, a famous shot — clearly enough to be identified, while placing it in a new context. It signals admiration and situates the new work within a tradition. Related but distinct practices include pastiche (imitation of a style), parody (imitation for comic or critical effect), the cover version in popular music, and the tribute album or concert.

Examples

  • Pablo Picasso's series of variations on Delacroix's Women of Algiers and Manet's Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe.
  • James Joyce's Ulysses, structured as an homage to Homer's Odyssey.
  • Brian De Palma's and other directors' reworkings of Hitchcock's Vertigo and Psycho; numerous films quote the Odessa Steps sequence from Eisenstein's Battleship Potemkin.
  • Composers' homage pieces, such as Ravel's Le tombeau de Couperin.

Homage versus copying

Because an homage depends on the audience recognising the source, it is generally treated as legitimate artistic practice rather than infringement, though extensive borrowing can still raise copyright questions. The term derives from the feudal ceremony of homage, in which a vassal publicly acknowledged a lord — the artistic homage similarly acknowledges a predecessor's mastery.
