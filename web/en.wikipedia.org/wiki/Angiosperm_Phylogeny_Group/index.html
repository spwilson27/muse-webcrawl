The Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) is an informal international collaboration of botanists that produces a modern, phylogenetically based classification of the flowering plants (angiosperms). Its classifications, published in 1998, 2003, 2009 and 2016, are based primarily on molecular evidence — DNA sequence data — rather than on morphology alone, and have become the standard reference used by herbaria, databases and floras worldwide.

The APG system

The APG approach recognises only monophyletic groups (clades) and avoids formal ranks above the level of order where they would misrepresent relationships. The first APG paper (1998) recognised 462 families grouped into 40 orders; APG II (2003) allowed optional bracketed families; APG III (2009) settled on 415 families in 59 orders; and APG IV (2016) recognised 416 families in 64 orders, refining groups such as the lilioid monocots and the Boraginales.

Impact

The system superseded morphology-based schemes such as the Cronquist and Takhtajan systems. It clarified that the traditional division into "dicotyledons" is paraphyletic, placing the magnoliids, Chloranthales and Ceratophyllales among the early-diverging lineages between the basal angiosperms (such as Amborella and the water lilies) and the great clades of monocots and eudicots. Major gardens including Kew reorganised their collections according to APG, and the Angiosperm Phylogeny Website maintains the classification's continuous updates between editions.
