Self-perception theory (SPT) is an account of attitude formation developed by psychologist Daryl Bem. It asserts that people develop their attitudes (when there are no strong pre-existing attitudes, or when they are ambiguous) by observing their own behaviour and concluding what attitudes must have caused it.

Theory

Bem proposed that individuals come to "know" their own attitudes, emotions, and internal states partially by inferring them from observations of their own overt behaviour and the circumstances in which it occurred — just as an outside observer would. Where internal cues are weak, ambiguous or uninterpretable, people are in the same position as an outside observer, who must therefore rely on external cues to infer their inner states.

Rivalry with cognitive dissonance

Bem offered self-perception as a parsimonious alternative to Leon Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory for experiments such as the famous $1/$20 induced-compliance study: participants paid little for praising a boring task appeared to like it more — dissonance theory says they reduced inner tension, while self-perception theory says they simply observed their own behaviour ("I said it was interesting for almost nothing, so I must have liked it"). Subsequent research suggested each theory applies in different conditions: dissonance where strong pre-existing attitudes are contradicted, self-perception where attitudes are weak or novel.

Applications

The theory explains the overjustification effect (extrinsic rewards undermining intrinsic interest), the foot-in-the-door technique, and has been applied in marketing, health behaviour, and studies of facial feedback and emotion.
