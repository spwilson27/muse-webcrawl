Deductive reasoning

Deductive reasoning
In deduction, the conclusion is contained in the premises.
FieldLogic, philosophy, psychology of reasoning

Deductive reasoning is the process of drawing valid conclusions from premises: if the premises are true and the rules of inference are correctly applied, the conclusion must be true. It is the form of reasoning studied by formal logic. Deductive arguments are characterised by validity — a structural property of the argument, not of its content — and by soundness, which holds when a valid argument also has true premises.

Deduction is contrasted with inductive reasoning, in which premises support but do not guarantee the conclusion (for example generalising from observed cases), and abductive reasoning, which infers the best explanation. Only deduction is truth-preserving with certainty: its conclusion makes explicit information already implicit in the premises, so a valid deductive argument cannot lead from truth to falsehood.

Validity and soundness

An argument is valid if it is impossible for its premises to be true while its conclusion is false. Validity depends only on logical form: "All cats are reptiles; Felix is a cat; therefore Felix is a reptile" is valid (though unsound, because a premise is false), whereas a formally broken argument with all true statements is still invalid. The classic example of a syllogism is: "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal."

Classical logic

Aristotle's syllogistic, set out in the Prior Analytics, was the first formal system of deduction and dominated logic for two millennia. Modern logic, founded by Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879), extended deduction to quantifiers and relations, giving propositional and predicate calculi with formal proof systems — natural deduction, sequent calculi, axiomatic (Hilbert) systems — whose inference rules are purely syntactic.

Rules of inference

Common deductive rules include modus ponens (from A and A → B, infer B), modus tollens (from ¬B and A → B, infer ¬A), universal instantiation, and proof by contradiction. Two notorious near-misses are the fallacies of affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent, which mimic valid forms but can lead from true premises to false conclusions.

History

Greek mathematics — above all Euclid's Elements — made deduction the paradigm of demonstrative knowledge, deriving theorems from axioms. Medieval scholastics refined syllogistic; Descartes and Leibniz dreamed of a universal calculus of reasoning; Boole and Frege realised it mathematically in the 19th century. In the 20th century, Hilbert's programme, Gödel's completeness and incompleteness theorems, and Gentzen's proof theory mapped both the power and the limits of formal deduction, while automated theorem provers and proof assistants turned deduction into a computational tool.

Psychology

The psychology of reasoning studies how people actually deduce. Experiments such as the Wason selection task show that untrained reasoners often err on abstract logical problems yet perform far better when the same structure is embedded in social or deontic content. Theories such as mental logic and mental models attempt to explain these patterns; dual-process accounts contrast fast intuitive responses with slower analytic deduction.
