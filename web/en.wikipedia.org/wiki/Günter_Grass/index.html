Günter Grass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Günter Grass in the 1980s, moustachioed and pipe-smoking, at his desk in Lübeck]

Günter Wilhelm Grass (16 October 1927 – 13 April 2015) was a German novelist, poet, playwright, illustrator and sculptor, recipient of the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1999, whose first novel The Tin Drum (1959) made him the literary conscience — and provocateur — of post-war Germany. The Danzig Trilogy (The Tin Drum, Cat and Mouse, 1961; Dog Years, 1963) confronted the Nazi past through grotesque comedy at the moment the economic miracle preferred amnesia.

Born in the Free City of Danzig to a German-Polish (Kashubian) family, Grass served as a teenage soldier, was wounded and captured, trained as a stonemason and sculptor, and wrote with the Group 47 circle. Oskar Matzerath — the boy who decides at three not to grow, drumming and shattering glass with his scream beneath the rostrums of the Third Reich — became world literature's image of refusal.

Public life and the 2006 revelation

Grass campaigned for Willy Brandt and the SPD, lectured Germans on their history for fifty years, opposed reunification's haste (fearing a Fourth Reich of power), and fought public duels with the Springer press. In 2006, preparing his memoir Peeling the Onion, he disclosed that at 17 he had served not in the Wehrmacht but in the Waffen-SS — a revelation that convulsed Germany and reframed, for many readers, both his moral authority and his silence.

Later work

Later novels — The Flounder (1977), The Rat (1986), Too Far Afield (1995), Crabwalk (2002, on the Wilhelm Gustloff sinking) — kept him at the centre of German argument until his death in Lübeck in 2015. Volker Schlöndorff's film of The Tin Drum (1979) shared the Palme d'Or and won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film.
