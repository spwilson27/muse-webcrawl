The cartilaginous fishes, class Chondrichthyes, are jawed vertebrates whose skeletons are made of cartilage rather than true bone. The class comprises the sharks, rays, skates and sawfish (subclass Elasmobranchii) and the chimaeras (subclass Holocephali). Chondrichthyans appeared in the fossil record in the early Devonian period, more than 400 million years ago, and today number over 1,200 species, found in oceans worldwide and in some freshwater systems.

Characteristics

Cartilaginous fishes lack a swim bladder and maintain buoyancy largely through a large, oil-rich liver; most must swim continuously or rest on the bottom pumping water over their gills. Their skin is covered in tooth-like placoid scales (dermal denticles). Unlike bony fishes, they have exposed gill slits (five to seven pairs in elasmobranchs), internal fertilisation — males possess claspers — and reproduction ranging from egg-laying (oviparity) to live birth (viviparity). They possess keen senses, including electroreception through the ampullae of Lorenzini, used to detect the electrical fields of prey. Sharks continually replace their teeth throughout life.

Conservation

Slow growth, late maturity and low fecundity make cartilaginous fishes highly vulnerable to overfishing. Around a third of chondrichthyan species are assessed as threatened with extinction, principally from targeted shark fisheries, bycatch and the trade in shark fins and ray gill plates.
