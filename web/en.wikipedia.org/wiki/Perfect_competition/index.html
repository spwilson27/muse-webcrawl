Perfect competition

In economics, perfect competition is an idealised market structure in which no participant is large enough to influence the market price. Firms are "price takers": each sells at the going market price, and the allocation of resources is Pareto-efficient in the long run under the model's assumptions. Perfect competition serves as the benchmark against which real market structures — monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition — are compared.

Assumptions

The standard model assumes: a very large number of buyers and sellers; a homogeneous product; perfect information about prices and quality; free entry and exit of firms; no transaction costs; and no externalities. Under these conditions each firm faces a perfectly elastic (horizontal) demand curve at the market price.

Equilibrium

In the short run a firm maximises profit by producing where marginal cost equals marginal revenue, which — since price equals marginal revenue for a price taker — means price equals marginal cost. Firms may earn supernormal profits or losses in the short run. In the long run, entry and exit drive economic profit to zero, so equilibrium has price equal to both marginal cost and minimum average total cost: output is produced at the lowest possible unit cost (productive efficiency) and price reflects marginal cost (allocative efficiency).

Criticism and approximation

No real market satisfies all the assumptions strictly, though agricultural commodity markets and some financial markets are often cited as close approximations. Critics note that the model is static, ignores innovation and product differentiation, and that perfect information is unrealistic; economists nevertheless use it as the reference case of welfare economics and for analysing the effects of taxes, quotas and market power.
