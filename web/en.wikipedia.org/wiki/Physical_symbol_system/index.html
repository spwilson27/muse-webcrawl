A physical symbol system (also called a formal system) takes physical patterns (symbols), combining them into structures (expressions) and manipulating them by processes to produce new expressions. In artificial intelligence and cognitive science, the concept is central to the work of Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon.

The hypothesis

In their 1976 Turing Award lecture, "Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry: Symbols and Search", Newell and Simon stated the physical symbol system hypothesis: "A physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action." On this view, human minds and suitably programmed computers are both instances of physical symbol systems, and intelligence arises from the manipulation of symbols according to rules.

Definition

A physical symbol system consists of a set of entities called symbols — physical patterns that can occur as components of expressions. At any time the system contains a collection of symbol structures; processes operate on expressions to create, modify, reproduce and destroy them. The system exists in a world of objects wider than its own expressions and can designate objects in that world and interpret expressions as instructions for action.

Criticism

The hypothesis has been challenged from several directions. John Searle's Chinese room argument denies that symbol manipulation alone yields understanding. Connectionists argue that cognition is better explained by distributed subsymbolic processes in neural networks. Hubert Dreyfus and others contend that much intelligent behaviour is embodied and non-representational. Symbol grounding — how symbols acquire meaning — remains a core problem.
