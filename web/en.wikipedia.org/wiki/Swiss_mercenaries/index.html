Swiss mercenaries

Swiss mercenaries (Reisläufer) were soldiers from the cantons of the Old Swiss Confederacy who, from the late Middle Ages until the 19th century, hired themselves out to foreign powers — especially France, Spain, the Papal States, the Dutch Republic and the Italian states. Valued for their discipline, cohesion and aggressive pike tactics, they were among the most sought-after infantry in Europe for roughly three centuries, and their service was a major source of income for the Swiss cantons. The tradition survives today only in the Pontifical Swiss Guard of the Vatican.

Swiss pikemen, the dominant infantry of the late 15th and early 16th centuries.

Rise

Swiss infantry won fame through victories over the heavy cavalry of Burgundy and Austria in the 14th and 15th centuries, including Sempach (1386) and the Burgundian Wars (1474–1477). Fighting in dense columns (Gewalthaufen) of pikemen supported by halberdiers and crossbowmen, they offered their services under formal capitulations negotiated by the cantons. Their motto was said to be "Pas d'argent, pas de Suisses" — "no money, no Swiss."

Service and decline

Swiss regiments fought throughout the Italian Wars (1494–1559), including at Marignano (1515), after which France and the Confederacy concluded the Perpetual Peace of Fribourg (1516), cementing a centuries-long French alliance. Swiss Guards defended the Tuileries during the French Revolution in 1792, an event commemorated by the Lion Monument in Lucerne. Foreign military service was progressively restricted after 1848 and definitively banned by the Swiss Federal Constitution and the 1859 federal law, with the sole exception of the Vatican's Swiss Guard, founded in 1506. The moral and social costs of the trade — chroniclers such as Ulrich Zwingli, himself a former military chaplain, denounced it — shaped Swiss debates about neutrality and national identity.

Legacy

Swiss tactics revolutionised late-medieval warfare, ending the dominance of armoured cavalry and inspiring the German Landsknechte, who became their great rivals. Pension payments from foreign crowns also enriched Swiss political elites, a practice that fuelled corruption scandals and reform movements within the Confederacy.
