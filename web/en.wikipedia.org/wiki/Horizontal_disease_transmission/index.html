Horizontal transmission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Horizontal transmission is the transmission of organisms, such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, or prions, between members of the same generation. It contrasts with vertical transmission, in which a pathogen passes from parent to offspring, for example from mother to child during pregnancy, birth, or breastfeeding.

Routes

Horizontal transmission can occur through several routes:

Direct contact — physical contact between an infected and a susceptible individual, including touching, kissing, and sexual contact.

Indirect contact — contact with contaminated objects or surfaces (fomites), such as door handles, shared utensils, or medical equipment.

Droplet transmission — respiratory droplets expelled by coughing or sneezing that travel short distances.

Airborne transmission — smaller particles that remain suspended in the air over longer distances and times.

Vehicle-borne transmission — contaminated food, water, or blood products.

Vector-borne transmission — organisms such as mosquitoes, ticks, or fleas carrying pathogens between hosts.

Control measures

Because horizontal transmission depends on contact between contemporaries, control measures include hand hygiene, isolation and quarantine, ventilation, vaccination to reduce the pool of susceptible hosts, sterilisation of medical equipment, and vector control.

Horizontal gene transfer

A related concept in microbiology is horizontal gene transfer, the movement of genetic material between organisms of the same generation other than by parent-to-offspring inheritance, which is a major driver of antibiotic resistance spread among bacteria.
