EFL Championship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A match in the EFL Championship.

The English Football League Championship, known simply as the Championship, is the second tier of the English football league system and the highest division of the English Football League (EFL). It consists of 24 clubs. Each season, the two top-finishing teams are promoted automatically to the Premier League, together with the winners of a play-off series contested by the teams finishing third to sixth; the bottom three teams are relegated to EFL League One.

History

The division was introduced as the Football League Championship in 2004, replacing the Football League First Division as the second tier — a role that tier had held since the creation of the Premier League in 1992, and before that by the old Second Division. When the Football League rebranded as the English Football League in 2016, the division became the EFL Championship. Title sponsors have included Coca-Cola, Npower and Sky Bet.

The Championship is among the most-watched second-tier leagues in world football, with total attendances rivalling many top divisions. The Championship play-off final at Wembley Stadium is often described as the richest single game in football, because promotion to the Premier League brings a club well over £100 million in additional revenue over subsequent seasons, including parachute payments if it is immediately relegated.

Format

Each club plays the other 23 home and away, for 46 matches, between August and May. Three points are awarded for a win and one for a draw. At the end of the season the semi-finals of the play-offs are played over two legs between the third- and sixth-placed and fourth- and fifth-placed teams, followed by a one-off final at Wembley.

Notable records

Reading's 106 points in 2005–06 remains the record points total for the division. Sunderland, Newcastle United and Norwich City are among the clubs to have won the second-tier title most often in the modern era, while the division is known for its unpredictability and the frequency with which relegated Premier League clubs struggle — or, conversely, bounce straight back.
