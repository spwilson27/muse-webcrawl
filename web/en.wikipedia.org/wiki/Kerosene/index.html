Kerosene

Kerosene and a kerosene lamp

Kerosene, or paraffin, is a combustible hydrocarbon liquid which is derived from petroleum. It is widely used as a fuel in aviation as well as households. Its name derives from the Greek kērós (κηρός) meaning "wax", and was registered as a trademark by Canadian geologist and inventor Abraham Gesner in 1854 before evolving into a generic trademark. It is sometimes spelled kerosine in scientific and industrial usage.

Properties

Kerosene is a thin, clear liquid consisting of hydrocarbons with roughly 10 to 16 carbon atoms per molecule. It is less volatile than gasoline, with a flash point between about 37 and 65 °C, and a density of about 0.78–0.81 g/cm³. It is miscible in petroleum solvents but immiscible in water.

Uses

Kerosene's major uses are as the basis of jet fuel (such as Jet A and Jet A-1), as a heating and lighting fuel — it remains a common household fuel in many developing countries for lamps and stoves — and as a solvent and pesticide carrier. Highly refined kerosene fuels such as RP-1 are used in rocketry.

History

Distillation of coal and oil shale to produce kerosene-like oils was described by the Persian scholar Razi in the 9th century. Gesner's process of the 1840s–50s, and the petroleum refining industry that followed the 1859 Drake oil well, made kerosene the dominant illuminating oil of the late 19th century, displacing whale oil.
