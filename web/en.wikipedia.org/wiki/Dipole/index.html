Dipole

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, a dipole (from Greek di- "two" and polos "pole") is an electromagnetic phenomenon with two equal and opposite poles separated by a distance. The term is used for several related concepts: the electric dipole, consisting of two equal and opposite electric charges; the magnetic dipole, a closed circulation of electric current or a pair of magnetic poles; and, more generally, the leading term in the multipole expansion of a field produced by a localised source.

Electric dipole

An electric dipole consists of charges +q and −q separated by a vector d, giving a dipole moment p = qd, pointing from the negative to the positive charge. Physical dipoles include polar molecules such as water, whose charge distribution is permanently asymmetric; induced dipoles arise when an external electric field distorts a neutral atom or molecule. In an external field, a dipole experiences a torque tending to align it with the field and a net force in a non-uniform field.

Magnetic dipole

A magnetic dipole is characterised by a magnetic moment, as produced by a small current loop or by intrinsic spin. No isolated magnetic poles (magnetic monopoles) have been observed, so the dipole term is the leading contribution to the field of any localised magnetic source; the Earth's magnetic field is approximately a dipole tilted relative to the rotation axis.

Radiation and other uses

An oscillating electric dipole is the simplest radiating source: the Hertzian dipole model underlies antenna theory, and the half-wave dipole antenna is named for its field pattern. The term also appears in meteorology and oceanography (for example the Indian Ocean Dipole) for paired regions of opposite anomaly.
