Chahalgani

Also calledTurkan-i-Chahalgani ("the Forty")
ContextDelhi Sultanate, 13th century
Associated withSultan Iltutmish; later Balban
NatureCorps of elite Turkish slave officers (amirs)

The Chahalgani — in Persian Turkan-i-Chahalgani, "the Forty (Turkish chiefs)" — was a powerful corps of elite Turkish slave officers (amirs) of the early Delhi Sultanate in 13th-century India. Raised and patronised by Sultan Shamsuddin Iltutmish (r. 1211–1236), its members were granted important governorships (iqtas) and court offices, and for three decades after Iltutmish's death they dominated the politics of the Sultanate.

Rise to power

Iltutmish built up the Forty from his own purchased Turkish slaves (bandagan), trained for military and administrative service, as a counterweight to the older free-born nobility. After his death in 1236, the Chahalgani became kingmakers: they raised and deposed a succession of his descendants, including Razia Sultan (r. 1236–1240), whose attempts to build an independent power base led the Forty to rebel against her, and the weak sultans Bahram Shah and Masud Shah, before installing Nasiruddin Mahmud in 1246.

Balban and the destruction of the Forty

Ghiyasuddin Balban, himself originally a member of the Chahalgani, rose as the power behind Nasiruddin Mahmud's throne and became sultan in 1266. Determined to exalt the crown and end the oligarchy, Balban systematically broke the Forty: its surviving members were transferred, disgraced, or, according to the chronicler Ziauddin Barani, eliminated by poison and assassination. His destruction of the corps cleared the way for the centralised, court-centred monarchy of his reign and ended the era of slave-noble dominance in Delhi politics.

Significance

The Chahalgani illustrates the central tension of the early Sultanate between the sultan and the Turkish military aristocracy created by the institution of military slavery (mamluk system), a pattern paralleled in other contemporary Islamic states.
