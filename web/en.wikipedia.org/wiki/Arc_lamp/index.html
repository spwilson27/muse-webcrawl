An arc lamp or arc light is a lamp that produces light by means of an electric arc sustained between two electrodes, typically of carbon, tungsten, or metal, in air or in a gas such as xenon or mercury vapour. Arc lamps were the first practical form of electric lighting and remain unmatched in compact high-brightness applications such as cinema projection and searchlights.

History

Humphry Davy demonstrated the carbon arc at the Royal Institution in the first decade of the 19th century using a large battery. Practical lamps awaited cheap generators in the 1870s: Pavel Yablochkov's "electric candle" lit Paris avenues in 1878, and Charles F. Brush's arc systems lit streets and public squares in American cities from 1879. Arc lighting dominated large-space illumination until the spread of incandescent lamps in the 1890s and after, but the carbon arc remained standard for film projection, lighthouses, and military searchlights through much of the 20th century.

Operation

The arc is struck by touching the electrodes together and separating them; the current ionises the gas and vaporised electrode material, forming a plasma whose temperature in a carbon arc crater can exceed 3,500 °C. Because the arc has negative resistance, a ballast is required to stabilise the current. In open-air carbon arcs the electrodes burn away and historically had to be fed together by clockwork or electromagnetic regulators, producing a characteristic hissing and intense ultraviolet-rich light.

Types and uses

  • Carbon arc — searchlights, older cinema projectors, arc welding (related technology), and the limelight-class stage effects.
  • Xenon short-arc — modern digital cinema projection, IMAX projectors, solar simulators, and medical endoscopy.
  • Mercury and metal-halide arcs — stadium, industrial and street lighting (largely supplanted by LEDs).
  • Flaming arc — electrodes impregnated with metal salts to colour and intensify the light, once used for street lighting.

Hazards

Arc lamps produce intense ultraviolet radiation, ozone, and (for carbon arcs) carbon monoxide; enclosed lamps guard against UV exposure and the risk that a hot pressurised xenon bulb may explode if mishandled.
