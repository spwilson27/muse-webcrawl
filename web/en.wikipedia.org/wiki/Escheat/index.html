Escheat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Escheat is a common law doctrine that transfers the real property of a person who has died without heirs to the Crown or state. It serves to ensure that property is not left in limbo without a legal owner. The word derives from the Old French escheoir, "to fall to one's lot", ultimately from Latin excadere, "to fall out".

Origins in feudal law

Under medieval English feudal land law, land was held of a lord, ultimately of the king, in return for services. When a tenant died without heirs, or was convicted of a felony, the land escheated — fell back — to the lord of whom it was held. The two classic grounds were propter defectum sanguinis (failure of blood, i.e. no heir) and propter delictum tenentis (for the tenant's crime, especially treason and felony, where the doctrine of attainder and "corruption of blood" barred inheritance).

Escheat for felony was abolished in England by the Forfeiture Act 1870, and escheat on intestacy was largely superseded by the doctrine of bona vacantia under the Administration of Estates Act 1925, under which ownerless property passes to the Crown, the Duchy of Lancaster, or the Duchy of Cornwall.

Modern usage

In the modern United States, "escheat" has broadened to cover unclaimed property generally: dormant bank accounts, uncashed cheques, forgotten safe-deposit contents, and unclaimed securities are transferred to the custody of the state after a statutory dormancy period. In most states the government takes custody rather than outright title, and the true owner (or their heirs) may reclaim the property, sometimes indefinitely. State unclaimed-property statutes generate billions of dollars annually, much of it eventually returned to owners.

Similar doctrines exist in many jurisdictions. In Canada, escheat is governed by provincial law; in Scotland, ownerless property falls to the Crown as ultimus haeres (last heir); civil-law systems have analogous rules for hereditas jacens and vacant successions.
