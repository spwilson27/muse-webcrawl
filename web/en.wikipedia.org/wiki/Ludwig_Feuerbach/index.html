Ludwig Andreas von Feuerbach (German: [ˈluːtvɪç ˈfɔʏɐbax]; 28 July 1804 – 13 September 1872) was a German philosopher and anthropologist. He was a leading figure among the Young Hegelians and is best known for his book The Essence of Christianity (1841), which argued that God is nothing other than the outward projection of humanity's inward nature. His materialist philosophy profoundly influenced Karl Marx, Friedrich Engels, and the development of dialectical and historical materialism.

Life

Feuerbach was born in Landshut, Bavaria, the fourth son of the eminent jurist Paul Johann Anselm von Feuerbach. He studied theology at Heidelberg before moving to Berlin to attend the lectures of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel, whose philosophy initially dominated his thought. After teaching briefly at Erlangen, his academic career was effectively ended by the scandal over his anonymous Thoughts on Death and Immortality (1830), which denied personal immortality. He spent most of the rest of his life as a private scholar at Bruckberg and later at Rechenberg near Nuremberg.

Philosophy

In The Essence of Christianity (1841), Feuerbach argued that religion is the dream of the human mind: human beings project their own highest qualities — reason, will and love — onto an imaginary divine being, and then worship their own essence as something alien to themselves. Theology, he concluded, is really anthropology. His famous formulation held that "man is what he eats" (Der Mensch ist, was er isst), expressing his turn towards a sensuous, naturalistic materialism grounded in human need and feeling.

Feuerbach's critique of Hegel's speculative philosophy — his insistence on beginning from the concrete, embodied human being rather than abstract Spirit — shaped Marx's early writings, including the Theses on Feuerbach (1845), in which Marx both adopted and criticised Feuerbach's contemplative materialism.

Major works

  • The Essence of Christianity (1841)
  • Preliminary Theses on the Reform of Philosophy (1842)
