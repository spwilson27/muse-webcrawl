Guidonian hand

The Guidonian hand was a mnemonic device used to assist singers in learning to sight-sing in medieval and Renaissance music. It takes its name from Guido of Arezzo (c. 991–after 1033), the Italian Benedictine monk credited with the invention of modern staff notation and with the solmisation syllables derived from the hymn Ut queant laxis.

Description

The hand maps the twenty notes of the Guidonian gamut onto the tips and joints of the fingers and thumb of the left hand, arranged in a spiral from the tip of the thumb upward. Each location on the hand corresponds to a pitch identified by its letter and solmisation syllables within the system of overlapping six-note hexachords (natural, hard, and soft). A teacher could point to a joint with the right hand and the student, who had memorised the scheme, would know which pitch to sing.

Use

Because the hand was always available and required no materials, it became the standard elementary teaching tool of Western music theory for several centuries. Diagrams of the hand appear in numerous theoretical treatises and manuscripts from the eleventh through the eighteenth century. Guido himself described his method of teaching the gamut by reference to the joints of the hand in his writings, though the fully developed diagram is the work of later theorists.
