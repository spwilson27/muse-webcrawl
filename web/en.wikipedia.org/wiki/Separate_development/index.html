Separate development

Separate development was the official policy framework of apartheid South Africa under Prime Minister Hendrik Verwoerd from the late 1950s onward. It held that each of the country's officially defined racial and ethnic groups should govern itself and "develop" within its own designated territory, providing the stated rationale for the creation of the Bantustans, or "homelands."

Policy

The Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act of 1959 divided the Black population into ethnic units — Zulu, Xhosa, Tswana, Sotho, Venda, Tsonga, Swazi, and others — each assigned a homeland comprising roughly 13 percent of South Africa's land, much of it fragmented and agriculturally poor. The Bantu Homelands Citizenship Act of 1970 made Black South Africans citizens of their assigned homelands rather than of South Africa itself, and from 1976 four homelands — Transkei, Bophuthatswana, Venda, and Ciskei — were granted nominal "independence," recognized by no other country.

Purpose and effects

In practice, separate development supplied a veneer of self-determination for a system designed to preserve white political control and a supply of migrant labour while stripping Black South Africans of citizenship and political rights in most of the country. Millions of people were forcibly removed to the homelands under the policy. The homelands were reincorporated into South Africa with the end of apartheid and the first democratic elections in 1994.
