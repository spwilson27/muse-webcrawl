Duel

Duel
Type: Ritualised combat over honour
Weapons: Swords, later pistols
Era: Early modern period to 19th century

A duel is an arranged engagement in combat between two people, with matched weapons, in accordance with agreed-upon rules, fought to settle a point of honour. Duels were practised from the 15th to the 20th century in Western societies, despite being frequently outlawed.

History

The duel of honour developed from medieval judicial combat and the Germanic tradition of trial by battle. In Renaissance Italy and France it became codified among gentlemen: an insult required satisfaction, delivered through a challenge, the appointment of seconds, and a formal encounter. Elaborate codes, such as the Irish Code Duello of 1777, governed apologies, choice of weapons and the degree of injury that satisfied honour — often first blood with swords, or a single exchange of pistol shots.

Famous duels

The 1804 Burr–Hamilton duel, in which U.S. Vice President Aaron Burr fatally wounded Alexander Hamilton, shocked America. The Russian poet Alexander Pushkin was killed in an 1837 duel. In 1832 the mathematician Évariste Galois died in a duel at age 20. British Prime Minister the Duke of Wellington fought a duel in 1829 while in office.

Decline

Changing attitudes, legal prosecution and public ridicule drove duelling out of the English-speaking world by the mid-19th century; it lingered longer in France, Germany (student Mensur fencing) and Latin America, with isolated affairs of honour recorded into the 20th century.
