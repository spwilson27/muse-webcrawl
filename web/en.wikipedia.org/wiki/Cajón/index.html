Cajón

[Image placeholder: A musician seated on a wooden cajón, hands striking the tapa]

A cajón (Spanish: [kaˈxon], "box", "crate" or "drawer") is a box-shaped percussion instrument originally from Peru, played by slapping the front or rear faces with the hands, fingers, or sometimes implements such as brushes, mallets, or sticks. The cajón is used in Tondero, Zamacueca, Peruvian Marinera Norteña, festejo and Peruvian vals, and other styles of Afro-Peruvian music; since the 1970s it has become a staple of flamenco and of acoustic popular music worldwide.

Construction and playing

The instrument is a five-sided wooden box, typically about 48 cm tall, with a thin plywood front plate (the tapa) that serves as the striking surface and a circular sound hole in the back. The player sits astride the cajón, tilting it slightly, and strikes the tapa: low bass tones come from the centre of the face, high slap tones from the upper corners and edges. Modern cajones often include guitar strings, wires or snare elements pressed against the inside of the tapa to add a buzzing snare-like timbre.

History

The cajón is thought to have originated with enslaved Africans in colonial Peru, who — forbidden drums — turned shipping crates and drawers into instruments; it became central to Afro-Peruvian music, revived in the 1950s–60s by musicians such as Caitro Soto. In 1977 the flamenco guitarist Paco de Lucía encountered the instrument in Peru, and his percussionist Rubem Dantas began using it in flamenco, where its portability and palette suited the music so well that it is now considered a standard flamenco instrument. From there it spread globally as the default percussion seat for acoustic and unplugged sets.
