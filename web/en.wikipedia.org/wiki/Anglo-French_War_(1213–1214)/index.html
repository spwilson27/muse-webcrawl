The Anglo-French War of 1213–1214 was the culminating campaign of King John of England's efforts to recover the Angevin lands in France lost to King Philip II Augustus in 1203–1206, including Normandy, Anjou, and Maine. John constructed a grand coalition against Philip, combining his own expedition to Poitou with an invasion of northern France by his allies — the Emperor Otto IV, the counts of Flanders and Boulogne, and John's nephew-by-marriage allies in the Low Countries, funded by English subsidies.

Background

John had lost Normandy in 1204 after the scandal surrounding the disappearance of his nephew Arthur of Brittany and Philip's exploitation of John's legal vulnerabilities as a French vassal. A planned recovery expedition in 1206 achieved little. Thereafter John rebuilt his finances — provoking baronial resentment in the process — quarrelled with Pope Innocent III over the appointment of Stephen Langton as Archbishop of Canterbury (leading to an interdict on England from 1208 and John's excommunication), and finally submitted to the Pope in 1213, receiving England back as a papal fief. This freed him to campaign abroad.

The campaign of 1214

In February 1214 John landed at La Rochelle and campaigned in Poitou and Anjou, winning over Lusignan lords and advancing as far as Angers, but he retreated before Prince Louis of France at Roche-au-Moine on 2 July after his Poitevin allies deserted. Meanwhile the northern army under Otto IV and the Earl of Salisbury invaded Flanders. On Sunday 27 July 1214 Philip II met them at the Battle of Bouvines and won a decisive victory: Otto fled, and the counts of Flanders and Boulogne and the Earl of Salisbury were captured. Bouvines destroyed John's coalition and his hopes of restoring the Angevin Empire; it is regarded as one of the most consequential battles of medieval Europe, strengthening the French monarchy and, by weakening John, setting the stage for the English barons' revolt.

Aftermath

John agreed the Truce of Chinon in September 1214 and returned to England to face his barons, whose rebellion forced him to seal Magna Carta at Runnymede in June 1215. When John repudiated the charter, the barons invited Prince Louis of France to take the English throne, opening the First Barons' War.
