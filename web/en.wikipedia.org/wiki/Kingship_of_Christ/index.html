The Kingship of Christ is a doctrine of Christian theology affirming that Jesus Christ is king and ruler of all creation. Together with his roles as prophet and priest, it forms the threefold office (Latin: munus triplex) by which systematic theology, especially in the Reformed tradition following John Calvin, describes the work of Christ.

Biblical basis

Scriptural foundations cited for the doctrine include the angel's announcement that Jesus would receive "the throne of his father David" (Luke 1:32), the testimony that God has made him "both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:36), the hymn of Philippians 2, in which every knee bows to Jesus, and the visions of Revelation, where Christ is called "King of kings and Lord of lords". The titulus on the cross, "Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews", is traditionally read as a proclamation of this kingship.

Teaching and feast

In Catholic teaching the doctrine was given prominent expression by Pope Pius XI in the encyclical Quas primas (1925), which instituted the Feast of Christ the King to counter rising secularism and to assert that Christ's authority extends not only to individual hearts but to societies and nations. Since 1970 the feast has been celebrated on the last Sunday of the liturgical year. The kingship has often borne an eschatological character: Christ reigns now from heaven, but his kingdom will be fully manifested at his return.
