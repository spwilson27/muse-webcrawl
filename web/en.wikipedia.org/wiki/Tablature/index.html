Tablature

Tablature (or tabulature, or tab for short) is a form of musical notation indicating instrument fingering rather than musical pitches. It was widely used from the Renaissance for lute, vihuela, guitar, and keyboard instruments (organ tablature); a six-line system indicating strings and frets for guitar is ubiquitous today in popular music, folk, and instructional publishing, and is often shared online in ASCII form.

How it works

In modern guitar tablature, six horizontal lines represent the strings (highest string on top), and numbers on the lines indicate the fret to be pressed; special symbols mark bends, slides, hammer-ons, pull-offs, harmonics, and muting. Unlike staff notation, tablature prescribes where to place the fingers but gives rhythm only approximately (unless rhythmic stems are added), and it is specific to a tuning and instrument layout. Historically, French lute tablature used letters (a = open string, b = first fret, …) on lines representing courses, while Spanish and Italian systems used numbers, with Italian tablature placing the lowest course on top.

History

Keyboard tablature appeared in 14th-century Germany, and lute tablature flourished in the 16th and 17th centuries in the printed books of composers such as Francesco da Milano and John Dowland. As staff notation became standard for art music, tablature survived among folk and amateur players and saw a massive revival with rock and metal guitar playing, tablature magazines, and internet tab archives — raising copyright disputes with music publishers over user-transcribed tabs.

Other instruments

Related systems exist for banjo, mandolin, ukulele, harmonica (arrow-and-hole systems), drums (drum tablature maps lines to kit pieces), and East Asian instruments such as the guqin, whose classical notation combines fingering instructions in abbreviated characters.
