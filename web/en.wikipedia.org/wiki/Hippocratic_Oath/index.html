Hippocratic Oath

The Hippocratic Oath is an oath of ethics historically taken by physicians. It is one of the most widely known of Greek medical texts. In its original form, it requires a new physician to swear, by a number of healing gods, to uphold specific ethical standards. The oath is the earliest expression of medical ethics in the Western world, establishing several principles of medical ethics which remain of paramount significance today.

History

The oath was traditionally attributed to Hippocrates, the Greek physician of the Age of Pericles regarded as the father of medicine, though modern scholarship suggests it may have been written later, possibly by his followers. The oldest surviving fragment dates to about AD 275. The original oath was sworn before the gods Apollo, Asclepius, Hygieia and Panacea, and included promises to treat one's teacher as family, to pass on medical knowledge, to use treatments for the benefit of the sick and never for harm, to refuse deadly drugs and abortive remedies, to abstain from surgery (leaving it to specialists), to avoid sexual misconduct with patients, and to keep patient information confidential.

Modern versions

Many medical schools now administer modernised versions of the oath. The best-known is the Declaration of Geneva, adopted by the World Medical Association in 1948 and revised several times since, which frames the physician's duties in contemporary terms, including respect for patient autonomy and dignity. The phrase "first, do no harm" (primum non nocere) is often attributed to the oath but actually appears in another Hippocratic work, Of the Epidemics.

Translation excerpt

I swear by Apollo Healer, by Asclepius, by Hygieia, by Panacea, and by all the gods and goddesses, making them my witnesses, that I will carry out, according to my ability and judgment, this oath and this indenture… I will use treatment to help the sick according to my ability and judgment, but never with a view to injury and wrong-doing.

Significance

Although the original oath is rarely sworn verbatim today, it remains a rite of passage for medical graduates in many countries and a symbol of the ethical obligations of the medical profession, including beneficence, non-maleficence and confidentiality.
