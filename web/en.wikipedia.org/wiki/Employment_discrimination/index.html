Employment discrimination

Employment discrimination is a form of illegal discrimination in the workplace based on legally protected attributes such as race, gender, national origin, religion, age, disability, sexual orientation or gender identity. Discrimination may involve disparate treatment — deliberately treating a person differently because of a protected characteristic — or disparate impact, in which a seemingly neutral policy disproportionately harms a protected group without sufficient business justification.

United States law

Federal law in the United States prohibits employment discrimination through several statutes. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits discrimination in employment on the basis of race, colour, religion, sex, and national origin. The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 protects workers aged 40 and over, and the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 prohibits discrimination against qualified individuals with disabilities and requires reasonable accommodation. The Equal Pay Act of 1963 addresses sex-based wage discrimination. These laws are principally enforced by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), and in 2020 the Supreme Court held in Bostock v. Clayton County that Title VII's prohibition of sex discrimination extends to sexual orientation and gender identity.

Other jurisdictions

The United Kingdom consolidated its anti-discrimination law in the Equality Act 2010, which lists nine protected characteristics. The European Union prohibits employment discrimination through directives covering gender, race, disability, age, religion, and sexual orientation, implemented in member states' national law.
