Power series

In mathematics, a power series (in one variable) is an infinite series of the form a₀ + a₁(x − c) + a₂(x − c)² + a₃(x − c)³ + …, where aₙ is the coefficient of the n-th term and c is a constant called the centre of the series. Power series are useful in mathematical analysis, where they arise as Taylor series of infinitely differentiable functions; indeed, Borel's theorem implies that every power series is the Taylor series of some smooth function.

Radius of convergence

A power series may converge for some values of x and diverge for others. There is always a number R, the radius of convergence (possibly 0 or ∞), such that the series converges absolutely whenever |x − c| < R and diverges whenever |x − c| > R. By the Cauchy–Hadamard theorem, R is given by 1/R = lim sup |aₙ|1/n. Inside its disc of convergence, a power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term, and defines an analytic (holomorphic) function.

Examples

The geometric series 1/(1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + … converges for |x| < 1. The exponential function has the everywhere-convergent series eˣ = Σ xⁿ/n!, and the trigonometric functions have analogous expansions. The series for log(1 + x) converges for |x| < 1 and conditionally at x = 1 (Abel's theorem extends continuity to that boundary point).

Formal power series

In algebra, formal power series are treated as purely algebraic objects: convergence is ignored, and the series form a ring used throughout combinatorics (through generating functions), number theory, and the theory of formal groups. The ring of formal power series over a field is complete with respect to the ideal of series with zero constant term.
