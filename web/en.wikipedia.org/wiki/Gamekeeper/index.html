Gamekeeper

A gamekeeper (often shortened to keeper) is a person employed by a landowner to manage an area of countryside to make it more suitable for game animals such as pheasants, partridges, grouse and deer, and to control predators so that the game can flourish. The role is most strongly associated with the shooting estates of Great Britain.

Duties

A keeper's year revolves around the shooting season. On lowland estates, keepers rear pheasants and partridges from chicks in release pens, feed them through the winter, maintain cover crops and woodland rides, and organise beaters and pickers-up on shoot days. On upland grouse moors, the keeper's central tasks are the rotational burning of heather (muirburn) to produce the mosaic of young and old growth that red grouse need, and the control of foxes, crows and stoats.

Historically, a large part of the keeper's job was war against poachers and "vermin". Victorian keepers waged notorious campaigns against birds of prey, polecats and pine martens, driving some species to the edge of British extinction; the gibbet, on which dead predators were displayed, was a standard part of estate equipment. Modern gamekeeping operates within wildlife law, and protected species may no longer be harmed.

History

The profession grew with the Game Laws of the 18th and 19th centuries, which restricted the taking of game to the landed classes. Poaching gangs and keepers fought famous nocturnal battles, and keepers could be equipped with man-traps and spring guns until these were outlawed. The two world wars devastated the profession — thousands of keepers were killed in the First World War and never replaced — and estate gamekeeping contracted sharply thereafter. Several thousand full-time keepers nevertheless remain employed in the United Kingdom, represented by bodies such as the National Gamekeepers' Organisation.

In culture

The gamekeeper is a familiar figure in British fiction, from Mellors in D. H. Lawrence's Lady Chatterley's Lover (1928) to the Dursleys' host in the Harry Potter novels, and keepers feature in many country-house dramas as symbols of the rural class order.
