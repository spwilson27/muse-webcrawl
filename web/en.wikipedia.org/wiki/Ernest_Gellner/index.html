Ernest Gellner

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ernest André Gellner (9 December 1925 – 5 November 1995) was a Czech-British philosopher and social anthropologist, professor at the London School of Economics and later Cambridge, and the author of Nations and Nationalism (1983), a landmark modernist theory of nationalism.

Gellner's aphorism was that “nationalism is not the awakening of nations to self-consciousness: it invents nations where they do not exist”. Industrial society, he argued, requires a mobile, literate, culturally homogeneous workforce socialised by universal state education into a shared “high culture”; nationalism is the political demand that culture and polity coincide. Agrarian societies, by contrast, neither need nor produce nations: culture there marks status vertically rather than uniting whole populations.

Life and other work

Born in Paris to Czech Jewish parents and raised in Prague, Gellner fled to England in 1939, served with the Czechoslovak forces, and studied at Oxford. His first book, Words and Things (1959), was a celebrated polemic against Oxford linguistic philosophy. He wrote widely on Islam (Muslim Society, 1981), Soviet anthropology, psychoanalysis and civil society (Conditions of Liberty, 1994), and after 1989 directed a centre for nationalism studies in Prague, where he died.
