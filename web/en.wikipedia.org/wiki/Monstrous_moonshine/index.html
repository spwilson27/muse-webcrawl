Monstrous moonshine

In mathematics, monstrous moonshine, or moonshine theory, is the unexpected connection discovered by John Conway and Simon Norton in 1979 between the Monster group M and modular functions, in particular the j function. The term was coined by Conway; "moonshine" refers both to the apparent absurdity ("crazy talk") of the connection and to illicitly distilled liquor, suggesting something smuggled in without proper explanation.

The observation

The j-invariant has the Fourier expansion j(τ) = q−1 + 744 + 196884q + 21493760q² + …, where the coefficient 196884 equals 196883 + 1 — and 196883 is the dimension of the smallest nontrivial representation of the Monster, the largest sporadic simple group. Conway and Norton conjectured that for each element g of the Monster, a certain generating function (a McKay–Thompson series) is a Hauptmodul for a genus-zero subgroup of SL(2, ℝ), completely determined by g's conjugacy class.

Resolution

Richard Borcherds proved the Conway–Norton conjectures in 1992, using the monster vertex algebra constructed by Frenkel, Lepowsky, and Meurman and his own theory of generalized Kac–Moody algebras, drawing on the no-ghost theorem from string theory. Borcherds received the Fields Medal in 1998 partly for this work. Moonshine has since inspired further "moonshine" phenomena linking other groups to modular forms and connections to conformal field theory and string theory.
