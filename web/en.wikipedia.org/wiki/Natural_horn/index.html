Natural horn

Family: Brass / labrosones
Pitch: Determined by tube length; changed with crooks
Technique: Harmonics plus hand-stopping
Era: Principal orchestral horn until c. 1840s

The natural horn is a brass instrument, the predecessor of the modern French horn, that has no valves or keys. Its pitch is governed by the length of its tubing and by the player's embouchure and hand position, so it can naturally play only the notes of one harmonic series at a time.

Technique

To change the fundamental key, the player inserts U-shaped tubes called crooks that lengthen the instrument. Within a series, missing chromatic notes can be obtained by hand-stopping — inserting the right hand further into the bell — a technique developed in the mid-eighteenth century and associated with Anton Joseph Hampel. Stopped notes sound muffled and veiled, a colour composers learned to exploit.

Repertoire and history

Derived from the hunting horn (cor de chasse), the natural horn entered the orchestra in the Baroque era and is the instrument for which Handel, Mozart (four horn concertos, written for Joseph Leutgeb) and Beethoven wrote. Valved horns appeared in the 1810s–1840s and gradually displaced it, though Brahms still preferred the natural horn's tone and wrote for the Waldhorn. Today the natural horn is played by period-instrument specialists in historically informed performance.
