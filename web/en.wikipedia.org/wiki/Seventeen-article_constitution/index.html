Seventeen-article constitution
Prince Shōtoku (574–622)

AuthorAttributed to Prince Shōtoku

The Seventeen-article constitution (十七条憲法, Jūshichijō Kenpō) is, according to the Nihon Shoki, a document authored by Prince Shōtoku in 604, during the reign of Empress Suiko. Despite its conventional name, it is not a constitution in the modern legal sense but a set of moral and political maxims addressed to officials, drawing on Buddhist, Confucian and Legalist ideas.

Content

The articles enjoin harmony (wa) as the highest value — the first article declares that "harmony is to be valued, and the avoidance of wanton opposition to be honoured" — reverence for the Three Treasures of Buddhism, obedience to imperial edicts, impartial conduct by officials, diligence in public service, and decision-making by consultation rather than by individuals. Other articles condemn corrupt litigation, tax oppression of peasants by local lords, and officials pursuing private gain.

Authenticity

The document is preserved only in the Nihon Shoki (720). Its authenticity was long debated: some scholars argued the polished Chinese and terminology implied a later date, but many now accept a core dating to Shōtoku's era, noting that it contains few of the anachronisms a later forger might have introduced.

Legacy

The constitution is regarded as one of Japan's earliest political texts and an expression of the centralising reforms of the Asuka period. Its first article, on harmony, remains widely quoted in Japanese public life.
