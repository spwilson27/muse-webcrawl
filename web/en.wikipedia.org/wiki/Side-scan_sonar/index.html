Side-scan sonar

Side-scan sonar (also sidescan or side scan sonar) is a category of sonar system used to efficiently create an image of large areas of the sea floor. A side-scan transducer, usually mounted in a towed body called a "towfish" (or on an autonomous underwater vehicle or ship's hull), emits fan-shaped pulses of sound to both sides, perpendicular to the path of travel, and records the strength of the echoes returned from the seabed. As the vessel advances, successive pings build up a strip map of acoustic backscatter that reveals the texture of the bottom and the shapes and shadows of objects standing proud of it.

How it works

The returning echo intensity varies with the hardness and roughness of the bottom and the grazing angle: hard, rough surfaces and objects return strong echoes (bright on the record), while soft mud and the acoustic "shadow" behind an object return little or none (dark). The length of a shadow lets analysts estimate an object's height. Systems operate at frequencies from tens of kilohertz (for ranges of kilometres) to several hundred kilohertz or more (for centimetre-scale resolution at short range). Although side-scan produces plan-view imagery of striking photographic quality, it does not directly measure depth across the swath, a task performed by multibeam echosounders; the two are often operated together.

History and applications

Side-scan was developed in the 1950s and 1960s, drawing on wartime sonar work; early pioneers included Harold Edgerton at MIT, whose "mud-penetrating" and side-looking sonars were used in marine geology, and British groups at the National Institute of Oceanography. It became the standard tool for locating shipwrecks and downed aircraft — famously used to find the wreck of the Titanic's debris field region, the Air France Flight 447 recorders' search area, and countless lost vessels — and is used in pipeline and cable route surveys, harbour and mine countermeasures, dredging, fisheries habitat mapping, archaeology, and geological seabed classification.
