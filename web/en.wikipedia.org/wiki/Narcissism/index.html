Narcissism

Narcissism is a self-centred personality style characterised as having an excessive preoccupation with oneself and one's own needs, often at the expense of others. The term originates from the Greek myth of Narcissus, a beautiful youth who fell in love with his own reflection in a pool and wasted away beside it. Narcissism exists on a spectrum: a degree of healthy self-regard is considered normal and adaptive, while extreme forms constitute the mental health diagnosis of narcissistic personality disorder (NPD).

Psychology and psychiatry

Sigmund Freud's 1914 essay "On Narcissism: An Introduction" distinguished primary narcissism (the infant's normal self-love) from pathological secondary narcissism. Later theorists diverged: Heinz Kohut's self psychology viewed narcissistic needs as developmental and treatable through empathic mirroring, while Otto Kernberg described pathological narcissism as a defensive organisation bordering on borderline pathology. The DSM-5 defines narcissistic personality disorder by a pervasive pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration and lack of empathy, with prevalence estimates around 0.5–5 per cent of the general population. Researchers distinguish grandiose ("overt") from vulnerable ("covert") narcissism, the latter marked by hypersensitivity and resentful withdrawal beneath apparent modesty.

Measurement and culture

The trait is commonly measured with the Narcissistic Personality Inventory (NPI, Raskin and Hall, 1979). Narcissism correlates with leadership emergence but also with aggression when the ego is threatened ("threatened egotism"), exploitativeness, and difficulty in long-term relationships. Cultural critics, beginning with Christopher Lasch's The Culture of Narcissism (1979), have treated rising narcissism as a symptom of modern consumer society; claims of a generational "narcissism epidemic" (Twenge and Campbell, 2009) remain contested.
