Competitive exclusion principle

In ecology, the competitive exclusion principle, sometimes called Gause's law, is a proposition named for Georgy Gause that states that two species competing for the same limiting resource cannot coexist at constant population values. When one species has even the slightest advantage over another, the one with the advantage will dominate in the long term. This leads either to the extinction of the weaker competitor or to an evolutionary or behavioral shift toward a different ecological niche. The principle has been paraphrased in the maxim "complete competitors cannot coexist".

History

The competitive exclusion principle is commonly attributed to the work of the Russian ecologist Georgy Gause, who published it in 1934 based on laboratory experiments with Paramecium species competing for yeast as food. When Paramecium aurelia and Paramecium caudatum were grown together, P. caudatum was eventually driven to extinction. Earlier formulations of the idea appear in the work of Joseph Grinnell (1904) and in the mathematical models of Vito Volterra and Alfred J. Lotka, whose Lotka–Volterra competition equations provide a theoretical basis for the principle.

Experimental evidence

Beyond Gause's protozoan experiments, classic evidence includes Thomas Park's laboratory studies of flour beetles (Tribolium), in which the outcome of competition between two species depended on environmental conditions, and Joseph Connell's field experiments on barnacles on the Scottish coast, which showed that the realized niche of Chthamalus was restricted by competition with Balanus.

Criticism and paradoxes

The principle has been criticized as nearly tautological and difficult to test in the field, since competing species that coexist can always be said to differ in their niches. The "paradox of the plankton", noted by G. Evelyn Hutchinson in 1961, observes that many species of phytoplankton coexist in apparently homogeneous environments despite competing for few resources; explanations invoke temporal environmental variation, predation, and spatial heterogeneity that prevent competitive equilibrium from being reached.
