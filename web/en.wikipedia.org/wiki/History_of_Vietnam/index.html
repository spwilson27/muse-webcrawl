History of Vietnam

This article covers the history of Vietnam from prehistoric times to the present.


Vietnam, stretching along the eastern coast of mainland Southeast Asia.

The history of Vietnam spans several millennia, from early Bronze Age cultures in the Red River Delta through a thousand years of Chinese imperial rule, a long era of independent dynasties, French colonisation, partition and war in the twentieth century, and the unified socialist republic of today. Vietnamese civilisation is traditionally traced to the legendary Hồng Bàng dynasty, while the earliest historically attested states include Văn Lang and Âu Lạc.

Prehistory and early kingdoms

Paleolithic and Neolithic settlements are known across the region, including the Hoabinhian culture. The Đông Sơn culture (c. 1000 BC – 1st century BC), famed for its bronze drums, flourished in the Red River valley. According to tradition, the Hùng kings ruled Văn Lang, after which An Dương Vương founded Âu Lạc and built the Cổ Loa Citadel. In 111 BC the Han dynasty conquered the region, beginning a period of Chinese domination that lasted, with interruptions, for over a thousand years.

Chinese domination and independence

Vietnamese resistance to Chinese rule produced famous revolts, including those of the Trưng Sisters (40–43 AD) and Lady Triệu (248 AD). In 938, Ngô Quyền defeated the Southern Han at the Battle of Bạch Đằng, restoring independence. Successive dynasties — Ngô, Đinh, Early Lê, Lý, Trần and Hồ — consolidated the state, known as Đại Việt. The Trần dynasty repelled three Mongol invasions in the thirteenth century under generals such as Trần Hưng Đạo.

The Lê dynasty and expansion

Lê Lợi's Lam Sơn uprising expelled the Ming in 1428, founding the Lê dynasty. Under Lê Thánh Tông the state expanded southward in the process known as Nam tiến ("southward advance"), absorbing Champa and reaching into Khmer territory. The sixteenth to eighteenth centuries saw the country divided between the Trịnh lords in the north and Nguyễn lords in the south. The Tây Sơn rebellion reunified the country, but in 1802 Nguyễn Phúc Ánh founded the Nguyễn dynasty and named the country Vietnam.

French colonisation

France conquered Vietnam piecemeal between 1858 and 1884, organising it with Cambodia and Laos into French Indochina, divided into Cochinchina, Annam and Tonkin. Colonial rule brought infrastructure and export agriculture but also heavy taxation and repression. Nationalist and communist movements grew in response; in 1930 Hồ Chí Minh founded the Indochinese Communist Party.

The Indochina Wars

After Japan's surrender in 1945, Hồ Chí Minh proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. War with France followed, ending with the French defeat at Điện Biên Phủ in 1954. The Geneva Accords partitioned Vietnam at the 17th parallel. The ensuing Vietnam War (1955–1975) pitted the communist North, supported by the Soviet Union and China, against the US-backed Republic of Vietnam in the South. The war ended with the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975.

Reunification and reform

Vietnam was formally reunified in 1976 as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam. After years of economic hardship, border wars with Cambodia and China, and a US embargo, the Communist Party launched Đổi Mới ("renovation") market reforms in 1986. Vietnam normalised relations with the United States in 1995, joined ASEAN the same year, and entered the WTO in 2007, becoming one of Asia's fastest-growing economies while remaining a one-party socialist state.
