Conservatism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conservatism is a cultural, social and political philosophy and ideology that seeks to promote and preserve traditional institutions, customs and values. The central tenets of conservatism may vary in relation to the culture and civilisation in which it appears, but in general, conservative movements seek to preserve a range of institutions such as organised religion, parliamentary government and property rights, emphasising continuity, stability and gradual change over rapid or revolutionary transformation.

History

Modern conservatism is dated from Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), which defended inherited institutions as repositories of accumulated wisdom against abstract rationalist redesign. In the nineteenth century, conservatives accommodated themselves by degrees to parliamentary government and (often) to market economics; in the twentieth, conservative parties became the principal centre-right force in most democracies, alternating in power with liberals and social democrats. The 1980s governments of Margaret Thatcher and Ronald Reagan fused conservatism with free-market economics, an alliance later strained by populist and national-conservative currents.

Variants

Varieties include liberal conservatism (market-friendly, socially moderate), national conservatism (emphasising national identity and sovereignty), social conservatism (traditional views on family and morality), fiscal conservatism (low taxes, restrained spending), one-nation or paternalistic conservatism, and Christian democratic parties in continental Europe, which blend conservative social views with welfare-state acceptance.

Ideas

Recurring conservative arguments include scepticism of utopian schemes, the value of prescription and experience, the fragility of social order, the importance of intermediary institutions between individual and state, and prudence as a governing virtue. Critics characterise conservatism as defence of privilege and hierarchy; its defenders reply that it is a disposition to preserve what works and reform carefully — in Michael Oakeshott's image, preferring the familiar to the unknown, the actual to the possible.
