Tree tapping

Tree tapping is the process of extracting sap or resin from a tree by making an incision or boring a hole into its trunk. The best-known application is the tapping of maple trees to collect sap for maple syrup, but tapping is also used for rubber (from the Pará rubber tree), pine resin, birch sap, palm sap and other products.

Maple tapping

In maple sugaring, a spout (spile) is inserted into a hole drilled into the trunk of a sugar maple or related species in late winter or early spring, when freeze–thaw cycles drive sap flow. Sap is collected in buckets or through plastic tubing systems, often under vacuum, and then boiled down to syrup — roughly 40 litres of sap yield one litre of syrup. Modern practice limits the number of taps per tree by diameter to avoid harming it.

Rubber tapping

Rubber tappers cut a thin spiral groove in the bark of Hevea brasiliensis, allowing latex to exude and run down into a collecting cup. Trees are typically tapped on alternate days for many years; careful tapping that does not damage the cambium is essential for the tree's productive life.

Other products

Pine tapping yields oleoresin, distilled into turpentine and rosin. Birch sap is tapped in northern Europe and Russia as a spring drink, and palm tapping produces sap used fresh or fermented, or boiled into palm sugar.
