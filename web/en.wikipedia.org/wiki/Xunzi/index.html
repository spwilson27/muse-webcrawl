Xunzi (c. 310 – c. 235 BCE), born Xun Kuang, was a Chinese Confucian philosopher of the late Warring States period, and one of the most systematic thinkers of early China. He taught at the Jixia Academy in Qi and served as a magistrate; his students included the Legalist theorist Han Fei and the Qin chancellor Li Si — an irony much noted, since Xunzi himself was a Confucian.

Teachings

Xunzi is famous for the thesis that "human nature is bad" (xìng è) — directly opposing Mencius, who held nature to be good. For Xunzi, people are born with desires that, left unchecked, produce conflict; goodness results from conscious activity (wei) — the rituals, education and social norms created by the sage kings that reshape nature as a warped board is straightened by a press-frame. He defended ritual (li) as the essential technology of civilisation, argued for the "rectification of names", and gave naturalistic explanations of Heaven (Tian) as impersonal nature rather than a moral judge.

Influence

The Xunzi is among the best-organised texts of early Chinese philosophy, written in argued essays rather than aphorisms. Although later Confucian orthodoxy canonised Mencius and sometimes marginalised Xunzi, his accounts of ritual, learning and language remained deeply influential.
