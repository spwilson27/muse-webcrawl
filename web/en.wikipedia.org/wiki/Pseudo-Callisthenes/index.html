Pseudo-Callisthenes is the conventional name given to the unknown author (or compilers) of the Alexander Romance, a legendary life of Alexander the Great written in Greek, probably in Alexandria in Egypt, with its surviving form usually dated to the 3rd century AD but incorporating material that had accumulated since shortly after Alexander's death. The work was falsely attributed to Callisthenes of Olynthus, Alexander's official historian and a nephew of Aristotle — hence the prefix "Pseudo-".

The Alexander Romance

The Romance relates Alexander's birth (in which the Egyptian pharaoh-sorcerer Nectanebo II fathers the conqueror on Olympias), his campaigns reimagined as wonders — battles against fantastical peoples, a descent to the ocean floor in a glass bell, an ascent to heaven in a basket borne by griffins, letters to Aristotle describing marvels in India — and his early death, framed by prophecies and lamentation. Unlike sober histories of Alexander, the Romance blends history, epistolary fiction, Jewish, Egyptian and Persian motifs and outright fantasy.

Transmission and influence

The Greek text survives in several recensions (α, β, γ and others) that differ considerably, complicating reconstruction of any "original". The Romance was translated into Latin (by Julius Valerius around AD 300, and later in the Historia de proeliis), Armenian, Syriac, Coptic, Ethiopic, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew and most European vernaculars — by some counts versions exist in more than eighty languages. It shaped the image of Alexander across the medieval world, from the Old French Roman d'Alexandre (which gave the alexandrine verse its name) to the Persian Iskandarnama and the Qur'anic figure often identified with Dhu al-Qarnayn.
