Kīlauea (Hawaiian: [kiːlɐwˈwɛə], "spewing" or "much spreading") is an active shield volcano in the Hawaiian Islands, on the southeastern flank of Mauna Loa on the island of Hawaiʻi. It is one of the world's most active volcanoes and the youngest of the five volcanoes that form the island, erupting almost continuously for much of the period since 1983. Kīlauea is the centrepiece of Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park and, in Native Hawaiian tradition, the home of the volcano goddess Pele.

Geological setting

Kīlauea is fed by the Hawaiʻi hotspot and is in the shield-building stage of Hawaiian volcanism. Its summit is a caldera about 3 by 5 kilometres across, containing the pit crater Halemaʻumaʻu, long occupied by a lava lake. Two rift zones radiate from the summit—the East Rift and the Southwest Rift—along which flank eruptions occur.

Eruptive history

The 1983–2018 eruption from Puʻu ʻŌʻō on the East Rift Zone was Kīlauea's longest modern eruption, adding hundreds of hectares of new land while destroying the communities of Kalapana and Royal Gardens. In 2008 a new summit vent opened in Halemaʻumaʻu, hosting a lava lake for a decade. In May 2018 the eruption shifted to the lower East Rift Zone: fissures in Leilani Estates destroyed more than 700 homes, and the summit caldera collapsed dramatically, deepening by hundreds of metres. Activity later returned to the summit, with a water lake in 2019–2020 followed by renewed lava-lake eruptions in 2020, 2021, and 2023, and episodic high fountaining episodes resuming in late December 2024.

Monitoring and hazards

The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, founded in 1912 on the caldera rim, monitors Kīlauea with networks of seismometers, GPS, gas sensors, and webcams. Principal hazards include lava flows, volcanic smog ("vog") rich in sulfur dioxide, explosive steam-driven eruptions, and ground collapse.
