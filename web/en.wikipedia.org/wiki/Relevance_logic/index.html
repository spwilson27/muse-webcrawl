Relevance logic

Relevance logic, also called relevant logic, is a kind of non-classical logic requiring the antecedent and consequent of implications to be relevantly related. They may be viewed as a family of substructural logics, or as modal logics modified to avoid the paradoxes of material implication and strict implication.

Motivation

In classical logic, a false proposition implies anything, and a true proposition is implied by anything. Relevance logicians regard such "paradoxes of implication" as showing that classical implication fails to capture the intuitive requirement that premises must be relevant to the conclusion they are used to prove. In relevance logic, a proof of A → B must actually use the assumption A, so the premises and conclusion share content — formally, they must share at least one propositional variable (the variable-sharing property).

History and systems

The field grew out of work by Wilhelm Ackermann in the 1920s and was developed systematically by Alan Ross Anderson and Nuel Belnap, whose system E (of entailment) and system R (of relevant implication) are the best-known relevance logics. Relevance logics are usually paraconsistent, allowing them to tolerate some contradictions without explosion.
