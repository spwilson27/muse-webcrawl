The Company of Scotland Trading to Africa and the Indies, usually called the Company of Scotland, was an overseas trading company created by an act of the Parliament of Scotland in 1695. It is chiefly remembered for the disastrous Darien scheme, an attempt to establish a Scottish colony in Panama, whose failure contributed to the union of Scotland and England in 1707.

Foundation

The company was promoted by the Scots financier William Paterson, a founder of the Bank of England. English political and commercial opposition, driven by the East India Company's interests, forced English and Dutch investors to withdraw, leaving the capital — some £400,000, a huge share of Scotland's available money — to be raised from Scots alone.

The Darien scheme

The company's plan was to found a colony, "Caledonia", on the Isthmus of Darien (Panama), creating an entrepôt for trade between the Atlantic and Pacific. The first expedition of five ships sailed in July 1698. The colony, New Edinburgh, was devastated by disease, poor supplies, and Spanish hostility, and was abandoned in 1699. A second expedition arrived to find the settlement deserted and was also forced to surrender to the Spanish in 1700. Around 2,000 colonists died.

Aftermath and union

The losses ruined many Scottish investors, from nobles to small burghs. Under the Acts of Union 1707, shareholders were compensated through the "Equivalent", a payment of £398,085 10s, and the Company of Scotland was dissolved.
