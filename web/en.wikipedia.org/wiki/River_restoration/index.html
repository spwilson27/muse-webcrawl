River restoration is the set of activities that aim to restore the natural state and functioning of a river system in support of biodiversity, recreation, flood management and landscape development. For much of the twentieth century rivers were straightened, deepened, dammed, culverted and confined between levees for navigation, drainage, hydropower and flood control; restoration seeks to reverse these modifications by giving rivers more space, reconnecting them to their floodplains, and reinstating natural processes of erosion, deposition and seasonal inundation.

Approaches

Common techniques include:

  • Re-meandering — restoring bends to a channelised reach to slow flow and diversify habitat.
  • Dam and weir removal or bypass — restoring fish passage and sediment transport; the removal of the Elwha River dams in Washington, completed in 2014, is among the largest such projects.
  • Daylighting — bringing culverted urban streams back to the surface, as with Seoul's Cheonggyecheon.
  • Floodplain reconnection — setting back or lowering levees so high flows can spread over wetlands, as in the Netherlands' "Room for the River" programme.
  • Riparian planting and buffer strips — stabilising banks, shading water and intercepting agricultural runoff.
  • Reintroduction of large wood — creating pools and cover for fish and invertebrates.

Examples

The Kissimmee River in Florida, channelised in the 1960s, has been partly re-meandered in one of the world's largest river restoration projects, restoring wetlands and wading-bird habitat. On the River Skerne in north-east England and the River Cole in Wiltshire, demonstration projects in the 1990s helped establish restoration practice in Britain. In the United States, thousands of small dams have been removed since 2000.

Evaluation

Although widely supported, river restoration has been criticised where projects are designed from aesthetic templates rather than catchment processes, or where monitoring is too brief to show ecological recovery. Scientists emphasise that restored reaches exist within modified catchments, so expectations for full recovery of historic conditions must be tempered; measurable benefits are most consistent for hydromorphology, riparian habitat and some fish communities.
