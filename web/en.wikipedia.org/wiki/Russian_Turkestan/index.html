Russian Turkestan (Russian: Русский Туркестан) was the western part of Turkestan within the Russian Empire's Central Asian territories, administered as the Governor-Generalship of Turkestan (Turkestansky krai) from 1867 to 1917. It was created after the Russian conquest of Central Asia in the mid-19th century and had its capital at Tashkent.

Conquest and administration

Between the 1860s and 1880s Russian forces under generals such as Mikhail Chernyayev and Mikhail Skobelev captured Tashkent (1865), Samarkand (1868), Khiva (1873), Kokand (1876), and the Turkmen stronghold of Geok Tepe (1881), and reached Merv (1884), bringing Russia's frontier to Persia and Afghanistan. The Emirate of Bukhara and the Khanate of Khiva survived as Russian protectorates, while the directly ruled territory was organised first under Konstantin von Kaufman, the first governor-general (1867–1882), who pursued a policy of "ignoring" Islam while integrating the region into the empire's economy, especially cotton production.

Administratively the krai was divided into oblasts — Syr-Darya, Samarkand, Fergana, Semirechye (after 1882 transferred from the Steppe krai), and the Transcaspian region — with Russian settlement towns beside the older Muslim cities, and railways (the Trans-Caspian and Orenburg–Tashkent lines) binding the region to European Russia.

End of rule

The Central Asian revolt of 1916, provoked by a decree conscripting local men for labour battalions, was bloodily suppressed. After the revolutions of 1917, Russian Turkestan disintegrated amid civil war and the Basmachi resistance; the Soviet authorities reorganised the territory, and in 1924 it was divided along national lines into the Uzbek, Turkmen, Tajik, Kyrgyz, and Kazakh republics — the ancestors of today's Central Asian states.
