Bride kidnapping, also known as marriage by abduction or marriage by capture, is a practice in which a man abducts the woman he wishes to marry. It has occurred in various cultures throughout history, including in parts of Central Asia, the Caucasus, Africa, and Southeast Asia, and in historical European and Mediterranean societies. In some places the practice survives as a symbolic ritual within consensual marriages; in others it remains a form of forced marriage condemned by international human rights law.

Forms

Anthropologists distinguish several forms. In ritualized or mock abduction, the "capture" is staged with the bride's consent, sometimes as part of elopement when families object or to avoid bride-price costs. In genuinely non-consensual abduction, the woman is seized — sometimes by strangers — and taken to the captor's home, where social pressure, confinement, or sexual violence is used to compel acceptance of the marriage, since leaving would bring stigma.

Regional practices

Central Asia

In Kyrgyzstan the practice is known as ala kachuu ("take and flee"). Although illegal, studies have estimated that a substantial share of ethnic Kyrgyz marriages historically began with abduction, ranging from consensual elopement to coercion. Kyrgyzstan increased penalties in 2013, and campaigns by women's groups have pushed for enforcement after widely publicized killings of abducted women.

The Caucasus and elsewhere

Abduction traditions were documented historically in Chechnya, Georgia, and Armenia, and among peoples of the North Caucasus. Similar practices have been reported among the Hmong in Southeast Asia (zij poj niam), in parts of Ethiopia, and in historical Rwanda. In imperial China, and among some Romani communities, mock capture appeared in wedding customs. Ancient Roman tradition attributed the founding of Rome partly to the abduction of the Sabine women.

Legal status and human rights

Bride kidnapping is prohibited under the laws of the countries where it persists and contravenes international instruments including the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women. It is classified as a form of forced marriage and, where it involves coercion or minors, is treated as a serious human rights violation. Enforcement, however, is often weak, and victims may be pressured by families and communities not to report.
