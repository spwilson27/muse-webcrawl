General Motors Chapter 11 reorganization

[Image placeholder: GM headquarters, Detroit]

The General Motors Chapter 11 reorganization of 2009 was one of the largest industrial bankruptcies in American history and a central episode of the 2008–2009 automotive industry crisis. On June 1, 2009, General Motors — then the largest U.S. automaker — filed for Chapter 11 protection in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York, listing about $82.3 billion in assets and $172.8 billion in debt.

Background

GM had lost money for years amid declining U.S. market share, high legacy pension and healthcare costs, and an overextended brand portfolio. The 2008 financial crisis and collapse in vehicle sales pushed it to the brink; from December 2008 the company survived on emergency loans from the U.S. Treasury under the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP). President Barack Obama's auto task force rejected GM's initial restructuring plan in March 2009, forced out CEO Rick Wagoner, and set a June 1 deadline for a viable plan.

The sale and the new GM

Under a court-supervised "363 sale", the viable assets of the old company were sold to a new entity, NGMCO Inc. — the "New GM" — backed by the U.S. Treasury (about 60.8% initial stake), the Canadian and Ontario governments, and the UAW's retiree healthcare trust (VEBA). The sale closed on July 10, 2009, just 40 days after filing. The old company was renamed Motors Liquidation Company and wound down. The restructured GM shed the Pontiac, Saturn, Hummer, and Saab brands, thousands of dealerships, and large amounts of debt; it returned to public markets with an IPO in November 2010, and the Treasury sold its last shares in December 2013.
