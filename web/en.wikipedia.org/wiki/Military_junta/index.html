Military junta

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A military junta is a government led by a committee of military leaders. The word junta comes from the Spanish for a council or board, used for the governing committees that seized power in Spain and Latin America from the nineteenth century. Twentieth-century juntas ruled, among others, Greece (1967–1974), Chile under Pinochet's junta (1973–1990), Argentina (1976–1983), Myanmar/Burma for long periods, Thailand repeatedly, and Nigeria under successive military councils.

Juntas typically justify seizure of power by claims of corruption, disorder or communist threat among civilian politicians, suspend constitutions and parties, rule by decree, and censor or repress opposition. Some have acted as “caretakers” promising return to civilian rule; others entrenched themselves for decades. Their internal politics often involve rivalry among services and factions, and their endings range from negotiated transitions (Chile's 1988 plebiscite) to collapse after military defeat (Argentina after the Falklands War, 1982) or economic failure.

Analysis

Political scientists distinguish juntas from single military dictatorships (one strongman in uniform) and from regimes where the military rules through a civilian façade. Military regimes have on average been shorter-lived than party-based or personalist dictatorships, partly because the officer corps can decide collectively to return to barracks.
