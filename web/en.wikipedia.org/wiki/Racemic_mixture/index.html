Racemic mixture

In chemistry, a racemic mixture, or racemate, is one that has equal amounts of left- and right-handed enantiomers of a chiral molecule or salt. A racemate is optically inactive: the two enantiomers rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions by equal amounts, so the net rotation is zero. Racemic mixtures are designated by the prefix (±)-, rac-, or by the symbol (RS) or (SR) for the pair of enantiomers.

History

The term derives from racemic acid, which Louis Pasteur obtained from grapes; racemus is Latin for "bunch of grapes". In 1848 Pasteur manually separated the mirror-image crystals of a racemic tartrate salt under a microscope, founding the study of stereochemistry. Racemic acid was notable as the only known substance of biological origin that was optically inactive, because fermentation produced both enantiomers.

Properties

A racemate may crystallise in several ways: as a racemic compound, in which both enantiomers occupy regular positions in the same crystal lattice; as a conglomerate, a mechanical mixture of crystals of each pure enantiomer; or as a solid solution. Racemic compounds often have melting points, solubilities and spectra different from those of the pure enantiomers, whereas a conglomerate behaves as a simple mixture.

Racemization and resolution

Racemization is the conversion of an optically active sample into a racemic mixture, for example by heat, light or acid/base catalysis. Separating a racemate into its enantiomers is called resolution and is important in the pharmaceutical industry, where the two enantiomers of a drug may have very different biological effects. Methods include crystallisation of diastereomeric salts, chiral chromatography, and enzymatic resolution.
