Land law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Land law is the body of law concerning rights to use, alienate or exclude others from land. In common-law systems it is built on the doctrine of estates — nobody “owns” land absolutely except the Crown (in England's theory); rather, persons hold estates in land, principally the fee simple (freehold) and the leasehold term of years — together with interests such as easements, covenants, mortgages, and trusts of land. The Law of Property Act 1925 in England and Wales reduced the legal estates to two and underpinned modern registered conveyancing.

Land law balances security of title against the needs of commerce and occupation: registration systems guarantee purchasers against hidden interests; landlord-and-tenant law regulates the largest temporary interest; planning law controls use in the public interest; and doctrines such as adverse possession (possession ripening into title) reward long, open occupation over paper owners who neglect their land. Indigenous land rights — recognised in Australia by Mabo (1992) and in various forms elsewhere — overlay settler-state land law with prior native title.
