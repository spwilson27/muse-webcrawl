COBOL

COBOL (COmmon Business-Oriented Language) is a compiled English-like computer programming language designed for business use. It is an imperative, procedural, and, since 2002, object-oriented language. COBOL is primarily used in business, finance, and administrative systems for companies and governments, and remains in heavy use on mainframe computers in banking, insurance, and government services.

History

COBOL was designed in 1959 by CODASYL, the Conference/Committee on Data Systems Languages, drawing on earlier work including Grace Hopper's FLOW-MATIC and on commercial languages such as COMTRAN. The U.S. Department of Defense sponsored its development and effectively mandated its availability on computers purchased by the government, driving widespread adoption during the 1960s. Successive standards were published in 1968, 1974, 1985, 2002, 2014, and 2023.

Features

COBOL syntax was intended to be self-documenting and readable by managers as well as programmers, using English-like sentences divided into four divisions: IDENTIFICATION, ENVIRONMENT, DATA, and PROCEDURE. Its strongest areas are exact decimal arithmetic and record-based file handling, which fit business data processing. A typical program begins famously with:

       IDENTIFICATION DIVISION.
       PROGRAM-ID. HELLO-WORLD.
       PROCEDURE DIVISION.
           DISPLAY "Hello, world!".
           STOP RUN.

Legacy and modern use

Estimates commonly cite hundreds of billions of lines of COBOL still in production, processing a large share of the world's daily banking and ATM transactions. COBOL systems figured prominently in the Year 2000 problem (Y2K) remediation effort. Shortages of COBOL programmers became news again during the COVID-19 pandemic, when U.S. state unemployment systems built on COBOL were overwhelmed. Modern implementations include IBM Enterprise COBOL and the open-source GnuCOBOL compiler.
