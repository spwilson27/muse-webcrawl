Belief–desire–intention software model

The belief–desire–intention (BDI) software model is a framework for programming intelligent agents, developed principally by Michael Bratman in philosophy and realised computationally by Anand Rao and Michael Georgeff at the Australian Artificial Intelligence Institute in the 1990s. It implements the folk-psychological theory of human practical reasoning that Bratman set out in his 1987 book Intention, Plans, and Practical Reason.

The three attitudes

  • Beliefs represent the agent's information about the world — facts it takes to be true, which may be incomplete or wrong.
  • Desires represent objectives or goals the agent would like to bring about; unlike beliefs they need not be consistent with one another.
  • Intentions represent the course of action the agent has committed itself to. Intentions constrain future deliberation and give an agent's behaviour persistence and stability.

Architecture

A BDI agent continually senses its environment, updates its beliefs, generates options (desires) consistent with its current beliefs and intentions, deliberates to select new intentions, and executes plans from a plan library to achieve them. This sense–deliberate–act cycle, formalised in the Procedural Reasoning System (PRS), the first working BDI implementation — originally used for fault diagnosis on NASA's space shuttle Discovery — balances deliberation against reaction: intentions are reconsidered only when circumstances justify it, avoiding both blind commitment and constant re-planning.

Implementations and applications

BDI ideas underpin agent platforms and languages such as PRS, dMARS, JACK, JADEX, Jason (an implementation of AgentSpeak) and SPARK. The model has been applied to air-traffic management, military simulation, robotics, business process management and computer games. Critics note the difficulty of the deliberation problem and the rise of learning-based approaches, but BDI remains the most influential architecture in symbolic agent-oriented programming.
