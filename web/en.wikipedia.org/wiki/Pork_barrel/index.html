Pork barrel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pork barrel
Type: Political metaphor / spending practice
Country of origin: United States
Related terms: Earmark, logrolling, patronage
Meaning: Government spending for localised projects benefiting a legislator's constituents
A barrel of salt pork, the origin of the metaphor: legislators "reaching into the pork barrel" for projects in their home districts.

Pork barrel, or simply pork, is a metaphor for the appropriation of government spending for localised projects secured solely or primarily to direct money to a representative's district or constituency and thereby gain political support. The phrase originated in the United States, where barrels of salt pork were a common food reserve, and came to describe legislators dipping into the public treasury for their districts.

Earmarks

The classic vehicle for pork in the U.S. Congress is the earmark — a provision in an appropriations bill directing funds to a specific project, such as a bridge, research centre, or military installation. Alaska's proposed Gravina Island Bridge, dubbed the "Bridge to Nowhere", became a prominent symbol in 2005. After criticism, Congress adopted a moratorium on earmarks in 2011; a reformed system of "community project funding" and "congressionally directed spending" returned in 2021 with disclosure requirements.

Arguments

Critics contend that pork distorts priorities, rewards seniority and committee membership, and wastes money on projects that could not survive merit-based allocation. Defenders respond that such spending is a small share of budgets, that legislators may know local needs better than agencies, that it lubricates compromise needed to pass major legislation, and that the power of the purse legitimately rests with elected representatives.

Other countries

Equivalent practices exist worldwide — for example "pork-barrelling" in Australian politics, where grants to marginal electorates have drawn scrutiny from the Australian National Audit Office, and constituency development funds in countries such as Kenya and India, which give legislators direct influence over local project spending.
