Complementarity (international law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Complementarity
Type: Principle of admissibility
Instrument: Rome Statute, Articles 1 and 17
Applies to: International Criminal Court
Effect: Cases inadmissible where genuinely investigated or prosecuted nationally
The seat of the International Criminal Court in The Hague, whose jurisdiction is complementary to national criminal jurisdictions.

Complementarity is the principle governing the relationship between the International Criminal Court (ICC) and national courts: the Court's jurisdiction is complementary to national criminal jurisdictions, so a case is inadmissible before the ICC where it is being — or has been — genuinely investigated or prosecuted by a state with jurisdiction over it. The principle is stated in the preamble and Article 1 of the Rome Statute and operationalised in Article 17.

Article 17 test

A case is inadmissible if (a) it is being investigated or prosecuted by a state with jurisdiction, (b) the state has investigated and decided not to prosecute, or (c) the person has already been tried for the same conduct — unless, in each case, the state is unwilling or unable genuinely to carry out the proceedings. Unwillingness is assessed by reference to shielding the accused, unjustified delay, or proceedings lacking independence or impartiality in a manner inconsistent with an intent to bring the person to justice. Inability covers the total or substantial collapse or unavailability of the national judicial system.

"Same person, same conduct"

In its case law — notably in the admissibility challenges brought by Kenya and Libya — the Court has held that national proceedings must concern the same person and substantially the same conduct alleged before the ICC. Libya's challenge succeeded for one accused (Abdullah Senussi, whose case was ruled inadmissible in 2013) and failed for another (Saif al-Islam Gaddafi), illustrating how fact-specific the assessment is.

Rationale and practice

Complementarity was the key compromise that made the Rome Statute acceptable to states protective of sovereignty: the ICC acts as a court of last resort, and states retain the primary duty to prosecute core crimes. Its proponents describe "positive complementarity" — encouraging and assisting genuine national proceedings — as the most effective route to ending impunity, given the ICC's small caseload.
