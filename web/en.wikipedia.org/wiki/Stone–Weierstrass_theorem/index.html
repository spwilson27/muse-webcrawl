Stone–Weierstrass theorem

In mathematical analysis, the Weierstrass approximation theorem states that every continuous function defined on a closed interval can be uniformly approximated as closely as desired by a polynomial function. Because polynomials are among the simplest functions, and because computers can directly evaluate polynomials, this theorem has both theoretical and practical relevance.

The Stone–Weierstrass theorem, due to Marshall H. Stone (1937), greatly generalises this result:

Let X be a compact Hausdorff space and A a subalgebra of C(X), the algebra of real-valued continuous functions on X, which contains a non-zero constant function and separates points. Then A is dense in C(X) in the uniform norm.

Background

Karl Weierstrass proved the original theorem in 1885. Stone's version applies not only to polynomial functions on an interval but to trigonometric polynomials on the circle, continuous functions on arbitrary compact spaces, and a complex-valued variant in which the subalgebra must also be closed under complex conjugation. The theorem is a cornerstone of functional analysis and underlies practical approximation in numerical analysis.

Applications

Consequences include the density of polynomial functions among continuous functions, approximation of periodic functions by trigonometric polynomials, and approximation results used in probability theory and signal processing. The theorem also yields proofs that certain function spaces are separable.
