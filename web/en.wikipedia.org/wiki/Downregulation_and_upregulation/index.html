Downregulation and upregulation

Downregulation (or desensitisation) is the process by which a cell decreases the number of its receptors to a given hormone or neurotransmitter, reducing its sensitivity to that molecule. Upregulation (or supersensitivity) is the converse: an increase in receptor number that makes the cell more sensitive. Both are forms of negative or positive feedback by which cells adapt to persistent stimulation or its absence.

Mechanisms

Downregulation typically involves receptor phosphorylation, internalisation by endocytosis, and either recycling to the membrane or degradation in lysosomes; gene transcription of the receptor may also fall. Upregulation occurs when signalling is chronically blocked or the ligand is scarce, prompting the cell to synthesise more receptors.

Examples

Classic examples include downregulation of insulin receptors in chronic hyperinsulinaemia (contributing to insulin resistance in type 2 diabetes), beta-adrenoceptor downregulation with sustained agonist exposure, and dopamine receptor upregulation following long-term antipsychotic blockade, implicated in tardive dyskinesia. In genomics, "upregulated" and "downregulated" also refer to increased or decreased gene expression generally.
