Hejaz

The Hejaz (Arabic: ٱلْحِجَاز, al-Ḥijāz, "the barrier") is a region in the western part of present-day Saudi Arabia, running along the Red Sea coast. It contains the holy cities of Islam, Mecca and Medina, as well as the port city of Jeddah and the city of Taif. The region's escarpment mountains separate the coastal plain (Tihamah) from the Najd plateau to the east.

History

The Hejaz was the cradle of Islam: Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570 CE, received the first revelations there, and migrated to Medina in 622 CE, the Hijrah that begins the Islamic calendar. The region was ruled successively from Damascus, Baghdad, Cairo, and — for four centuries after 1517 — Istanbul, with local authority exercised by the Sharifs of Mecca, Hashemite descendants of Muhammad.

In 1916, Sharif Hussein bin Ali launched the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire in alliance with Britain; his son Faisal's forces, advised by T. E. Lawrence, attacked the Hejaz railway and Ottoman garrisons. Hussein proclaimed the Kingdom of Hejaz, but in 1924–25 the region was conquered by Abdulaziz Ibn Saud of Nejd; the union formed the basis of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, proclaimed in 1932.

Geography and economy

The Hejaz mountains reach over 2,000 metres near Taif. The economy historically rested on the pilgrimage (Hajj and Umrah), which remains an enormous source of activity centred on Mecca, Medina, and Jeddah's port and airport. The Haramain high-speed railway, opened in 2018, links the holy cities with Jeddah and King Abdullah Economic City.
