A Carnegie library is a library built with money donated by the Scottish-American industrialist and philanthropist Andrew Carnegie. Between 1883 and 1929 a total of 2,509 Carnegie libraries were built around the world, including 1,795 in the United States, 660 in Britain and Ireland, and others in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the Caribbean and elsewhere.

The Carnegie formula

Carnegie's grants followed a standard model, later called the "Carnegie formula": the community had to demonstrate the need for a library, provide a building site, agree to fund staff and maintenance from public taxation at not less than ten per cent of the grant per year, and provide free service to all. The requirement that towns tax themselves for libraries helped establish public library provision on a permanent footing. Early grants went to places connected with Carnegie, beginning with Dunfermline, his birthplace in Scotland (1883), and Braddock, Pennsylvania, site of one of his steelworks (1889).

Architecture

Carnegie libraries were usually built to designs chosen locally, but guidance from the Carnegie Corporation encouraged a recognisable pattern: a raised main floor reached by a flight of steps (symbolising elevation through learning), a basement level, and lamp standards flanking the entrance representing enlightenment. Many were built in classical revival, Beaux-Arts, Italianate or, in Britain and Ireland, Arts and Crafts styles. Hundreds remain in use as libraries; others survive as museums, offices and community centres, and many are listed historic buildings.

Legacy

The Carnegie programme transformed library provision in the English-speaking world, seeding public library systems in thousands of communities. It has also been reassessed critically, both for the paternalism of its conditions and for its role in segregated library provision in the American South.
