Dawit II (Ge'ez: ዳዊት, c. 1496 – 2 September 1540), also known by his throne name Lebna Dengel ("Incense of the Virgin"), was Emperor of Ethiopia from 1508 until his death. A member of the Solomonic dynasty, he is remembered for his embassy exchanges with Portugal and for his doomed resistance to the invasion of Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, which nearly destroyed Christian Ethiopia.

Early reign

Dawit came to the throne as a boy of about twelve after the death of his father Na'od in battle, and for some years the empire was effectively governed by his grandmother, the formidable regent Eleni, a convert from Islam and the power behind the throne for a generation. Eleni conducted Ethiopia's first diplomacy with Portugal, dispatching the Armenian Matthew as ambassador in 1509 to seek an alliance against the Muslim powers of the Red Sea. In 1517 Dawit won a major victory over the Adal Sultanate at the Battle of Zeila, killing its sultan and capturing its capital — a triumph that proved short-lived.

Portuguese contacts

In 1520 a Portuguese embassy under Rodrigo de Lima reached Dawit's court, accompanied by the chaplain Francisco Álvares, whose account The Prester John of the Indies gave Europe its first detailed picture of Ethiopia. Dawit received the embassy courteously, and an Ethiopian mission under Saga za Ab visited Lisbon and Rome, but the young emperor — confident after his victory over Adal, and wary of foreign entanglement — allowed the proposed alliance to lapse. Historians often contrast his coolness with Eleni's urgency.

War with Ahmad Gran

In 1529 the imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ("Ahmad Gran", the left-handed) launched a jihad from Adal that overwhelmed Ethiopia. Dawit's armies were shattered at Shimbra Kure (1529) and Antukya, and the invaders burned churches and monasteries across the highlands, including Debre Libanos; by the mid-1530s most of the empire was under Muslim occupation. Driven from province to province, Dawit appealed desperately to Portugal. He died on 2 September 1540 at Debre Damo, still a fugitive in his own kingdom, before the help he had summoned arrived.

His son and successor Gelawdewos, aided by a small Portuguese expedition of musketeers under Cristóvão da Gama, finally defeated and killed Ahmad at the Battle of Wayna Daga in 1543, saving the remnant of the Christian empire.

Legacy

Dawit II presided over both the height of Solomonic court culture — royal chronicles praise his learning, horsemanship and patronage — and the greatest catastrophe in medieval Ethiopian history. His reign also saw the production of the Fetha Negest legal tradition's consolidation and the royal chronicle that preserves his campaigns in detail.
