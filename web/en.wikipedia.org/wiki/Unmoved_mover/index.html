Unmoved mover

The unmoved mover as the outermost cause of celestial motion

The unmoved mover (Greek: ὃ οὐ κινούμενον κινεῖ, ho ou kinoumenon kinei, lit. "that which moves without being moved") or prime mover (Latin: primum movens) is a concept advanced by Aristotle as a primary cause (or "first uncaused cause") or "mover" of all the motion in the universe. As is implicit in the name, the unmoved mover moves other things, but is not itself moved by any prior action. In Book 12 (Λ) of his Metaphysics, Aristotle describes the unmoved mover as being perfectly beautiful, indivisible, and contemplating only self-contemplation, and he equates this being with God.

First philosophy

Aristotle argues, in Book 8 of the Physics and Book 12 of the Metaphysics, that there must be an immortal, unchanging being ultimately responsible for all wholeness and orderliness in the sensible world. Aristotle believed that motion is eternal: it had no beginning and will have no end, because a first motion would itself require a cause. However, an infinite regress of moved movers is impossible, so there must exist a first mover that is itself unmoved — a being that initiates motion without being moved by anything else.

Unlike Plato's demiurge, which fashions the cosmos, Aristotle's unmoved mover does not create the world or intervene in it. It moves as a final cause: it is the object of desire and thought that the heavens imitate. The outermost sphere of the fixed stars rotates because it "desires" the perfection of the unmoved mover, and this rotation drives all subordinate celestial and terrestrial motion.

Nature of the prime mover

Aristotle characterizes the unmoved mover as pure actuality (energeia) without any potentiality, since anything with potentiality could change and would require a prior cause to actualize it. It is immaterial, eternal, indivisible, and without magnitude. Its activity is noesis noeseos — "thinking of thinking" — a self-contemplation that Aristotle identifies as the highest and most pleasant activity, the life of God. Because it thinks only itself, the prime mover does not know particular facts about the changing world.

Spheres and subordinate movers

In Aristotle's cosmology, each celestial sphere — moved by the planetary motions described by Eudoxus and Callippus, whose systems Aristotle expanded to some 47 or 55 spheres — has its own unmoved mover. Above all of them stands the prime mover of the outermost sphere, the primum mobile, whose motion is the uniform daily rotation of the heavens. Aristotle concludes Book 12 with a quotation from Homer: "The rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler."

Influence

The concept profoundly influenced medieval philosophy and theology. Islamic philosophers such as al-Farabi and Avicenna developed it in their proofs for God's existence, and Maimonides adapted it in Jewish philosophy. Thomas Aquinas incorporated the unmoved mover into his "Five Ways", identifying Aristotle's prime mover with the God of Christianity, although Aquinas's God, unlike Aristotle's, creates and sustains the universe and knows and wills particulars.
