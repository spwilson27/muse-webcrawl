Battle of Cannae

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of Hannibal's double envelopment at Cannae, showing the closing pocket]

The Battle of Cannae was fought on 2 August 216 BC near the ancient village of Cannae in Apulia, south-east Italy, between the Carthaginian army of Hannibal and a larger Roman army under the consuls Lucius Aemilius Paullus and Gaius Terentius Varro. It is regarded as one of the greatest tactical masterpieces in military history: by a planned double envelopment — his centre giving ground while his infantry wings and cavalry closed around the Roman mass — Hannibal annihilated the Roman army, killing some 50,000–70,000 men (including Paullus and much of Rome's senatorial class) in an afternoon. 'Cannae' has been a byword for the perfect battle of annihilation ever since.

The envelopment

Hannibal drew up his weaker Iberian and Gallic infantry in a convex crescent that bent backward under Roman pressure, drawing the dense Roman legions into a pocket; his veteran African infantry on the flanks then turned inward, while Hasdrubal's cavalry, having routed both Roman cavalry wings, closed the rear. Packed so tightly they could barely wield their swords, the Romans were cut down until evening. Roman losses in Polybius' account approach 70,000 dead; Livy gives about 48,000. Carthaginian losses were some 6,000–8,000, mostly Gauls of the sacrificed centre.

Aftermath

Southern Italy revolted to Hannibal — Capua above all — but Rome, refusing battle and terms alike (the Senate would not even ransom the Cannae prisoners), ground on. Cannae became the supreme case study of envelopment: Schlieffen's plan of 1914 was consciously modelled on it, and staff colleges still teach it. For Hannibal it was the summit and the paradox of his career: he could destroy Rome's armies but not Rome's alliance system or will.
