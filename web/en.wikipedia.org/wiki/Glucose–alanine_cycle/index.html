Glucose–alanine cycle

The glucose–alanine cycle – also known simply as the alanine cycle or Cahill cycle – is the series of reactions in which amino groups and carbons from muscle are transported to the liver. It is quite similar to the Cori cycle in the cycling of nutrients between skeletal muscle and the liver. When muscles degrade amino acids for energy needs, the resulting nitrogen is transaminated to pyruvate to form alanine.

Alanine is released into the bloodstream and taken up by the liver, where the enzyme alanine aminotransferase converts it back to pyruvate. The liver uses the pyruvate for gluconeogenesis, returning glucose to the blood for use by muscle, while the nitrogen enters the urea cycle for disposal as urea.

The cycle, described by George Cahill and colleagues, allows muscle to offload toxic ammonia in a non-toxic form and links protein catabolism during fasting or exercise with hepatic glucose production.
