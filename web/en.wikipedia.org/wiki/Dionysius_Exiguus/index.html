Dionysius Exiguus (Latin for "Dionysius the Humble"; c. 470 – c. 544 AD) was a monk from Scythia Minor living in Rome who is best known as the inventor of the Anno Domini (AD) era, which counts years from the birth of Jesus Christ and has become the dominant system of year numbering in the world. He devised it in 525 AD while constructing tables for calculating the date of Easter.

Life

Little is known of Dionysius's biography. Born in Scythia Minor (modern Dobruja, on the Black Sea coast of Romania and Bulgaria), he came to Rome around the year 500 and joined a monastery, becoming known as a learned translator of Greek ecclesiastical texts into Latin and a canon lawyer who compiled collections of church canons. His epithet Exiguus ("small" or "humble") was self-applied, a gesture of monastic modesty. Cassiodorus praised his learning and his command of both Greek and Latin.

The Easter tables and Anno Domini

The existing tables for computing Easter, used at Rome, counted years by the era of Diocletian, the persecutor of Christians. In 525 Dionysius prepared a continuation of the Alexandrian Easter tables for the next 95 years. Unwilling to perpetuate the memory of a tyrant, he instead numbered the years ab incarnatione Domini nostri Jesu Christi — "from the incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ" — thereby creating the AD system. His tables, with their explanatory preface, were adopted gradually; the Venerable Bede's use of AD dating in his Ecclesiastical History (731) did much to spread the system through Western Europe.

How accurate was the calculation?

Dionysius placed the birth of Jesus in the year he designated AD 1 (there is no year zero in his scheme, since Roman numerals lacked the concept). Modern scholarship generally concludes he erred by several years: King Herod the Great, who in the Gospel narrative sought to kill the infant Jesus, died in 4 BC, so Jesus was most likely born between about 7 and 4 BC. Dionysius's identification of the relevant consular year and lunar cycles was nevertheless a remarkable synthesis of the available chronological data. He is also credited with popularising the practice of numbering years BC ("before Christ") in reverse, though that convention was systematised by later writers such as Bede.
