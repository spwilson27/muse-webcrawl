The Ashikaga shogunate (足利幕府, Ashikaga bakufu), also known as the Muromachi shogunate, was the feudal military government of Japan during the Muromachi period from 1336 to 1573. It was founded by Ashikaga Takauji, who, after defeating the forces of Emperor Go-Daigo's Kenmu Restoration, was appointed shōgun in 1338 and established his government in the Muromachi district of Kyoto. The shogunate is noted both for its political weakness and fragmentation and for a brilliant flowering of Japanese culture.

History

For its first decades the regime was challenged by the rival Southern Court of Go-Daigo's line, in the Nanboku-chō (Northern and Southern Courts) conflict, until the third shogun, Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, reunified the imperial courts in 1392. Yoshimitsu consolidated shogunal authority, opened formal trade with Ming China (the tally trade), and patronised the elegant Kitayama culture symbolised by his Golden Pavilion, the Kinkaku-ji. Under the eighth shogun, Ashikaga Yoshimasa, the more austere Higashiyama culture flourished — tea ceremony, Noh theatre, ink painting and rock gardens — but his indecisive succession arrangements helped trigger the devastating Ōnin War (1467–1477).

The Ōnin War devastated Kyoto and destroyed the shogunate's effective authority, ushering in the Sengoku period of warring regional daimyō. Later Ashikaga shoguns were puppets of their deputies, the Hosokawa and Miyoshi clans. The fifteenth and last shogun, Ashikaga Yoshiaki, was installed in 1568 with the backing of the warlord Oda Nobunaga, but after turning against his patron he was driven from Kyoto in 1573, an event conventionally taken as the end of the shogunate.

Government

Unlike the Kamakura shogunate, which ruled from eastern Japan, the Ashikaga governed from Kyoto, within the imperial capital, and their authority depended on a balance with powerful provincial constables (shugo) who evolved into territorial daimyō. The shogun's administration included the kanrei (deputy), the samurai-dokoro and boards of finance and documents, but it never achieved the centralised control of its predecessors or of the later Tokugawa shogunate.
