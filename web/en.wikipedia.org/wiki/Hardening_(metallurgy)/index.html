Hardening (metallurgy)

Hardening
FieldMetallurgy, materials science
PurposeIncrease hardness and strength of metals
Common methodsQuenching, case hardening, precipitation hardening, work hardening

Hardening is a metallurgical metalworking process used to increase the hardness of a metal. The hardness of a metal is directly proportional to the uniaxial yield stress at the location of the imposed strain. A harder metal will have a higher resistance to plastic deformation than a less hard metal.

Processes

The five hardening processes are the Hall–Petch method, work hardening, solid solution strengthening, precipitation hardening, and martensitic transformation. Each method increases hardness by introducing obstacles to dislocation motion within the crystal structure of the material.

Quench hardening

In quench hardening, a metal such as steel is heated to a high temperature, typically above its upper critical temperature, and then rapidly cooled in a quenching medium such as water, oil, or air. The rapid cooling traps carbon atoms within the iron crystal lattice, forming a hard, brittle phase called martensite. The hardened part is often subsequently tempered to reduce brittleness while retaining much of the hardness.

Case hardening

Case hardening produces a hard, wear-resistant surface layer (the "case") over a softer, tougher core. Techniques include carburizing, in which carbon is diffused into the surface of a low-carbon steel at high temperature, and nitriding, in which nitrogen is diffused into the surface. Gear teeth, camshafts, and bearings are frequently case hardened.

Work hardening

Work hardening, also known as strain hardening, increases the hardness and strength of a ductile metal through plastic deformation, such as rolling, hammering, or drawing. The deformation increases the density of dislocations, which impede one another's movement and make further deformation more difficult.
