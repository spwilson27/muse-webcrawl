Neurogenic claudication

Neurogenic claudication, also known as pseudoclaudication, is a common symptom of lumbar spinal stenosis, or inflammation of the nerves emanating from the spinal cord. Neurogenic means that the problem originates within the nervous system. Claudication, from the Latin for "to limp", refers to painful cramping or weakness in the legs. It should not be confused with vascular claudication, which stems from inadequate blood flow.

Lumbar spinal stenosis is the usual cause of neurogenic claudication.

Signs and symptoms

Neurogenic claudication commonly describes pain, paresthesia, and cramping in one or both lower extremities, brought on by walking or prolonged standing and relieved by sitting or bending forward (as when leaning on a shopping cart, the "shopping cart sign"). Flexion opens the spinal canal and neural foramina, whereas lumbar extension narrows them and provokes symptoms. Unlike vascular claudication, symptoms are not reliably relieved simply by stopping walking while remaining standing, and pedal pulses are normal.

Causes

The usual cause is degenerative narrowing of the lumbar spinal canal from a combination of facet joint hypertrophy, thickening of the ligamentum flavum, and disc bulging, which compresses the cauda equina. Symptoms are thought to arise from ischemia and venous congestion of the nerve roots during activity.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on the history and physical examination, confirmed by MRI showing canal narrowing. Initial management is conservative: physical therapy emphasizing flexion-based exercise, weight management, epidural steroid injections, and analgesia. Severe or progressive cases may be treated with surgical decompression, such as laminectomy.
