Right-to-work law

A right-to-work law is a statute in the United States that prohibits union security agreements between employers and labor unions — in particular, it forbids requiring employees to join a union or pay union fees as a condition of employment. Supporters argue the laws protect workers' freedom of association; opponents argue they allow "free riding" on union-negotiated benefits and weaken collective bargaining.

Legal basis

The Labor Management Relations Act of 1947 (the Taft–Hartley Act), section 14(b), expressly permits states to outlaw union security agreements. An earlier system under the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 had permitted "closed shops" and "union shops"; Taft–Hartley banned closed shops outright and let states go further. In 2018, Janus v. AFSCME extended right-to-work principles to all public-sector employment nationwide on First Amendment grounds.

Spread and debate

As of the mid-2020s, a majority of U.S. states — concentrated in the South, Midwest, and Mountain West — have right-to-work laws; Michigan repealed its law in 2023 (effective 2024), the first repeal in nearly six decades. Empirical studies disagree on effects on wages and union density; union membership is consistently lower in right-to-work states.
