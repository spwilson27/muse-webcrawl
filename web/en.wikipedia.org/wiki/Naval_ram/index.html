Naval ram

A naval ram is a weapon fitted to the bow of a warship, used to strike and puncture the hull of an enemy vessel. Ramming was the principal offensive tactic of oared warships in antiquity and was revived, in altered form, in the steam warships of the nineteenth century.

Ancient rams

The ram emerged in the Mediterranean by the 8th century BCE, appearing in Phoenician and Greek art. Mounted at the waterline on the bow of galleys such as the trireme, it was typically cast in bronze over a reinforced wooden structure — the massive Athlit ram, recovered from the sea off Israel, is the finest surviving example. Tactics such as the diekplous (breakthrough) and periplous (outflanking) aimed to shear off an enemy's oars or hole its hull below the waterline. Rams decided battles from Salamis (480 BCE) to Actium (31 BCE). As ships grew heavier and boarding tactics dominated, the ram declined, though the above-water spur survived into medieval galley warfare.

Nineteenth-century revival

The success of ramming in the American Civil War — notably CSS Virginia at Hampton Roads in 1862 — and the Austrian victory at the Battle of Lissa in 1866, where Ferdinand Max sank the Italian flagship Re d'Italia by ramming, convinced navies that steam warships needed ram bows. For several decades nearly all capital ships were built with prominent underwater rams, despite the growing range and power of naval guns making deliberate ramming nearly impossible against a manoeuvring enemy. Rams proved more dangerous to friends than foes: in 1893 HMS Victoria was accidentally rammed and sunk by HMS Camperdown with heavy loss of life.

Decline

By the First World War the ram bow had disappeared from new designs, though ramming remained an occasional tactic against surfaced submarines and in desperate actions, such as HMS Dreadnought's sinking of U-29 in 1915. Modern bow shapes — notably the bulbous bow — are hydrodynamic devices, not weapons.
