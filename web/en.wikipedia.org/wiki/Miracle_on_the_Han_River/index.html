Miracle on the Han River

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Miracle on the Han River (Korean: 한강의 기적) refers to the period of rapid economic growth in South Korea following the Korean War (1950–1953), during which the country transformed from one of the poorest nations in the world into a developed, high-income economy. The name invokes the Han River, which flows through the capital, Seoul, and echoes the German "Miracle on the Rhine" (Wirtschaftswunder).

Background

At the end of the Korean War, South Korea's infrastructure lay in ruins and its per-capita income was comparable to that of the poorest countries in Africa and Asia. The economy depended heavily on American aid. From the early 1960s, under President Park Chung-hee, the government pursued export-oriented industrialisation through a series of Five-Year Economic Plans, beginning in 1962.

Growth

The state directed credit to chosen industries, promoted large family-controlled conglomerates known as chaebol (such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG), and invested heavily in education. Growth averaged nearly 10 percent a year for three decades, first in light manufactures such as textiles and footwear, then — through the Heavy and Chemical Industry drive of the 1970s — in steel, shipbuilding, automobiles and electronics. Per-capita income rose from around $100 in the early 1960s to more than $10,000 by the mid-1990s. South Korea joined the OECD in 1996, and Seoul hosted the 1988 Summer Olympics, often seen as the miracle's coming-out party.

Crises and legacy

The 1997 Asian financial crisis forced an IMF bailout and major reforms, but the economy recovered quickly and South Korea went on to become a world leader in semiconductors, displays, shipbuilding and popular culture. Critics note the miracle's costs: authoritarian labour repression, environmental damage, chaebol dominance and one of the world's lowest fertility rates. The phrase remains central to South Korea's national narrative.
