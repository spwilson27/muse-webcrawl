A wheelset is a pair of railroad vehicle wheels rigidly mounted on a common axle, so that both wheels rotate together. Wheelsets are the fundamental interface between a train and the track: they support the vehicle's weight, guide it along the rails, and — on driven axles — transmit traction and braking forces.

Design

The wheels are typically steel, either monobloc or fitted with replaceable tyres, and are press-fitted or shrunk onto the axle. Each wheel tread is slightly coned (typically 1 in 20), a geometry that centres the wheelset between the rails and, because the effective rolling radius differs on the two sides in a curve, allows the rigid axle to negotiate curves without one wheel slipping excessively. A flange on the inner face of each wheel provides guidance against the rail head, especially through switches and crossings.

Because both wheels are fixed to the axle, the wheelset exhibits a characteristic sinusoidal lateral oscillation at speed called hunting, which suspension design and wheel-profile maintenance must control. Wheelsets carry axle boxes with roller bearings at their journals, transmit load to the bogie frame through the primary suspension, and may carry brake discs, gear drives, and (on axles of multiple units) traction motors.

Maintenance and failure

Wheel profiles are restored by turning on underfloor or portal lathes; wear, rolling-contact fatigue, thermal damage from tread brakes, and (rarely) axle fatigue cracking are monitored by ultrasonic inspection. Catastrophic axle failures — notably the 1998 Eschede derailment in Germany, caused by a fractured resilient-wheel tyre — have strongly influenced modern inspection regimes and the shift toward monobloc wheels.
