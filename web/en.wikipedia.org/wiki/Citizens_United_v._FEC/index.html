Citizens United v. FEC

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), is a landmark decision of the US Supreme Court holding that the First Amendment prohibits the government from restricting independent expenditures for political campaigns by corporations, including non-profit corporations, labour unions and other associations. The 5–4 decision (Kennedy J.) held that political speech cannot be suppressed on the basis of the speaker's corporate identity, overruling Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce (1990) and part of McConnell v. FEC (2003), which had upheld the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act's ban on “electioneering communications” (the case arose over a pay-per-view film critical of Hillary Clinton in the 2008 primary season).

The decision (with the D.C. Circuit's SpeechNow ruling) enabled super PACs — committees raising unlimited sums for independent spending — transforming American campaign finance, while direct corporate contributions to candidates remain banned and disclosure rules were upheld 8–1 in the same judgment. Justice Stevens's dissent warned that corporations' “vast pools of Treasury funds” would drown out ordinary citizens; the majority answered that the remedy for speech is more speech, not censorship. Citizens United became the most politically salient campaign-finance decision of its era — a staple of reform platforms promising constitutional amendment — and its reasoning frames continuing litigation over money in politics. Its holding is settled law; its evaluation remains one of the sharpest divides in American public law.
