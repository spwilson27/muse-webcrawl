In mathematics, the Riemann sphere, named after mathematician Bernhard Riemann, is a model of the extended complex plane (also called the closed complex plane): the complex plane plus one point at infinity. This extended plane represents the extended complex numbers, that is, the complex numbers plus a value ∞ for infinity. With the Riemann model, the point ∞ is near very large numbers, much like the point 0 is near very small numbers.

Extended complex numbers

The extended complex numbers consist of the complex numbers C together with ∞. Addition and multiplication extend by the usual rules ∞ + z = ∞ for z ≠ ∞, z · ∞ = ∞ for z ≠ 0, and z / 0 = ∞, z / ∞ = 0 for z ≠ 0, while expressions such as ∞ − ∞ and 0 · ∞ remain undefined. The extended complex numbers make many expressions in complex analysis more natural: non-constant polynomials have roots counting multiplicity when ∞ is handled properly, and meromorphic functions on C become holomorphic maps into the sphere.

As a complex manifold

As a one-dimensional complex manifold, the Riemann sphere can be described by two charts, both with domain C: the identity chart and the reciprocal chart z ↦ 1/z. The transition map between them is holomorphic, so the sphere is a Riemann surface. It is the unique compact, connected Riemann surface of genus zero, and it is naturally identified with the complex projective line CP1. Holomorphic maps between Riemann spheres are exactly the rational functions, and the automorphisms of the sphere are the Möbius transformations z ↦ (az + b) / (cz + d).

As a sphere

Geometrically, the Riemann sphere is the unit sphere in R3, identified with the extended plane by stereographic projection: each point of the plane is projected onto the sphere along the line joining it to the north pole, and the north pole itself corresponds to the point at infinity. Under this correspondence, circles and lines in the plane map to circles on the sphere, and angles are preserved. With the chordal (spherical) metric, distances on the sphere give a natural way of measuring separation between extended complex numbers.

Applications

The Riemann sphere is central to complex analysis and algebraic geometry, and it appears in other areas of mathematics and physics: in quantum mechanics the state space of a two-level system (a qubit) is pictured as the Bloch sphere, an instance of the same construction; in relativity, the celestial sphere of an observer is naturally a Riemann sphere on which Lorentz transformations act as Möbius transformations.
