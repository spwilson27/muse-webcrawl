Insular cortex

The insular cortex (also called the insula, insulary cortex, or Island of Reil) is a portion of the cerebral cortex folded deep within the lateral sulcus, the fissure separating the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes. It is covered by parts of those lobes called opercula and is sometimes described as the fifth lobe of the brain. The insula is involved in interoception — the sense of the body's internal state — as well as taste, emotion, empathy, autonomic control and aspects of self-awareness.

Anatomy

The insular cortex is divided by the central insular sulcus into a larger anterior part and a smaller posterior part. The anterior insula is composed of several short gyri, while the posterior insula contains long gyri. It receives projections from the ventromedial nucleus of the thalamus and from the amygdala, and is richly interconnected with the frontal, parietal and temporal lobes, the cingulate cortex and the basal ganglia. Functionally, a gradient runs from the posterior insula, which encodes primary interoceptive signals, to the anterior insula, where these are integrated with emotional and cognitive information.

Functions

The insula is the primary cortical area for taste and a key region for interoceptive awareness, including heartbeat perception, hunger, thirst and visceral sensations. The anterior insula, together with the anterior cingulate cortex, forms a core node of the salience network, which detects personally relevant stimuli and switches between brain networks. It is implicated in empathy and social emotions, disgust, craving and addiction, pain perception, speech articulation and autonomic regulation of heart rate and blood pressure.

Clinical significance

Insular damage from stroke can cause disorders of taste, autonomic dysfunction, neglect-like symptoms and loss of craving in addiction — famously, smokers who suffer insular lesions have been reported to quit suddenly and easily. Insular seizures can mimic visceral or gustatory symptoms. Abnormal insular activity has been reported in anxiety disorders, depression, schizophrenia and eating disorders, and the insula's role in interoception makes it a focus of research on somatic symptom disorders.
