Dominicans (Spanish: Dominicanos) are the citizens and nationals of the Dominican Republic, a Caribbean country occupying the eastern two-thirds of the island of Hispaniola. Dominican identity is rooted in a mixed heritage of Spanish, African and indigenous Taíno ancestry, shaped by the country's colonial history, its wars of independence from Haiti and Spain, and a large modern diaspora.

History and heritage

Santo Domingo, founded in 1496, was the first permanent European settlement in the Americas. The population descends from Spanish colonists, enslaved Africans brought to work sugar plantations, and the Taíno, whose genetic and cultural legacy persists despite catastrophic early decline. The republic declared independence from Haiti in 1844 under Juan Pablo Duarte, one of the Padres de la Patria.

Culture

Spanish is the national language. Dominican culture is famous for merengue and bachata music and dance, for baseball — the country's passion and a route to Major League careers — and for cuisine built on rice, beans and plantains, as in la bandera ("the flag"). Carnival, especially in La Vega, is a major festival.

Diaspora

Emigration accelerated after the 1960s; well over a million Dominicans and their descendants live in the United States, concentrated in New York City, and large communities exist in Spain and Puerto Rico. Remittances from the diaspora are a major pillar of the economy.
