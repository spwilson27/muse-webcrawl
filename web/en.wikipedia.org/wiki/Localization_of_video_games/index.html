Localization of video games

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Video game localization (or localisation) is the process of adapting a video game for release in a different country or language market. It goes beyond simple translation of text: dialogue, graphics, packaging, manuals, audio, and sometimes gameplay itself are modified to suit the language, culture, laws, and technical standards of the target region. Localization is a standard stage in the production of most major commercial games, and games are commonly localized into the so-called "FIGS" languages (French, Italian, German, Spanish) plus Japanese, Chinese, Korean, Portuguese, and Russian, among others.

History

In the early decades of the industry, localization was often minimal or nonexistent. Games imported from Japan to North America and Europe in the 1980s were sometimes released with garbled "Engrish" text produced by non-native speakers under tight deadlines; the arcade game Zero Wing (1989) later became famous for its mistranslated opening line "All your base are belong to us". As production budgets grew through the 1990s and 2000s, publishers built dedicated localization departments and specialist outsourcing companies, and simultaneous worldwide releases ("sim-ship") became the norm for major titles.

Process

Localization typically involves several stages. Internationalization prepares the software so text can be extracted and replaced without reprogramming. Translators then work from string files, often with context notes, glossaries, and style guides. Culturalization adapts content that might confuse or offend the target audience: gestures, symbols, jokes, religious imagery, and depictions of alcohol, violence, or historical events. Germany's strict rules on Nazi symbolism, for example, long required games sold there to remove swastikas, leading the Wolfenstein series to be heavily altered or withheld; China requires government approval and routinely mandates changes to depictions of skeletons, blood, and maps.

Audio localization ranges from subtitling the original voice track to full re-recording ("dubbing") with local actors, which is standard for major releases in markets such as France, Germany, Spain, Brazil, and Japan. Technical constraints also matter: text expansion (German and Russian strings are often much longer than English), fonts for character sets such as Chinese and Arabic, right-to-left text layout, and regional hardware or rating requirements (ESRB, PEGI, CERO, USK) all shape the final product.

Notable examples

Nintendo's Treehouse division in North America is noted for extensive cultural adaptation of series such as Pokémon, Fire Emblem, and Animal Crossing, including renaming characters and rewriting jokes. The Final Fantasy series, localized in its early years by Ted Woolsey, is frequently cited for expressive English scripts produced under severe character limits. Conversely, "censorship" changes—such as altered costumes in some Japanese role-playing games released in the West—regularly provoke debate among fans about fidelity versus marketability.
