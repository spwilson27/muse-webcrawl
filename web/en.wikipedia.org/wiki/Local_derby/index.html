Local derby

A local derby is a sporting fixture between two rival clubs from the same town or city, or from geographically close areas. The term is most strongly associated with association football, where derbies are among the most eagerly anticipated and intensely contested matches of a season, but it is also used in rugby, cricket, ice hockey, basketball and other team sports. Rivalries within derbies frequently draw on deeper social, religious, political or class divisions between the clubs' supporters.

Origin of the term

Several derivations of "derby" have been proposed. One theory connects it to the horse race founded by the Earl of Derby in 1780, by analogy implying a major local sporting event. Another traces it to the town of Derby, where neighbouring parishes of All Saints and St Peter's contested a violent folk football match on Shrove Tuesday from at least the eighteenth century. The term came into general sporting use in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as league football developed in industrial towns with more than one club.

Notable derbies

  • Old Firm derby — Celtic vs Rangers, Glasgow
  • North London derby — Arsenal vs Tottenham Hotspur
  • Manchester derby — Manchester City vs Manchester United
  • Merseyside derby — Liverpool vs Everton
  • Derby della Madonnina — AC Milan vs Inter Milan
  • El Clásico and the Madrid derby — Real Madrid vs Atlético Madrid
  • Superclásico — Boca Juniors vs River Plate, Buenos Aires
  • Intercontinental Derby — Fenerbahçe vs Galatasaray, Istanbul

Characteristics

Derbies are noted for heightened atmosphere, large travelling supports in close proximity, and "bragging rights" that supporters value independently of league position or trophies; form is often said to "go out of the window". They can also bring increased policing, early kick-off times imposed to limit disorder, and occasionally crowd trouble or violence. In some sports and cities, supporters of the two clubs are intermingled in families and workplaces — the Merseyside derby was once nicknamed the "friendly derby" — whereas others map onto sectarian or ethnic divides.
