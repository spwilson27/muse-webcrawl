Scare tactics

Scare tactics (also known as fearmongering or scaremongering) are strategies that deliberately arouse fear or alarm in the public in order to influence behaviour, policy, or opinion. The use of fear as a persuasive tool is documented in rhetoric, politics, propaganda, advertising, and public health campaigns.

Usage

In politics, scare tactics may involve exaggerating the danger posed by an opponent's policies, by crime, immigration, or external threats, in order to win support for one's own position. In media, alarming or sensational coverage of rare dangers can distort the public's perception of risk. Fear appeals are also used in public health — for example, graphic anti-smoking campaigns — where research suggests they can be effective when paired with information that helps people feel able to act on the warning.

Psychology

Protection motivation theory and the extended parallel process model describe how fear appeals succeed or fail depending on perceived threat and perceived efficacy. When fear is high but a person's sense of efficacy is low, the response may instead be denial, avoidance, or reactance.

Criticism

Critics argue that habitual scaremongering desensitizes audiences, erodes trust in institutions, and can lead to harmful policy overreactions. The term "fearmongering" is itself often used rhetorically against opponents.
