Welsh folklore is the collective term for the folk tales, legends, customs and beliefs of the people of Wales. Much of it was transmitted orally for centuries before being written down in the Middle Ages — most famously in the prose tales of the Mabinogion — or collected by antiquarians in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Mythology and the Mabinogion

The Four Branches of the Mabinogi tell of figures such as Pwyll, Prince of Dyfed; the tragic Branwen; the wizard-figure Math fab Mathonwy; and the hero Lleu Llaw Gyffes, while the native tales include the dream-vision The Dream of Macsen Wledig and the Arthurian romance Culhwch and Olwen, the earliest surviving Arthurian tale. The Welsh Triads preserve further traditional lore in grouped lists of legendary persons and events.

Fairies and supernatural beings

The Tylwyth Teg ("the Fair Folk") are the fairies of Welsh tradition, said to live in an otherworld (Annwn) or beneath lakes and hills, and associated with fairy rings and changeling children. Other beings include the Cŵn Annwn, spectral white hounds with red ears belonging to Arawn, king of Annwn, later attached to Gwyn ap Nudd; the lake-monster Afanc; the bwbach or house spirit; and corpse candles (canwyll corff) said to foretell a death.

Customs

Distinctive calendar customs include the Mari Lwyd, a horse-skull figure carried from house to house at New Year in south Wales, engaging the household in a contest of improvised verse (pwnco); Calennig gift-giving on New Year's Day; and wassailing and plygain carol services. Folk tales of King Arthur, of the sleeping warriors said to rest in a cave awaiting Wales's hour of need, and of drowned lands such as Cantre'r Gwaelod beneath Cardigan Bay remain prominent in Welsh popular culture.
