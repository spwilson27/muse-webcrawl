Koryū

Koryū (古流, "old school" or "traditional school") is a Japanese term used to describe Japanese martial arts traditions (koryū bujutsu) that were established before the beginning of the Meiji era in 1868. The term stands in contrast to gendai budō, the modern martial arts founded after the Meiji Restoration.

Definition and characteristics

Koryū traditions are schools (ryū) of martial art originating in feudal Japan, generally established before the Haitōrei edict of 1876, which prohibited the wearing of swords in public. They typically include training in a range of battlefield arts—such as kenjutsu (swordsmanship), iaijutsu (sword drawing), jujutsu (grappling), sōjutsu (spear), naginatajutsu (glaive), bōjutsu (staff), and in some cases ninjutsu—taught as integrated systems for warriors of the samurai class.

Instruction is transmitted from teacher to student through a hierarchy of licensed practitioners. Advancement is marked by menkyo certificates and licences such as menkyo kaiden ("licence of total transmission"), and documentation is preserved in scrolls (makimono) and catalogues of technique (mokuroku).

Notable schools

Well-known surviving koryū include Tenshin Shōden Katori Shintō-ryū (one of the oldest extant systems, founded in the 15th century), Kashima Shintō-ryū, Musō Jikiden Eishin-ryū, Musō Shinden-ryū, Shindō Musō-ryū (jōjutsu), Kukishin-ryū, and Takenouchi-ryū, considered the oldest of the jujutsu traditions.

Modern practice

Today koryū are studied by a small number of practitioners in Japan and abroad, typically as a means of preserving historical technique, combative principles, and cultural heritage. Umbrella organizations such as the Nihon Kobudō Kyōkai and the Nihon Kobudō Shinkōkai recognize and support legitimate schools.
