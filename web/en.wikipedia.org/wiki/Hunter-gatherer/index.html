A hunter-gatherer or forager is a human living in a community, or according to an ancestrally derived lifestyle, relying on forage as its primary means of subsistence — the hunting of wild animals and the gathering of wild plant foods, rather than agriculture or animal husbandry. Hunting and gathering was humanity's original and only way of life for most of its history, until the transition to agriculture in the Neolithic Revolution beginning around 12,000 years ago gradually displaced foraging societies across most of the world.

Characteristics

Foraging societies typically live in small, mobile, egalitarian bands with fluid membership. Food sharing is widespread, property is minimal, and leadership tends to be informal and situational. Studies of groups such as the !Kung San of the Kalahari found that subsistence needs could often be met with relatively few hours of work per day, leading anthropologist Marshall Sahlins to call foragers "the original affluent society" — a claim later debated and qualified.

Archaeology

The Palaeolithic and Mesolithic archaeological records are dominated by hunter-gatherer societies, characterised by stone tool industries, rock art, and seasonal camps. The transition to farming occurred independently in several regions, including the Fertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica and the Andes.

Modern foragers

A small number of peoples — including the San of southern Africa, the Hadza of Tanzania, the Pirahã of Brazil and various groups in the Congo Basin and Southeast Asia — continued to rely substantially on foraging into the modern era, though all are affected by contact with agricultural and industrial neighbours, land loss and state policies.
