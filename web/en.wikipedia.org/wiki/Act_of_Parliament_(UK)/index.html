Act of Parliament (UK)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An Act of Parliament in the United Kingdom is primary legislation passed by the Parliament of the United Kingdom and given royal assent. Acts are the highest form of law in the UK and can only be overridden by another Act. Before royal assent, a proposed Act is known as a bill.

Terminology and citation

Acts are cited by their short title and year (for example, the Human Rights Act 1998) or by regnal year and chapter number for older statutes. Since 1963, Acts have been numbered by calendar year. Each Act is assigned a chapter number, abbreviated "c." The official copies are printed by the King's Printer and published by The Stationery Office; the authoritative archive of original Acts since 1497 is held by the Parliamentary Archives.

Stages of a bill

Most bills go through the following stages in each House:

  1. First reading – a formal introduction; the bill is printed.
  2. Second reading – a debate on the general principles of the bill.
  3. Committee stage – detailed examination, usually by a public bill committee in the Commons or a committee of the whole House in the Lords.
  4. Report stage – further consideration of amendments.
  5. Third reading – final debate and vote on the bill as amended.

If both Houses pass the bill in identical form, it proceeds to royal assent. Where the Houses disagree, amendments pass back and forth ("ping-pong") until agreement is reached. Under the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949, the House of Commons can in certain circumstances present a bill for royal assent without the consent of the House of Lords.

Types of bill

  • Public bills – affect the general public; most government legislation falls in this category.
  • Private bills – affect particular persons, organisations, or localities.
  • Hybrid bills – public bills that affect private interests in a particular way, such as bills authorising major infrastructure projects.
  • Private members' bills – introduced by backbench MPs rather than the government.
  • Money bills – concerned solely with taxation or public spending; certified by the Speaker.

Commencement and interpretation

An Act comes into force on royal assent unless it provides otherwise; many Acts commence on dates appointed by ministers through commencement orders, or in stages. Courts interpret Acts using the rules of statutory interpretation, and since the Human Rights Act 1998 may issue a declaration of incompatibility where primary legislation cannot be read compatibly with the European Convention on Human Rights.

Devolution

Since 1999, the devolved legislatures in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland pass their own primary legislation in devolved areas: Acts of the Scottish Parliament, Acts of Senedd Cymru, and Acts of the Northern Ireland Assembly. The UK Parliament retains the legal power to legislate on all matters, though by convention (the Sewel convention) it does not normally legislate on devolved matters without consent.
