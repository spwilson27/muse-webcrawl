Inflation

[Image: a shopper at a supermarket checkout with a trolley of groceries, symbolising rising consumer prices]

Inflation is a sustained general increase in the prices of goods and services in an economy over time, equivalently a fall in the purchasing power of Currency. It is usually measured by a consumer price index (CPI) — the price of a representative basket of goods and services — or by the GDP deflator, and quoted as an annual percentage rate. Moderate inflation of around 2% a year is targeted by most central banks; falling prices are called deflation, and a slowing inflation rate disinflation.

Causes

Textbooks distinguish demand-pull inflation (aggregate demand outrunning supply — "too much money chasing too few goods"), cost-push inflation (rising wages or imported input prices, as in the 1970s oil shocks) and monetary explanations: in the long run, sustained inflation accompanies money-supply growth faster than real output, as formalised in Monetarism and the quantity theory of money. Expectations are central: if workers and firms expect inflation, they build it into wage claims and prices, making inflation self-sustaining and costly to remove.

Effects and policy

Inflation redistributes wealth from savers and lenders to borrowers, erodes fixed incomes, distorts relative prices ("menu costs" and "shoe-leather costs" in the textbook phrase) and, at high rates, destroys the functions of money altogether. Extreme hyperinflation — Hungary in 1946, Weimar Germany in 1923, Zimbabwe in 2008 — has accompanied fiscal and political collapse. Central banks fight inflation chiefly with monetary policy: raising interest rates reduces demand and credit growth. The 2021–23 global inflation surge, driven by pandemic supply disruption, energy prices and fiscal stimulus, pushed rates to forty-year highs in many economies before easing.
