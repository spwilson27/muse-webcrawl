Shaped canvas

A shaped canvas is a painting support whose outer contour deviates from the conventional rectangle — taking forms such as triangles, circles, diamonds, trapezoids, free-form curves or three-dimensional constructions. Shaped canvases became a signature device of postwar American painting in the late 1950s and 1960s, challenging the rectangle inherited from centuries of easel painting.

History

Although non-rectangular paintings existed earlier — medieval tondi, Baroque irregular frames, and works by artists such as Piet Mondrian (lozenge canvases) — the shaped canvas emerged as a self-conscious movement around 1959–62. Frank Stella's shaped "pinwheel" and concentric paintings made the support's outline coincide with the composition's internal logic; Kenneth Noland, Ellsworth Kelly, Charles Hinman, Richard Tuttle, Elizabeth Murray and Frank Gehry's contemporaries such as Robert Mangold explored curves, notches and modular multi-panel constructions. The Guggenheim Museum's 1964 exhibition "The Shaped Canvas", organised by Lawrence Alloway, canonised the term.

Debates

Critics argued about whether such works remained paintings or had become objects — a tension central to debates between Greenbergian modernism and Minimalism, since a shaped support insists on the painting as a literal thing with edges in real space. The technique continues in contemporary practice, from screen-shaped supports to CNC-cut panels.
