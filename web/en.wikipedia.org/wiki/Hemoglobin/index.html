Hemoglobin

Hemoglobin (also spelled haemoglobin, abbreviated Hb or Hgb) is the iron-containing oxygen-transport metalloprotein in the red blood cells of almost all vertebrates and the tissues of some invertebrates. Hemoglobin in blood carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues, where it releases it to power aerobic respiration, and carries carbon dioxide back toward the lungs. In mammals the protein makes up about 96% of the red blood cell's dry content.

Structure

Hemoglobin is a tetramer of two alpha-like and two beta-like globin chains, each cradling a haem group whose ferrous iron atom binds one O2 molecule — four in total. Binding is cooperative: oxygenation shifts the tetramer from the tense (T) to the relaxed (R) state, increasing affinity, which produces the sigmoid oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve. Affinity is reduced by low pH and CO2 (the Bohr effect), by temperature, and by 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, all favouring oxygen release in active tissues.

Types and development

Developmental stage determines the chains expressed: embryonic hemoglobins give way to fetal hemoglobin (HbF, α2γ2), whose higher oxygen affinity draws oxygen across the placenta, and then to adult HbA (α2β2), with a minor fraction of HbA2 (α2δ2). Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) reflects average blood glucose over the preceding two to three months and is central to diabetes management.

Disorders

Mutations in globin genes cause the hemoglobinopathies. In sickle cell disease, a single amino-acid substitution in the β chain (glutamate → valine) makes deoxygenated hemoglobin polymerize, deforming red cells. The thalassemias arise from reduced production of α or β chains. Deficiency of hemoglobin overall constitutes anaemia. Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin with about 240 times the affinity of oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin and impairing delivery — the basis of CO poisoning.

History

Hemoglobin was among the first proteins whose structure was solved: Max Perutz determined its three-dimensional structure by X-ray crystallography in 1959–60, sharing the 1962 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Its cooperativity made it the classic model of allosteric proteins in biochemistry.
