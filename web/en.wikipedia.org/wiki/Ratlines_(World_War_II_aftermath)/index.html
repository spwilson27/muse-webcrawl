Ratlines (World War II aftermath)

"Ratlines" (German: Rattenlinien) were a system of escape routes for German Nazis and other fascists fleeing Europe in the aftermath of World War II to escape prosecution as war criminals. These escape routes mainly led toward havens in Latin America, particularly Argentina, Paraguay, Brazil, Uruguay and Chile, as well as to Spain, Switzerland, and the Middle East.

Routes

Two principal routes developed: one through Spain, organised in part under Franco's regime, and another through Italy, where the Croatian priest Krunoslav Draganović and networks around the San Girolamo monastery in Rome helped fugitives obtain Red Cross travel documents and visas. Bishop Alois Hudal, an Austrian cleric sympathetic to Nazism, was also implicated in assisting escapes. Routes often ran Germany → Austria → Italy (South Tyrol, Genoa) and then by ship to South America.

Notable fugitives

Prominent war criminals who escaped along the ratlines included Adolf Eichmann, Josef Mengele, Klaus Barbie, Erich Priebke and Josef Schwammberger. Argentine president Juan Perón's government welcomed many of the fugitives and issued numerous landing permits. Some ratline activity was tolerated or exploited by Western intelligence services in the early Cold War.
