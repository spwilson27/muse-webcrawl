Early Irish literature

Early Irish literature is the body of writing in the Irish language (and Latin) produced in Ireland from the earliest written records in the 5th–6th centuries AD through the medieval period. It is among the oldest vernacular literatures in Europe, preserving heroic sagas, mythology, poetry, law texts and annals that were first transmitted orally by the learned class of poets known as the filid.

Early Irish texts survive in manuscripts such as the Book of Leinster and Lebor na hUidre.

Origins

The earliest Irish writing consists of short inscriptions in the Ogham alphabet on standing stones, dating from about the 4th century AD. With the coming of Christianity and Latin literacy, monastic scribes began recording native tradition in manuscripts. Primitive Irish gave way to Old Irish (c. 600–900) and then Middle Irish (c. 900–1200), the languages of the great saga literature.

The cycles of tales

Scholars traditionally group the prose tales into four cycles:

  • The Mythological Cycle, concerning the Tuatha Dé Danann and the invasions of Ireland, including the Lebor Gabála Érenn (Book of Invasions).
  • The Ulster Cycle, tales of the hero Cú Chulainn and the court of King Conchobar at Emain Macha, centred on the great cattle-raid epic Táin Bó Cúailnge (The Cattle Raid of Cooley).
  • The Fenian Cycle, tales of Fionn mac Cumhaill and his warrior band, the Fianna.
  • The Historical Cycle (Cycle of the Kings), tales of historical and legendary kings of Ireland.

Other genres

Early Irish literature also includes nature and religious lyric poetry attributed to hermits and monks, extensive legal tracts (the Brehon laws), genealogies, place-name lore (dindsenchas), voyage tales (immrama) such as the Voyage of Saint Brendan, and annals recording events year by year. Saints' lives and translations from Latin added further depth to the corpus.

Manuscripts

Key surviving manuscripts include Lebor na hUidre (the Book of the Dun Cow, c. 1100), the Book of Leinster (c. 1160), the Yellow Book of Lecan and the Great Book of Lecan. Though many texts were lost, Ireland's manuscript tradition preserved a remarkably complete picture of a pre-Christian heroic world filtered through Christian scribes.
