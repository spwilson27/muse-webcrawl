Hashimoto's thyroiditis

Hashimoto's thyroiditis, also known as chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis and Hashimoto's disease, is an autoimmune disease in which the thyroid gland is gradually destroyed. Early on there may be no symptoms. Over time the thyroid may enlarge and a painless goiter may form. Some people eventually develop hypothyroidism with accompanying weight gain, feeling tired, constipation, mild depression, dry skin, and thinning hair. The condition usually appears between 30 and 50 years of age and is much more common in women than men.

Causes

Hashimoto's thyroiditis is thought to result from a combination of genetic and environmental factors. The immune system produces antibodies against thyroid peroxidase and thyroglobulin, leading to lymphocytic infiltration of the gland and progressive loss of function. Risk factors include a family history of thyroid disease, other autoimmune conditions such as type 1 diabetes, and possibly excess iodine intake or certain infections.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically confirmed by blood tests showing elevated levels of anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) antibodies and an elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) with low or normal free thyroxine. Ultrasound may show a diffusely enlarged, hypoechoic gland. The disease is named after Japanese physician Hakaru Hashimoto, who first described it in 1912.

Treatment

There is no cure, but hypothyroidism resulting from the disease is readily treated with levothyroxine hormone replacement. Most patients require lifelong therapy and periodic monitoring of TSH levels. With appropriate treatment, the prognosis is excellent.
