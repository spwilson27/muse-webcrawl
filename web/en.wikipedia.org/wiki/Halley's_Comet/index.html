Halley's Comet, officially designated 1P/Halley, is the best-known short-period comet, visible from Earth roughly every 75–76 years. It is named after the English astronomer Edmond Halley, who in 1705 computed that the comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 were a single object returning periodically, and predicted its return in 1758 — a prediction fulfilled after his death, confirming Newtonian gravitation.

History

Records of the comet extend back to at least 240 BC in Chinese annals. Famous apparitions include 1066, when it was depicted in the Bayeux Tapestry as an omen before the Battle of Hastings, and 1910, when Earth passed near its tail amid public anxiety about cyanogen gas. The 1986 return, though poorly placed for Northern Hemisphere observers, was met by an international fleet of spacecraft ("Halley's Armada"), and the European Space Agency's Giotto probe passed within about 600 km of the nucleus, returning the first close-up images of a comet's dark, irregular nucleus.

Orbit and structure

The comet travels on a retrograde, highly elliptical orbit inclined about 162° to the ecliptic. Its nucleus measures roughly 15 by 8 kilometres and has a very low albedo. It is the parent body of two meteor showers, the Eta Aquariids and the Orionids.
