Dracunculus medinensis

Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Nematoda
Order: Camallanida
Family: Dracunculidae
Genus: Dracunculus
Species: D. medinensis

Dracunculus medinensis, the Guinea worm, is a parasitic nematode that causes dracunculiasis, also known as Guinea-worm disease. It is one of the largest known nematodes parasitising humans: the adult female measures 60–100 centimetres (24–39 in) in length, while the male is much smaller.

Life cycle

Humans become infected by drinking unfiltered water containing copepods (tiny crustaceans of the genus Cyclops) carrying worm larvae. Over about a year the larvae mature; a fertilised female migrates to the skin — usually of the lower leg — and forms a painful blister. When the limb is immersed in water, the worm releases larvae, completing the cycle. The traditional removal method, wrapping the emerging worm slowly around a stick over days or weeks, is ancient and is sometimes suggested as the origin of the Rod of Asclepius symbol of medicine.

Eradication

Guinea-worm disease has been the target of a global eradication campaign led by the Carter Center since 1986. Reported human cases have fallen from an estimated 3.5 million per year in the mid-1980s to only a handful of cases annually in recent years, making dracunculiasis a candidate to become the second human disease eradicated worldwide, after smallpox. There is no vaccine or drug treatment; prevention relies on filtering drinking water and keeping infected people from entering water sources.
