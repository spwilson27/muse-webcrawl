Succulent plant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Succulents such as this Echeveria store water in their thickened leaves.

In botany, succulent plants, also known as succulents, are plants with parts that are thickened, fleshy, and engorged, usually to retain water in arid climates or soil conditions. The word succulent comes from the Latin word sucus, meaning "juice" or "sap".

Succulent plants may store water in various structures, such as leaves and stems. The water content of some succulent organs can get up to 90–95%. Some definitions also include roots, thus geophytes that survive unfavourable periods by dying back to underground storage organs may be regarded as succulents. In horticulture, the term is sometimes used in a way that excludes cacti (Cactaceae). However, in botanical terms, cacti are also succulents.

Families and genera

There are approximately sixty different plant families that contain succulents. Well-known succulent families include the Cactaceae (cacti), Crassulaceae (stonecrops, including jade plants and hens-and-chicks), Aizoaceae (ice plants and living stones), Asphodelaceae (aloes and haworthias), and Apocynaceae. Genera such as Echeveria, Sedum, Lithops, Agave, and Sempervivum are widely cultivated.

Adaptations

Many succulents come from dry areas such as steppes, semi-desert, and desert. High temperatures and low precipitation force plants to collect and store water to survive long dry periods. Adaptations include thickened, fleshy tissues, a waxy or hairy surface coating to reduce water loss, reduced or absent leaves, and crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), in which stomata open at night to reduce water loss during photosynthesis.

Cultivation

Succulents are favoured as houseplants because of their striking and unusual appearance, as well as their ability to thrive with relatively minimal care. They generally require well-draining soil, bright light, and infrequent watering; overwatering is the most common cause of failure in cultivation.
