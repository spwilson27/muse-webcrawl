Common stock

Common stock is a form of corporate equity ownership, a type of security. In the United States the terms voting share or ordinary share are also used, while in the United Kingdom and other Commonwealth realms the standard term is ordinary share. A share of common stock represents fractional ownership of the issuing corporation, entitling the holder to a portion of the company's profits and assets, and usually — though not always — to voting rights in the election of the board of directors and on major corporate matters.

Rights of common shareholders

Common shareholders stand last in line in the priority of claims on a company's assets: if the company is liquidated, common shareholders receive whatever remains only after bondholders, creditors, and preferred shareholders have been paid in full. In return for this residual claim, common stock offers theoretically unlimited upside, since dividends and capital gains are not capped. Typical rights include voting (commonly one vote per share), receiving dividends when declared by the board, pre-emptive rights to maintain a proportional stake in some jurisdictions, and inspection of certain corporate records.

Classes of shares

Many companies issue multiple classes of common stock (for example, Class A and Class B shares) with different voting rights. Dual-class structures, used by many technology companies, allow founders and insiders to retain voting control while holding a minority of the total equity. Common stock contrasts with preferred stock, which usually carries a fixed dividend, priority in liquidation, and little or no voting power.

Issuance and trading

Companies raise capital by issuing common stock to the public in an initial public offering (IPO) and in later follow-on offerings; the shares then trade on stock exchanges. The market value of a company's common stock multiplied by the number of shares outstanding gives its market capitalisation, a standard measure of corporate size.
