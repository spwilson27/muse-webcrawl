Allen Ginsberg

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Allen Ginsberg reading poetry in the 1960s, bearded and ecstatic]
Allen Ginsberg reading poetry in the 1960s, bearded and ecstatic

Irwin Allen Ginsberg (3 June 1926 – 5 April 1997) was an American poet and writer, a leading figure of both the Beat Generation of the 1950s and the counterculture that followed. His poem Howl (1956) — prosecuted for obscenity and vindicated in court — denounced the conformity and materialism of postwar America and became one of the most famous American poems of the twentieth century.

Life and Howl

Ginsberg was born in Newark to a poet father and a mother whose mental illness shadows his work (the long elegy Kaddish, 1961). At Columbia University he formed the Beat circle with Jack Kerouac, William S. Burroughs and Neal Cassady. Reading Howl's first part at San Francisco's Six Gallery in October 1955 launched his fame; its publication by Lawrence Ferlinghetti's City Lights led to the 1957 obscenity trial, where Judge Clayton Horn ruled the poem had redeeming social importance — a landmark for free expression.

Counterculture elder

Ginsberg travelled India, chanted at protests from Chicago 1968 to anti-nuclear and gay-liberation rallies, testified for the defence in obscenity and draft cases, co-founded the Jack Kerouac School of Disembodied Poetics at Naropa University with Anne Waldman (1974), and recorded with musicians from Bob Dylan to The Clash. His lifelong partner was the poet Peter Orlovsky.

Legacy

Ginsberg won the National Book Award for The Fall of America (1974) and became American poetry's most visible public figure — the bearded uncle of every subsequent counterculture — while scholars debate the unevenness of his later verse. His archive at Stanford and his photographic portraits of the Beats document the era he helped create.
