In chemistry, a disulfide (or disulphide) is a compound containing a sulfur–sulfur covalent bond, conventionally written R–S–S–R′. The linkage is also called an SS-bond or disulfide bridge. The term also covers the disulfide anion S22− found in inorganic solids such as pyrite (FeS2). Disulfides are typically formed by the oxidation (coupling) of two thiol (–SH) groups, and they can be cleaved again by reduction — a reversible chemistry of great importance in biology.

In biochemistry

Disulfide bonds between cysteine residues are crucial cross-links that stabilise the three-dimensional structure of many proteins, especially secreted and extracellular proteins. The amino acid cystine consists of two cysteines joined by a disulfide bond. Insulin, for example, is held together by two interchain disulfide bridges; keratin in hair owes its properties to disulfide cross-links, which are broken and re-formed in permanent-wave treatments. Enzymes of the protein disulfide isomerase family catalyse the correct formation and rearrangement of these bonds in cells.

Occurrence and uses

Organic disulfides occur in garlic and other alliums (e.g. diallyl disulfide), in the defence spray of skunks, and in vulcanised rubber, where sulfur cross-links between polymer chains give the material its elasticity. Inorganic polysulfides are used in lithium–sulfur batteries and in the kraft process for paper manufacture.
