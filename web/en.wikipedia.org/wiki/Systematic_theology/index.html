Systematic theology
FieldChristian theology
BranchesTheology proper, Christology, pneumatology, soteriology, ecclesiology, eschatology
Key figuresThomas Aquinas, John Calvin, Karl Barth, Wolfhart Pannenberg

Systematic theology is a discipline of Christian theology that formulates an orderly, rational and coherent account of the doctrines of the Christian faith. It draws on the biblical texts, church history, philosophy and ethics, organising its material by topic (loci) rather than following the narrative order of Scripture, as biblical theology does, or the development of doctrine through time, as historical theology does.

Traditional loci

Systematic works are conventionally organised into branches: prolegomena (method and sources) and the doctrine of Scripture (bibliology); theology proper (the doctrine of God, including the Trinity); anthropology and hamartiology (humanity and sin); Christology (the person and work of Christ); pneumatology (the Holy Spirit); soteriology (salvation); ecclesiology (the church and sacraments); and eschatology (last things).

History

Early attempts at ordered doctrinal exposition include Origen's On First Principles and John of Damascus's Exposition of the Orthodox Faith. In the medieval West, Peter Lombard's Sentences and Thomas Aquinas's Summa Theologiae set the scholastic pattern. The Reformation produced confessional systems such as Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536–1559). In the modern period, Friedrich Schleiermacher re-founded the discipline around religious consciousness, while Karl Barth's Church Dogmatics (1932–1967) dominated twentieth-century Protestant systematics.

Approaches

Different traditions—Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed, evangelical, liberation and feminist theologies—pursue systematic theology with distinct sources and emphases, variously weighting Scripture, tradition, reason and experience (the so-called Wesleyan Quadrilateral).
