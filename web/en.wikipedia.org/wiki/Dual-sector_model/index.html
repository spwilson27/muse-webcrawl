Dual-sector model

The dual-sector model is a model in development economics, published by the economist W. Arthur Lewis in his 1954 article "Economic Development with Unlimited Supplies of Labour". It is often called the Lewis model. It explains the growth of a developing economy in terms of a labour transition between two sectors, the capitalist sector and the subsistence sector.

The model

Lewis divided a developing economy into two parts. The traditional, subsistence sector — typically overpopulated rural agriculture — has a very large supply of labour whose marginal productivity is near zero, so workers can be withdrawn without reducing output. The modern capitalist sector (manufacturing and commercial agriculture) pays a wage somewhat above subsistence and can draw on this effectively unlimited supply of labour at a constant wage.

Because the wage is fixed while the marginal product of labour in the capitalist sector exceeds it, capitalists earn a surplus (profits), which Lewis assumed they reinvest. Reinvestment raises the capital stock and shifts the demand for labour outward, absorbing more workers from the subsistence sector. The process continues until the surplus labour is exhausted — the "Lewis turning point" — after which labour becomes scarce and wages begin to rise across the economy.

Criticism and extensions

Critics have questioned the assumptions that surplus labour has zero marginal product, that profits are reinvested domestically rather than consumed or sent abroad, and that the agricultural sector remains passive; neglect of agriculture can cause food prices to rise and choke off industrialisation. The Fei–Ranis model extended Lewis's framework by treating agricultural productivity growth explicitly. Despite its simplifications, the Lewis model was foundational for development economics and helped earn Lewis the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1979 (shared with Theodore Schultz).
