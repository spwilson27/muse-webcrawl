Marcan priority

In the two-source hypothesis, Mark and the lost Q source underlie Matthew and Luke.

Marcan priority (or Markan priority) is the hypothesis that the Gospel of Mark was the first of the three Synoptic Gospels to be written and was used as a source by the authors of the Gospels of Matthew and Luke. It is a central element of the scholarly treatment of the synoptic problem — the question of how the striking literary agreements and disagreements among Matthew, Mark, and Luke are to be explained — and underlies the widely held two-source hypothesis.

Evidence

Several arguments support Mark's priority. (1) Content: almost the whole of Mark appears, in some form, in Matthew and Luke; very little Markan material is absent from both. (2) Order: when Matthew and Luke agree in the sequence of episodes, they agree with Mark; when they depart from Mark's order, they depart in different directions, making Mark the "middle term". (3) Style and theology: Mark's Greek is rougher, his Aramaisms more frequent, and his portrayal of Jesus and the disciples more unpolished; Matthew and Luke repeatedly smooth Mark's grammar, clarify difficulties, and soften statements that could appear theologically awkward (for example, Mark 6:5, "he could do no mighty work there", compared with Matthew 13:58, "he did not do many mighty works there"). Such editorial improvements are easier to explain if Mark came first than if Mark clumsily abridged the more polished Gospels.

History of the hypothesis

Ancient tradition, following Papias, generally held Matthew to be first (the "Augustinian hypothesis"), and the order of the Gospels in the canon reflects this. Some early modern scholars, notably Hugo Grotius and, systematically, G. C. Storr (1786), proposed Mark's priority, but the hypothesis was fully argued in the 1830s by Karl Lachmann, Christian Gottlob Wilke, and Christian Hermann Weisse, the latter combining it with a sayings source to form the two-source hypothesis. H. J. Holtzmann's work of 1863 established Marcan priority as the dominant critical view, a position refined by B. H. Streeter (1925) and held by the majority of New Testament scholars since.

Alternatives

Alternatives include the Augustinian hypothesis (Matthew first, then Mark, then Luke), the Farrer hypothesis (Mark first, but Luke used Matthew directly, dispensing with Q), the Griesbach or two-gospel hypothesis (Matthew first, Luke second, Mark conflating the two), and the Wilke hypothesis. Marcan priority is compatible with both the two-source and Farrer hypotheses; its main rivals deny that Mark was first at all.

Consequences

If Mark is the earliest Gospel, it is the closest surviving narrative source for the historical Jesus, and Matthew's and Luke's changes to Mark become evidence for their distinct theological programmes — the foundation of redaction criticism.
