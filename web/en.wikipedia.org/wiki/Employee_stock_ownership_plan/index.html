Employee stock ownership plan

An employee stock ownership plan (ESOP) in the United States is a defined contribution plan, a form of retirement plan as defined by 4975(e)(7) of the Internal Revenue Code, which became a qualified retirement plan in 1974. It is one of the methods of employee participation in corporate ownership. An ESOP is a stock bonus plan or a combination stock bonus and money purchase plan designed to invest primarily in employer stock, and it is the predominant form of employee ownership in the United States.

How ESOPs work

A company sets up a trust fund into which it contributes new shares of its own stock or cash to buy existing shares. Alternatively, the ESOP can borrow money to buy new or existing shares, with the company making cash contributions to the plan to enable it to repay the loan — a "leveraged ESOP". Allocations are made to individual employee accounts, typically in proportion to compensation, and employees receive the value of their vested shares when they leave the company or retire.

Tax treatment and uses

Contributions to an ESOP are tax-deductible for the employer, and owners of private companies can obtain tax advantages when selling shares to an ESOP; under Section 1042 of the Internal Revenue Code, a selling shareholder in a C corporation may defer capital gains taxes if the ESOP owns at least 30% of the company after the sale and the proceeds are reinvested in qualifying securities. ESOPs are commonly used as a succession strategy for retiring owners of closely held businesses, as a benefit that may improve employee retention and productivity, and occasionally as a corporate finance or takeover-defence tool.
