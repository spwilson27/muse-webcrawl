Filibuster

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A filibuster is a political tactic, most associated with the United States Senate, in which one or more legislators delay or block a vote on a proposal by extending debate — or threatening to — exploiting rules that allow unlimited speech. The word derives from the Spanish filibustero (freebooter/pirate), via Dutch vrijbuiter.

The US Senate practice

Because the Senate historically permitted unlimited debate, minorities could talk measures to death until the chamber adopted cloture (Rule 22, 1917, requiring two-thirds; reduced to three-fifths — the famous 60 votes — in 1975). The modern filibuster rarely involves floor marathons (Strom Thurmond's 24-hour-18-minute speech against the Civil Rights Act of 1957 remains the record); instead the mere threat, signalled through holds and unanimous-consent breakdowns, imposes a 60-vote supermajority requirement for most legislation and many nominations. Exceptions carve through it: budget reconciliation bills, and the "nuclear option" precedents of 2013 (executive and most judicial nominations) and 2017 (Supreme Court nominations) restored majority cloture for nominees. Defenders call the filibuster a consensus-forcing protection of minority rights and deliberation; critics call it an anti-majoritarian accident that biases policy towards the status quo — the debate recurs every Congress. State legislatures and other parliaments have analogous devices (and names: "dilation", talking out).
