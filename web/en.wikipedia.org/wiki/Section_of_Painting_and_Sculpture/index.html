Section of Painting and Sculpture

The Section of Painting and Sculpture was a New Deal art program administered by the United States Department of the Treasury from 1934 to 1943. Unlike the relief-based programs of the era, the Section commissioned works of art for federal buildings — principally post offices and courthouses — through anonymous competitions, with the stated aim of securing high-quality, locally meaningful art for public spaces.

History

The Section was established in October 1934 under the direction of George Biddle's advocacy and administered by Edward Bruce, with funding drawn from one percent of the construction appropriations for federal buildings. Edward Rowan served as assistant director and supervised much of the day-to-day work.

Over its nine years, the Section awarded roughly 1,400 commissions for murals and sculpture in more than 1,100 communities. Artists were encouraged to depict scenes from local history, industry, and everyday life, producing the body of work popularly known as "post office murals." Commissions typically paid several hundred to a few thousand dollars, and competitions were judged anonymously to open opportunities beyond established artists.

Legacy

The Section ended in 1943 as wartime priorities redirected federal spending. Its murals remain in hundreds of post offices, and many have become focal points for preservation efforts. Art historians regard the program as the most aesthetically ambitious of the New Deal art projects because its awards were based on artistic merit rather than financial need.
