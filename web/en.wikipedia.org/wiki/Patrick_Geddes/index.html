Patrick Geddes

Sir Patrick Geddes (2 October 1854 – 17 April 1932) was a Scottish biologist, sociologist, geographer, philanthropist, and pioneering town planner. He is known for his innovative thinking in the fields of urban planning and sociology, for coining or popularising terms such as "conurbation", and for the maxim "survey before plan".

Life and work

Born in Ballater, Aberdeenshire, Geddes studied biology under T. H. Huxley in London, though eye trouble ended his laboratory career and pushed him toward synthesising biology, geography, and social questions. In Edinburgh he and his wife Anna Morton undertook practical urban renewal in the Old Town, and he established the Outlook Tower on Castlehill as a museum of regional and world survey — a "sociological laboratory" using a camera obscura to study the city.

Planning ideas

Geddes advocated "conservative surgery" — improving existing neighbourhoods rather than sweeping demolition — and planning based on regional survey, summarised in his triad of "Place–Work–Folk" (following Frédéric Le Play's lieu, travail, famille). His report on Dunfermline (1904), his work in India (1914–1919), where he wrote reports for dozens of cities including Indore, and his plan for Tel Aviv (1925) spread his influence. His book Cities in Evolution (1915) remains a founding text of modern planning.

Legacy

Geddes influenced Lewis Mumford, who acknowledged him as a mentor, and helped establish town planning as a profession and academic discipline, including the chair of sociology and civics at the University of Bombay. He was knighted in 1932, shortly before his death in Montpellier, France.
