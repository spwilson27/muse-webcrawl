Responsible government

[Image: A colonial-era parliament building in British North America, where responsible government was first won in the British Empire in 1848]

Responsible government is a principle of parliamentary democracy under which the executive government is accountable to, and must retain the confidence of, the elected legislature rather than ruling in the name of a monarch or governor alone. It is a defining feature of the Westminster system.

Principles

Under responsible government, ministers are chosen from (or are answerable to) the legislature, are individually and collectively responsible for their departments and policies, and must resign — individually over departmental failures, or collectively as a government — if they lose the confidence of the lower house. A government that loses a vote of confidence must resign or call an election.

History in the British Empire

The concept developed in Britain during the nineteenth century and was extended to the settler colonies following Lord Durham's Report (1839) on British North America. Nova Scotia in January 1848 became the first colony to achieve responsible government, followed by the Province of Canada and the other colonies. The principle spread to Australia, New Zealand and South Africa, shaping their constitutional development.

Mechanisms

Accountability is maintained through instruments such as Question Time, confidence votes, control of supply, and ministerial responsibility to the parliament.
