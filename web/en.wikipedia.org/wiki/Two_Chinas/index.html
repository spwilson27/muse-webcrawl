Two Chinas

The concept of the Two Chinas refers to the political situation in which two governments each claim to be the legitimate government of "China": the People's Republic of China (PRC), based in Beijing and governing mainland China, and the Republic of China (ROC), based in Taipei and governing Taiwan and associated islands. The situation arose from the Chinese Civil War.

Background

Following the Communists' victory in 1949, Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China in Beijing, while the Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek retreated to Taiwan. Both regimes claimed sovereignty over all of China, and each sought international recognition as the sole Chinese government during the Cold War.

International recognition

For decades the ROC held China's seat at the United Nations, but in 1971 UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 transferred the seat to the PRC. Most states now recognise the PRC and adhere to some form of "One China" policy, while a small number of states maintain diplomatic relations with the ROC. The PRC rejects any "two Chinas" or "one China, one Taiwan" formulation, regarding Taiwan as part of its territory.

Cross-strait relations

Since the 1990s, proposals such as the 1992 Consensus and various formulas for peaceful coexistence have been debated. Public opinion in Taiwan increasingly identifies with a distinct Taiwanese identity, while the ROC's constitutional framework retains its historical claim. The "Two Chinas" idea remains a central and contested theme in East Asian geopolitics.
