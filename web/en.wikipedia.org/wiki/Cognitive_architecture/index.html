Cognitive architecture

A cognitive architecture is a theory of the fixed structures and mechanisms underlying cognition — in the human mind or in an artificial agent — together with a computational framework implementing that theory. Unlike a cognitive model of a single task, an architecture aims to explain a broad range of behaviour with one set of mechanisms: perception, memory, learning, problem solving, and action. The term was popularised by Allen Newell, who proposed that cognitive science needed "unified theories of cognition".

Major architectures

ACT-R, developed by John Anderson and colleagues at Carnegie Mellon University, models cognition as a set of modules (declarative memory, procedural memory, vision, motor) coordinated by a central production system whose rules fire roughly every 50 milliseconds; it is widely used to predict human response times and errors and to fit neuroimaging data. Soar, created by Newell, John Laird, and Paul Rosenbloom, represents knowledge as production rules and learns by "chunking" the results of problem solving in subgoals; it has modelled tasks from syllogisms to simulated air-combat pilots. Other architectures include the dual-process CLARION, which combines symbolic and connectionist processing, and architectures based on predictive processing or global workspace theory.

Debates

Critics question whether a single fixed architecture can capture the flexibility and embodiment of human cognition, and whether fitting laboratory tasks generalises to real-world behaviour. The field divides loosely between symbolic production-system architectures, emergent/connectionist systems, and hybrid approaches; benchmark efforts such as the "Common Model of Cognition" attempt to identify consensus components, including working memory buffers, procedural learning, and declarative memory.
