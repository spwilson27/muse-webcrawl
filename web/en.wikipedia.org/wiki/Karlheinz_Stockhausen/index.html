Karlheinz Stockhausen

Karlheinz Stockhausen (22 August 1928 – 5 December 2007) was a German composer, among the most influential and controversial figures in post-war music. A leading figure of the Darmstadt avant-garde, he pioneered serial composition, electronic music, spatial music and aleatory procedures, and influenced composers, jazz musicians and rock and electronic artists from Miles Davis and Frank Zappa to Kraftwerk and Björk.

Early work

Orphaned by the war — his father died at the front and his mother was killed in the Nazi "euthanasia" programme — Stockhausen studied in Cologne and Paris, where Olivier Messiaen's class shaped his technique. His early serial works such as Kreuzspiel (1951) and Punkte (1952) applied total serial organisation. At the WDR studio in Cologne he produced foundational electronic pieces, notably Gesang der Jünglinge (1955–56), which fused electronically generated sound with a recorded boy's voice, and Gruppen (1955–57) for three orchestras playing simultaneously in different tempi.

Later work

Stockhausen explored open form and "intuitive music" in the 1960s, lived briefly in Japan, and from 1977 devoted himself to Licht ("Light"), a cycle of seven operas, one for each day of the week, completed in 2003 and running to about 29 hours of music. A famous late commission, the Helikopter-Streichquartett (1995), places the four string players in separate helicopters. After 2001 he controversially described the September 11 attacks as a "work of art" — remarks he said were taken out of context but which damaged his public standing. He composed until his sudden death in 2007.
