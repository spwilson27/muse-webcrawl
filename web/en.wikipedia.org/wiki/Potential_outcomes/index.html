Potential outcomes

The potential outcomes framework defines causal effects by comparing outcomes under treatment and control for the same unit.

The potential outcomes framework, also called the Rubin causal model, is a framework for causal inference in statistics, formalised by Jerzy Neyman in 1923 in the context of agricultural experiments and developed by Donald Rubin from the 1970s. For each unit (such as a person or plot), and each possible treatment, there is a hypothetical outcome that would be observed if that unit received that treatment. A causal effect is defined as a comparison between potential outcomes for the same unit under different treatments.

The fundamental problem

Because a unit can receive only one treatment at a time, at most one potential outcome is observed; the others are counterfactual and unobservable. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference. Inference therefore proceeds by comparing groups, relying on assumptions such as random assignment, unconfoundedness (no unmeasured confounders), and the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), which requires that one unit's outcome does not depend on another's treatment and that there are no hidden versions of treatments.

Estimands and methods

Common quantities of interest include the average treatment effect (ATE) and the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT). Under random assignment, simple differences in group means unbiasedly estimate the ATE. In observational studies the framework motivates methods such as matching, stratification, propensity score adjustment, inverse probability weighting and regression on covariates, all aimed at mimicking the balance a randomised experiment would create.

Influence

The potential outcomes approach is a foundation of modern causal inference in econometrics, epidemiology, medicine and the social sciences, and complements Judea Pearl's structural causal model based on directed graphs. It underlies the design and analysis of randomised controlled trials and quasi-experimental designs such as instrumental variables and regression discontinuity when these are expressed in potential-outcomes terms.
