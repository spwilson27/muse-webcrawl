Io (pronounced EYE-oh) is the innermost and third-largest of the four Galilean moons of Jupiter. Slightly larger than Earth's Moon, Io is the most volcanically active body known in the Solar System, with more than 400 active volcanoes. This extreme geological activity results from tidal heating caused by friction inside Io as it is pulled between Jupiter and the other Galilean moons, Europa and Ganymede.

Discovery and naming

Io was discovered by Galileo Galilei on 8 January 1610, together with the other Galilean satellites. The names of the moons were suggested by Simon Marius; Io is named after a priestess of Hera in Greek mythology who was a lover of Zeus. Surface features are named after figures and places from the Io myth and from fire, volcano, sun and thunder gods of various cultures.

Orbit and rotation

Io orbits Jupiter at a distance of about 421,700 km from the planet's centre, completing one orbit in roughly 42.5 hours. It is in a 4:2:1 mean-motion orbital resonance with Ganymede and Europa: for every four orbits of Io, Europa completes exactly two and Ganymede one. This resonance forces Io's orbit to remain slightly eccentric, and the resulting flexing of the moon by Jupiter's gravity generates enormous internal heat — over 100 terawatts dissipated, more than twice the amount produced by radioactive decay in the Earth. Io is tidally locked, rotating once per orbit.

Physical characteristics

Unlike most moons of the outer Solar System, which are largely water ice, Io is composed primarily of silicate rock surrounding a molten or partially molten iron or iron–sulphide core. It has almost no impact craters, because volcanism continually resurfaces it. Its colourful surface — yellow, orange, red, white and black — results from sulphur and sulphur dioxide frost and lava flows; sulphur takes on different colours at different temperatures. The surface features include mountains (some taller than Mount Everest), caldera-like volcanic depressions called paterae, lava lakes and extensive flows of silicate and sulphurous lava.

Volcanism

Volcanism on Io was first observed by the Voyager 1 spacecraft in 1979, which imaged an umbrella-shaped plume rising above the surface. Two styles of eruption occur: intra-patera eruptions within volcanic depressions, and fissure-fed flows that can extend hundreds of kilometres. Plumes of gas and dust can rise 500 km above the surface before falling back. Galileo and later Juno observations found extremely hot lavas, suggesting silicate (basaltic to ultramafic) compositions at temperatures above 1,200 °C.

Atmosphere and magnetospheric interaction

Io has a tenuous atmosphere of sulphur dioxide, supported partly by sublimation of surface frost and partly by volcanic outgassing. Material escaping from Io feeds the Io plasma torus, a ring of ionised sulphur and oxygen around Jupiter, and a neutral cloud along its orbit. Io interacts strongly with Jupiter's magnetosphere; electric currents flowing between Io and Jupiter produce the Io footprint aurorae on the planet and make Io a powerful source of radio emissions.
