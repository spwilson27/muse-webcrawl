A storm surge, storm flood, tidal surge, or storm tide is a coastal flood or tsunami-like phenomenon of rising water commonly associated with low-pressure weather systems, such as cyclones. It is measured as the rise in water level above the normal tidal level, excluding waves. Storm surge is primarily caused by a storm's winds pushing water onshore.

Mechanism

At least five processes contribute to a surge: the pressure effect (water rises roughly 1 cm for every millibar drop in pressure), the direct wind effect, the effect of the Earth's rotation, the effect of waves, and the rainfall effect. The wind stress on the ocean surface is generally the dominant factor, especially where the continental shelf is wide and shallow.

Historical events

The deadliest storm surge in recorded history struck the Ganges delta in the 1970 Bhola cyclone, killing an estimated 300,000–500,000 people. Hurricane Katrina (2005) produced a surge of up to about 8.5 m (28 ft) along the Mississippi coast. The North Sea flood of 1953 devastated the Netherlands and eastern England, prompting major flood-defence works such as the Delta Works and the Thames Barrier.

Mitigation

Defences include levees, sea walls, surge barriers, dune restoration, and improved forecasting and evacuation planning. Forecast models such as SLOSH are used to predict surge heights for landfalling hurricanes.
