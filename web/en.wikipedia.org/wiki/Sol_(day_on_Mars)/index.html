Sol (borrowed from the Latin word for sun) is a solar day on Mars; that is, a Mars-day. A sol is the apparent interval between two successive returns of the Sun to the same meridian (sundial time) as seen by an observer on Mars. It is one of several units used for timekeeping on Mars.

A sol is about 24 hours, 39 minutes, and 35 seconds long — roughly 2.75% longer than an Earth day. Mars mission planners, working with landers and rovers such as Viking, Pathfinder, Spirit, Opportunity, Curiosity, and Perseverance, number each surface day sequentially from landing ("Sol 0" or "Sol 1").

Landed mission timekeeping

Because the sol is longer than the Earth day, mission operations teams on Earth gradually shift their working hours ("Mars time") to stay synchronized with the landed spacecraft. Missions have traditionally run surface operations for a prime mission of 90 sols, and rovers have far exceeded that, with Opportunity operating for over 5,000 sols.

Martian time of day

Mission clocks divide the sol into 24 "Mars hours" (each 1/24 of a sol, slightly longer than an Earth hour), with correspondingly stretched minutes and seconds. Local mean solar time conventions, such as LMST and LTST, are used to timestamp images and activities.
