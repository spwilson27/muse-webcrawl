Jan Moretus

Jan Moretus, also Jan Moerentorf or Joannes Moretus (2 May 1543 – 22 September 1610), was a Flemish printer and bookseller. As son-in-law and successor of Christophe Plantin, he headed the Plantin Press in Antwerp — the largest printing house of early modern Europe — from 1589 until his death, steering it through the aftermath of the Dutch Revolt and consolidating the family business that his descendants ran for nearly three centuries.

Early life

Moretus was born in Antwerp, the son of a silk merchant who had changed the family name from Moerentorf to the Latinised Moretus. He entered Plantin's service as a young man, working as a corrector and clerk in the Officina Plantiniana, and in 1570 married Plantin's second daughter, Martina. His command of languages — Dutch, French, Latin, Italian and Spanish — made him indispensable to the firm's international trade.

Head of the Plantin Press

When Plantin died in 1589, Moretus inherited the Antwerp house and the famous compass device with the motto Labore et Constantia ("by labour and constancy"). Antwerp had fallen to Spanish forces in 1585 and its commerce was strangled by the Dutch blockade of the Scheldt, yet Moretus maintained the firm's output, concentrating on the profitable liturgical books — breviaries, missals and books of hours — for which Plantin had secured Spanish royal privileges, as well as scholarly editions and the works of the humanist Justus Lipsius, a close friend.

Legacy

Moretus was painted by Peter Paul Rubens, who also supplied designs for the firm's title pages. On his death in 1610, the business passed to his sons Balthazar and Jan II Moretus. The Plantin-Moretus printing dynasty continued in the same premises until 1876, when the site became the Plantin-Moretus Museum — now a UNESCO World Heritage Site preserving two of the world's oldest surviving printing presses and the firm's complete archives.
