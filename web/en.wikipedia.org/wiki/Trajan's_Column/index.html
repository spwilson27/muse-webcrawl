Trajan's Column (Italian: Colonna Traiana; Latin: Columna Traiani) is a Roman triumphal column in Rome, Italy, that commemorates Roman emperor Trajan's victory in the Dacian Wars. It was probably constructed under the supervision of the architect Apollodorus of Damascus at the order of the Roman Senate. It is located in Trajan's Forum, north of the Roman Forum. Completed in AD 113, the freestanding column is most famous for its spiral bas relief, which artfully describes the epic wars between the Romans and Dacians (101–102 and 105–106). Its design has inspired numerous victory columns, both ancient and modern.

The structure is about 30 metres (98 feet) in height, 35 metres (115 feet) including its large pedestal. The shaft is made from a series of 20 colossal Carrara marble drums each weighing about 32 tons, with a diameter of 3.7 metres (12.1 feet). The 190-metre (620-foot) frieze winds around the shaft 23 times. Inside the shaft, a spiral staircase of 185 steps provides access to a viewing platform at the top. The capital block of Trajan's Column weighs 53.3 tons, which had to be lifted to a height of about 34 metres (112 feet).

History

The column was dedicated in AD 113. According to an inscription on its pedestal, it was erected to show how high the hill and the place removed for the forum had been: the column marks the height of the saddle between the Quirinal and Capitoline hills that was cut away to build the forum. After Trajan's death in AD 117, the Senate voted to bury his ashes, together with those of his wife Plotina, in a golden urn at the base of the column, where they remained until at least the time of the visit of Constantius II in AD 357.

The original bronze statue of Trajan on top of the column disappeared in the Middle Ages. On 4 December 1587, Pope Sixtus V had the top crowned with a bronze statue of Saint Peter, sculpted by Leonardo Sormani and Tommaso della Porta, which remains to this day.

The frieze

The continuous spiral frieze, about 190 metres long, depicts the two Dacian wars in roughly 155 scenes, beginning with Roman soldiers preparing for war and ending with the suicide of the Dacian king Decebalus and the deportation of Dacian prisoners. Trajan himself appears in the narrative some 58 times. The reliefs were originally painted in colour, and metal weapons were inserted into the hands of the carved figures, making the frieze far more vivid than it appears today. Casts of the reliefs, made in the 19th and 20th centuries, are held by museums including the Victoria and Albert Museum in London and the Museo della Civiltà Romana in Rome.

Inscription

The dedicatory inscription, carved in the classical Roman square capitals known as capitalis monumentalis, is considered a masterpiece of Roman epigraphy and became the model for the Trajan typeface. It records that the Senate and People of Rome gave the column to the emperor to declare how high the hill and site removed for these works had been.
