Clean Development Mechanism

A registered CDM wind-power project.

The Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) is an arrangement under the Kyoto Protocol (Article 12) that allows industrialized countries with greenhouse-gas reduction commitments to implement emission-reduction projects in developing countries and earn saleable certified emission reduction (CER) credits, each equivalent to one tonne of CO2, which can be counted toward their Kyoto targets. It was the first international environmental investment and credit scheme of its kind.

Purposes

The mechanism has two stated aims: to assist developing countries in achieving sustainable development and contributing to the ultimate objective of the climate convention, and to help developed countries achieve compliance with their quantified emission limitation commitments in a cost-effective manner, since reductions may be cheaper where marginal abatement costs are lower.

Operation

Projects—such as renewable-energy installations, methane capture, or destruction of industrial gases like HFC-23—must demonstrate that emission reductions are additional to what would have occurred without the project. Proposals pass through a project cycle of design, validation by designated operational entities, registration by the CDM Executive Board under the UNFCCC, monitoring, verification, and issuance of CERs. An Adaptation Fund, financed by a levy on CERs, was established to help vulnerable countries adapt to climate change.

Criticism and legacy

The CDM has been criticized over the environmental integrity of some project types, uneven geographic distribution of projects (heavily concentrated in China and India), and perverse incentives, notably for HFC-23 destruction. Several thousand projects were registered, generating over a billion CERs. After the Kyoto commitment periods, the role of the CDM diminished as attention shifted to the Paris Agreement's Article 6 mechanisms, including the Article 6.4 sustainable development mechanism intended as its successor.
