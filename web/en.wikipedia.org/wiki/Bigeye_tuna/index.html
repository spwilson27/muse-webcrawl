The bigeye tuna (Thunnus obesus) is a species of true tuna of the genus Thunnus, belonging to the wider mackerel family Scombridae. Bigeye tuna are found in the open waters of all tropical and temperate oceans, but not in the Mediterranean Sea. Together with the yellowfin tuna and the Atlantic bluefin tuna, it is one of the principal market species of tuna and a staple of the sashimi trade.

Description

The bigeye is named for its notably large eyes, an adaptation for seeing in the dim light of deeper water. The body is deep and rounded — stockier than that of the yellowfin — with a long pectoral fin and dark metallic blue coloration above and silvery white below. Adults commonly reach lengths of 180 cm and weights of over 100 kg; exceptional individuals exceed 200 kg. Like other tunas, the bigeye is a regional endotherm, maintaining its body temperature above that of the surrounding water with a countercurrent heat-exchange system (rete mirabile), which allows it to forage in cold, deep water.

Behaviour and ecology

Bigeye tuna make pronounced daily vertical migrations, descending to depths of 300–500 metres during the day to feed on squid, crustaceans, and deep-water fishes, and returning to warmer surface waters at night. Their diet, depth tolerance, and physiological tolerance for low oxygen allow them to exploit prey unavailable to most other tuna species. Individuals may live for more than a decade, and tagging studies have shown migrations spanning entire ocean basins.

Fisheries

Bigeye is heavily fished by longline fleets, which target large adults for the high-value Japanese sashimi market, and by purse-seine fleets, which take juveniles around floating objects (FADs), often mixed with skipjack and yellowfin. The species is managed by regional fisheries management organisations including the IOTC, WCPFC, ICCAT, and IATTC. Because of sustained fishing pressure — especially the incidental harvest of juveniles by purse seiners — the IUCN lists the bigeye tuna as Vulnerable, and several stocks have been assessed as overfished.
