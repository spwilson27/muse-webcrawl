Dynkin diagram

In the mathematical field of Lie theory, a Dynkin diagram, named for Eugene Dynkin, is a type of graph with some edges doubled or tripled (drawn as a double or triple line). Dynkin diagrams arise in the classification of semisimple Lie algebras over algebraically closed fields, in the classification of root systems, and in other contexts.

Construction

Given a root system with a set of simple roots, the Dynkin diagram has one node per simple root; two nodes are joined by 4 cos²θ edges (0, 1, 2, or 3), where θ is the angle between the roots, and an arrow points toward the shorter root when the roots joined by a multiple edge have different lengths. The diagram encodes the Cartan matrix completely.

Classification

The connected Dynkin diagrams of finite type are exactly the four infinite families An, Bn, Cn, Dn and the five exceptional diagrams E6, E7, E8, F4, and G2. They classify, among other things:

  • finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebras over ℂ (or any algebraically closed field of characteristic zero);
  • irreducible finite root systems;
  • finite reflection groups (via the closely related Coxeter diagrams);
  • finite-type quivers (Gabriel's theorem) and cluster algebras of finite type.

Related diagrams

Removing the arrows gives a Coxeter–Dynkin diagram, used for Coxeter groups; allowing indefinite types yields generalized Cartan matrices and Kac–Moody algebras, whose diagrams include the affine (extended) Dynkin diagrams, such as Ẽ8. The ADE pattern recurs throughout mathematics, for example in the McKay correspondence and the classification of simple singularities.

History

The diagrams are named after Eugene Dynkin, who used them in 1947 to simplify the classification of semisimple Lie algebras; essentially the same classification had been obtained earlier by Wilhelm Killing and Élie Cartan via Cartan matrices.
