The Nguyễn dynasty (Vietnamese: Nhà Nguyễn) was the last dynasty of Vietnam, ruling from 1802 until 1945. Its emperors, descended from the Nguyễn lords who had governed southern Vietnam (Đàng Trong) since the 16th century, reunified the country after the Tây Sơn wars and ruled first as independent monarchs and, from the 1880s, as figureheads under the French protectorate.

Founding and early reigns

Nguyễn Phúc Ánh, with French assistance arranged through the missionary Pierre Pigneau de Béhaine, defeated the Tây Sơn and proclaimed himself Emperor Gia Long in 1802, giving the country the name Việt Nam (formalized in 1804). He built the great citadel at Huế on the model of Beijing's Forbidden City. His son Minh Mạng (r. 1820–1841) centralized the administration, organized the country into provinces, and pursued Confucian orthodoxy while persecuting Catholic missionaries. Thiệu Trị and Tự Đức (r. 1847–1883) continued these conservative policies even as French pressure grew.

French colonization

France seized Saigon in 1859 and Cochinchina by 1867, then imposed protectorates over Annam and Tonkin by the treaties of 1883–84. A series of short-reigned emperors followed — several deposed or exiled for resistance, including Hàm Nghi, who led the Cần Vương movement, and Duy Tân, exiled after a failed revolt in 1916. Khải Định and his son Bảo Đại reigned as nominal sovereigns within French Indochina.

End of the dynasty

In March 1945 the Japanese coup d'état ended French administration and Bảo Đại proclaimed the Empire of Vietnam. Following the August Revolution, he abdicated on 30 August 1945 in favour of Hồ Chí Minh's Democratic Republic of Vietnam, formally ending 143 years of Nguyễn rule. The dynasty's monuments at Huế — the Imperial City, royal tombs, and temples — survive as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
