Approval voting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Approval voting is a single-winner electoral system in which voters may vote for any number of candidates they approve of, and the candidate approved by the most voters wins. Formalised by political scientists in the 1970s (Brams and Fishburn) and used historically in various forms (medieval Venetian doge elections among them), it makes every contest's ballot a set of independent yes/no judgments rather than a single mark.

Properties and use

Advocates argue it eliminates vote-splitting and the spoiler effect (voters can approve both a favourite and a compromise), rewards broadly acceptable candidates, is exceptionally simple to explain and count, and always lets voters support their true favourite without penalty — sidestepping some classic impossibility results that bind ranked systems (no ranking means no cycles of the Arrow type). Critics answer that it discards preference intensity information and can be strategically gamed by "bullet voting". Real-world adoption is modest but real: several professional societies elect by it, and US cities including Fargo (2018) and St. Louis (2020, in a combined approval-primary form) adopted it for municipal elections — giving electoral reformers a live experiment alongside ranked-choice voting.
