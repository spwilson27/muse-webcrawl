Monetarism

[Image: a central bank printing hall producing banknotes]

Monetarism is a school of Macroeconomics associated above all with Milton Friedman and the Chicago school, holding that the supply of Money is the dominant determinant of nominal income and, in the long run, of Inflation. Its slogan form: inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon".

Doctrine and policy

Building on the quantity theory (MV = PY) and restated by Friedman in 1956, monetarism held money demand to be a stable function, so erratic money growth — as in the Federal Reserve's failures documented in Friedman and Schwartz's Monetary History (1963) — causes cycles, while steady growth of money at a fixed rate (the k-percent rule) would largely stabilise the economy. Monetarists argued the Phillips curve trade-off vanishes once expectations adjust, leaving a natural rate of unemployment. In the late 1970s–80s, central banks in the US, UK and Germany adopted money-growth targets during the war on inflation — a practical monetarist moment — but unstable velocity made the targets unworkable and they were quietly dropped in favour of direct inflation targeting, the regime now standard in monetary policy. Monetarism's long-run neutrality and expectations insights were absorbed into the mainstream even as its operating rule was abandoned.
