Relict (biology)

In biogeography and ecology, a relict is a population or taxon that was once widespread or is found in a much larger and continuous area in the past, but now survives in a limited, isolated area. The term is also used in geology for a landform left behind from a retreating process.

Definitions

A relict population may represent a surviving remnant of a formerly diverse group, a species whose range has contracted because of environmental change, or a population that has been left behind as neighbouring populations migrated. Relicts often persist in refugia — areas whose local conditions remained suitable while the surrounding region changed, such as mountain tops, deep valleys, islands, or areas near glaciers.

Glacial relicts

During the Pleistocene ice ages, many cold-adapted species ranged far to the south. When the climate warmed, most retreated northward, but some populations became stranded in cool microclimates at high elevations. The arctic-alpine flora of European and North American mountains contains many such glacial relicts, including species of Dryas and various saxifrages.

Examples

  • The coelacanth, a lobe-finned fish known from fossils and thought extinct until a living specimen was caught in 1938, is often called a relict (and a "living fossil").
  • The tuatara of New Zealand is the sole survivor of the order Rhynchocephalia.
  • Ginkgo biloba is the only living species of the division Ginkgophyta.
  • Relict populations of the arctic fox and other boreal species persist on isolated Scandinavian mountains.
