Huangjiu

Huangjiu (Chinese: 黃酒, "yellow wine") is a type of Chinese alcoholic beverage made by fermenting water and cereal grains such as rice, sorghum, millet or wheat with a starter culture (qu). Unlike baijiu, it is not distilled, and it typically contains less than 20% alcohol. Huangjiu has been produced in China for millennia and is traditionally drunk warm, as well as used extensively in Chinese cooking.

Production

The grain is steamed, cooled and inoculated with qu, which contains yeasts, moulds and bacteria that saccharify the starch and ferment the sugars simultaneously. After fermentation the wine is pressed, clarified, pasteurised and aged — premium grades in sealed earthenware jars for years or decades.

Styles

The most famous production centre is Shaoxing in Zhejiang province, whose wines are categorised by sweetness: dry yuanhong, semi-dry jiafan (so named because extra rice is added), semi-sweet shanniang and sweet xiangxue. A celebrated custom is to bury jars of wine at a daughter's birth and present them at her wedding, giving rise to the name nü'erhong ("daughter's red"). Regions elsewhere in China — Shandong with its millet-based jimo laojiu, and Fujian with its red-yeast wines — make distinctive local styles. Huangjiu is also an ingredient in dishes such as red-braised pork and drunken chicken.
