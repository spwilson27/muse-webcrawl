Out-of-body experience

An out-of-body experience (OBE) is an experience in which a person feels as if they are perceiving the physical world from a location outside their physical body. In some cases an experient claims to see their own body lying below them. OBEs are reported in an estimated 5–10% of the general population, and are associated with sleep paralysis, lucid dreaming, extreme physical exertion, meditation, psychedelic substances, and near-death experiences.

Phenomenology

Typical features include a sensation of floating, vibration or a "snap" at onset, autoscopy (seeing one's own body from outside), movement by intention, and passage through solid objects. Experiences may occur spontaneously on the border of sleep (hypnagogic or hypnopompic states) or be deliberately induced.

Scientific explanations

Neuroscience research attributes OBEs to disrupted multisensory integration of bodily signals. Electrical stimulation and lesions of the temporo-parietal junction (TPJ), which integrates visual, tactile and vestibular information, can induce OBE-like phenomena; in 2002 Olaf Blanke and colleagues triggered out-of-body sensations in a patient by stimulating the right TPJ. OBEs are also linked to the vestibular system and to body-schema disorders, and are studied as a model for understanding bodily self-consciousness.

Cultural and historical aspects

Beliefs in the soul leaving the body appear in many cultures, from ancient Egyptian concepts of the ba to shamanic journeying. In the 19th and 20th centuries the phenomenon was taken up by Theosophy and by writers on "astral projection" such as Sylvan Muldoon, Robert Monroe and Celia Green, whose 1968 study catalogued cases scientifically. Parapsychological experiments testing veridical perception during OBEs have not produced replicable evidence of perception from a genuinely disembodied viewpoint.
