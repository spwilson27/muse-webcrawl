Cork (Irish: Corcaigh, from corcach, "marsh") is the second-largest city in the Republic of Ireland and the largest city in the province of Munster. It stands on the River Lee in the south-west of Ireland, where the river splits around the city centre island before flowing into Cork Harbour, one of the world's largest natural harbours. Cork is nicknamed the "Rebel City" (or "Rebel County") from its history of opposition to English rule.

The River Lee flows through the centre of Cork.

History

Cork originated as a monastic settlement founded by Saint Finbarr around the 6th century, became a Viking trading port in the 10th century, and received its first charter from Prince John in 1185. The walled medieval town grew wealthy on trade; later centuries saw expansion onto reclaimed marshland, giving the centre its channels and quays. Cork was a stronghold of nationalist sentiment: during the Irish War of Independence, much of the city centre was burned by British forces in December 1920 ("the Burning of Cork"), and its lord mayor Terence MacSwiney died on hunger strike the same year.

Economy and sights

Modern Cork is a centre for pharmaceuticals, medical devices, food, and technology (Apple's European headquarters is at Hollyhill), and its port handles substantial trade. Landmarks include St Fin Barre's Cathedral, Shandon Church with its famous bells, the English Market (operating since 1788), Blackrock Castle, University College Cork (founded 1845), and the Crawford Art Gallery. The city was European Capital of Culture in 2005.

Transport

Cork Airport lies south of the city; Kent Station provides rail links to Dublin, and ferry services connect Ringaskiddy with France.
