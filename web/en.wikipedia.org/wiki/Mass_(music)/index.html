The Mass (Latin: missa), a form of sacred musical composition, is a choral composition that sets the invariable portions of the Eucharistic liturgy (principally that of the Catholic Church, the Anglican Communion, and Lutheranism) to music. Musical masses take their name from the Catholic liturgy called the Mass. Most masses are settings of the Latin texts of the Ordinary of the Mass: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus (with Benedictus) and Agnus Dei.

History

Gregorian chant supplied monophonic settings of the Mass texts in the Middle Ages. The earliest complete polyphonic setting of the Ordinary by a single composer is Guillaume de Machaut's Messe de Nostre Dame (c. 1365). In the Renaissance, cyclic masses — unified by a common cantus firmus, often a secular tune such as L'homme armé — became the dominant large-scale genre, with composers including Dufay, Ockeghem, Josquin des Prez, Palestrina and Byrd. The Council of Trent's concern for textual intelligibility influenced the restrained polyphony associated with Palestrina's Missa Papae Marcelli.

Baroque to modern settings

Baroque and Classical composers wrote concerted masses for voices, orchestra and continuo — Bach's Mass in B minor and the masses of Haydn and Mozart among them. Beethoven's Missa solemnis (1823) and Bruckner's masses extended the form to symphonic scale. In the 20th and 21st centuries composers such as Stravinsky, Britten (War Requiem, which interleaves the Requiem Mass with war poetry) and James MacMillan have continued the tradition.

Structure

The five sections of the Ordinary — Kyrie eleison, Gloria in excelsis Deo, Credo, Sanctus and Agnus Dei — form the standard core; some settings add movements such as the Offertory or the concluding Ite, missa est. A Requiem Mass sets the texts of the Mass for the Dead, substituting the Introit, Dies irae and other Proper texts.
