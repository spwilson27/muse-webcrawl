Sense and reference

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sense (German: Sinn) and reference (Bedeutung) — Gottlob Frege's two components of meaning, 1892.

In the philosophy of language, the distinction between sense and reference was introduced by the German philosopher and logician Gottlob Frege in his 1892 essay "Über Sinn und Bedeutung" ("On Sense and Reference"). Frege argued that a proper name or other referring expression has both a reference (Bedeutung) — the object it stands for — and a sense (Sinn) — the mode of presentation of that object, the way the object is given to thought.

The motivating puzzles

The distinction explains how an identity statement can be informative. "The morning star is the evening star" is a genuine astronomical discovery, while "the morning star is the morning star" is trivial — yet both names refer to the same object, Venus. On Frege's account the two names have the same reference but different senses. The distinction also handles substitution failures in propositional attitude reports: "Lois Lane believes Superman can fly" may be true while "Lois Lane believes Clark Kent can fly" is false, because in such contexts, Frege held, expressions refer to their customary senses.

Extension of the theory

Frege extended the distinction to whole sentences: the sense of a sentence is the thought (Gedanke) it expresses, and its reference is its truth value — the True or the False. Definite descriptions and other complex expressions have senses composed from the senses of their parts, making Fregean semantics systematically compositional.

Influence and criticism

The sense–reference distinction shaped analytic philosophy through Russell's theory of descriptions and the work of Carnap, Church, and Dummett. It was challenged by direct reference theorists such as Saul Kripke and Hilary Putnam, who argued that proper names have no descriptive sense but refer rigidly through causal-historical chains — though most philosophers continue to find some notion of sense indispensable for explaining cognitive significance.
