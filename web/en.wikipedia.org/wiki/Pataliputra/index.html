Pataliputra

Pataliputra (IAST: Pāṭaliputra), adjacent to modern-day Patna in Bihar, India, was a city in ancient India, originally built by Magadha ruler Ajatashatru in 490 BCE as a small fort (Pataligrama) near the Ganges river. It became the capital of major powers in India, including the Nanda Empire, the Maurya Empire, the Shunga Empire, and the Gupta Empire.

Excavated wooden palisade of ancient Pataliputra at Kumhrar, Patna.

History

During the Maurya period (around 320–180 BCE), Pataliputra was among the largest cities in the world, with a population estimated at between 150,000 and 400,000. The Greek ambassador Megasthenes, at the court of Chandragupta Maurya, described it in his Indica as a vast fortified city at the confluence of the Ganges and Son rivers, protected by a wooden palisade with 570 towers and 64 gates, and administered by committees of officials. His account is echoed by the Arthashastra attributed to Kautilya.

Under Ashoka, Pataliputra was the seat of the Mauryan empire at its greatest extent and, by tradition, the site of the Third Buddhist council. The city remained the Gupta capital in the 4th–6th centuries CE, visited by the Chinese pilgrim Faxian, who described its prosperity and charitable institutions. Thereafter it declined, and when Xuanzang visited in the 7th century he found it largely in ruins. Sher Shah Suri refounded the city in the 16th century as Patna, which became a major trading centre.

Excavations

Archaeological excavations at Kumhrar and Agam Kuan in Patna have uncovered the remains of an eighty-pillared hall, wooden palisades, and other Mauryan-period structures, confirming the classical accounts of the city's scale.
