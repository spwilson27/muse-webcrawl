Mantle convection

Mantle convection is the very slow creeping motion of Earth's solid silicate mantle as it flows in response to heat escaping from the planet's interior. Although the mantle is solid, over geological time it behaves as an extremely viscous fluid: hot material rises from depth, cools at the top boundary, and sinks again. Mantle convection is the fundamental mechanism by which Earth loses internal heat and is the driving process behind plate tectonics, volcanism and the generation of much of the planet's topography.

Mechanism

Heat reaches the mantle from the core below and from radioactive decay within the mantle itself. Because rock expands when heated, hot material is buoyant and rises, while the cold upper boundary layer — the lithosphere, broken into tectonic plates — becomes dense and eventually sinks at subduction zones. Typical flow speeds are a few centimetres per year. The mantle's Rayleigh number is enormously supercritical, but its huge viscosity means flow is laminar rather than turbulent.

Whole-mantle versus layered convection

Whether convection involves the entire mantle or is layered at the 660 km discontinuity was long debated. Seismic tomography images of subducted slabs penetrating into the lower mantle, and geochemical arguments, now support predominantly whole-mantle convection, with some slabs temporarily stalled at the boundary. Rising mantle plumes from the core–mantle boundary are thought to feed hotspots such as Hawaii and Iceland.

Heat budget

Earth's total surface heat flow is about 47 terawatts, roughly half radiogenic and half secular cooling of the planet, including heat from the core that also powers the geodynamo.
