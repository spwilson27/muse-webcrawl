Diastereomer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Diastereomers of a molecule with two stereocentres]

In stereochemistry, diastereomers (or diastereoisomers) are a type of stereoisomer: compounds with the same molecular formula and the same connectivity of atoms that are not mirror images of each other and are not superposable. Diastereomers can occur when a molecule has two or more stereogenic centres and the configuration differs at some, but not all, of them; stereoisomers that differ at every centre in exact mirror-image fashion are enantiomers instead.

Properties

Unlike enantiomers, which have identical physical properties in an achiral environment, diastereomers have different physical and chemical properties: different melting and boiling points, solubilities, densities, NMR spectra, and rates of reaction. This makes them separable by ordinary techniques such as fractional crystallisation, distillation, and chromatography — the basis of resolutions of racemic mixtures via diastereomeric salt formation with a chiral resolving agent.

Examples and nomenclature

Tartaric acid illustrates the distinction: (R,R)- and (S,S)-tartaric acid are enantiomers, while the meso form (R,S) is a diastereomer of each. For alkenes, cis/trans (or E/Z) isomers are diastereomers. In carbohydrate chemistry, diastereomers differing at a single stereocentre are called epimers — D-glucose and D-mannose, for example, are C-2 epimers — and those differing at the anomeric centre are called anomers.

Diastereoselectivity

A reaction that produces one diastereomer preferentially is called diastereoselective, and the ratio of products is expressed as the diastereomeric ratio (dr) or, relative to the pure diastereomer, the diastereomeric excess (de). Controlling diastereoselectivity — through substrate control, chiral auxiliaries, or catalysts — is a central concern of modern organic synthesis, since diastereomers of a drug or natural product may have very different biological activity.
