Patrona Halil (c. 1690 – 25 November 1730) was an Ottoman rebel leader whose uprising in 1730 overthrew Sultan Ahmed III and brought an end to the Tulip Period of Ottoman history. A former sailor and Janissary of Albanian or Greek origin, Halil became the spokesman and effective ruler of the Istanbul mob for some two months before he was lured into a trap and killed on the orders of the new sultan, Mahmud I.

Background

Halil's nickname Patrona came from his service as a sailor aboard the Ottoman vessel Patrona ("vice-admiral's ship"). He had earlier served as a Janissary in the provinces, had been involved in unrest on the Persian frontier, and by 1730 was working as an attendant in a hammam (public bath) in Constantinople. Discontent in the capital was rising: the war against Persia was going badly, taxes were heavy, and the court of Ahmed III and his grand vizier Nevşehirli Damat Ibrahim Pasha was widely resented for its extravagant spending and westernising fashions during the Tulip Period.

The rebellion of 1730

On 28 September 1730, Halil and a small group of followers — traditionally said to be only seventeen men — marched through the city calling on the populace to rise, starting near the Beyazit Mosque and the Grand Bazaar. Janissaries, artisans and the urban poor flocked to the standard. The rebels demanded the execution of the grand vizier and other officials; Ibrahim Pasha and two other officials were handed over and killed. Within days Ahmed III was forced to abdicate in favour of his nephew Mahmud I.

For some weeks Halil, though holding no formal office, dictated appointments, rode through the city escorted by armed followers, and terrified the new regime. The rebels' demands included the abolition of new taxes and the demolition of the pleasure palaces of the Tulip Period along the Golden Horn and Bosphorus, including much of the Sadabad complex.

Death

Sultan Mahmud I and his advisers, unwilling to remain puppets, plotted Halil's removal. On 25 November 1730 Halil and his closest associates were summoned to the palace, ostensibly to receive honours and appointments — Halil was to be made governor of Rumelia. In the imperial audience chamber they were set upon by the sultan's guards; Halil fought fiercely but was killed along with some 7,000 of his followers in the ensuing purge over the following days. His death restored the authority of the palace, though the Tulip Period was not revived.

Legacy

The Patrona Halil rebellion is remembered as one of the great urban uprisings of Ottoman history, ending an era of cultural opening to the West. The episode is dramatised in a famous painting by Jean-Baptiste van Mour and in later Ottoman and Turkish literature; the hammam where Halil worked stood for centuries near the Beyazit square in Istanbul.
