Jester

A jester, also known as a court jester or fool, was a member of the household of a nobleman or monarch employed to entertain the court during the medieval and Renaissance eras. Jesters entertained with a wide variety of skills: songs and music, storytelling, acrobatics, juggling, and joke-telling. Many jesters made contemporary jokes about people or events well known to their audiences, giving their employers something of a satirical news service.

Costume and role

The jester's traditional costume, called motley, was brightly coloured and patchwork, and he wore a distinctive hat — often a hood with donkey ears or a cap with three points, each tipped with a bell — and carried a mock sceptre called a bauble, sometimes topped with a carved fool's head. Jesters occupied a paradoxical position: low-status entertainers, yet enjoying a unique licence to speak frankly, mocking courtiers and even the monarch in ways that would have been dangerous for anyone else. The figure of the "wise fool", who uses humour to deliver unwelcome truth, became a staple of literature.

History

Fools and entertainers were kept at courts from ancient times: pharaonic Egypt, imperial Rome, China and India all had court clowns and dwarfs in attendance. In medieval Europe the jester developed into a recognisable court office. Famous English jesters include Will Sommers, fool to Henry VIII, and Archy Armstrong, jester to James I and Charles I, dismissed in 1637 for insulting Archbishop Laud. In Poland, Stańczyk, jester to three 16th-century kings, became a national symbol of patriotic conscience. In France the office survived into the 17th century, and jesters such as Triboulet served Francis I.

In literature

Shakespeare gave the fool some of his most penetrating lines: the Fool in King Lear, Touchstone in As You Like It and Feste in Twelfth Night all use wit to expose folly and grief. Other notable fictional jesters include Rigoletto in Verdi's opera, based on Victor Hugo's play Le roi s'amuse, and Hop-Frog in Edgar Allan Poe's short story. The jester's image survives in the joker of playing cards and in the modern figure of the clown and the political satirist.
