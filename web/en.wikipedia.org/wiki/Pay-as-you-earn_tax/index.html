Pay-as-you-earn tax

A pay-as-you-earn tax (PAYE), or pay-as-you-go (PAYG) as it is known in Australia, is a withholding tax on income payments to employees. Under such systems, income tax and, in many countries, social security contributions are deducted by the employer directly from wages or salary before payment, and remitted to the tax authority on the employee's behalf.

A typical payslip, showing tax withheld at source under a PAYE system.

History

Withholding income tax at source was pioneered in the United Kingdom during the Second World War. Before PAYE, most British workers paid income tax in arrears, often in lump sums they had not saved for. PAYE was introduced in 1944 following work by Sir Kingsley Wood and civil servant Paul Chambers, spreading the collection of tax across the year and dramatically widening the tax base as wartime wages brought millions of workers into tax for the first time. The United States adopted a similar system in 1943 under the Current Tax Payment Act.

Operation

Employers calculate the tax due on each payment using tax tables or a tax code issued by the revenue authority, which reflects the employee's personal allowances and other income. Amounts withheld are paid over to the authority monthly or quarterly. At the end of the tax year, the employee's total withholding is reconciled against their actual liability; overpayments are refunded and underpayments collected. PAYE systems are used in the United Kingdom, Ireland, New Zealand, South Africa and many other countries; Australia uses the analogous PAYG withholding system.

Advantages and criticism

Advocates note that withholding smooths government revenue, reduces evasion and spares employees large end-of-year bills. Critics argue it lowers the salience of taxation, imposes compliance costs on employers, and can produce persistent errors where tax codes are inaccurate.
