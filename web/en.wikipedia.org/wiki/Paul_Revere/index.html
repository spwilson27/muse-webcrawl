Paul Revere (1735 – 10 May 1818) was an American silversmith, engraver, and Patriot in the American Revolution. He is best remembered for his ride on the night of 18–19 April 1775, warning the Massachusetts militia of approaching British forces before the battles of Lexington and Concord — a feat immortalised, with considerable embellishment, in Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's 1861 poem "Paul Revere's Ride". A prosperous Boston craftsman, Revere also produced influential propaganda engravings and later became a pioneering industrialist in copper and iron.

Early life and craft

Revere was born in Boston's North End to Apollos Rivoire (later de Rivoire/Revere), a French Huguenot silversmith, and Deborah Hichborn, of an old Boston family. Apprenticed to his father, he took over the silver shop in his early twenties. Revere's surviving silver — tea services, bowls, and spoons — is considered among the finest of colonial America and is held by major museums. He also worked as a dentist, engraved political cartoons and bookplates, and printed currency for the revolutionary government of Massachusetts.

Revolutionary activity

A member of the Sons of Liberty and of the North End caucus with Samuel Adams and Joseph Warren, Revere took part in the Boston Tea Party of December 1773 and served as a courier between Boston and the Continental Congress. His famous engraving of the Boston Massacre (1770), copied from a design by Henry Pelham, became a potent piece of Patriot propaganda.

The Midnight Ride

On the night of 18 April 1775, warned that British regulars were marching to seize stores at Concord, Revere rowed across the Charles River (having arranged the famous lantern signal in the Old North Church steeple — "one if by land, and two if by sea", as Longfellow put it), borrowed a horse, and rode through Charlestown and Lexington, warning households and the militia, and alerting John Hancock and Samuel Adams. Fellow riders William Dawes and Samuel Prescott joined the errand; Revere was captured by a British patrol near Lincoln, questioned, and released without his horse, reaching Lexington on foot in time to witness the aftermath. Prescott alone completed the ride to Concord. The battles the next morning opened the American Revolutionary War. Revere later served as a militia officer, including in the disastrous Penobscot Expedition of 1779, after which he faced a court of inquiry and was eventually cleared.

Later life

After the war Revere expanded into iron casting, bell founding (his firm cast nearly 400 church bells), copper rolling — his Canton, Massachusetts plant supplied sheathing for the USS Constitution and the dome of the Massachusetts State House — and hardware. The company he founded endures in successor form in the American copper industry. He died in Boston in 1818 and is buried in the Granary Burying Ground. His Boston home, the Paul Revere House (c. 1680), is now a museum on the Freedom Trail.

Legacy

Longfellow's poem transformed a capable but second-tier revolutionary organiser into a lone folk hero, a mythology historians have both corrected and recognised as central to American memory of the Revolution.
