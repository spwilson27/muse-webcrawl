Geʾez (ግእዝ, Gəʾəz) is an ancient South Semitic language that originated in the Horn of Africa, in the region of the northern Ethiopian highlands and present-day Eritrea. It was the language of the Kingdom of Aksum and served for centuries as the official language of the Ethiopian Empire. Although it ceased to be a commonly spoken vernacular by around the 10th–12th centuries, Geʾez remains in use today as the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and Beta Israel Jewish communities.

Geʾez is written in its own script, the Geʾez script, an abugida in which each character represents a consonant–vowel syllable. The script was later adapted to write many other Ethiopian languages, including Amharic and Tigrinya.

History

The earliest inscriptions in a precursor of Geʾez date to the pre-Aksumite period (roughly the 9th–5th centuries BC), written in the Ancient South Arabian (Epigraphic South Arabian) script. By the 4th century AD, under King Ezana of Aksum, the distinct Geʾez script had developed and the Aksumite court had adopted Christianity, prompting a major programme of translation of the Bible and other religious texts from Greek into Geʾez. This period produced a substantial literary corpus, including works such as the Kebra Nagast tradition and numerous translations of patristic writings, some of which survive only in their Geʾez versions.

As vernacular speech in the highlands shifted toward languages such as Amharic and Tigrinya, Geʾez persisted as the language of literature, royal chronicles, liturgy, law and scholarship. Its classical grammatical tradition is described in works such as the Säwasəw, the traditional grammar of Geʾez.

Phonology and grammar

Geʾez preserves a number of archaic Semitic features, including a series of emphatic (glottalised) consonants — ṭ, ṣ, ṗ — characteristic of Ethiopian Semitic languages. Its verbal system is built on triconsonantal roots in the Semitic mould, with derived stems marking causative, reflexive and passive formations. The language distinguishes two genders, masculine and feminine, and has singular and plural number, with plurals often formed by internal ("broken") patterns as well as suffixation.

Literature

The Geʾez canon includes the Ethiopian biblical canon — notably broader than most others, containing books such as the Book of Enoch and Jubilees preserved in full only in Geʾez — hagiographies (gädl), royal chronicles, theological treatises, hymnody (qene poetry), and juridical texts such as the Fetha Nagast. Manuscript production continued in Geʾez well into the modern era, and the language is still taught in church schools.
