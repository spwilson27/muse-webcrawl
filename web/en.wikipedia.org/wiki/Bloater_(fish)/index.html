The bloater (Coregonus hoyi) is a species or form of freshwater whitefish in the family Salmonidae. It is a silvery-coloured fish with a pink and purple iridescence that lives in the deep waters of the Great Lakes of North America. It is named for the swollen appearance it develops when hauled rapidly to the surface from great depth, as gases in its swim bladder expand.

Description

The bloater is a small cisco, averaging about 23 cm (9 in) in length, though individuals may reach 36 cm. Its body is laterally compressed and silvery, and the snout is somewhat blunt with the lower jaw slightly projecting. Like other ciscoes it possesses an adipose fin and feeds primarily on plankton and benthic invertebrates such as Mysis and amphipods.

Distribution and habitat

Bloaters inhabit deep, cold water, typically between 30 and 180 metres deep. Historically they were found in all of the Great Lakes except Lake Erie. They are most abundant in Lake Michigan and Lake Huron, and have been reintroduced to Lake Ontario. Spawning occurs in winter over deep-water reefs.

Fishery and decline

The bloater was once one of the most valuable commercial fishes of the Great Lakes, prized when smoked. Populations collapsed in the mid-20th century owing to overfishing and predation by the invasive sea lamprey and alewife. Although commercial catches remain much reduced, populations in Lake Michigan have partially recovered, and the species is an important forage fish for lake trout and introduced salmon.

Taxonomy

The species was described by James Wood Milner in 1874 and named in honour of Dr Philo Romayne Hoy, a Wisconsin naturalist. Some authorities have treated it as a subspecies of the Arctic cisco complex.
