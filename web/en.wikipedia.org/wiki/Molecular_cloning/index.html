Molecular cloning

Molecular cloning is a set of experimental methods in molecular biology used to assemble recombinant DNA molecules and to direct their replication within host organisms. The word cloning refers to the method's production of many identical copies of a DNA molecule from a single ancestor molecule: in outline, a DNA fragment of interest is inserted into a self-replicating vector (commonly a bacterial plasmid or viral genome), the recombinant molecule is introduced into host cells, and cells carrying it are selected and grown, yielding large amounts of the cloned DNA and, often, of the protein it encodes.

Outline of the method

A classic cloning experiment proceeds in four steps. First, the DNA to be cloned is fragmented or amplified — traditionally with restriction enzymes that cut at specific sequences, today often by the polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Second, the fragments are joined to the vector with DNA ligase. Third, the recombinant DNA is introduced into host cells by transformation or transduction. Fourth, cells that have taken up the vector are identified using selectable markers, most often antibiotic resistance genes carried on the vector, and screened for the desired insert, historically by blue–white screening and today commonly by colony PCR and DNA sequencing.

Vectors

Plasmid vectors are small circular DNA molecules carrying an origin of replication, a selectable marker and a multiple cloning site. Variants include cosmids and bacterial artificial chromosomes (BACs), which carry much larger inserts, and yeast artificial chromosomes (YACs). Expression vectors add promoters and other elements so that the cloned gene's protein product is produced in the host.

Applications and history

The foundational experiments of 1972–1973 by Paul Berg, Stanley Cohen, Herbert Boyer and colleagues created the first recombinant DNA molecules and launched modern biotechnology; commercial products of cloning include recombinant human insulin and growth hormone. The technology underlies genome sequencing projects, functional genomics, transgenic organisms and gene therapy research. Concern over its safety prompted the 1975 Asilomar Conference, whose guidelines shaped subsequent regulation of recombinant DNA work. Newer assembly methods — Gibson assembly, Golden Gate cloning and CRISPR-based editing — have extended and partly displaced restriction-enzyme cloning in routine practice.
