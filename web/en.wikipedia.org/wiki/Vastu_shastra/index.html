Vastu shastra

Vastu shastra (Sanskrit: vāstu śāstra, "science of architecture/dwelling") is a traditional Indian system of architecture and design described in a body of Sanskrit texts. It prescribes principles for the layout, orientation, proportions and spatial geometry of buildings and settlements, aiming to harmonise structures with nature, the cardinal directions and cosmic order. Its concepts underlie much historic Hindu temple architecture and continue to influence building practice in India today.

Principles

The core device of vastu shastra is the vastu purusha mandala, a square diagram divided into a grid of smaller squares (commonly 64 or 81), assigned to deities and to the Vastu Purusha, the spirit of the site, whose body is imagined pinned face-down upon the plan by the gods. Parts of a building are placed according to this grid and to the elements (pancha bhuta): kitchens traditionally in the fire quarter (southeast, Agni), water elements in the northeast, heavy structures in the southwest, and entrances oriented to auspicious directions.

Texts and history

Rules for construction appear in the Vedas and later shilpa shastra literature, including the Manasara, Mayamata and Samarangana Sutradhara. Temple treatises applied the mandala to shrines whose towers (shikhara or vimana) embody sacred geometry. Town planning in texts such as the Arthashastra and the planning of Jaipur in the 18th century also reflect vastu principles.

Modern practice

Vastu consultants are widely employed in India for homes and offices, and some modern buildings — and even corporate headquarters and government buildings — have been oriented or modified to comply with vastu rules, a practice sometimes criticised as pseudoscientific by rationalist organisations. The system is frequently compared with the Chinese geomantic tradition of feng shui, with which it shares an emphasis on orientation and energy flow, though the two developed largely independently.
