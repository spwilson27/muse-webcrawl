Social identity approach

The social identity approach is an umbrella term in social psychology covering two related theories: social identity theory, originally developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner, and self-categorization theory, developed by Turner and colleagues. Together they explain how group memberships shape self-definition, perception, and behaviour — how thinking of oneself as "we" rather than "I" transforms individual psychology and underpins collective action, leadership, intergroup conflict, and organizational life.

Social identity theory

Tajfel's minimal group experiments in the early 1970s showed that even arbitrary categorization into groups (e.g., by coin toss or painting preference) produced in-group favouritism in resource allocation. Tajfel and Turner proposed that people derive part of their self-concept from group memberships and strive for a positive social identity, achieved through comparisons with out-groups. When social identity is unsatisfactory, people may leave their group (if boundaries are permeable) or pursue social creativity or social competition strategies for change.

Self-categorization theory

Turner's self-categorization theory (1980s) explains the cognitive processes by which people come to see themselves and others as group members. It proposes self-categorization at different levels of abstraction (personal, group, human) and the principle of meta-contrast: a category is formed when differences within it are smaller than differences from other categories. When a social identity is salient, self-perception becomes depersonalized — people see themselves as embodiments of the in-group prototype — enabling group phenomena such as conformity, polarization, leadership emergence, and influence.

Applications

The approach has been applied to prejudice and stereotyping, crowd behaviour (reinterpreting crowd action as identity-driven rather than irrational), organizational behaviour and leadership (the "social identity model of leadership"), health (the "social cure" research programme showing group ties benefit well-being), and collective action and social change. It is one of the most influential frameworks in contemporary social psychology.
