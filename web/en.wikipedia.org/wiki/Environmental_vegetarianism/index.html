Environmental vegetarianism

Environmental vegetarianism
MotivationReducing the environmental impact of meat production
Related dietsVegetarianism, veganism, flexitarianism
Key concernsGreenhouse gases, land and water use, deforestation, pollution

Environmental vegetarianism is the practice of adopting a vegetarian (or reduced-meat) diet out of concern for the environmental impacts of meat production, as distinct from vegetarianism motivated by animal welfare, religion, or health. Its advocates argue that shifting diets away from meat — especially beef and lamb — is one of the most effective ways for individuals and societies to reduce greenhouse-gas emissions, land use, and water consumption.

Rationale

Livestock production contributes a substantial share of global greenhouse-gas emissions through enteric fermentation, manure, feed-crop cultivation, and land-use change including deforestation. Animal agriculture also occupies a large fraction of agricultural land relative to the calories and protein it supplies, and contributes to eutrophication and biodiversity loss. Life-cycle analyses generally find plant foods have lower footprints than animal products, though impacts vary widely by food and production method.

Debates

Critics note that some grazing land is unsuitable for crops, that well-managed livestock can contribute to soil fertility, and that dietary change alone cannot substitute for decarbonising energy and transport. Environmental vegetarianism overlaps with, but is distinct from, ethical vegetarianism and veganism; many adherents are "flexitarians" who reduce rather than eliminate meat.
