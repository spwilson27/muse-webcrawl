Joseph Hooton Taylor Jr. (born March 29, 1941) is an American astrophysicist and Nobel Prize laureate in Physics for his discovery with Russell Alan Hulse of a "new type of pulsar, a discovery that has opened up new possibilities for the study of gravitation."

Binary pulsar

In 1974, while Taylor was a professor at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, his graduate student Russell Hulse discovered PSR B1913+16 — the first binary pulsar — using the Arecibo Observatory in Puerto Rico. Precise timing of the pulsar's signals over subsequent years showed that the two neutron stars' orbit is decaying exactly as predicted by general relativity's account of energy loss through gravitational radiation — the first indirect evidence for gravitational waves. Hulse and Taylor shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. The system remains a precision laboratory for testing general relativity.

Later work

Taylor moved to Princeton University in 1980 and later served as dean of the faculty. Outside his research he is a notable amateur radio operator (call sign K1JT) and the author of the WSJT software suite, whose weak-signal digital protocols such as FT8 have been widely adopted by radio amateurs and have also been used for moonbounce communication.
