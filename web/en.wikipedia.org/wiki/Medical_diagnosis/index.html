Medical diagnosis

Diagnosis begins with the history and physical examination

Medical diagnosis (often abbreviated Dx or Dx) is the process of determining which disease or condition explains a person's symptoms and signs. It is most often referred to simply as diagnosis in a medical context. The information required is usually gathered from a history and physical examination of the person seeking care, and one or more diagnostic procedures — such as laboratory tests, medical imaging, or biopsy — are often used during the process.

Process

Diagnosis is an iterative process of hypothesis generation and refinement. The clinician gathers information, forms an initial list of candidate conditions (the differential diagnosis), and then orders further history, examination, or tests to discriminate among them, weighing the prior probability of each condition against the likelihood ratios of new findings. A diagnosis may be described as provisional, working, or definitive depending on the certainty achieved. Diagnostic reasoning is a core skill taught in clinical medicine, and errors in diagnosis are a recognised source of patient harm.

Types

A differential diagnosis lists the possible causes of a presentation. A dual diagnosis refers to two coexisting conditions, and comorbidity to additional conditions alongside a primary diagnosis. Remote diagnosis takes place without the clinician being in the same location as the patient, as in telemedicine and radiology. Diagnosis by exclusion is reached by eliminating all other plausible causes. New categories also include diagnoses made or assisted by computer algorithms and artificial intelligence systems.

History

Systematic diagnosis dates to ancient Egyptian and Babylonian medicine; the Hippocratic corpus emphasised prognosis and observation, and Galen systematised diagnosis in the Roman era. The 19th and 20th centuries added laboratory medicine, bacteriology, radiography, and eventually cross-sectional imaging and molecular diagnostics, transforming diagnosis from an art of bedside observation into a technology-intensive discipline.

Challenges

Diagnostic error — missed, delayed, or wrong diagnoses — is estimated to affect a significant fraction of patients and is a leading cause of medical malpractice claims. Cognitive biases such as anchoring and premature closure, fragmented care, and time pressure contribute. Overdiagnosis, the detection of conditions that would never cause harm, is a growing concern, particularly in screening.
