STAT protein

STAT proteins (signal transducer and activator of transcription proteins) are a family of intracellular transcription factors that mediate immunity, cellular division, cell death, and tumour formation. They transmit information from extracellular chemical signals, such as cytokines and growth factors, through transmembrane receptors directly to the nucleus, where they activate gene transcription — the basis of the JAK-STAT signalling pathway.

There are seven mammalian STAT family members: STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5A, STAT5B, and STAT6. STAT proteins were first characterised in the early 1990s in studies of interferon signalling by James Darnell and colleagues.

Activation

When a cytokine binds its receptor, receptor-associated Janus kinases phosphorylate the receptor, creating docking sites for STAT proteins. The recruited STATs are themselves phosphorylated on a key tyrosine residue, dimerise via their SH2 domains, and translocate into the nucleus, where they bind specific DNA sequences (gamma-activated sequences) and switch on target genes. Negative regulators include SOCS proteins and protein tyrosine phosphatases.

Clinical relevance

Dysregulation of STAT signalling underlies many diseases: loss-of-function mutations in STAT3 cause hyper-IgE (Job's) syndrome, while gain-of-function mutations cause autoimmunity; constitutively active STAT3 is found in many cancers and is a drug target, and JAK inhibitors such as ruxolitinib act upstream of STATs in myeloproliferative neoplasms.
