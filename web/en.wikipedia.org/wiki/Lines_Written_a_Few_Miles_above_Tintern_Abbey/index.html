"Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey" is a poem by William Wordsworth, composed during a walking tour of the Wye valley in July 1798 and published the same year as the closing poem of Lyrical Ballads, the volume he produced with Samuel Taylor Coleridge that launched English Romanticism. It is often known simply as "Tintern Abbey", although the abbey itself is never described in the poem.

Content

Written in conversational blank verse, the poem revisits a landscape Wordsworth had first seen five years earlier, in 1793. It traces the growth of his response to nature: from the unthinking sensory delight of youth, through a period in which remembered landscapes sustained him in "lonely rooms, and 'mid the din / Of towns and cities", to a mature pantheistic awareness of "a sense sublime / Of something far more deeply interfused". The poem closes with an address to his sister Dorothy, in whose voice and eyes he hopes she will one day read her own future consolation.

Significance

The poem is a landmark of Romantic meditative verse: it made memory, the moral influence of nature, and the poet's own developing mind the proper subjects of serious poetry. Its vision of reciprocal influence between mind and landscape underlies Wordsworth's later autobiographical epic, The Prelude.
