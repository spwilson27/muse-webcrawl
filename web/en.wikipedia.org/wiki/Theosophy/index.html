Theosophy is a religious and philosophical system established in the United States in the late 19th century. Founded principally by Russian-born Helena Blavatsky, it draws its teachings predominantly from Blavatsky's writings.

Beliefs

Theosophy teaches an emanationist cosmology: a single absolute reality unfolds into a universe governed by karma and reincarnation, evolving through cycles and 'root races'. Hidden adepts or Mahatmas, notably Morya and Koot Hoomi, are said to preserve ancient wisdom. Core aims of the Theosophical Society are universal brotherhood, the comparative study of religion, and investigation of latent powers in humanity.

History

The Society was founded in New York in 1875 by Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott and William Quan Judge. It moved to Adyar, India, in 1882. Schisms produced rival organisations, including the Theosophical Society in America and the United Lodge of Theosophists. Under Annie Besant, the Society promoted Jiddu Krishnamurti as a world teacher, a role he renounced in 1929.

Influence

Theosophy helped introduce South and East Asian religious ideas—karma, reincarnation, meditation—to Western audiences and influenced modern art, the New Age movement, and later esoteric currents.
