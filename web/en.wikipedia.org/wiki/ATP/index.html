ATP

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

ATP
Full name: Adenosine triphosphate
Role: Universal energy currency of cells
Structure: Adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is an organic compound that provides energy for nearly every process in living cells — muscle contraction, nerve impulses, chemical synthesis, and cell division (see mitosis). It is often called the energy currency of the cell.

ATP stores energy in the bonds between its three phosphate groups. When the terminal phosphate is removed by hydrolysis, ATP becomes ADP (adenosine diphosphate) and releases energy that enzymes couple to work. Cells continuously recycle ADP back to ATP using energy from food — chiefly through cellular respiration: glycolysis, the citric-acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, in which the enzyme ATP synthase spins like a turbine as protons flow across the mitochondrial membrane. A human body turns over roughly its own mass in ATP each day, though at any instant it holds only a few dozen grams.

ATP was discovered in 1929 by Karl Lohmann (and independently by Fiske and Subbarow); its role as energy currency was proposed by Fritz Lipmann in 1941, and the chemiosmotic mechanism of its production earned Peter Mitchell the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
