Keloid

Keloid
SpecialtyDermatology
SymptomsRaised, firm scar growing beyond the original wound; itching, pain
CausesExcess collagen production during wound healing
Risk factorsGenetics; darker skin types; ear piercing, surgery, burns, acne

A keloid is a type of raised scar that results from an overgrowth of dense fibrous tissue (collagen) after an injury to the skin has healed. Unlike a hypertrophic scar, a keloid extends beyond the boundaries of the original wound, does not regress spontaneously, and tends to recur after surgical removal. The word derives from the Greek khēlē, meaning a crab's claw, describing the way the scar spreads into surrounding tissue.

Causes and risk factors

Keloids can develop after even minor skin trauma, including ear piercing, acne, insect bites, burns, surgery and vaccination. They are considerably more common in people with darker skin, particularly of African, Asian and Hispanic ancestry, with reported prevalence several times higher than in lighter-skinned populations, indicating a strong genetic component. Common sites include the earlobes, shoulders, upper back, chest and cheeks. Keloids usually appear in people between the ages of 10 and 30.

Signs and symptoms

A keloid is a firm, rubbery, shiny lump or ridge of tissue that may be pink, red, brown or darker than the surrounding skin. It grows slowly over months or years and may become itchy, painful or tender. Large keloids over joints can restrict movement.

Treatment

There is no universally effective treatment, and recurrence is common. Options include intralesional corticosteroid injections, silicone gel sheeting, pressure therapy, cryotherapy, laser treatment, surgical excision (often combined with steroids or radiotherapy to reduce recurrence), and intralesional chemotherapy agents such as 5-fluorouracil. Prevention in susceptible individuals focuses on avoiding unnecessary skin trauma such as elective piercing.
