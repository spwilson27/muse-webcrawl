Etruscan religion

Etruscan religion was the set of beliefs and practices of the Etruscan civilisation of ancient Italy. Unusually for the ancient world, the Etruscans understood their religion as a revealed body of knowledge — the disciplina Etrusca — said to have been delivered to them by divine messengers, notably the child-prophet Tages, who was believed to have sprung from a ploughed field, and the nymph Vegoia. The sacred books were held to govern every aspect of life and interpretation of the divine will.

Divination

The centrepiece of Etruscan religious practice was divination: reading the will of the gods from natural signs. Priests called haruspices examined the entrails of sacrificed animals, especially the liver; a bronze model of a sheep's liver found at Piacenza, its surface divided into regions inscribed with gods' names, served as a teaching and reference tool. Other specialists interpreted lightning, dividing the sky into sixteen regions, each assigned to a deity — a system the Romans later adopted. Omens in the flight of birds and in prodigies were likewise catalogued.

Gods and beliefs

The Etruscan pantheon included Tin (or Tinia), chief god of the sky and lightning, comparable to Zeus or Jupiter; his consort Uni, corresponding to Juno; and Menrva, corresponding to Minerva. Many Etruscan gods were later absorbed into Roman religion, and Etruscan ritual specialists continued to be consulted by the Roman state for centuries. The Etruscans believed in an afterlife that early art depicts as a joyful banquet, though later imagery grows darker, with underworld figures such as Charun (a hook-nosed psychopomp) and Vanth (a winged female spirit) guiding the dead.

Roman writers such as Seneca remarked on the contrast between Roman and Etruscan attitudes: whereas Romans believed the gods cared for human affairs, the Etruscans held that everything that happens is a sign through which the gods communicate.
