Mu'tazila

The Mu'tazila (Arabic: المعتزلة, al-muʿtazila) was an early school of Islamic theology that arose in Basra and Baghdad in the 8th century CE and flourished during the 9th and 10th centuries. The Mu'tazilites championed the use of reason and rational inquiry in religious matters, holding that the truths of Islam could be known through human reason as well as revelation, and that good and evil are objective facts accessible to rational judgment. They are best known for their doctrines of the absolute oneness and justice of God, their position that the Quran was created rather than eternal, and their insistence on human free will. Under the Abbasid caliphs al-Ma'mun, al-Mu'tasim, and al-Wathiq, Mu'tazilite doctrine became state orthodoxy and was enforced by an inquisition (the mihna), but it later fell from favor and was largely displaced within Sunni Islam by the Ash'ari and Maturidi schools—though its influence persisted in Shia theology and in modern Muslim reform movements.

Origins

Tradition attributes the school's name—"those who withdraw" or "seceders"—to an episode in the circle of the Basran teacher Hasan al-Basri (d. 728). Asked whether a Muslim who commits a grave sin remains a believer or becomes an unbeliever, Wasil ibn Ata (d. 748) answered that such a sinner occupies an "intermediate position" (al-manzila bayna al-manzilatayn), neither believer nor unbeliever, and withdrew from the circle; Hasan remarked, "Wasil has withdrawn (i'tazala) from us." Whether or not the anecdote is historical, the Mu'tazila coalesced in Basra in the early 8th century in debate with Kharijites, Murji'ites, and early predestinarians, and spread to Baghdad, where it attracted caliphal patronage.

The five principles

Mu'tazilite doctrine was systematized around five principles (al-usul al-khamsa):

  • Divine unity (tawhid). God is absolutely one and without partners or distinct eternal attributes; the divine attributes are identical with God's essence. Passages of the Quran describing God in anthropomorphic terms must be interpreted allegorically, and because God's speech is an attribute, the Quran itself must be created in time rather than co-eternal with God.
  • Divine justice (adl). God is necessarily just and can do no evil; therefore humans must possess genuine free will and are responsible for their acts. Evil and unjust acts proceed from human choice, not from God.
  • The promise and the threat. God will unfailingly reward the obedient and punish unrepentant sinners in the afterlife; no intercession can nullify deserved punishment.
  • The intermediate position. The grave sinner is neither believer nor unbeliever in this world, though unrepentant, he is damned in the next.
  • Commanding right and forbidding wrong. Believers have an obligation to promote good and resist evil, by force if necessary—a principle with political implications for resistance to unjust rulers.

Reason and revelation

The Mu'tazilites held that reason precedes revelation in one respect: rational reflection establishes God's existence and the obligation to seek truth, and moral categories such as justice and gratitude are known by reason independently of scripture. Revealed texts that conflict with reason must be interpreted figuratively. They developed sophisticated arguments influenced by, and in turn influencing, the Greek philosophical tradition translated into Arabic, and Mu'tazilite atomism and theories of accidents and substances shaped early kalam (Islamic scholastic theology) generally—even among their opponents, who adopted Mu'tazilite dialectical methods.

The mihna and decline

In 833 the caliph al-Ma'mun instituted the mihna, an inquisition requiring judges and scholars to affirm the createdness of the Quran. The most famous victim was the traditionalist Ahmad ibn Hanbal, who refused and was flogged and imprisoned. The policy continued under two successors but was abandoned by al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861), who championed traditionalism. Thereafter Mu'tazilism declined as official doctrine, though it remained influential in Buyid-era Iraq and Persia, was absorbed in part into Zaydi and Twelver Shia theology, and produced major late figures such as the chief judge Abd al-Jabbar (d. 1025). The Ash'ari school, founded by a former Mu'tazilite, Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 936), positioned itself as a middle way between Mu'tazilite rationalism and Hanbali literalism and became dominant in Sunni Islam.

Legacy

From the 19th century onward, some Muslim modernists and reformers have looked to the Mu'tazila as a precedent for reconciling Islam with reason and science, and contemporary "neo-Mu'tazilite" currents are sometimes identified in modern Islamic thought. Critics, by contrast, have long regarded the school as having subordinated revelation to human speculation. Either way, the Mu'tazila's questions—about free will, divine justice, and the interpretation of scripture—remain foundational to Islamic theology.
