Kalam

Kalām (Arabic: عِلْم الكَلام, ʿilm al-kalām, "science of discourse") is the discipline of Islamic speculative theology that seeks to establish religious doctrine through rational argument and debate. Its practitioners are called mutakallimūn. The word kalām literally means "speech" or "words"; the discipline may have been named for its concern with the "speech of God" (the Qur'an's status as divine speech), or for its dialectical, question-and-answer method. It is sometimes characterised in English as "Islamic scholastic theology".

Origins

Kalām emerged in the 8th century CE amid debates in Basra and Baghdad over free will, the divine attributes, and the status of sinners. The first great kalām school was the Muʿtazila, whose "five principles" — divine unity, divine justice, the promise and the threat, the intermediate position of the grave sinner, and commanding right and forbidding wrong — structured their system. Encounters with Greek philosophy through translation, and religious disputation with Christians, Jews, Manichaeans and others, supplied questions and methods.

Method and content

Kalām proceeds by rational proofs: for God's existence (from the temporal origination of the world, ḥudūth), unity, and attributes; for the possibility and fact of prophecy; and for eschatology, which reason alone cannot establish and which is accepted on the authority of revelation once prophecy is proven. Many mutakallimūn adopted an atomist physics — the world composed of indivisible atoms and accidents recreated at each instant — which supported doctrines such as occasionalism. Argument followed the dialectical form of claim, objection and reply found in manuals (masāʾil).

Schools

The principal Sunni schools are the Ashʿariyya and the Māturīdiyya; the Muʿtazila persisted chiefly within Zaydī and Twelver Shiʿi theology. Traditionalist critics, notably Ibn Taymiyya in his Darʾ taʿāruḍ al-ʿaql wa-l-naql, argued that kalām contradicted scripture. After al-Ghazālī, kalām increasingly absorbed the logic and metaphysics of Avicennian philosophy, producing the sophisticated "later kalām" (kalām al-mutaʾakhkhirīn) of al-Rāzī, al-Ījī, al-Taftazānī and al-Jurjānī, taught in madrasas across the Ottoman, Persian and Indian worlds.

Legacy

Kalām shaped Islamic intellectual life for a millennium and parallels medieval Christian and Jewish scholasticism. In the modern period it has been both criticised by Salafi movements and revived by reformers; a deliberate "new kalām" (kalām jadīd) addressing science and modern philosophy has been proposed from the 19th century to the present.
