Ash'ari

The term Ashʿarī (Arabic: أشعري) refers most commonly to the school of Sunni Islamic theology (kalām) founded by the Basran theologian Abū al-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī (c. 873–935/6), known as Ashʿarism (al-Ashʿariyya). It is also used of the Arab tribe of al-Ashʿar (the Ashʿarites of Yemen, from whom al-Ashʿarī's family traced descent) and as the nisba of individuals from that tribe. In theological usage, the Ashʿarī school became, alongside the Māturīdī school, the dominant creed of Sunni Islam.

Al-Ash'ari and the founding

Al-Ashʿarī began as a Muʿtazilite, studying under al-Jubbāʾī, but around the age of forty he broke with Muʿtazilism — traditionally after a famous debate about the fate of three brothers (the believer, the unbeliever and the child) which, it is said, his teacher could not answer satisfactorily. He set out to defend traditional Sunni positions on the divine attributes, the uncreatedness of the Qur'an, and human acts using the rational methods of kalām, positioning himself between Muʿtazilite rationalism and Ḥanbalī traditionalism.

Doctrines

Characteristic Ashʿarī doctrines include: the eternity of God's attributes (such as speech, hence the Qur'an as God's speech is uncreated), affirmed "without asking how" (bilā kayf) in early formulations and later through interpretation (taʾwīl); occasionalism about causation, God being the direct creator of all events; the doctrine of kasb ("acquisition"), by which God creates human acts and humans "acquire" them, preserving moral responsibility; and the view that reason can know God exists but that good and evil are known primarily through revelation.

Later development and influence

The school was systematised by al-Bāqillānī, al-Juwaynī, and above all al-Ghazālī (1058–1111), whose works spread Ashʿarism across the Shāfiʿī and Mālikī legal schools. Later Ashʿarīs such as Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī engaged deeply with Avicennian philosophy. Ashʿarism became the official creed in much of the Sunni world, taught in institutions from al-Azhar to the madrasas of West Africa and Southeast Asia, and remains one of the two principal orthodox Sunni theologies.
