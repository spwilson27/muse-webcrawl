Mihna

The Miḥna (Arabic: محنة, "ordeal" or "inquisition") was a period of religious persecution instituted by the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun in 833 CE, in which the caliphal authorities imposed a test of belief on judges, scholars and officials: they were required to affirm the Muʿtazilite doctrine that the Qur'an was created (khalq al-Qurʾān), rather than co-eternal with God. The policy continued under al-Muʿtasim and al-Wāthiq, and was finally abandoned by al-Mutawakkil around 848–851.

Background

Whether the Qur'an, as God's speech, was eternal and uncreated or created in time became a central question of kalām. The Muʿtazila held it created, arguing that an eternal Qur'an compromised divine unity. Traditionalist scholars, heirs of the early pietists, held it uncreated. Al-Ma'mun, sympathetic to Muʿtazilite theology and eager to assert caliphal authority over religious doctrine, wrote to his governor of Baghdad in 833 ordering that judges and witnesses be examined; the interrogations were extended to leading scholars.

Course of the inquisition

Most of those examined eventually assented, some under threat of dismissal, imprisonment or flogging. The most famous resister was Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, eponym of the Ḥanbalī school of law, who refused to affirm the created Qur'an and was flogged under al-Muʿtasim (c. 834–835), becoming a popular hero for his steadfastness. Others, such as the traditionist Nuʿaym ibn Ḥammād, died in prison. Under al-Wāthiq the tests were applied in the provinces and to prisoners exchanged with Byzantium.

Reversal and significance

Al-Mutawakkil suspended the miḥna around 848, releasing prisoners and by 851 forbidding disputation about the Qur'an. The episode is widely regarded as decisive: its failure ended the caliphs' claim to define doctrine and confirmed the ʿulamāʾ (religious scholars) as the custodians of Sunni orthodoxy. It also helped fix the uncreatedness of the Qur'an as standard Sunni belief and elevated Ibn Ḥanbal's prestige, shaping the traditionalist current within Sunnism.
