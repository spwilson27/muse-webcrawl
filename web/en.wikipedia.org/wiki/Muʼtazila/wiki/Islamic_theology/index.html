Islamic theology

Islamic theology is the body of doctrine concerning God, prophecy, revelation and the last things within Islam, classically studied as ʿilm al-kalām ("science of discourse") and structured around the uṣūl al-dīn ("principles of religion"). Theology (kalām) developed in the early Islamic centuries through debates among Muslims — and with Christians, Jews and others — over questions such as the attributes of God, free will and predestination, the nature of the Qur'an, and the status of the grave sinner.

Core doctrines

The central affirmation of Islam is tawḥīd, the absolute oneness of God, as in the shahāda: "There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the Messenger of God." The "pillars of faith" (arkān al-īmān) traditionally enumerate belief in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and divine decree (qadar). Theology elaborates each: the divine essence and attributes; prophecy and the finality of Muhammad's prophethood; the revealed scriptures culminating in the Qur'an; resurrection, judgement, paradise and hell.

Early debates and schools

Early controversies produced the first schools: the Qadariyya defended human free will against the Jabriyya's determinism; the Khārijites and Murjiʾa disputed whether grave sinners remain Muslims; and the Muʿtazila, arising in 8th-century Basra with Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ and ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd, championed divine unity and justice, held the Qur'an to be created, and made reason a primary religious authority. The Muʿtazilite doctrine was briefly enforced by the state during the miḥna (833–c. 848).

The mature Sunni schools of kalām — the Ashʿariyya of al-Ashʿarī (d. 936) and the Māturīdiyya of al-Māturīdī (d. 944) — mediated between revelation and reason and became dominant among Sunnis. Traditionalists such as the Ḥanbalīs, and later Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328), criticised kalām's rationalism. Shiʿi theology developed its own tradition: Twelver (Imāmī) doctrine centres on the imamate and divine justice and absorbed Muʿtazilite-influenced rationalism, while Ismāʿīlī thought engaged deeply with Neoplatonism. Ibāḍī theology preserves another early tradition.

Relationship to philosophy

Kalām interacted with falsafa (Islamic philosophy), especially Avicenna's system. Al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers attacked philosophical claims on eternity of the world, God's knowledge of particulars and bodily resurrection, while himself adopting logical method; Averroes replied in kind. Later kalām manuals integrated Avicennian logic and metaphysics to a high degree of sophistication.

Modern period

From the 19th century, reformers such as Jamāl al-Dīn al-Afghānī and Muhammad ʿAbduh sought to restate theology in dialogue with modern science and philosophy, while Salafi movements rejected much of the kalām heritage in favour of a literalist traditionalism. Academic study of kalām today extends to manuscripts, Ottoman and South Asian traditions, and contemporary questions of religion and science.
