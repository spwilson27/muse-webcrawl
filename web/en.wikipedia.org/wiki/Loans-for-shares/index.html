The loans-for-shares scheme (Russian: залоговые аукционы, zalogovye auktsiony) was a programme of privatisations carried out in Russia in 1995–1996 under President Boris Yeltsin, in which a group of commercial banks lent money to the cash-strapped Russian government in exchange for temporary stakes in state-owned companies as collateral. When the government predictably failed to repay the loans, the banks kept the shares, acquiring controlling stakes in some of Russia's most valuable oil, metals and shipping companies at prices far below their market value. The scheme is widely regarded as one of the most controversial episodes of post-Soviet economic transition and the founding moment of the Russian "oligarch" class.

Background

By 1995 the Yeltsin government faced a large budget deficit, an unpopular war in Chechnya, and a presidential election in 1996 against resurgent Communists. Banker Vladimir Potanin of Oneximbank proposed that banks lend the state money secured against shares in state enterprises not yet privatised. The proposal was adopted by decree in August 1995, championed by First Deputy Prime Minister Anatoly Chubais, architect of the earlier voucher privatisation.

The auctions

Between November and December 1995, stakes in twelve companies were auctioned, among them the oil companies Yukos, Sibneft, Sidanko, TNK and Lukoil, the metals giant Norilsk Nickel, Novolipetsk Steel, and the shipping company Murmansk Shipping. The auctions were widely criticised as rigged: the banks organising each auction commonly disqualified rival bids on technicalities and won through affiliated companies, often at prices only slightly above the reserve. Mikhail Khodorkovsky's Menatep bank acquired Yukos, then Russia's second-largest oil producer, for roughly $350 million; Potanin's Oneximbank obtained Norilsk Nickel for about $170 million; Boris Berezovsky and Roman Abramovich gained Sibneft for about $100 million.

Consequences

The new owners in turn financed Yeltsin's 1996 re-election campaign and mobilised their media holdings in his support. The government never repaid the loans, and the shares passed permanently to the lenders in autumn 1996. The scheme consolidated the wealth and political influence of a small circle of businessmen, deepened public cynicism about privatisation — often described in Russia as "prikhvatizatsiya" ("grabitisation") — and remains a central episode in debates about the legitimacy of property rights in post-Soviet Russia. Under President Vladimir Putin in the 2000s, several of the assets changed hands again amid tax claims and renationalisation pressures, most famously Yukos.
