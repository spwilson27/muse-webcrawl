Fair Credit Reporting Act

Fair Credit Reporting Act
Enacted: 26 October 1970
Codified: 15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.
Enforced by: FTC and CFPB
Purpose: accuracy, fairness and privacy of consumer credit information

The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) is a United States federal law enacted in 1970 to promote the accuracy, fairness and privacy of consumer information contained in the files of consumer reporting agencies, such as the credit bureaus Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. It was one of the first data protection laws enacted in the United States and forms the basis of consumer credit rights in the country.

Provisions

The FCRA regulates how consumer reports may be furnished and used: they may be provided only for "permissible purposes" such as credit, employment, insurance and housing decisions. Consumers have the right to know what is in their files, to receive a free copy of their report annually from each major bureau (via AnnualCreditReport.com), to dispute inaccurate or incomplete information (which agencies must investigate, generally within 30 days), and to have outdated negative information removed — most adverse items may not be reported after seven years, and bankruptcies after ten.

Amendments and enforcement

The act was substantially amended by the Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act of 2003 (FACTA), which added identity-theft protections and free-report rights, and by the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010, which transferred much rulemaking authority to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The FCRA is enforced by the Federal Trade Commission and the CFPB, and consumers may sue agencies and furnishers for negligent or wilful violations.
