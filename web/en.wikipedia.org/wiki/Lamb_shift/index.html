Lamb shift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Energy-level diagram of hydrogen showing the small splitting between the 2S and 2P levels]
The Lamb shift splits hydrogen levels that Dirac theory made degenerate.

In physics, the Lamb shift, named after Willis Lamb, is a small difference in energy between two energy levels (²S₁/₂ and ²P₁/₂) of the hydrogen atom that was not predicted by the Dirac equation, according to which these states should have the same energy. It arises from the interaction between the electron and the quantum fluctuations of the vacuum, and is one of the foundational precision phenomena of Quantum electrodynamics.

Lamb and Robert Retherford measured the shift in 1947 using microwave spectroscopy of hydrogen — about 1057 MHz — a result announced at the Shelter Island Conference that electrified theorists: Hans Bethe immediately produced a non-relativistic calculation by renormalisation, and within a few years Feynman, Schwinger and Tomonaga had the full relativistic theory. Lamb received the 1955 Nobel Prize in Physics (shared with Polykarp Kusch).

Origin

Most of the shift comes from the electron's interaction with vacuum fluctuations of the electromagnetic field (which jitters its position and reduces the attraction it feels when close to the nucleus) and from vacuum polarisation (virtual electron–positron pairs screening the proton's charge). The Lamb shift's modern role is metrological: measuring it in hydrogen-like systems determines the proton's charge radius, and a 2010 measurement in muonic hydrogen gave a significantly smaller radius than expected — the 'proton radius puzzle', partly resolved in favour of the smaller value by later electron-scattering and spectroscopy results.
