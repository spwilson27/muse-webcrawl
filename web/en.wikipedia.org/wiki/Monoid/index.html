Monoid

[Image placeholder: algebraic structures]

In abstract algebra, a monoid is a set equipped with an associative binary operation and an identity element. Monoids occur in several branches of mathematics and are fundamental in theoretical computer science and category theory. A monoid may be thought of as a semigroup with an identity, or as a group without the requirement that every element have an inverse.

Definition

A monoid is a set S together with a binary operation • : S × S → S satisfying two axioms: associativity — for all a, b, c in S, (a • b) • c = a • (b • c); and identity — there exists an element e in S such that e • a = a • e = a for every a. The identity element is necessarily unique.

Examples

The natural numbers under addition form a commutative monoid with identity 0; under multiplication they form a monoid with identity 1. The set of all strings over an alphabet, with concatenation as the operation and the empty string as identity, forms the free monoid — the basic object behind formal language theory. Functions from a set to itself under composition, matrices under multiplication, and the powerset of a set under union are further examples.

Applications

Monoids model sequential processes: the behaviour of finite automata is captured by transition monoids, and in functional programming the monoid laws (associativity and identity) justify parallel reduction patterns such as map-reduce — summations, counts and maxima can be combined in any order. Monoids are also single-object categories, linking algebra to category theory.
