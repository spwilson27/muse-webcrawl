John Theophilus Desaguliers

Born: 12 March 1683, La Rochelle, France
Died: 29 February 1744 (aged 60), London, England
Fields: Natural philosophy, engineering
Known for: Popularising Newtonian science; early Freemasonry

John Theophilus Desaguliers FRS (12 March 1683 – 29 February 1744) was a French-born British natural philosopher, clergyman, engineer and freemason who was elected to the Royal Society in 1714 as an experimental assistant to Isaac Newton. He had studied at Oxford and later popularised Newtonian theories and their practical applications in public lectures on experimental philosophy.

Early life

Desaguliers was born in La Rochelle, France, the son of a Huguenot pastor. After the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685, his family fled to England, settling in London. He was educated at Westminster School and Christ Church, Oxford, where he took holy orders and lectured in experimental philosophy, succeeding John Keill.

Scientific work

Desaguliers became one of the most effective public demonstrators of Newtonian mechanics, giving lecture courses in London illustrated with apparatus of his own design. His two-volume A Course of Experimental Philosophy (1734–44) described experiments on mechanics, hydrostatics, pneumatics, and optics, and discussed practical machinery including steam engines. He advised on engineering projects, improved ventilation for the House of Commons, and patented a chimney design. He was awarded the Royal Society's Copley Medal three times.

Freemasonry

Desaguliers was a central figure in the organisation of modern speculative Freemasonry. He served as the third Grand Master of the Premier Grand Lodge of England in 1719 and was instrumental in drafting its early regulations and in compiling Anderson's Constitutions (1723). He has been called one of the founding fathers of modern Freemasonry.
