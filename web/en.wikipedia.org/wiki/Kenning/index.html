Kenning

A medieval Icelandic manuscript of skaldic verse

A kenning (Old English kenning, Old Norse kenning) is a figure of speech consisting of a compound metaphorical phrase — typically two words — used in place of a simple noun, characteristic of Old Norse and Old English poetry. Kennings replace the ordinary word with an elaborate periphrasis: the sea is a "whale-road", a ship a "sea-steed", battle a "spear-din", and a warrior a "feeder of ravens".

Structure

A kenning consists of a base-word and a determinant, usually joined in the genitive or as a compound. In "whale-road" (Old English hronrād), the base-word "road" is qualified by the determinant "whale", yielding the sea. Skaldic poetry developed kennings to extraordinary complexity, nesting determinants within determinants — the so-called rekit ("driven") kennings that can run to five or more elements — making the poetry a learned riddle for initiates.

Sources and study

Kennings are most densely used in skaldic verse and in Old English poems such as Beowulf. Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (13th century) was written partly as a handbook explaining the mythological kennings to younger poets. Modern study of kenning systems was advanced by scholars such as Rudolf Meissner, whose classification of kenning types remains standard.
