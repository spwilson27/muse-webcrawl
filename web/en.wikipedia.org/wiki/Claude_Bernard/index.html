Claude Bernard

Claude Bernard (12 July 1813 – 10 February 1878) was a French physiologist. Historian I. Bernard Cohen of Harvard University called Bernard "one of the greatest of all men of science". Among many other accomplishments, he was one of the first to suggest the use of a blinded experiment to ensure the objectivity of scientific observations. He originated the term milieu intérieur (internal environment), and the concept of homeostasis developed from it.

Life and work

Bernard was born in the village of Saint-Julien near Villefranche-sur-Saône. He studied medicine in Paris and became a student of François Magendie at the Collège de France. His discoveries included the role of the pancreas in digestion, the glycogenic function of the liver, and the vasomotor system.

Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine

His 1865 book Introduction à l'étude de la médecine expérimentale (An Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine) is a classic of scientific methodology, arguing that medicine should be based on controlled laboratory experiments rather than observation alone. In it he famously wrote that "the science of life is a superb and dazzlingly lighted hall which may be reached only by passing through a long and ghastly kitchen."

Legacy

Bernard is regarded as a founder of modern physiology. His concept of the constancy of the internal environment was later developed by Walter Cannon into the theory of homeostasis.
