External validity

External validity concerns how far study results generalise beyond the study itself

External validity is the validity of applying the conclusions of a scientific study outside the context of that study. In other words, it is the extent to which the results of a study can be generalised to other situations, groups, settings and time periods. It is one of the principal forms of research validity, conventionally contrasted with internal validity, which concerns whether a study's design supports causal conclusions within the study itself.

Types and threats

The concept was developed in the work of Donald T. Campbell and collaborators on experimental and quasi-experimental design. Generalisation may be sought across populations (from a sample to other people), across settings (from laboratory to field), across treatments and outcomes, and across times. Threats to external validity include unrepresentative samples — a persistent concern in psychology, where participants are often university students from Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic (WEIRD) societies — artificial laboratory conditions that change behaviour, and interactions between selection and treatment, whereby an effect found in one group does not occur in others.

Trade-offs and responses

Researchers often face a trade-off: tight experimental control raises internal validity but can reduce the realism — and hence the external validity — of a study. Common responses include replication in varied populations and settings, field experiments, representative sampling, and meta-analysis across many studies. Critics note that external validity is not a property a study simply has or lacks, but a matter of degree argued case by case, and that identifying the causal mechanism behind an effect helps judge where the effect should and should not be expected to recur.
