1905 French law on the Separation of the Churches and the State

The 1905 French law on the Separation of the Churches and the State (Loi du 9 décembre 1905 concernant la séparation des Églises et de l'État) is the statute that established state secularism (laïcité) in France, ending the Napoleonic Concordat of 1801 in most of the country.

Provisions

The law's first article guarantees freedom of conscience and the free exercise of religion, while the second declares that the Republic does not recognise, pay, or subsidise any religious denomination. Church property built before 1905 largely passed to public ownership, with buildings made available for worship.

Background and passage

Reported by Aristide Briand and supported by the government of Émile Combes's successors, the law followed decades of tension between the Republic and the Catholic Church, intensified by the Dreyfus affair and disputes over religious congregations. Pope Pius X condemned the law, and the taking of church inventories in 1906 provoked protests.

Legacy

The law remains the cornerstone of French laïcité. It does not apply in Alsace-Moselle, which was German territory in 1905 and retains the Concordat system.
