Qianlong Emperor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Qianlong Emperor in court dress, from an imperial scroll portrait]

The Qianlong Emperor (1711–1799), personal name Hongli, was the fifth emperor of China's Qing dynasty (r. 1735–1796, and power behind the throne until his death), under whom the Qing reached its greatest territorial extent and cultural splendour. He completed the conquest of Xinjiang — destroying the Zunghar Khanate in campaigns accompanied by what many historians describe as genocide — patronised the enormous Siku Quanshu library while censoring and destroying 'seditious' books, received (and famously rebuffed) Britain's Macartney Embassy in 1793, and retired after sixty years so as not to exceed his grandfather Kangxi's reign. His long rule saw China become the world's most populous and arguably largest economy — and the first hairline cracks of Qing decline.

Apex of the Qing

Qianlong's 'Ten Great Campaigns' extended or defended the frontiers at enormous cost: victory in Xinjiang (1755–1759) doubled the empire's western reach, while campaigns in Burma, Vietnam and Nepal (the Gurkha war, 1790–1792) ended equivocally. At home his reign combined prosperity and population growth (toward 300 million by 1790) with corruption personified by his favourite Heshen, the White Lotus Rebellion (1796–1804) that outlasted him, and literary inquisitions within the Siku Quanshu project.

The Macartney Embassy

In 1793 Lord Macartney's embassy, sent to open trade and establish relations, was received as a tributary mission; Qianlong's edict to George III — declaring China self-sufficient and without need of British manufactures — became the emblem of a fateful mutual incomprehension. Trade friction at Canton, opium smuggling and the balance-of-payments problem in silver set the stage for the Opium Wars two generations later.
