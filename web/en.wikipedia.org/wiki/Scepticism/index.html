Scepticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scepticism (American spelling: skepticism) is a family of philosophical positions that question the possibility or justification of knowledge claims. Rather than a single doctrine, it is an attitude and a method — suspend judgement, demand evidence proportioned to the claim — that has driven philosophy from ancient Greece to the modern scientific-scepticism movement. Sceptical arguments are the whetstone of epistemology: every theory of knowledge, from empiricism to rationalism, defines itself partly by how it answers them.

Ancient and early-modern scepticism

Ancient scepticism had two schools: the Pyrrhonists, followers of Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BC), who suspended judgement (epoché) on all non-evident matters and reported that tranquillity (ataraxia) followed — preserved in the works of Sextus Empiricus, whose tropes (arguments from disagreement, regress, relativity) still structure sceptical debate; and the Academic sceptics of Plato's Academy (Arcesilaus, Carneades), who argued against Stoic claims to certainty and acted on the plausible. Rediscovered in the Renaissance, Sextus helped provoke Descartes' methodical doubt — scepticism wielded as a tool to reach certainty — and Hume's radical empiricist scepticism about induction, causation and the self. In contemporary usage, "scientific scepticism" also names a social movement (associated with figures like Carl Sagan and organisations such as the Skeptics Society) that applies sceptical scrutiny to pseudoscience and paranormal claims, distinct from both philosophical scepticism and mere denialism.
