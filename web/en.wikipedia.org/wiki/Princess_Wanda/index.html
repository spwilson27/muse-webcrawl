Princess Wanda

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Princess Wanda in Polish legendary tradition.

Princess Wanda (Polish pronunciation: [ˈvanda]) is a legendary princess of Polish folklore, said to be the daughter of Krakus, the legendary founder of Kraków. According to the most popular version of the legend, she succeeded her father and became queen of the Poles, but chose death by drowning in the Vistula River rather than marry a German prince, whose invasion she thereby averted.

The legend

The story first appears in the chronicle of Wincenty Kadłubek (Chronica seu originale regum et principum Poloniae, c. 1200). In Kadłubek's Latin version, Wanda ruled wisely and defeated an invading "Alamannic" tyrant. Later versions, including that of Jan Długosz in the 15th century, added the details by which the legend is best known today: the German prince Rytygier (Rüdiger) demanded her hand and threatened war; Wanda led her army to victory but, unwilling to marry and wishing to remain devoted to her people or the gods, threw herself into the Vistula.

Kopiec Wandy

The Wanda Mound (Kopiec Wandy) in Mogiła, now part of Kraków's Nowa Huta district, is traditionally held to be her burial place. The prehistoric mound stands near the Vistula, and on certain days of the year the setting sun aligns with it in relation to the nearby Krakus Mound—a pairing sometimes interpreted in archaeoastronomical terms.

Cultural legacy

Wanda became a symbol of Polish patriotism and female self-sacrifice. Her story has been retold in Polish literature and music, including Cyprian Kamil Norwid's drama Wanda and Antonín Dvořák's opera Vanda (1876). The name Wanda, borne by the legendary princess, became a popular Polish given name; etymologists have debated its Slavic origins, and Kadłubek himself fancifully derived it from unda, "wave".
