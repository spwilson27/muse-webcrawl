Corpus Juris Civilis

The Corpus Juris Civilis (“Body of Civil Law”) is the modern name for the great codification of Roman law ordered by the Byzantine emperor Justinian I and promulgated between 529 and 534 AD. It is one of the most influential works in the history of law and the foundation of the civil law tradition.

Components

The compilation consists of four parts:

  • The Code (Codex Justinianus, 529, revised 534), a collection of imperial constitutions arranged by subject.
  • The Digest or Pandects (533), an enormous compilation of excerpts from classical jurists such as Ulpian, Paulus, Papinian, and Gaius, edited by a commission under the jurist Tribonian.
  • The Institutes (533), an elementary textbook for law students based partly on Gaius's Institutes, which had the force of law.
  • The Novellae, new constitutions issued by Justinian after 534, mostly in Greek, collected privately.

Compilation

Justinian sought to restore the Roman Empire and its law. His commissions condensed some 2,000 books and three million lines of juristic writing into about 150,000 lines in the Digest, eliminating contradictions and obsolete material. Commentary on the texts was initially forbidden, though translations and summaries into Greek soon became necessary in the eastern empire.

Influence

In the East, the Corpus was eventually superseded by the Greek Basilika of Leo VI. In the West, it was largely lost until its revival at Bologna around 1070, where scholars such as Irnerius and the Glossators — culminating in Accursius's Glossa Ordinaria — made it the basis of university legal study and the European jus commune. It profoundly influenced canon law, the law of continental Europe and Latin America, and modern codifications such as the French Code civil of 1804.
