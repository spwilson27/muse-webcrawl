Mist

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mist is a weather phenomenon consisting of tiny water droplets suspended in the air near the Earth's surface, which reduces visibility. By international convention, mist is distinguished from fog by visibility: when visibility is between 1 and 2 kilometres (in some definitions up to 5 km), the condition is called mist; below 1 km it is fog. Mist droplets are generally smaller and less dense than fog, and mist tends to feel less damp.

Formation

Mist forms when moist air cools to its dew point and water vapour condenses on microscopic nuclei, or when water evaporates into cool air. Common settings include early mornings after clear, calm nights (radiation cooling), valleys and hillsides where moist air rises and cools (often called hill fog or cloud touching the ground), coasts where warm moist air passes over cold water, and after rain. From a distance, mist appears as a thin veil, often blue-grey, lying over fields, forests and water.

Effects

Mist can slow road and air travel and creates hazardous driving conditions, though it is generally less disruptive than dense fog. Ecologically, mist and fog drip sustain certain forests — notably coastal redwood forests — by supplying moisture during dry seasons. The word is also used figuratively, and "mist" devices spray fine water droplets for cooling, irrigation and dust suppression.
