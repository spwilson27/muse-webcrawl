Georges-Eugène Haussmann

Georges-Eugène Haussmann (27 March 1809 – 11 January 1891), commonly known as Baron Haussmann, was a French official who supervised a massive urban renewal program of new boulevards, parks and public works in Paris that decisively transformed the city during the Second Empire.

Georges-Eugène Haussmann
Born: 27 March 1809, Paris
Died: 11 January 1891, Paris
Known for: Haussmann's renovation of Paris
Office: Prefect of Seine, 1853–1870

Renovation of Paris

Appointed Prefect of the Seine by Napoleon III in 1853, Haussmann demolished crowded medieval quarters and cut wide, straight, tree-lined boulevards — for circulation, light, sanitation and military control — lined with uniform stone apartment buildings (Haussmann buildings) of regulated height and façade. He built sewers, aqueducts, parks (Bois de Boulogne, Bois de Vincennes, Parc des Buttes Chaumont), markets (Les Halles), railway stations, the Paris Opéra project, and annexed surrounding communes in 1860, expanding Paris to its present 20 arrondissements.

Criticism and fall

The works displaced many poor residents, cost huge sums financed by creative debt — attacked by Jules Ferry in Les Comptes fantastiques d'Haussmann — and destroyed much historic Paris. Haussmann was dismissed in 1870 shortly before the fall of the Empire. His model influenced urban planning worldwide, and modern Paris is still recognisably his creation.
