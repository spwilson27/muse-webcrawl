Synaptotagmin

Synaptotagmins (SYTs) constitute a family of membrane-trafficking proteins that are characterized by an N-terminal transmembrane region, a variable linker, and two C-terminal C2 domains (C2A and C2B). There are 17 isoforms in the mammalian synaptotagmin family. Synaptotagmin-1 (SYT1) is best known as the calcium (Ca2+) sensor at the presynaptic axon terminal that triggers synchronous neurotransmitter release.

Function

When an action potential depolarizes the presynaptic membrane, voltage-gated calcium channels open and Ca2+ floods into the terminal. Synaptotagmin-1, embedded in the membrane of synaptic vesicles, binds Ca2+ through its C2 domains and interacts with phospholipids and the SNARE fusion machinery (syntaxin, SNAP-25, and synaptobrevin/VAMP), coupling calcium influx to the rapid fusion of vesicles with the plasma membrane and release of neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft.

Different isoforms serve different roles: SYT1, SYT2, and SYT9 act as Ca2+ sensors for fast synchronous release in different neuron types, while other family members (such as SYT4 and SYT7) participate in asynchronous release, endocytosis, and calcium-dependent exocytosis in non-neuronal secretory cells, including hormone release from endocrine cells.

Discovery and genetics

Synaptotagmin was first identified as a synaptic vesicle protein (originally called p65) in the early 1980s, and cloned by the end of that decade. In humans, SYT1 is encoded by the SYT1 gene on chromosome 12; de novo mutations in SYT1 cause a neurodevelopmental disorder (Baker–Gordon syndrome) characterized by intellectual disability and movement abnormalities, reflecting the protein's central role in synaptic transmission.
