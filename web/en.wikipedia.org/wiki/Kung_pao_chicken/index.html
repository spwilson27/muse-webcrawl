Kung Pao chicken

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A plate of kung pao chicken with peanuts, dried chillies and Sichuan pepper]

Kung Pao chicken (gōng bǎo jī dīng) is a Sichuan stir-fry of diced chicken with peanuts, dried red chillies and Sichuan pepper in a glossy, sweet-sour-savoury sauce built on black vinegar, sugar and soy. Its name is an official title: Gongbao ("palace guardian") was an honour held by Ding Baozhen, a Qing-dynasty official from Guizhou who governed Sichuan in the 1870s–80s and whose household — by the accepted story — made the dish famous; it was served to guests, entered the restaurants, and took his title with it. During the Cultural Revolution the name was politically awkward (an imperial official's title) and the dish was renamed in some places; the old name returned with reform.

Contents
1. Versions

Versions

The Sichuan original is precise: chicken thigh cubes velveted and flash-fried, chillies snipped and bloomed until fragrant (the hū là "scorched chilli" profile), Sichuan pepper, a sauce balanced to the lychee-sour register (a faint sweet-sour, not a syrup), peanuts added at the end to stay crisp, and sections of spring onion or Chinese leek. Guizhou claims a wetter, chilli-paste version from Ding's home province. The Westernised kung pao of American-Chinese restaurants — sweeter, with bell peppers and celery — is a recognised descendant, and Sichuan's provincial standard for the dish is now a matter of official documentation, peanuts and all.
