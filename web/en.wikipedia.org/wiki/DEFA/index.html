DEFA (Deutsche Film-Aktiengesellschaft) was the state-owned film studio of East Germany (the German Democratic Republic). Founded in 1946 in the Soviet occupation zone, it held a monopoly on film production in the GDR until German reunification, producing features, documentaries, newsreels and animated films from its studios at Babelsberg.

History

DEFA was licensed by the Soviet Military Administration in May 1946, before the GDR itself was founded, and its first feature, Wolfgang Staudte's The Murderers Are Among Us (1946), was the first German film of the postwar era — a "rubble film" confronting Nazi guilt. Early DEFA productions included internationally praised anti-fascist films, but production was increasingly shaped by socialist cultural policy and censorship.

Productions

DEFA became known for its fairy-tale films (Märchenfilme) such as The Story of Little Mook (1953), its popular "Indianerfilme" westerns starring Gojko Mitić, and science-fiction films like The Silent Star (1960). Acclaimed dramas include Konrad Wolf's Stars (1959), Frank Beyer's Jacob the Liar (1974) — the only East German film nominated for an Academy Award — and Heiner Carow's Coming Out (1989). Several films, such as The Rabbit Is Me (1965), were banned after the 11th Plenum of 1965 and only released after 1989.

Dissolution and legacy

After reunification the studio was sold by the Treuhandanstalt in 1992. The DEFA Foundation (DEFA-Stiftung), established in 1998, preserves and promotes its archive of some 12,000 films, which remain an important record of East German culture.
