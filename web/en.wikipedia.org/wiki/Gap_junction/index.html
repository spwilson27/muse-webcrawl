Gap junction

Gap junctions are specialised intercellular connections between a multitude of animal cell-types. They directly connect the cytoplasm of two cells, allowing various molecules, ions and electrical impulses to pass through a regulated gate between cells. Gap junctions are found in nearly all animal tissues, where they allow cells to communicate and coordinate their activity.

A gap junction connects the cytoplasms of adjacent cells through paired connexon channels.

Structure

A gap junction channel is composed of two connexons (hemichannels), one in each cell membrane, which connect across the intercellular gap of about 2–4 nanometres. In vertebrates each connexon is an assembly of six connexin proteins; more than 20 connexin genes have been identified in humans. Invertebrates form equivalent channels from a different protein family, the innexins. Hundreds of channels may cluster together in a plaque at the cell surface.

Function

The channel pore passes molecules smaller than roughly 1,000 daltons, including ions, sugars, amino acids, nucleotides and second messengers such as cyclic AMP and IP3, while excluding proteins and nucleic acids. In cardiac muscle and some smooth muscle, gap junctions transmit action potentials directly between cells, producing rapid, synchronised contraction. In neurons they form electrical synapses, allowing fast bidirectional signalling. They also play roles in embryonic development, tissue homeostasis and the coordination of secretory activity, for example in pancreatic beta cells.

Regulation and disease

Gap junction channels gate in response to changes in voltage, intracellular pH, and calcium concentration; closure during cell damage helps isolate injured cells from their neighbours. Mutations in connexin genes cause human diseases including forms of hereditary deafness (notably connexin 26, encoded by GJB2), skin disorders, cataracts, and the nerve and skin condition Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease.
