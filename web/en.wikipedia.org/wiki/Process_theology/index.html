Process theology

Process theology is a school of theological thought that understands God and the world through the metaphysics of process philosophy, especially the system of Alfred North Whitehead in Process and Reality (1929) as developed by Charles Hartshorne, John B. Cobb Jr., and David Ray Griffin. Its central claim is that reality is fundamentally composed of events or "actual occasions" in becoming, rather than static substances; God, accordingly, is not an unchanging, omnipotent ruler external to the world but is involved in the world's ongoing creative process, experiencing it and being affected by it.

Key concepts

  • Dipolar God: Following Hartshorne, God has a "primordial nature" (eternal, envisaging all possibilities) and a "consequent nature" (God's actual experience, which grows as the world unfolds). God is thus both absolute and relative, eternal and temporal.
  • Divine persuasion: God influences the world not by coercion but by presenting each actual occasion with an "initial aim" — a lure toward the best possibility — which creatures may accept or reject. This preserves genuine creaturely freedom.
  • Panentheism: The world is "in" God and God is in the world, though God is more than the world; process theology is usually classified as panentheistic rather than pantheistic.
  • Open future: The future is not fully determined or foreknown as actual; God's knowledge grows with the world.

The problem of evil

Process theology addresses the problem of evil by denying that God has a monopoly of power: because every actual entity has some power of self-determination, God cannot unilaterally prevent evil. Evil arises from the discord and triviality possible in a world of genuinely free, interdependent creatures; God suffers with creation ("the fellow-sufferer who understands", in Whitehead's phrase) and redeems it by preserving all experience in the consequent nature of God — "objective immortality".

Influence and criticism

Process thought has influenced liberal Protestant theology, open theism (a related but distinct movement), ecological and feminist theology, and interreligious dialogue; Cobb applied it to environmental ethics and economics. Critics from classical theist traditions object that a God who cannot act unilaterally, and who changes, falls short of the biblical and classical attributes of omnipotence and immutability.
