Indiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An indiction (from Latin indictio, "declaration" or "proclamation") was a period of fifteen years used in the Roman Empire and medieval Europe as a fiscal cycle and, by extension, as a system of dating documents. The indiction was originally the periodic revaluation of agricultural land for taxation, proclaimed at fixed intervals; each successive census year began a new cycle.

History

Five-year valuation cycles existed under the early Empire, but the fifteen-year indiction became standard under Constantine I, with the first such cycle conventionally reckoned from 1 September 312. The indiction year in the Byzantine system began on 1 September; other starting dates were used in the West, including 24 September and 25 December or 1 January (the papal indiction). Justinian I required legal documents to be dated by the indiction alongside the regnal year, and the system remained in official use in the Byzantine Empire until its fall.

Use in dating

The indiction number of a year tells only its position (1–15) within the cycle, so it must be combined with other dating elements. Medieval charters and chronicles frequently date by indiction, and the figure is a standard tool for editors in verifying the dates of documents. To compute the Byzantine indiction of a year in the modern era, add 3 to the year number and divide by 15; the remainder (with 0 read as 15) gives the indiction, bearing in mind the September start.

Later use

The Catholic Church continued to use indictions in official documents into the modern period, and the term survives in chronology and in older systems of land taxation. The word also gave rise to no continuing practical calendar use today.
