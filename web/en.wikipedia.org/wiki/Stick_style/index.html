Stick style

The Stick style is a late-19th-century American architectural style of wooden houses characterised by decorative "stickwork" — boards applied to the exterior walls in patterns of verticals, horizontals and diagonals that suggest the building's underlying timber frame. Flourishing from about 1860 to 1890, it forms a transition between the Gothic Revival and Queen Anne styles.

Characteristics

Stick style houses are typically of wood-frame (balloon frame) construction with steep, gabled roofs, overhanging eaves with exposed rafter ends, and wrap-around porches. Their distinguishing feature is the applied stickwork trim, often painted in contrasting colours to emphasise the pattern. Interlaced bracing under the eaves, diagonal porch supports and trusses in the gable ends reinforce the impression of expressed structure.

History

The style was promoted through pattern books, notably those of Andrew Jackson Downing and his successors, and by architects such as Richard Morris Hunt, whose Griswold House in Newport, Rhode Island (1862–63), is an early masterpiece. The related Stick–Eastlake manner absorbed the geometric ornament popularised by Charles Eastlake. After 1890 the style merged into the Queen Anne, whose houses often retained stickwork decoration.

The architectural historian Vincent Scully championed the style, coining its modern name and praising its honest expression of wooden construction. Famous examples include the William Carson House ("the Gingerbread House") and other Newport and Cape May cottages — though the most flamboyant of these are often classified as Queen Anne.
