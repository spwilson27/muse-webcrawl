The Idea of a University is a collection of discourses and essays by the English theologian and cardinal John Henry Newman, setting out his philosophy of higher education. The first part, Discourses on the Scope and Nature of University Education, was delivered in Dublin in 1852 in connection with the founding of the Catholic University of Ireland, of which Newman became the first rector. A second part, Lectures and Essays on University Subjects, followed in 1858. The combined work, published in its familiar form in 1873, is regarded as a classic statement of the value of a liberal education.

Main arguments

Newman argued that a university is properly a place for the diffusion of universal knowledge, and that knowledge is an end in itself: "knowledge is capable of being its own end," cultivated for the enlargement of the mind rather than for any immediate professional utility. He defended the teaching of theology as a legitimate branch of knowledge within the university, held that research and teaching are distinct functions, and described the ideal product of liberal education as the "gentleman" — a person of cultivated intellect, tact and clarity of thought.

Influence

The book has shaped debates about the purpose of universities for more than a century and a half, invoked by defenders of the liberal arts against narrowly vocational models of education. Newman's formulation of education as the formation of "a cultivated intellect, a delicate taste, a candid, equitable, dispassionate mind" remains frequently quoted in discussions of higher education policy.
