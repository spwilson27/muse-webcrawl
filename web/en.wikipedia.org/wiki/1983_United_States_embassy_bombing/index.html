The 1983 United States embassy bombing was a suicide attack on the United States embassy in Beirut, Lebanon, on 18 April 1983, during the Lebanese Civil War. A van packed with about 2,000 pounds (910 kg) of explosives was detonated at the embassy entrance, killing 63 people — among them 17 Americans, including much of the CIA's Middle East station — and wounding about 120. It was the deadliest attack on a United States diplomatic mission up to that time and a precursor of the far larger Beirut barracks bombings six months later.

Background

Following Israel's 1982 invasion of Lebanon and the evacuation of the PLO, a multinational force including United States Marines was deployed to Beirut to stabilise the government of President Amine Gemayel. The American presence was increasingly seen by Lebanese Shia and leftist factions not as neutral peacekeeping but as support for the Phalangist-dominated government and, after the US-brokered 17 May Agreement with Israel, as a party to the conflict.

The attack

At about 1:00 p.m. a delivery van that had been stolen from the embassy the previous year, loaded with explosives, passed through the compound's defences and detonated in front of the embassy's central building. The explosion tore away the building's façade and brought down its central section. Among the American dead were CIA Near East director Robert C. Ames, station chief Ken Haas, his deputy James Lewis, and much of the Agency's Beirut station staff — the single largest loss of CIA personnel in one incident at the time. Rescue operations continued for days under the rubble.

Responsibility and aftermath

A group calling itself the Islamic Jihad Organization claimed responsibility, stating the attack was part of the "Iranian revolution's campaign" against imperialist targets. United States investigations and later court findings attributed the operation to the emerging Hezbollah movement with substantial support from Iran and Syria; in 2003 a US federal judge ruled Iran legally responsible and awarded damages to victims' families.

The bombing demonstrated the vulnerability of American facilities in Lebanon and began a sequence of escalations: in October 1983 suicide bombers destroyed the US Marine and French paratroop barracks in Beirut, killing 305; the US embassy annex in East Beirut was bombed in September 1984. The April 1983 attack is frequently described as the opening blow of the modern era of suicide terrorism directed against the United States.
