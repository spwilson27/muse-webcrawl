The aurochs (Bos primigenius) is an extinct species of large wild cattle that inhabited Asia, Europe and North Africa. It is the ancestor of both major types of domestic cattle: taurine cattle, domesticated in the Near East about 10,500 years ago, and zebu cattle, domesticated independently in the Indus valley. The last known aurochs, a female, died in Poland's Jaktorów forest in 1627.

Description and behaviour

Aurochs were among the largest herbivores of post-glacial Europe: bulls stood up to 180 cm at the shoulder and weighed as much as 1,000 kg, with massive horns curving forward and inward, up to 80 cm long. Bulls were black-brown with a pale "eel stripe" along the spine; cows were reddish brown. Calves were born chestnut coloured. Herds probably consisted of cows, calves and young bulls, with mature bulls solitary or in small groups; aurochs grazed open woodland and marshland and were preyed upon by wolves, lions and tigers.

History and extinction

Aurochs appear in Paleolithic cave art at Lascaux and Chauvet, in ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian art, and in Caesar's account of the Hercynian Forest. Hunting, habitat loss and disease from domestic cattle shrank the range through the Middle Ages; by the 13th century the species survived only in Poland, Lithuania and neighbouring regions, where it was protected as royal game. Despite protection, the herd at Jaktorów dwindled from hundreds to a handful; the last animal died in 1627, making the aurochs one of the first documented cases of human-caused extinction.

Breeding back

Twentieth-century "breeding back" programmes — beginning with the Heck brothers' Heck cattle in the 1920s–30s — selected primitive domestic breeds for aurochs-like traits; modern projects such as the Dutch Tauros Programme and the German Taurus project combine breeding with genome data from ancient aurochs DNA, first sequenced in 2015. The resulting proxies are used as large grazers in rewilding areas such as Oostvaardersplassen.
