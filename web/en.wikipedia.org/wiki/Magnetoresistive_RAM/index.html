Magnetoresistive RAM

Magnetoresistive random-access memory (MRAM) is a type of non-volatile random-access memory that stores data in magnetic domains rather than electric charges or current flows. Each cell holds a magnetic tunnel junction whose electrical resistance depends on the relative magnetization of two ferromagnetic layers, encoding a bit.

Operation

An MRAM cell consists of two ferromagnetic plates separated by a thin insulating barrier, forming a magnetic tunnel junction. One plate is a permanent magnet fixed in one direction; the other's magnetization can be switched. When the two magnetizations are parallel, the junction's resistance is low; when antiparallel, it is high — the tunnel magnetoresistance effect used to read the state. Early designs wrote bits with magnetic fields from current-carrying lines; modern spin-transfer torque MRAM (STT-MRAM) writes by passing a spin-polarized current through the junction itself, improving scalability.

Advantages and uses

MRAM combines non-volatility with fast read and write times, unlimited endurance in principle, and low standby power, making it attractive for embedded memory in microcontrollers, aerospace electronics tolerant of radiation, and industrial systems. Companies including Everspin Technologies have shipped discrete MRAM products, and major foundries offer embedded STT-MRAM as a replacement for embedded flash at advanced process nodes.

History

The concept traces to work in the 1970s and to the discovery of giant magnetoresistance in 1988. IBM, Motorola (later Freescale), and Honeywell were early developers; the first commercial MRAM chip, a 4 Mbit part from Freescale, appeared in 2006.
