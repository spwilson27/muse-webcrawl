Alkaline phosphatase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

EC number3.1.3.1
ReactionHydrolysis of phosphate monoesters at alkaline pH
Genes (human)ALPL (tissue-nonspecific), ALPI, ALPP, ALPG
Clinical useLiver function test; marker of bone turnover

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is a family of enzymes that remove phosphate groups from many kinds of molecules, including nucleotides, proteins and alkaloids, a process called dephosphorylation. As the name suggests, they work most effectively in an alkaline environment — the bacterial enzyme has a pH optimum around 10. ALP is a homodimeric metalloenzyme containing zinc and magnesium ions that are essential to its activity.

Isoenzymes

Humans possess four isozymes. The tissue-nonspecific isozyme (TNAP, encoded by ALPL) is expressed in liver, bone and kidney; separate intestinal, placental and germ-cell forms have their own genes. Mutations in ALPL cause hypophosphatasia, a disorder of bone mineralisation.

Clinical significance

Serum ALP is measured as part of the standard liver function panel. Elevated levels occur in cholestatic liver disease and bile-duct obstruction, in bone diseases with increased osteoblast activity such as Paget's disease of bone and healing fractures, and normally during growth and pregnancy (from the placental isozyme). Abnormally low values may indicate hypophosphatasia, severe malnutrition or hypothyroidism.

Laboratory use

In molecular biology, alkaline phosphatase (from calf intestine or shrimp) is used to remove 5′ phosphate groups from DNA to prevent self-ligation of vectors, and as a reporter enzyme in immunoassays such as ELISA and in chemiluminescent detection on blots. In the dairy industry, the ALP test exploits the fact that the enzyme in milk is inactivated by proper pasteurisation.
