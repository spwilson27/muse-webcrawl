Stories of the Prophets

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stories of the Prophets (Arabic: قصص الأنبياء, Qiṣaṣ al-Anbiyāʾ) is a genre of Islamic literature comprising collections of narratives about the prophets recognised in Islam, from Adam through Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, up to Muhammad. The collections expand on the often brief accounts given in the Quran, drawing on hadith, Quranic commentary (tafsir), and earlier Jewish and Christian traditions known as Isra'iliyyat.

Notable collections

Famous compilations include the Qisas al-Anbiya of al-Tha'labi (died 1035), valued for its literary style, and the more hadith-focused version of Ibn Kathir (died 1373). Other authors in the tradition include al-Kisa'i and al-Tabari, whose universal history contains extensive prophetic narratives. Persian and Turkish adaptations, often richly illustrated, were produced in the Timurid, Safavid, and Ottoman periods and were popular works of devotional reading.

Content and character

The stories typically follow a shared pattern: the prophet's miraculous birth and calling, his preaching to a resistant people, their punishment or deliverance, and moral lessons for the reader. They supply details absent from the Quran — names, dialogues, and episodes — and were widely used in sermons and popular teaching. Scholars historically debated the reliability of the narrative embellishments, since many derive from non-Islamic sources.
