The Infrared Astronomical Satellite performed the first all-sky survey in the infrared.

The Infrared Astronomical Satellite (IRAS) was a space telescope that performed the first complete survey of the entire sky at infrared wavelengths. A joint project of the United States (NASA), the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom, IRAS mapped 96% of the sky four times in the 12, 25, 60, and 100 micrometre bands during its ten months of operation in 1983, revolutionising infrared astronomy before its supply of coolant was exhausted.

Mission

IRAS was launched on 26 January 1983 into a polar Sun-synchronous orbit about 900 km high. Its 57 cm telescope and focal plane were cooled to a few kelvin by 720 litres of superfluid helium, which boiled away steadily and ended the mission on 21 November 1983. The satellite carried 62 detectors and, in addition to the survey, obtained low-resolution spectra of brighter sources with its Low Resolution Spectrometer.

Discoveries

IRAS catalogued roughly 350,000 infrared sources in its Point Source Catalog. Among its discoveries were:

  • The dust disk around the star Vega — the first direct evidence of a debris disk around a main-sequence star.
  • Infrared cirrus: filamentary clouds of interstellar dust pervading the galaxy.
  • Six new comets, including Comet IRAS–Araki–Alcock, which made one of the closest cometary approaches to Earth of the twentieth century in May 1983.
  • A burst of star formation in interacting galaxies and the class of ultraluminous infrared galaxies (ULIRGs), whose power comes mostly from obscured starbursts and active nuclei.
  • The dusty torus and starburst phenomena in numerous galaxies, and many asteroids, including the near-Earth asteroid 3200 Phaethon, the parent body of the Geminid meteor shower.

Legacy

The IRAS catalogs remained a standard reference for decades and were followed by later infrared survey missions including the Japanese Akari satellite and NASA's Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE). A rumoured detection of an unknown massive object — reported in press coverage at the time as a possible "tenth planet" — was later explained as distant galaxies and cirrus, and is remembered as an episode of 1983 astronomical folklore.
