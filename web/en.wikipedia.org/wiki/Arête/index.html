Arête

An arête is a thin, sharp, knife-edge ridge of rock that separates two glacial valleys or cirques. The word is French for "edge" or "ridge". Arêtes form where two glaciers erode parallel or opposing valleys, or where two cirques erode toward each other, leaving a narrow crest between them.

Formation

As glaciers pluck and abrade the rock on either side, the divide between them is steepened and narrowed. Frost weathering continues to sharpen the crest after the ice retreats. Where three or more cirques meet, the result may be a pyramidal peak or horn, such as the Matterhorn.

Examples

Famous arêtes include the Knife Edge on Katahdin in Maine, the Clouds Rest and Striding Edge (in England's Lake District) routes, the Peuterey and Brouillard ridges of Mont Blanc, and the Crib Goch ridge on Snowdon in Wales. Such ridges present classic scrambling and alpine climbing routes, often with severe exposure on both sides.
