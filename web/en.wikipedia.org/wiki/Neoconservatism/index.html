Neoconservatism

Neoconservatism is a political movement in the United States that arose in the 1960s among liberal hawks who became disenchanted with the Democratic Party's pacifist turn and the counterculture. Neoconservatives typically advocate the promotion of democracy and American interests abroad, including through military power, a strong national defence, and scepticism toward international institutions when they constrain American action.

History

The first generation — figures such as Irving Kristol (dubbed the movement's "godfather"), Norman Podhoretz, Jeane Kirkpatrick and Daniel Patrick Moynihan — emerged from anti-communist liberalism and the circle around journals like Commentary and The Public Interest. Neoconservatives supported Ronald Reagan's hard line against the Soviet Union and gained prominence through the Project for the New American Century (founded 1997).

Influence and criticism

Neoconservative thinkers were influential advocates of the 2003 invasion of Iraq within the administration of George W. Bush, arguing that removing Saddam Hussein would spread democracy in the Middle East. The troubled occupation damaged the movement's standing; critics associate neoconservatism with over-militarised foreign policy, while supporters credit it with moral clarity about authoritarian regimes. The label is rarely self-applied today, and the movement's role within American conservatism has been reshaped by populist challenges since 2016.
