U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton

U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779 (1995), was a landmark case in which the Supreme Court of the United States held that states may not enact qualifications for prospective members of the United States Congress that are stricter than those specified in the United States Constitution. The decision invalidated congressional term limits adopted by 23 states.

Background

In 1992, Arkansas voters adopted Amendment 73 to the state constitution, which barred congressional candidates who had already served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate from appearing on the ballot. Congressman Ray Thornton and others challenged the amendment, supported by the Arkansas attorney general; U.S. Term Limits, a national term-limits advocacy group, defended it.

Decision

Writing for a 5–4 majority, Justice John Paul Stevens held that the qualifications for Congress set out in the Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution are exclusive, and that neither states nor Congress may add to them. A state amendment limiting ballot access for long-serving incumbents was, in substance, an added qualification and therefore unconstitutional. Justice Kennedy concurred, emphasizing federalism and the national character of congressional office.

Dissent and significance

Justice Clarence Thomas, joined by three colleagues, dissented, arguing that the Constitution is silent on the question and that reserved powers allow states to set such rules. The ruling ended the state-level congressional term-limits movement, leaving a constitutional amendment as the only route to term limits for Congress.
