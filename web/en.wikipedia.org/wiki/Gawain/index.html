Sir Gawain (Welsh: Gwalchmai) is a knight of King Arthur's Round Table in the Arthurian legend. The eldest son of King Lot and Morgause — Arthur's sister — he is one of the oldest and most prominent figures of the tradition, celebrated as the model of courtesy and chivalry, though later prose cycles darken his character with vengefulness and moral failure.

Appearances

Gawain appears in Geoffrey of Monmouth and Chrétien de Troyes, and is the hero of numerous romances, most famously the 14th-century Middle English poem Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, in which he accepts a beheading game, resists temptation at a lord's castle, and returns to Camelot humbled by a single lapse of honesty. In Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Gawain's feud with Lancelot — after Lancelot kills Gawain's brothers — prevents reconciliation before the final battle; dying, Gawain forgives him. In some traditions his strength waxes until noon with the sun.
