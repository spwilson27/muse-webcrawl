Tarot card games

Tarot card games are a family of trick-taking card games played with a tarot pack, originally created in northern Italy in the 1440s. Tarot packs contain 78 cards: four ordinary suits of 14 cards (including four court cards), a fifth suit of 21 permanent trumps (the trionfi), and a single card, the Fool. Long before tarot was associated with fortune-telling, it was — and in parts of Europe still is — a gaming pack; indeed, tarot games are the pack's original purpose.

Common features

Tarot games are typically point-trick games: players aim not merely to win tricks but to capture valuable cards, since cards carry point values (kings, trumps I, XXI and the Fool usually being the most valuable). The three high-value trumps — I (the Magician, called Pagat or Bagatto), XXI (the World, Mond) and the Fool (the Sküs or Excuse) — form a group known in French as the bouts ("ends"). The Fool usually plays the role of an "excuse" that may be played instead of following suit. Many games feature bonus announcements and special plays, such as winning the last trick with trump I (pagat ultimo).

Major games

French Tarot (jeu du tarot), played with the full 78-card pack for three to five players, is France's second most popular card game and has an official federation and national championships. In Austria and neighbouring countries, Königrufen ("calling the kings") is the leading member of a large family of 54-card games. Italy preserves early forms such as scarto and the complex Bolognese game of tarocchini, while Piedmontese and Lombard games use reduced packs. Switzerland's Troccas and Scarto variants, Danish Tarok, and the German games of Dreierles and Baden Tarock survive in regional pockets.

Packs

Game packs differ from the packs used for cartomancy. Surviving patterns include the Tarot de Marseille, the Italian Tarocco Piemontese and Tarocco Bolognese, and the French-suited packs used for Königrufen, whose trumps depict rustic and genre scenes. French Tarot uses a purpose-designed French-suited pack whose trumps show traditional scenes in a distinctive mirrored style.
