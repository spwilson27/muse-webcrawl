Matrix Tree theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the mathematical field of graph theory, Kirchhoff's theorem, or Kirchhoff's matrix tree theorem, named after Gustav Kirchhoff, gives the number of spanning trees in a graph in terms of the determinant of a matrix derived from the graph. It is a generalisation of Cayley's formula, which gives the number of spanning trees in a complete graph.

Statement

Let G be a graph with n labelled vertices. Form its Laplacian matrix L: the entry Lii is the degree of vertex i, and the entry Lij for i ≠ j is minus the number of edges between vertices i and j. Delete any one row and the corresponding column to obtain a cofactor matrix. Kirchhoff's theorem states that the number of spanning trees of G equals the determinant of any such cofactor — equivalently, any cofactor of the Laplacian. All cofactors of the Laplacian are equal, and the number also equals (1/n) times the product of the non-zero eigenvalues of L.

Example

For a complete graph on n vertices, the theorem yields nn−2 spanning trees, Cayley's formula. For small graphs, the determinant can be computed directly: a graph shaped as a square with one diagonal, for instance, has a Laplacian cofactor with determinant 8, and indeed the graph has exactly eight spanning trees.

History and applications

Kirchhoff proved the theorem in 1847 in the course of his work on electrical networks, where spanning trees enter the solution of circuit equations. The theorem underlies algorithms for counting and randomly generating spanning trees (uniform random spanning trees can be sampled using the theorem's connection to effective resistance and random walks, as in Wilson's algorithm), and it appears in the analysis of network reliability, chemistry and statistical mechanics. Weighted versions, in which each edge contributes a weight (such as a conductance), give the sum over spanning trees of the product of their edge weights.
