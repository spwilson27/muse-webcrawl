Mass–energy equivalence

In physics, mass–energy equivalence is the relationship between mass and energy in a system's rest frame, expressed by the famous formula:

E = mc2

where E is energy, m is mass, and c is the speed of light in vacuum (approximately 299,792,458 metres per second). The formula, derived by Albert Einstein in 1905 as part of special relativity, states that mass is a form of energy: even an object at rest possesses energy proportional to its mass, with the speed of light squared as the conversion factor. Because c2 is enormous, a small amount of mass corresponds to a very large amount of energy.

History

Einstein introduced the relationship in his paper "Does the Inertia of a Body Depend Upon Its Energy Content?", published in November 1905, one of his annus mirabilis papers. Earlier thinkers including Isaac Newton, Emanuel Swedenborg, and Henri Poincaré had proposed qualitative or partial versions of the idea, but Einstein first derived the complete equivalence from the principles of relativity.

Consequences

The equivalence explains the energy released in nuclear fission and fusion: when nuclei split or combine, the products have slightly less mass than the reactants, and the difference appears as kinetic energy and radiation. It underlies nuclear power and nuclear weapons, the shining of the Sun (which converts about 4 million tonnes of mass into energy each second), and particle physics, where collisions convert kinetic energy into the mass of new particles and back. The general relation including momentum is E2 = (pc)2 + (mc2)2, which also applies to massless particles such as photons.
