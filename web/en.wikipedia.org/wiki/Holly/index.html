Holly

Holly (Ilex) is a genus of over 570 species of flowering plants in the family Aquifoliaceae, and the only living genus in that family. Hollies range from small shrubs to trees up to 25 metres tall and are distributed worldwide from the tropics to temperate zones. The best-known species is the European holly, Ilex aquifolium, long associated with Christmas decoration.

Description

Hollies are evergreen or deciduous, usually dioecious (with separate male and female plants). Leaves are often glossy with spiny or toothed margins — on European holly the lower leaves are spiny while higher leaves, out of reach of browsing animals, tend to be smooth-edged. The small white flowers are followed, on female plants, by red, black, or yellow berry-like drupes that persist through winter and are a food source for birds, though toxic to humans.

Cultivation and uses

Many hollies are popular garden ornamentals, including American holly (Ilex opaca) and numerous hybrids. Yerba mate (Ilex paraguariensis) is cultivated in South America for its caffeine-rich leaves used in the drink mate; guayusa and yaupon are other caffeinated hollies. Holly wood is dense, white, and fine-grained, historically used for inlay work, chess pieces, and whip handles.

Cultural significance

Holly has been a symbol of winter festivals since Roman Saturnalia and Celtic traditions, and remains a standard motif of Christmas cards and wreaths, famously paired with ivy in the carol "The Holly and the Ivy".
