Incorporation doctrine

The incorporation doctrine is a constitutional doctrine through which the first ten amendments of the United States Bill of Rights are made applicable to the states through the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

History

In Barron v. Baltimore (1833), the Supreme Court held the Bill of Rights bound only the federal government. After the Fourteenth Amendment (1868), litigants argued its Privileges or Immunities Clause incorporated the Bill of Rights, but the Slaughter-House Cases (1873) read that clause narrowly, pushing incorporation toward due process.

Selective incorporation

The Court adopted selective incorporation: rights deemed fundamental to ordered liberty are applied to the states case by case. Landmark steps include Gitlow v. New York (1925, speech), Mapp v. Ohio (1961, exclusionary rule), Gideon v. Wainwright (1963, counsel), McDonald v. Chicago (2010, Second Amendment) and Timbs v. Indiana (2019, excessive fines). Provisions not yet fully incorporated include the Third Amendment (in most circuits), the grand-jury requirement of the Fifth Amendment, and the Seventh Amendment civil jury right.

Theories

Justices have debated total incorporation (favoured by Hugo Black), selective incorporation, and no incorporation. The doctrine underpins most modern civil liberties litigation against state action.
