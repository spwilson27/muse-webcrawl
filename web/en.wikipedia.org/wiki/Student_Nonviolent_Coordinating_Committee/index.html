The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC, pronounced "snick") was the principal channel of student commitment in the United States to the civil rights movement during the 1960s. Emerging from the sit-in movement of 1960, SNCC became one of the most important organisations of the era, known for grassroots organising, voter-registration drives and an ethos of participatory democracy.

Origins

In February 1960 four students in Greensboro, North Carolina, began sit-ins at segregated lunch counters, sparking protests across the South. Ella Baker, executive director of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, convened a conference at Shaw University in April 1960 that founded SNCC as an independent student organisation. Early leaders included Diane Nash, Marion Barry, Bernard Lafayette and John Lewis, the organisation's chairman from 1963 to 1966.

Major campaigns

SNCC members were central to the 1961 Freedom Rides challenging segregation in interstate travel; to voter-registration projects in Mississippi under Bob Moses; to the 1963 Albany and Birmingham campaigns; and to Freedom Summer (1964), which brought hundreds of northern volunteers to Mississippi and saw the murders of civil-rights workers James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner. SNCC workers helped organise the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, whose challenge to the all-white delegation at the 1964 Democratic National Convention—highlighted by Fannie Lou Hamer's televised testimony—drew national attention.

Later years

From 1966, under chairman Stokely Carmichael, SNCC popularised the slogan "Black Power" and moved away from strict nonviolence and interracial staffing. H. Rap Brown succeeded Carmichael in 1967. Fractured by internal disputes and weakened by repression and the pressures of the late 1960s, the organisation declined and effectively disbanded in the mid-1970s.
