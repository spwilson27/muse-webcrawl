Souliotes

The Souliotes were an Eastern Orthodox community famed for their martial prowess and their resistance to Ottoman rule. They inhabited the mountainous region of Souli in Epirus, in modern northwestern Greece, from the 16th century onwards, forming a confederation of villages and clans that enjoyed virtual autonomy in exchange for guarding the mountain passes.

History

According to tradition, the Souliotes descended from Greek and Albanian-speaking Christian refugees who fled Ottoman expansion into the remote mountains near the Acheron river. Organized in clans (fares) such as the Botsaris and Tzavelas families, they lived by herding and raiding, and served at times as mercenaries for local powers.

From 1790 the Ottoman-Albanian ruler Ali Pasha of Ioannina campaigned to subdue them. After years of siege and blockade, the Souliotes evacuated Souli under a negotiated agreement in December 1803. As one column of Souliotes was attacked in violation of the terms, a group of Souliote women, according to tradition, danced their way to the cliffs of Mount Zalongo and threw themselves and their infants over the precipice rather than be captured — the celebrated Dance of Zalongo.

Role in the Greek War of Independence

Exiled mainly to the Ionian Islands, many Souliotes returned to fight in the Greek War of Independence (1821–1829). The Souliote Markos Botsaris became one of the war's most celebrated heroes, dying at Karpenisi in 1823. Others, including Kitsos Tzavelas, held commands and later posts in the independent Greek state. The Souliotes' resistance became a potent symbol of Greek national identity, celebrated in folk song and literature throughout Europe.
