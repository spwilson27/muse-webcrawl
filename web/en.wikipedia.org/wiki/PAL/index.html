PAL (Phase Alternating Line) is a colour encoding system for analogue television, used in broadcast systems in most of the world during the second half of the 20th century and the early 21st. It was developed in West Germany by Walter Bruch at Telefunken and introduced in 1967. Together with NTSC (used mainly in North America and Japan) and SECAM (France and its sphere), PAL was one of the three major analogue colour standards.

Technical details

PAL broadcasts 625 horizontal lines per frame at 25 interlaced frames per second (50 fields per second), of which 576 lines carry visible picture. Colour information is encoded on a subcarrier of 4.43361875 MHz. The system's defining feature is that the phase of one colour-difference component is reversed on alternate lines — "phase alternating line" — so that phase errors introduced in transmission, which cause hue shifts in NTSC, instead average out between neighbouring lines and appear only as slight saturation changes; this made PAL colour notably robust, earning it backronyms such as "Perfection At Last". Variants include PAL-M (Brazil, 525 lines) and PAL-N (Argentina and neighbours).

Adoption and legacy

PAL was adopted across most of Europe (except France), much of Asia, Australia, and parts of Africa and South America. The 50 Hz field rate matched the mains frequency in those regions. In video games, VHS and DVD distribution, "PAL" also loosely denoted the 576i/50 Hz video format and the regional release version. Analogue PAL broadcasts were progressively switched off in the 2000s–2020s as countries moved to digital terrestrial standards such as DVB-T, but PAL-derived timings persist in digital 576i production and in countless archived recordings.
