Scholasticism is the system of theology and philosophy taught in the medieval European universities, characterised by rigorous dialectical method: a question is posed, objections marshalled, an authoritative counter-text cited (sed contra), a determination given, and each objection answered in turn. Its monuments are the summae of Thomas Aquinas and the commentaries on Peter Lombard's Sentences and on Aristotle.

Development

Fed by translations of Aristotle, Avicenna and Averroes from Arabic, scholasticism integrated faith and reason at the new universities. Franciscan (Scotus) and Dominican (Thomist) schools diverged over universals, individuation and the will; nominalists like William of Ockham unsettled the synthesis. Long derided by humanists and early moderns as hair-splitting, it is now studied as a sophisticated research culture that invented much of the university's intellectual machinery.
