Rangeomorphs are a group of frondose organisms belonging to the Ediacara biota, the assemblage of large, complex life forms that flourished in the oceans roughly 571 to 539 million years ago, before the Cambrian explosion. They take their name from the genus Rangea, found in Namibia, and include well-known forms such as Charnia from Charnwood Forest in England and Fractofusus from Newfoundland.

Body plan

Rangeomorphs are built from repeated, branching units that iterate in a self-similar, fractal pattern across several orders of magnitude — a "modular" construction unlike that of any living animal group. Many species were frond-shaped, anchored to the deep-sea floor by a holdfast and stem, with a leaf-like petalodium held up into the water. Sizes ranged from a few centimetres to, in the case of some Charnia specimens and the giant reclining forms, over a metre or two. They had no mouth, gut, or appendages, and are thought to have absorbed nutrients directly from seawater across their large, finely divided surface.

Affinities and ecology

The relationships of rangeomorphs remain debated: they have been interpreted as early animals, as a separate extinct experiment in multicellular organization (the "Vendobionta"), or as stem-group members of living phyla. Fossil sites such as Mistaken Point in Newfoundland preserve entire deep-water communities of rangeomorphs smothered by volcanic ash, revealing tiered ecosystems in which different species occupied distinct heights above the seabed. Rangeomorphs declined and vanished near the end of the Ediacaran as mobile animals and burrowing transformed marine ecosystems.
