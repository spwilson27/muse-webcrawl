The Roman Warm Period, or Roman Climatic Optimum, was a period of unusually warm weather in Europe and the North Atlantic that ran approximately from 250 BC to AD 400, roughly spanning the height of the Roman Republic and Empire. Evidence from tree rings, ice cores, lake and ocean sediments, speleothems and glacier lengths, together with historical testimony, suggests that parts of the Roman world enjoyed warm, and in the Mediterranean relatively stable, conditions that favoured agriculture at higher latitudes and altitudes.

Evidence

Historians and palaeoclimatologists point to the cultivation of grapes and olives near their northern limits, Roman viticulture in Britain, high summer temperatures recorded in Alpine tree-ring series, and the retreat of Alpine glaciers exposing passes the Romans used. A 2012 study of tree rings from northern Europe reconstructed warm summers coinciding with the Empire's prosperity and cooler, more variable conditions during its later crises and the Migration Period. Marine sediment cores in the North Atlantic and Mediterranean likewise show warm sea-surface temperatures. Reconstructions differ on magnitude: many find Roman-era warmth in Europe comparable to, though generally slightly below, late twentieth-century levels, and the signal is strongest regionally rather than uniformly global.

Historical significance

Some scholars, including the historian Kyle Harper in The Fate of Rome (2017), argue that the benign climate helped sustain the agricultural surpluses underlying Roman expansion, and that its end — followed by the abrupt Late Antique Little Ice Age triggered by volcanic eruptions from AD 536 — contributed to the stresses of late antiquity, alongside plague and political upheaval. Others caution against climatic determinism, noting the Empire's resilience through earlier fluctuations and the regional heterogeneity of the evidence. The term is sometimes invoked in public debate about modern climate change, though scientists emphasise that the Roman Warm Period was regional and gradual, unlike today's global, rapid, human-caused warming.
