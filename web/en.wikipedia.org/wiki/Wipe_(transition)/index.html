Wipe (transition)

For other transitions, see Cut (transition) and Dissolve (filmmaking).

A wipe is a type of film transition in which one shot replaces another as a moving boundary travels across the frame: the new image appears to push the old image off the screen. Unlike a dissolve, in which the two shots blend, the wipe keeps a sharp edge between them, and unlike a cut, the change is gradual and visibly staged.

Forms

Wipes may run horizontally, vertically or diagonally, open as an iris or circle, or use shaped masks — stars, hearts, clocks and other patterns. The "clock wipe" sweeps around the frame like a rotating clock hand, and a "matrix wipe" tiles the transition in a grid of small elements. In the silent era and in early sound film, wipes were created optically; in video they were generated electronically, and modern editing software offers them as standard presets.

Usage and style

Wipes were especially common in 1930s and 1940s adventure serials and newsreels. George Lucas revived them deliberately in the Star Wars films in homage to those serials and to Kurosawa's films, and they are closely associated with that series' visual grammar. Akira Kurosawa used wipes extensively in films such as The Hidden Fortress. Because the effect calls attention to itself, contemporary dramas generally avoid it in favour of cuts, but it remains popular in sports broadcasting, comedy and stylized genres.
