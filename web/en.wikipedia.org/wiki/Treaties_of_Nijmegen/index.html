Treaties of Nijmegen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Treaties of Peace of Nijmegen (Traités de Paix de Nimègue; German: Friedensverträge von Nimwegen) were a series of treaties signed in the Dutch city of Nijmegen between August 1678 and December 1679. They ended the Franco-Dutch War (1672–1678) and the associated conflicts between France and its opponents, and marked the height of Louis XIV's power and territorial expansion in the seventeenth century.

Background

France had invaded the Dutch Republic in 1672, the rampjaar ("disaster year"), allied with England, Münster, and Cologne. Dutch resistance under William III of Orange, flooding of the countryside, and the entry of Spain, the Holy Roman Emperor, Brandenburg, and Denmark into the war turned the conflict into a general European struggle. Peace congresses convened at Nijmegen from 1676, though fighting continued while terms were negotiated.

Principal terms

  • France–Dutch Republic (10 August 1678): France returned Maastricht and other occupied Dutch territory, and the Dutch recovered their pre-war borders; France withdrew its punitive tariff of 1667.
  • France–Spain (17 September 1678): Spain ceded Franche-Comté and a chain of fortified towns in the Spanish Netherlands, including Valenciennes, Cambrai, Saint-Omer, Ypres, and Maubeuge, giving France a stronger north-eastern frontier — Vauban's pré carré.
  • France–Holy Roman Empire (5 February 1679): The Emperor confirmed French possession of Freiburg im Breisgau and recognised the arrangements in Alsace, while France restored Philippsburg and rights on the right bank of the Rhine.
  • Scandinavian and German settlements (1679): In the treaties of Celle, Saint-Germain, Fontainebleau, and Lund, France compelled Brandenburg to return most of its conquests from Sweden, restoring Sweden's German possessions, and made peace with Denmark.

Consequences

Nijmegen left France the dominant power in Europe, with improved frontiers in Flanders and full possession of Franche-Comté. Yet the settlement stored up future conflict: Louis XIV's subsequent "reunions" policy, the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, and French pressure on the Rhine convinced former enemies to combine in the League of Augsburg, producing the Nine Years' War a decade later. The congress itself was an early example of multilateral peace-making, with mediation — including by the papal nuncio and English envoys — prefiguring later European congress diplomacy.
