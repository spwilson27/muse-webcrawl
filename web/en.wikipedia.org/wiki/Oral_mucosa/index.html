The oral mucosa lines the lips, cheeks, palate, tongue, and floor of the mouth.

The oral mucosa is the mucous membrane lining the inside of the mouth. It comprises stratified squamous epithelium (termed oral epithelium) and the underlying connective tissue termed lamina propria. The oral cavity has been described as a mirror that reflects the health of the individual, since many systemic diseases manifest in the oral mucosa.

Classification

Oral mucosa can be divided into three main categories based on function and histology:

  • Masticatory mucosa — keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, found on the hard palate and gingiva, which withstands the forces of chewing.
  • Lining mucosa — nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium, found on the lips, cheeks, alveolar surfaces, floor of the mouth, ventral tongue, and soft palate. It is flexible and distensible.
  • Specialized mucosa — found on the dorsum of the tongue, containing the lingual papillae and taste buds.

Structure

The oral epithelium is separated from the lamina propria by a basement membrane. Unlike the skin, the oral mucosa has no stratum lucidum, and its surface cells in keratinized regions lack nuclei at maturity. Beneath the mucosa in many regions lies the submucosa, containing fat, muscle, and the minor salivary glands that keep the surface moist.

Functions

The oral mucosa protects the underlying tissues from mechanical, chemical, and biological insults; provides sensation (touch, temperature, taste); hosts immune defenses; and secretes saliva via associated glands. It heals rapidly and usually without scarring compared with skin.

Clinical significance

Diseases affecting the oral mucosa include oral cancer, lichen planus, aphthous ulcers (canker sores), candidiasis, and leukoplakia. Changes in the mucosa may indicate nutritional deficiencies, autoimmune disease, or systemic illness.
