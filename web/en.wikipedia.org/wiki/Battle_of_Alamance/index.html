The Battle of Alamance, fought on 16 May 1771, was the climactic confrontation of the War of the Regulation, a rebellion of backcountry colonists in the Province of North Carolina against corrupt local officials, excessive fees, and unrepresentative taxation. Royal Governor William Tryon led about 1,000 provincial militiamen, supported by artillery, against roughly 2,000 Regulators who lacked unified leadership and cannon.

Background

Farmers in the Piedmont counties had organized as "Regulators" from the mid-1760s, petitioning against dishonest sheriffs and court officials and eventually disrupting courts by force. After negotiations failed, Tryon marched west from Hillsborough to compel submission.

The battle

On Great Alamance Creek, Tryon's militia opened fire with artillery after the Regulators refused to disperse. The fighting lasted about two hours. The Regulators, fighting as skirmishers from the woods, initially held their ground but withdrew when their ammunition ran low and their improvised leadership collapsed. Casualty figures are uncertain; roughly nine militia were killed and dozens wounded, while Regulator losses were probably higher.

Aftermath

Tryon hanged one captured Regulator on the field and later tried twelve men at Hillsborough, six of whom were executed. Thousands of backcountry settlers were required to swear oaths of allegiance. The battle ended the Regulation, and the site is preserved today as Alamance Battleground State Historic Site. Some historians have called it a precursor to the American Revolution, though many former Regulators later remained loyalist in sympathy — aggrieved less by Britain than by the eastern colonial elite.
