Medical imaging

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medical imaging is the technique and process of imaging the interior of a body for clinical analysis and medical intervention, as well as visual representation of the function of some organs or tissues. It seeks to reveal internal structures hidden by the skin and bones, and to diagnose and treat disease.

Modalities

Radiography projects X-rays through the body onto a detector; fluoroscopy provides moving X-ray images in real time. Computed tomography (CT) combines many X-ray measurements to produce cross-sectional images. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) uses strong magnetic fields and radio waves (without ionizing radiation) to generate detailed soft-tissue images. Ultrasound uses high-frequency sound waves and is widely used in obstetrics and cardiology. Nuclear medicine techniques such as PET and SPECT image physiological function using radiotracers. Other modalities include mammography, angiography, and optical and photoacoustic imaging.

History

The field began in 1895 with Wilhelm Röntgen's discovery of X-rays and the first radiographs. CT scanning followed in the early 1970s (Godfrey Hounsfield and Allan Cormack shared the 1979 Nobel Prize), and clinical MRI developed in the late 1970s and 1980s following work by Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield.

Use and safety

Imaging guides diagnosis, treatment planning, image-guided surgery, and screening programs such as mammography. Techniques using ionizing radiation carry small stochastic risks, so their use is governed by justification and dose-optimization principles (ALARA). Images are stored and exchanged in the DICOM standard and interpreted by radiologists, increasingly assisted by artificial-intelligence tools.
