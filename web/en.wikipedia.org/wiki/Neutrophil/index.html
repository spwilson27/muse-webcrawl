Neutrophils are a type of white blood cell (leukocyte) and the most abundant granulocytes, making up 40–70% of all white blood cells in humans. They form an essential part of the innate immune system and are the first cells recruited to sites of infection or tissue injury. Their name derives from their neutral staining: neither strongly acidic nor basic dyes colour them distinctly. Mature neutrophils have a characteristic segmented, multi-lobed nucleus (hence "polymorphonuclear leukocytes," PMNs) and cytoplasm packed with granules.

Function

Neutrophils defend against bacterial and fungal infections primarily by phagocytosis: they engulf microbes into a phagosome that fuses with granules containing antimicrobial enzymes (such as myeloperoxidase, defensins and lysozyme), while a respiratory burst generates reactive oxygen species to kill the ingested organism. Neutrophils can also release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) — webs of decondensed chromatin and granule proteins that trap and kill pathogens extracellularly. Dead neutrophils are a major component of pus.

Clinical aspects

Low neutrophil counts (neutropenia) increase susceptibility to infection and can result from chemotherapy, bone-marrow disorders or autoimmune destruction; severe congenital neutropenia and chronic granulomatous disease illustrate the consequences of neutrophil deficiency. High counts (neutrophilia) commonly accompany acute bacterial infection and inflammation. The circulating half-life of neutrophils is short — on the order of hours to a day or so — and the marrow produces on the order of 10¹¹ cells daily to replace them.
