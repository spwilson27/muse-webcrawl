Sir John Oldcastle (c. 1378 – 14 December 1417) was an English Lollard leader. From his base in Kent, he headed a short-lived revolt against King Henry V in January 1414, was captured after three years on the run, and was hanged and burned as a heretic and traitor. He is a likely partial inspiration for William Shakespeare's character Sir John Falstaff.

Life

Oldcastle was a soldier who served in Wales during the Glyndŵr Rising and became a friend of the Prince of Wales, the future Henry V. Through marriage he became Lord Cobham and sat in the House of Lords. He was a committed adherent of the Lollards, followers of the teachings of John Wycliffe.

Heresy proceedings and revolt

In 1413 Oldcastle was tried for heresy before Archbishop Thomas Arundel. He escaped from the Tower of London and in January 1414 gathered Lollard supporters at St Giles's Fields outside London in an attempt to seize the king — the so-called Oldcastle Revolt. The plot was betrayed and easily suppressed; many rebels were executed, but Oldcastle escaped into the Welsh Marches.

Capture and death

Oldcastle remained at large until November 1417, when he was captured near Welshpool by men of Edward Charleton, Lord of Powys. He was brought to London, condemned in Parliament, and on 14 December 1417 hanged over a slow fire at St Giles's Fields — hanged as a traitor and burned as a heretic.

Legacy

Protestant martyrologist John Foxe later celebrated Oldcastle as a proto-Protestant martyr. Shakespeare's fat knight in Henry IV was originally named Oldcastle, but the name was changed to Falstaff, reportedly after complaints from Oldcastle's descendants, the Cobham family.
