A pointing device is an input device that allows a user to input spatial (continuous and multi-dimensional) data to a computer. Pointing devices control a pointer or cursor on a display, enabling the user to select, move, and manipulate objects in a graphical user interface (GUI). They are a central component of human–computer interaction and of the WIMP paradigm (windows, icons, menus, pointer).

History

The earliest pointing devices included the light pen (developed in the 1950s for the SAGE air-defence system) and the trackball, invented in Canada in the early 1950s for the DATAR project. The computer mouse was invented by Douglas Engelbart and Bill English at Stanford Research Institute in the 1960s and demonstrated in the famous 1968 "Mother of All Demos." Touchscreens, graphics tablets, and touchpads followed, becoming mainstream with personal computers, laptops, and mobile devices.

Common pointing devices

Mouse

The mouse is moved across a surface; mechanical (ball) designs were superseded by optical and laser sensors. Buttons and scroll wheels provide clicking and scrolling input.

Touchpad

A touchpad (trackpad) senses finger position on a flat surface and is standard on laptops; modern versions support multi-touch gestures.

Touchscreen

A touchscreen combines display and input: the user points directly at objects on screen using resistive, capacitive, or other sensing technologies.

Other devices

Trackballs, joysticks, pointing sticks, light pens, graphics tablets with styluses, eye-tracking systems, and motion controllers (such as the Wii Remote) all serve as pointing devices in particular contexts.

Performance

Pointing performance is commonly modelled by Fitts's law, which predicts movement time from target distance and size. Devices are compared by throughput, accuracy, and ergonomic factors; prolonged use is associated with repetitive strain considerations addressed by ergonomic designs.
