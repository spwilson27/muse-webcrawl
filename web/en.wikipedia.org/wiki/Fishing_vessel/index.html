A fishing vessel is a boat or ship used to catch fish and other valuable nektonic aquatic animals in the sea, ocean, lakes or rivers. Humans have used different kinds of surface vessels in commercial, artisanal and recreational fishing. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization, there are around 4.6 million fishing vessels worldwide, the great majority of them small, undecked and non-motorised artisanal craft, alongside a much smaller number of large industrial vessels.

Types

Commercial vessels are classified by the gear they deploy. Trawlers drag nets through the water or along the seabed; purse seiners surround schools of fish with a wall of netting; longliners set lines with thousands of baited hooks; dredgers scrape shellfish from the bottom; and factory ships — the largest fishing vessels — process and freeze their catch on board, remaining at sea for months. Other types include drifters, trollers, crabbers and squid jiggers, the latter attracting squid with bright lights at night.

History

Fishing boats evolved from dugout canoes and rafts to sailing smacks and schooners that worked the great cod and herring fisheries. Steam trawlers appeared in the late 19th century, diesel engines and hydraulic winches in the 20th, and the post-war combination of sonar, radar and factory stern trawlers transformed the scale of fishing — contributing to the collapse of stocks such as the Grand Banks cod fishery in 1992.

Safety and regulation

Commercial fishing remains one of the most dangerous occupations, and fishing vessels are subject to licensing, quotas, vessel monitoring systems and safety rules, though international safety conventions have been slow to enter into force. Fleets are increasingly tracked by satellite AIS data by organisations monitoring illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing.
