Curia Hostilia
LocationComitium, Roman Forum, Rome

FunctionMeeting house of the Roman Senate

The Curia Hostilia was one of the original senate houses of ancient Rome, located in the Comitium at the north-west corner of the Roman Forum. Tradition attributed its construction to Tullus Hostilius, the third king of Rome, from whom it took its name.

History

The building served as the principal meeting place of the Roman Senate for centuries. It was enlarged by the dictator Sulla around 80 BC to accommodate a Senate expanded to 600 members. In 52 BC, during the funeral of the murdered politician Publius Clodius Pulcher, a mob used the senate house as his pyre and the Curia Hostilia burned down. Faustus Sulla rebuilt it as the Curia Cornelia; Julius Caesar later demolished it and began a new senate house, the Curia Julia, completed by Augustus in 29 BC, which still stands.

Description

The Curia Hostilia faced south onto the Comitium and stood near the spot where, according to legend, the Sabine and Roman peoples were united. It contained tiered seating for the senators and was adorned with famous paintings, including a panel commemorating Manius Valerius Maximus Messalla's victory over Hiero of Syracuse in 263 BC.
