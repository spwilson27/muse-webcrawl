Australopithecus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Australopithecus
Lived: About 4.2–1.9 million years ago
Region: Eastern and southern Africa
Known species include: A. afarensis ('Lucy'), A. africanus, A. sediba

Australopithecus is a genus of early hominins that lived in Africa between about 4.2 and 1.9 million years ago, ancestors or close relatives of our own genus, Homo. They walked upright on two legs while retaining climbing adaptations, and had brains roughly a third the size of ours.

The genus entered science in 1924 with Raymond Dart's Taung Child (A. africanus) from South Africa — dismissed for decades because its small brain contradicted the Piltdown forgery's big-brain-first expectation. The most famous specimen is "Lucy" (1974, Ethiopia), a 40%-complete skeleton of A. afarensis (3.2 million years old), whose pelvis and knees prove habitual bipedalism; the Laetoli footprints (3.66 million years, Tanzania) show the gait itself, preserved in ash.

Australopiths split into gracile forms and the robust lineage (often separated as Paranthropus) with massive chewing apparatus; from the gracile stock, by about 2.8–2.3 million years ago, Homo emerged — the hinge on which the geologic story of the Pleistocene turns toward stone tools, fire and us (see Stone Age).
