Ages of Man

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Allegory of the ages of human life (image placeholder)

The Ages of Man are the historical stages of human existence according to Greek mythology. Two classical authors in particular offer accounts of a successive deterioration of the human condition: Hesiod in Works and Days (c. 700 BCE) names five ages — Golden, Silver, Bronze, Heroic, and Iron — and Ovid in the Metamorphoses simplifies the scheme to four metallic ages: Gold, Silver, Bronze, and Iron.

Hesiod's five ages

  • Golden Age — In the reign of Cronus, humans lived like gods, free from toil, sorrow, and old age, and the earth produced food spontaneously. After death they became benevolent guardian spirits.
  • Silver Age — A lesser race, childish for a hundred years, then brief and violent in adulthood; they refused to worship the gods and were destroyed by Zeus.
  • Bronze Age — A warlike generation who made weapons and houses of bronze, ate no bread, and destroyed themselves by their own hands.
  • Heroic Age — The one age better than the one before: the demigod heroes of Thebes and Troy, some of whom went after death to the Isles of the Blessed.
  • Iron Age — Hesiod's own age, of unceasing labour, strife between kin, dishonour of parents, and the eventual departure of Shame and Nemesis from the earth.

Other traditions

Ovid's four-age version omits the Heroic Age. Similar schemes appear elsewhere: the Hindu yuga cycle (Satya, Treta, Dvapara, Kali) likewise declines in virtue over time; the Book of Daniel portrays a statue of gold, silver, bronze, iron, and iron mixed with clay representing successive kingdoms; and Shakespeare's "Seven Ages of Man" speech in As You Like It instead describes the stages of an individual life.

Influence

The metallic ages became a durable metaphor in European literature and art for historical decline, echoed in primitivism, in the humanist periodization of antiquity, the Middle Ages, and the Renaissance, and in the archaeological Three-age system of Stone, Bronze, and Iron ages proposed by Christian Jürgensen Thomsen in the nineteenth century.
