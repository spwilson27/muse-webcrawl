Zuiderzee Works

The Zuiderzee Works (Dutch: Zuiderzeewerken) is a system of dams and dikes, land reclamation, and water drainage work in the Netherlands that closed off the Zuiderzee, a shallow bay of the North Sea, and converted it into the freshwater lake IJsselmeer. The works shortened the Dutch coastline by roughly 300 km, protected the interior from flooding, and created large areas of new agricultural land (polders) that became the province of Flevoland. Together with the later Delta Works, they are regarded as one of the great hydraulic engineering projects of the 20th century.

Planning

Plans to reclaim the Zuiderzee date to the 17th century, but technology and finance lagged. Engineer and minister Cornelis Lely produced a feasible plan in 1891. After the devastating flood of January 1916 and food shortages during the First World War, parliament passed the Zuiderzee Act in 1918. A trial polder, the Andijk pilot, tested drainage and soil management methods in 1926–1927.

The dams and polders

The centrepiece, the 32-km Afsluitdijk, was completed in 1932, separating the new IJsselmeer from the Wadden Sea. The Wieringermeer polder was drained in 1930, followed by the Noordoostpolder (1942), Eastern Flevoland (1957), and Southern Flevoland (1968) — the latter two forming the island-province of Flevoland, home to cities such as Almere and Lelystad (named after Lely). The Houtribdijk (1976) created the Markermeer; a planned Markerwaard polder was repeatedly postponed and finally abandoned for environmental and economic reasons. Pumping stations, including the steam-powered Leemans gemaal and the diesel Wortman and electric Colijn stations, keep the polders drained.

Effects

The works ended the Zuiderzee fishing economy centred on ports such as Volendam and the island of Urk, changed regional ecology, and provided flood safety and farmland. They remain a symbol of Dutch water management; the American Society of Civil Engineers listed them among the Seven Wonders of the Modern World.
