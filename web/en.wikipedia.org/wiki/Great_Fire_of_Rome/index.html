The Great Fire of Rome (Latin: incendium magnum Romae) began on the night of 18–19 July AD 64 in the merchant shops around Rome's chariot stadium, the Circus Maximus. Driven by wind, the fire swept through the city's narrow streets and timber tenements for about six days before being brought under control, then rekindled and burned for several more. According to the historian Tacitus, of Rome's fourteen districts, only four remained intact; three were completely destroyed and seven reduced to ruins.

Nero and the aftermath

The emperor Nero, who was at Antium when the fire began, returned to organise relief: opening public buildings and his own gardens to the homeless, importing grain and cutting its price. Nevertheless, rumour blamed him for starting the fire — a charge repeated by Suetonius and Cassius Dio, who depict him singing of the fall of Troy as Rome burned. Modern historians generally doubt Nero's responsibility. To deflect blame and, in Tacitus's words, "to get rid of the report," Nero accused the city's Christians, who were executed with theatrical cruelty — an episode among the earliest attested persecutions of Christians by Rome.

Rebuilding

Nero exploited the cleared land to build his vast Domus Aurea ("Golden House") palace and imposed a new building code: wider streets, height limits, fire-resistant materials and porticoes from which fires could be fought. The fire thus reshaped Rome's urban fabric for centuries.
