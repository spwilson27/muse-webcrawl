Codification (law)

The Napoleonic Code (1804), the most influential modern codification

Field: Jurisprudence; civil law

Related concepts: Code, statute law, legal reform

In law, codification is the process of collecting and restating the law of a jurisdiction in certain areas, usually by statute, forming a legal code — a codex. The resulting code is intended to be a complete, systematic and authoritative statement of the law in its field, replacing the pre-existing mix of customary law, scattered statutes and judicial decisions. Codification is a hallmark of civil law systems, though common law jurisdictions also codify particular subjects, such as criminal law and commercial law.

History

Ancient codifications include the Code of Ur-Nammu (c. 2100 BC), the Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BC) and Roman law compilations culminating in Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis (6th century AD). The modern codification movement arose from Enlightenment rationalism: the Prussian Allgemeines Landrecht (1794), the French Civil Code or Napoleonic Code (1804), and the Austrian ABGB (1811) sought accessible, uniform national law. The Napoleonic Code — emphasising clarity, property rights and legal equality of male citizens — was carried across Europe and imitated in Latin America, the Middle East and Asia. Germany's Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (BGB, effective 1900) became a second great model, influential in Japan, China, Brazil and elsewhere.

Codification in common law countries

England largely resisted comprehensive codification — Jeremy Bentham, who coined the word "codification", campaigned for it in vain at home — but British jurists drafted influential codes for India, including the Indian Penal Code (1860) drafted under Thomas Macaulay. In the United States, most law is organised into statutory codes such as the United States Code, and uniform acts like the Uniform Commercial Code (1952) harmonise state law on commerce; Louisiana retains a civil code reflecting its French and Spanish heritage.

Debate

Supporters argue codification makes law accessible, certain and democratic, placing the law's content in the legislature's hands rather than leaving it to case-by-case judicial development. Critics, notably the German jurist Friedrich Carl von Savigny, argued that law grows organically from a people's customs and that premature codification freezes development and creates false certainty — codes still require judicial interpretation to function.
