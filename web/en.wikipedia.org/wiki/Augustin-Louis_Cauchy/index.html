Augustin-Louis Cauchy (21 August 1789 – 23 May 1857) was a French mathematician, engineer and physicist who made pioneering contributions to mathematical analysis and continuum mechanics. He was one of the first to state and rigorously prove theorems of calculus, founding the modern theory of limits and continuity, and he created much of the theory of complex functions. Cauchy published some 789 papers — more than any mathematician of his era except Euler — and his name is attached to dozens of theorems, equations and concepts.

Life

Cauchy was born in Paris during the Revolution and educated at the École Polytechnique and École des Ponts et Chaussées, working briefly as a military engineer at Cherbourg. Encouraged by Lagrange and Laplace, he turned to mathematics, winning the Academy's Grand Prix in 1816 for work on wave propagation and taking chairs at the École Polytechnique, the Sorbonne and the Collège de France. A staunch royalist and Catholic, he followed Charles X into exile in 1830, teaching in Turin and Prague, and returned to Paris in 1838; he declined to swear allegiance to Napoleon III in 1852 but kept his post.

Contributions

Cauchy's Cours d'analyse (1821) rebuilt calculus on the limit concept, defining continuity and convergence precisely and giving the (ε, δ) approach its early form. He founded complex analysis with the integral theorem (1825), the residue calculus and the Cauchy–Riemann equations; he proved the fundamental theorem of algebra rigorously, developed the theory of determinants and permutation groups, and in continuum mechanics formulated the stress tensor and the theory of elasticity. The Cauchy distribution, Cauchy sequence and Cauchy horizon also bear his name.
