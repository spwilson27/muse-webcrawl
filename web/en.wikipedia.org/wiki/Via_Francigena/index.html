Via Francigena

The Via Francigena crossing Tuscany toward Rome.

The Via Francigena is an ancient road and pilgrimage route running from the cathedral city of Canterbury in England, through France and Switzerland, to Rome and then to Apulia, Italy, where there were ports of embarkation for the Holy Land. In medieval times it was an important road and pilgrimage route for those wishing to visit the See of Peter and the tombs of the apostles Paul and Peter.

History

The route is documented in the itinerary of Sigeric the Serious, Archbishop of Canterbury, who travelled to Rome in 990 to receive his pallium and recorded the 79 stages of his return journey — the earliest detailed record of the road. In Lombard times the route was called the Via Francigena ("the road that comes from France"). Archbishop Sigeric's itinerary is preserved in a British Library manuscript and underpins the modern officially promoted route.

The modern route

Today the Via Francigena is promoted as a cultural route of the Council of Europe (designated in 1994), covering about 1,700 km from Canterbury to Rome. Pilgrims cross the Channel, traverse France, cross the Alps at the Great St Bernard Pass — where the hospice founded by Saint Bernard of Menthon served travellers for centuries — descend into Italy through Piedmont, Liguria, Emilia-Romagna, Tuscany, and Lazio, and enter Rome to reach St Peter's Basilica. A southern extension runs from Rome to Santa Maria di Leuca in Apulia.
