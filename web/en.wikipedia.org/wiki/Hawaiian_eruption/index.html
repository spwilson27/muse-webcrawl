Hawaiian eruption

A Hawaiian eruption is a type of volcanic eruption named after the Hawaiian volcanoes, characterised by the effusion of very fluid basaltic lava from a central vent, fissures, or a summit caldera, with low explosivity. Because the lava has low viscosity and low gas content, eruptions are generally gentle: fire fountains may throw molten lava hundreds of metres into the air, and broad, thin lava flows travel great distances, gradually building wide shield volcanoes. Hawaiian eruptions sit at the lowest end of the Volcanic Explosivity Index scale, typically VEI 0–1.

Characteristics

Hawaiian eruptions produce both ʻaʻā lava (rough, blocky flows) and pāhoehoe lava (smooth, ropy flows), depending on temperature, gas content and slope. Eruptions may begin along rift-zone fissures as a "curtain of fire" before localising at a few vents, where spatter cones and cinder cones can accumulate. Lava lakes — such as the long-lived one in Halemaʻumaʻu crater at Kīlauea — are a characteristic feature.

Examples

The type examples are the eruptions of Kīlauea and Mauna Loa in Hawaii, including Kīlauea's 1983–2018 Puʻu ʻŌʻō eruption and its destructive 2018 lower East Rift Zone eruption. Similar effusive eruptions occur at other basaltic shield volcanoes and fissure systems, such as those of Iceland (for example Fagradalsfjall), Piton de la Fournaise on Réunion, and Mount Etna's effusive phases.

Hazards

Although rarely explosive, Hawaiian eruptions destroy property through lava inundation and produce volcanic smog ("vog") from sulfur dioxide emissions. Lava entering the sea can generate hydrochloric acid plumes ("laze"), and collapsing lava deltas pose additional dangers.
