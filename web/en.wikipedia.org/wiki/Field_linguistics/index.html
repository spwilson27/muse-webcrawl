Field linguistics

Field linguistics is the branch of linguistics concerned with collecting primary linguistic data by working directly with native speakers in their own communities. Field linguists typically aim to produce a grammatical description of a language, a dictionary, and a corpus of recorded and transcribed texts. Fieldwork is especially important for documenting endangered and previously undescribed languages, many of which exist only in oral form.

Methods

Field methods include elicitation — asking consultants for words, paradigms and translations of sentences — as well as the recording of spontaneous speech, narratives, conversation and ritual language. Elicitation may be conducted through a contact language or monolingually, using actions, pictures and stimuli. Modern fieldwork relies on digital audio and video recording, annotation software such as ELAN and FLEx, and standardized stimulus kits for semantic and grammatical domains.

History

The field was shaped by Franz Boas and his students, who documented Indigenous languages of North America in the early 20th century, and by Kenneth Pike and the Summer Institute of Linguistics, which trained large numbers of fieldworkers. Subsequent generations have emphasized collaboration with speaker communities, ethical research practices, and the return of materials to communities of origin through language archives.

Ethics and documentation

Because many of the world's roughly 7,000 languages are endangered, descriptive fieldwork is closely linked to language documentation and revitalization. Contemporary standards stress informed consent, community control over recordings, and deposit of annotated materials in repositories such as ELAR and the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America.
