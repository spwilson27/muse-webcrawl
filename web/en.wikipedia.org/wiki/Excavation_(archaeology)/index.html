Excavation (archaeology)

In archaeology, excavation is the exposure, processing, and recording of archaeological remains. An excavation site, or "dig", is the area being studied. Excavation involves the recovery of several types of data: artefacts, features, ecofacts, and the contexts in which they occur.

Principles

Because excavation destroys the deposits it examines, it is governed by careful documentation. The stratigraphic sequence is recorded following the law of superposition, often using the Harris matrix to represent relationships between contexts. Each context—a layer, fill, cut, or structure—is given a unique number and recorded in plan, section, and photographs.

Methods

Excavation may be total or partial (such as test pits and evaluation trenches), open-area or grid-based. Tools range from mechanical excavators for removing topsoil to trowels, brushes, and sieves for delicate work. Finds are bagged and labelled by context for later analysis.

Types of excavation

Research excavations pursue scholarly questions, while rescue (salvage) archaeology is undertaken ahead of construction or erosion. Excavation is complemented by non-destructive survey methods such as fieldwalking, geophysics, and aerial photography.
