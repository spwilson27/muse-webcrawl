Civil union

A civil union (also known as a civil partnership) is a legally recognised arrangement similar to marriage, created primarily to provide legal recognition for same-sex couples, and in some jurisdictions also available to opposite-sex couples.

Civil union
First country: Denmark (registered partnership, 1989)
Related: Marriage, domestic partnership

History

Denmark introduced the first registered partnerships in 1989. The Netherlands followed, then became the first country to legalise same-sex marriage in 2001. Vermont created the first U.S. civil unions in 2000 following the Baker v. Vermont decision; France's PACS (1999) is open to all couples and widely used.

Differences from marriage

Civil unions typically confer most state-level rights of marriage — inheritance, hospital visitation, tax and property — but historically lacked federal recognition in the U.S. and the social status of marriage, leading critics to call the arrangement "separate but equal". Where same-sex marriage has been legalised, some jurisdictions converted unions to marriages or closed them to new applicants, while others retain both options.

Around the world

Forms exist in countries including the United Kingdom (civil partnership, extended to opposite-sex couples in 2019), New Zealand, Uruguay, and parts of the United States. See Same-sex marriage.
