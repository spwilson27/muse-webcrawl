Freemasonry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freemasonry is a fraternal organisation that traces its origins to the local guilds of stonemasons of the late medieval period and emerged in its modern, "speculative" form in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Members, called Freemasons or Masons, belong to local Lodges overseen by Grand Lodges, and progress through a series of ritual degrees — traditionally Entered Apprentice, Fellow Craft, and Master Mason — which use allegory and the symbolism of the stonemason's craft to teach moral lessons about brotherly love, relief (charity), and truth. Estimates of worldwide membership in the twenty-first century run to several million, concentrated in the English-speaking world and continental Europe.

History

Operative masons' lodges existed in Scotland and England by the late 1500s, with the Schaw Statutes (1598–99) among the earliest records; during the 1600s lodges increasingly admitted non-operative "gentlemen" members. Modern Freemasonry is conventionally dated to 24 June 1717, when four London lodges formed the Grand Lodge of England, and to the publication of James Anderson's Constitutions in 1723. The fraternity spread rapidly through Britain, continental Europe, and the American colonies, attracting figures of the Enlightenment and the American Revolution. Rival "Antient" and "Modern" grand lodges in England united in 1813 to form the United Grand Lodge of England. In continental Europe, the Grand Orient de France's 1877 decision to drop the requirement of belief in a Supreme Being created a lasting split between "regular" (Anglo-American) and "liberal" or continental Freemasonry, which also includes women's and mixed lodges such as Le Droit Humain (founded 1893).

Organisation and practice

The basic unit is the Lodge, which meets in a Masonic temple, confers the three degrees, and conducts charitable and social activity. Freemasonry is not a religion, and discussion of religion and politics is traditionally prohibited in lodge, but regular Freemasonry requires candidates to profess belief in a Supreme Being; it is open to men of any faith (with parallel orders for women in many jurisdictions). Beyond the three Craft degrees stand numerous appendant bodies and side degrees, including the Royal Arch, the Knights Templar, and the Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite with its 33 degrees. Masons recognise one another through signs, grips, and words kept private as a mode of recognition, though most ritual has long been published.

Opposition and cultural impact

Freemasonry has faced sustained opposition: the Roman Catholic Church has prohibited membership since Pope Clement XII's bull In eminenti apostolatus (1738), and authoritarian regimes — including Nazi Germany, Franco's Spain, and the Soviet bloc — suppressed or persecuted Masons. It has also attracted conspiracy theories, from the eighteenth-century Illuminati scare to modern claims of secret world control, which historians reject. Masonic charity is substantial (hospitals, scholarships, disaster relief), and Masonic symbolism and membership have left marks on civic architecture, the Great Seal of the United States, and the biographies of numerous statesmen, artists, and scientists.
