Gilgamesh

Gilgamesh was a hero in ancient Mesopotamian mythology and the protagonist of the Epic of Gilgamesh, an epic poem written in Akkadian during the late 2nd millennium BC. He was possibly a historical king of the Sumerian city-state of Uruk, reigning probably around 2900–2700 BC; the Sumerian King List credits him with a reign of 126 years. In the epic he is portrayed as two-thirds divine and one-third human, a mighty but arrogant king whose friendship with the wild man Enkidu and grief at his death drive him on a quest for immortality.

In the epic

The gods create Enkidu to humble Gilgamesh; instead the two become inseparable friends after wrestling in Uruk. Together they journey to the Cedar Forest and slay its guardian Humbaba, and later kill the Bull of Heaven sent by the spurned goddess Ishtar. In punishment the gods decree Enkidu's death. Devastated and fearing his own mortality, Gilgamesh travels to the ends of the earth to find Utnapishtim, survivor of the great Flood, who tells him immortality is not for men; a plant of rejuvenation he wins from the sea floor is stolen by a serpent. He returns to Uruk reconciled to finding permanence in the city's great walls — his works, not his body.

Historical evidence

Poems in Sumerian about Bilgames (the Sumerian form of the name) circulated by about 2100 BC, and his name appears among deified early kings. Whether a historical Gilgamesh existed cannot be proved, but inscriptions mention names of associates, and later Mesopotamian tradition revered him as a judge of the dead in the underworld. The epic's standard version, compiled by the scholar Sîn-lēqi-unninni, was rediscovered on clay tablets from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh in the nineteenth century; George Smith's translation of its Flood tablet in 1872 caused a sensation for its parallel to the story of Noah.
