English auxiliary verbs

The English auxiliary verbs are a small set of verbs that combine with a main verb to express tense, aspect, mood and voice. The principal auxiliaries are be, have and do, together with the modal auxiliaries can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will and would. Auxiliaries differ from lexical verbs in their grammatical behaviour: they invert with the subject in questions, take negation directly, and can appear in elliptical constructions.

Primary auxiliaries

Be is used to form the progressive aspect (She is running) and the passive voice (It was broken). Have forms the perfect aspect (They have finished). Do appears in questions, negatives and emphatic clauses when no other auxiliary is present, a construction known as do-support (Do you agree? He does not smoke.).

Modal auxiliaries

The modal auxiliaries express meanings such as possibility, permission, obligation and futurity. They are defective verbs: they have no infinitive, no participles and no third-person singular -s. Examples include can (ability), must (obligation or strong inference) and might (possibility).

Properties

Auxiliaries display the so-called NICE properties: Negation (they host not directly), Inversion (they swap places with the subject in questions), Code (they stand for an omitted verb phrase, as in John can) and Emphasis (they can bear stress for emphasis). Semi-modals such as ought to, used to and need share some of these properties.
