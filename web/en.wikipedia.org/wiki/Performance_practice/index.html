Performance practice

Performance practice, also called historically informed performance (HIP), is the study and application of the conventions by which music of earlier periods was performed in its own time. It encompasses the instruments used, their tuning and setup, techniques of playing and singing, ornamentation, improvisation, tempo, articulation, and the size and disposition of ensembles.

Scope

Because musical notation has never captured every aspect of performance, musicians of past centuries relied on unwritten conventions. A Baroque score, for example, might expect the performer to add ornaments, realise a figured bass on a chordal instrument, and phrase differently from a modern player. Treatises by writers such as Johann Joachim Quantz, C. P. E. Bach, and Leopold Mozart are principal sources for eighteenth-century practice, alongside surviving instruments, iconography, and early recordings.

Historically informed performance

The modern early-music movement, gathering pace from the 1960s and 1970s, applied this research to performance, using original instruments or faithful copies, lower historical pitch standards, and expressive conventions recovered from treatises. Pioneers included Nikolaus Harnoncourt, Gustav Leonhardt, Christopher Hogwood, and Trevor Pinnock. The movement began with medieval, Renaissance, and Baroque repertory and later extended to Classical and even Romantic music.

HIP has been debated: critics question whether a uniquely "authentic" performance is recoverable or desirable, and note that modern historically informed playing also reflects modern tastes. The term "historically informed" replaced the stronger claim of "authenticity" partly in acknowledgement of these criticisms.
