Developmental psychology is the scientific study of how and why human beings change over the course of their lives — physically, cognitively, socially and emotionally — from conception through infancy, childhood and adolescence to adulthood and old age. Originally concerned mainly with children, the field now takes a lifespan perspective, asking both what remains stable and what changes, and how nature (maturation, genetics) and nurture (experience, culture) interact to produce development.

Major theories

  • Piaget's stages of cognitive development — sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational and formal operational stages, through which children actively construct understanding of the world.
  • Vygotsky's sociocultural theory — development driven by social interaction and language; the zone of proximal development defines what a child can achieve with guidance.
  • Erikson's psychosocial stages — eight crises (e.g. trust vs. mistrust, identity vs. role confusion) spanning the whole lifespan.
  • Attachment theory — Bowlby and Ainsworth: early bonds with caregivers shape later relationships; the "Strange Situation" classified secure, avoidant and ambivalent attachment.
  • Kohlberg's stages of moral development — pre-conventional, conventional and post-conventional reasoning.
  • Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory — development nested in family, school, culture and historical context.
  • Learning theories — behaviourist accounts (conditioning) and Bandura's social learning theory.

Domains and milestones

Research tracks motor development (sitting, walking), language acquisition (babbling, first words, grammar), theory of mind, moral reasoning, identity formation in adolescence, and cognitive ageing. Methods include naturalistic and laboratory observation, experiments, longitudinal cohorts (following the same individuals for decades) and cross-sectional comparisons, supplemented since the cognitive revolution by computational modelling and neuroimaging. Developmental findings inform education — notably the constructivist tradition — childcare policy, clinical practice and the design of interventions for developmental disorders.

Debates

Enduring issues include continuity versus stages, stability versus change, the degree of early experience's lasting influence, and the universality of developmental sequences across cultures — a criticism of research conducted mainly on Western, educated populations.
