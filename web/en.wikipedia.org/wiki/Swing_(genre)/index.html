Swing (genre)

Swing is a style of jazz that developed in the United States during the late 1920s and early 1930s and became the dominant form of popular music in America from roughly 1935 to 1946 — the "swing era". It is characterised by a strong rhythmic feel known as swing (delayed subdivisions of the beat), big bands, written arrangements built around riff-based call and response, and featured soloists improvising over the ensemble.

The swing era

Benny Goodman's triumph at the Palomar Ballroom in Los Angeles in August 1935 is conventionally taken as the start of the swing era. Major bandleaders included Count Basie, Duke Ellington, Glenn Miller, Tommy Dorsey, Artie Shaw, Woody Herman and Chick Webb; the music filled ballrooms where couples danced the Lindy Hop and jitterbug. Kansas City swing (Basie) was looser and blues-based, while New York arrangers such as Fletcher Henderson supplied the sophisticated charts that powered Goodman's band.

Decline and revival

The era ended with the wartime recording ban, the rise of singers over instrumental soloists, and the emergence of bebop, which rejected swing's commercial arrangements. Swing has been repeatedly revived — in jump blues, in the 1990s neo-swing movement, and in modern electro swing — and its repertoire remains core material for jazz education.
