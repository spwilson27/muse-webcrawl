J Harlen Bretz (2 September 1882 – 3 February 1981) was an American geologist whose research on the Channeled Scablands of eastern Washington State led to the recognition of catastrophic megafloods — the Missoula floods — as a legitimate geological process, overturning decades of uniformitarian orthodoxy.

Career

Bretz earned his doctorate at the University of Chicago and taught there and at the University of Washington. Beginning with fieldwork in 1922–23, he described the Scablands' enormous coulees, dry falls (including Dry Falls, far larger than Niagara), giant gravel bars and current ripples, and concluded they could only have been carved by a sudden flood of almost unimaginable size — the "Spokane flood".

The flood controversy

The proposal was fiercely resisted: at a famous 1927 meeting in Washington, D.C., assembled geologists attacked the hypothesis, which seemed to revive discredited catastrophism and lacked a known water source. Joseph Pardee's subsequent work identified glacial Lake Missoula in Montana as the source, repeatedly drained when its ice dam failed. Satellite imagery and analysis of giant ripple marks in the 1960s–70s vindicated Bretz; in 1979, aged 96, he received the Geological Society of America's Penrose Medal.

Later work

Bretz also studied caves and karst in Missouri, the Chicago region's glacial geology, and limestone features. His vindication is a classic case study in the sociology of science, and Bretz reportedly remarked, "I had no source for my flood, then I had a source — all my critics were wrong."
