HDR10 is an open standard for high dynamic range (HDR) video, announced by the Consumer Technology Association (CTA) on 27 August 2015. It is the most widely supported HDR format and is mandatory on Ultra HD Blu-ray and supported by virtually all HDR televisions, streaming services and game consoles. The "10" in the name refers to its 10-bit colour depth.

Technical details

An HDR10 signal is defined by the following characteristics:

  • Transfer function: the perceptual quantizer (PQ) curve, standardised as SMPTE ST 2084, which maps code values to absolute luminance up to 10,000 cd/m² (nits).
  • Colour space: the wide-gamut Rec. 2020 colour primaries, with 4:2:0 chroma subsampling in consumer distribution.
  • Bit depth: 10 bits per colour channel, giving about 1.07 billion possible colours, compared with 16.7 million for 8-bit SDR.
  • Static metadata: a single set of values describing the whole programme — the mastering display colour volume (ST 2086), the maximum content light level (MaxCLL) and the maximum frame-average light level (MaxFALL). The display uses these to tone-map the entire film or episode in a single, fixed way.

The use of static metadata is HDR10's principal limitation compared with the dynamic-metadata formats Dolby Vision and HDR10+, which can adjust tone mapping scene by scene or even frame by frame.

HDR10+

HDR10+, developed by Samsung and Amazon Video and announced in 2017, extends HDR10 with dynamic metadata (standardised as SMPTE ST 2094-40) while remaining royalty-free in the manner of HDR10. HDR10+ content is backwards compatible: a display that does not support HDR10+ simply reads the base HDR10 layer.

Adoption

HDR10 is the baseline HDR format of the UHD Alliance specifications and is required on all discs and players bearing the Ultra HD Blu-ray format. Streaming services including Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+ and Apple TV+ deliver large parts of their HDR catalogues in HDR10 (often alongside Dolby Vision). The PlayStation, Xbox and Nintendo platforms support HDR10 for games, and it is the HDR format used by the AV1, HEVC and VP9 codec profiles for HDR delivery on services such as YouTube.
