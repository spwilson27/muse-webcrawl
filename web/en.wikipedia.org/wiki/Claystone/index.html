Claystone

Claystone
Type: Sedimentary rock
Grain size: < 1/256 mm (clay grade)
Composition: Clay minerals, quartz silt
Related: Mudstone, siltstone, shale

Claystone is a sedimentary rock composed mainly of clay-sized particles — mineral grains less than 1/256 of a millimetre in diameter, largely clay minerals with subordinate quartz and feldspar. It is the lithified equivalent of clay and forms the finest-grained end of the mudrock family.

Formation

Claystones form where clay particles settle out of suspension in quiet water — deep marine basins, lake beds, floodplains and estuaries — and are subsequently buried and compacted. Unlike shale, claystone lacks fissility: it breaks into blocky or massive chunks rather than splitting along fine laminations, because its particles are not strongly aligned.

Distinguishing features

In the mudrock classification, grain size separates claystone (clay grade) from mudstone (a mixture of clay and silt) and siltstone (silt grade), while the presence or absence of fissility separates shale from its non-fissile counterparts. Claystone feels smooth to the touch and does not feel gritty when chewed — a traditional field test.

Occurrence and uses

Claystones are widespread in sedimentary sequences of all ages. Because of their very low permeability they often act as seals ("cap rocks") above petroleum reservoirs and aquifers. Weathered claystone is a raw material for bricks, tiles and pottery where purer clay deposits are unavailable.
