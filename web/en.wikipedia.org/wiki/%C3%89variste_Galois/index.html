Évariste Galois

Born25 October 1811, Bourg-la-Reine, France
Died31 May 1832 (aged 20), Paris, France
Known forGalois theory, group theory, Galois fields
FieldsMathematics

Évariste Galois (25 October 1811 – 31 May 1832) was a French mathematician and political activist. While still in his teens, he was able to determine a necessary and sufficient condition for a polynomial to be solvable by radicals, thereby solving a problem that had stood for 350 years. His work laid the foundations for Galois theory and group theory, two major branches of abstract algebra.

Biography

Galois was born in Bourg-la-Reine, near Paris, the son of Nicolas-Gabriel Galois and Adélaïde-Marie Demante. He was educated first by his mother, then entered the Lycée Louis-le-Grand in 1823. There his interest shifted from rhetoric to mathematics after reading Legendre's Éléments de géométrie. He twice failed the entrance examination to the École Polytechnique, France's leading school of mathematics, and instead entered the École Normale.

At seventeen he published his first paper on continued fractions, and soon began making fundamental discoveries in the theory of polynomial equations. After his father died by suicide in 1829 amid a political scandal, Galois became increasingly involved in republican politics.

Contributions to mathematics

Galois's most significant contribution was Galois theory, which connects field theory and group theory. He showed that whether a polynomial equation can be solved by radicals depends on the structure of a group of permutations associated with the equation — the Galois group of its splitting field. In doing so he introduced the concept of a group in something close to its modern sense, along with ideas such as normal subgroups and solvable groups.

His manuscripts were repeatedly lost or rejected by the Academy of Sciences; Cauchy and Poisson both failed to grasp their importance. The work was finally published by Joseph Liouville in 1846, fourteen years after Galois's death.

Death

On 30 May 1832, Galois was shot in the abdomen during a duel under circumstances that remain obscure — possibly involving a love affair, possibly a political provocation. He died the following day at the Hôpital Cochin, aged twenty. The night before the duel, anticipating his death, he wrote his famous letter to Auguste Chevalier setting out his mathematical ideas.

Legacy

Galois's collected works run to fewer than 100 pages but reshaped algebra. Galois theory, Galois groups, Galois fields (finite fields), and Galois connections all bear his name. He is often described as the founder of modern algebra and one of the most striking figures in the history of mathematics, both for his genius and the brevity of his life.
