The Saadian dynasty (Arabic: السعديون, as-saʿdiyyūn) was a Sharifian dynasty that ruled Morocco from 1549 to 1659. Rising from the south of the country, the Saadians unified Morocco, repelled Portuguese and Ottoman expansion, and presided over a cultural and architectural flourishing centred on Marrakesh. They are regarded as the first of Morocco's two Sharifian dynasties, followed by the Alaouites, who still rule Morocco today.

Origins

The Saadians claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad through the Banu Saad lineage and settled in the Draa valley at Tagmadert. From 1509 their leader, Muhammad ibn Abd al-Rahman ("al-Qa'im"), led resistance against Portuguese enclaves on the Atlantic coast, such as Santa Cruz de Aguer. His sons Ahmad al-Araj and Mohammed ash-Sheikh extended Saadian control northward, capturing Marrakesh in 1524 and eventually overthrowing the Wattasid dynasty at Fez in 1549, unifying the country.

Reign of Mohammed ash-Sheikh and Abdallah al-Ghalib

Mohammed ash-Sheikh (r. 1549–1557) consolidated the sultanate, resisted Ottoman attempts to subjugate Morocco, and was assassinated in 1557 by Ottoman agents. His son Abdallah al-Ghalib (r. 1557–1574) continued centralising rule and built extensively in Marrakesh, including the Ben Youssef Mosque complex.

Battle of Alcácer Quibir and Ahmad al-Mansur

In 1578, at the Battle of Alcácer Quibir (the "Battle of the Three Kings"), Sultan Abd al-Malik defeated a Portuguese invasion led by King Sebastian of Portugal; both kings and the deposed sultan Muhammad II died in the battle. His brother Ahmad al-Mansur (r. 1578–1603), called ad-Dhahabi ("the Golden"), brought the dynasty to its height: ransoms and captured wealth filled the treasury; in 1591 his forces crossed the Sahara and destroyed the Songhai Empire at the Battle of Tondibi, seizing the gold and salt trade of the Niger bend. His court built the El Badi Palace and the Saadian Tombs in Marrakesh.

Decline

After al-Mansur's death in 1603, a civil war between his sons fragmented the sultanate. Saadian power shrank to Marrakesh and the south while religious brotherhoods and local powers (including the Dala'iyya and eventually the rising Alaouites of the Tafilalt) took over elsewhere. The last Saadian sultan, Ahmad al-Abbas, was killed in 1659, ending the dynasty.

Legacy

Saadian rule is credited with preserving Moroccan independence from both the Ottoman Empire and Iberian expansion, and with monuments such as the Saadian Tombs, the Ben Youssef Madrasa, and the Kasbah Mosque in Marrakesh.
