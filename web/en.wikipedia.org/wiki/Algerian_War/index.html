Algerian War

The Algerian War (1 November 1954 – 19 March 1962; also known as the Algerian War of Independence, and in Algeria as the 1 November 1954 Revolution) was a major armed conflict between France and the Algerian National Liberation Front (FLN). Algeria had been conquered from 1830 and, uniquely among French colonies, was legally part of France itself, divided into departments and home to about a million European settlers (the pieds-noirs) alongside nine million Muslim Algerians who were denied equal citizenship. The war began with the FLN's coordinated attacks of the Toussaint Rouge (Red All Saints' Day) of 1 November 1954 and escalated into one of the most brutal decolonisation wars of the century: guerrilla war and terrorism in the countryside and cities, massive French counter-insurgency using up to half a million troops, systematic torture, and the displacement of millions of villagers. It destroyed the French Fourth Republic in 1958, brought Charles de Gaulle back to power, provoked settler and military insurrections and an attempted putsch in 1961, and ended with the Évian Accords of March 1962 and Algerian independence on 5 July 1962 — followed by the exodus of nearly the whole pied-noir population and the massacre of tens of thousands of harkis, Algerians who had served France. Death tolls remain fiercely contested: French historians commonly estimate 250,000–300,000 Algerian Muslim deaths; the Algerian state commemorates 1.5 million martyrs.

Origins

French Algeria rested on a century of expropriation: land confiscations after every revolt, a legal order (the code de l'indigénat) that made Muslims subjects rather than citizens, and a settler minority monopolising political power in Algiers and Paris. Algerian nationalism grew between the wars — Messali Hadj's movements, Ferhat Abbas's manifestos — and radicalised with the Sétif and Guelma massacres of May 1945, when VE-Day demonstrations were answered by repression that killed thousands of Algerians. Electoral fraud against the legal nationalist parties in the late 1940s convinced a younger generation — among them Ahmed Ben Bella, Hocine Aït Ahmed and Mohamed Boudiaf, veterans of the clandestine Special Organisation — that only arms would work. On the night of 31 October–1 November 1954 the newly proclaimed FLN launched some seventy coordinated attacks across Algeria.

Escalation and the Battle of Algiers

France answered that Algeria was France ("L'Algérie, c'est la France", declared Interior Minister François Mitterrand) and poured in conscripts — ultimately over a million French soldiers served. Governor-General Jacques Soustelle and then Robert Lacoste combined repression with reform promises; after the Philippeville massacres of August 1955, in which FLN attacks and French reprisals killed thousands, the war became total. France quadrillaged the countryside, built the electrified Morice Line along the Tunisian border to seal off the ALN's external sanctuaries, and "regrouped" over two million peasants into camps to drain the guerrillas' support. In 1956–57 the FLN brought the war into the capital with an urban bombing campaign, and General Massu's paratroopers dismantled the network in the Battle of Algiers (1957) — a tactical victory won by the systematic use of torture, whose exposure (Henri Alleg's La Question, the Audin affair) corroded support for the war in France itself. The FLN, militarily contained inside Algeria, won the diplomatic war: its provisional government (GPRA, 1958) was recognised across the Arab and Afro-Asian world.

De Gaulle, the putsch and Évian

The settler-army rising of 13 May 1958 in Algiers destroyed the Fourth Republic; de Gaulle returned as premier and then as president of the new Fifth Republic, ambiguous to the end about his intentions ("Je vous ai compris" — "I have understood you" — he told the Algiers crowd). By 1959 he had accepted self-determination; the settlers and much of the army felt betrayed, and in April 1961 four generals (Challe, Salan, Jouhaud, Zeller) attempted a putsch in Algiers, which collapsed in four days when conscripts refused to obey. The clandestine OAS (Secret Army Organisation) of settler ultras and renegade officers waged a terrorist campaign, including attempts on de Gaulle's life, into 1962. Negotiations at Évian-les-Bains produced the accords of 18 March 1962: a ceasefire, a referendum, and independence, with guarantees for the settlers that events immediately swept away. A referendum in France approved the accords overwhelmingly; on 1 July 1962 Algerians voted almost unanimously for independence, proclaimed on 5 July — the anniversary, pointedly, of the French landing of 1830.

Aftermath and memory

Nearly a million pieds-noirs fled to France within months, abandoning everything; the harkis, disarmed by France and largely refused evacuation, were massacred in numbers estimated from tens of thousands upward. Algeria became a one-party FLN state under Ben Bella, then Boumédiène. In France the war was officially unnameable — the state called it "operations for the maintenance of order" until a 1999 law at last recognised the "Algerian War" — and its wounds (the police massacre of Algerian demonstrators in Paris on 17 October 1961, the fate of the harkis' children in French camps, the amnesties that barred prosecution for torture) remained live political questions into the twenty-first century, as did the war's contested death toll and the return of colonial memory to the centre of French-Algerian relations.
