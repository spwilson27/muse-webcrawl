Guest star (astronomy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Crab Nebula, remnant of the "guest star" of 1054

The term guest star is a literal translation of the Chinese astronomical term kè xīng (客星), used in ancient Chinese astronomy for a star that suddenly appeared where none had been seen before and faded again after some time. Modern astronomers recognise that most historical guest stars were novae or supernovae, with some possibly being comets (distinguished in the records by movement or tails, and sometimes called "broom stars").

Historical records

Chinese court astronomers systematically recorded guest stars from at least the Han dynasty, regarding them as omens for the emperor. These records, preserved also in Korean and Japanese sources, are now invaluable for identifying historical supernova remnants and dating them.

Scientific use

Matching guest-star reports to supernova remnants constrains the ages and expansion rates of remnants and the light curves of the explosions, and has helped identify unusual events such as Type Iax supernovae. Non-supernova guest stars have also been used to study historical novae and recurrent novae in the process of re-identification.
