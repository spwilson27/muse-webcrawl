Ventilation (architecture)


Stack-effect natural ventilation

Ventilation is the intentional introduction of outdoor air into a building and the circulation of air within it, in order to maintain indoor air quality, remove moisture, odours, and pollutants, and provide thermal comfort. It is a central concern of building services engineering and of passive architectural design.

Purposes

Ventilation supplies oxygen and dilutes carbon dioxide and contaminants generated by occupants, materials, and processes; controls humidity and condensation; and can remove excess heat. Building codes and standards such as ASHRAE 62.1 specify minimum outdoor-air rates, commonly expressed in litres per second per person or air changes per hour.

Natural ventilation

Natural ventilation uses wind pressure and buoyancy (the stack effect) rather than fans. Techniques include operable windows, cross-ventilation, windcatchers (badgir) of Persian and Middle Eastern architecture, solar chimneys, courtyards, and night-purge cooling of thermal mass. Natural strategies can greatly reduce energy use but depend on climate, site, and occupant behaviour.

Mechanical ventilation

Mechanical systems use fans and ducts to deliver controlled airflows: extract-only systems (e.g., bathroom and kitchen fans), supply-only systems, and balanced systems with heat recovery (MVHR), which transfer heat from outgoing to incoming air to save energy. Hospitals, laboratories, and cleanrooms use specialised systems with filtration and pressure control. Demand-controlled ventilation adjusts rates using CO2 or occupancy sensors.

History

Nineteenth-century concerns about "vitiated air" and disease — promoted by reformers such as Florence Nightingale — made ventilation a public-health priority in hospitals, schools, and factories. The rise of air conditioning in the twentieth century shifted design toward sealed, mechanically serviced buildings, while energy crises and sick building syndrome renewed interest in well-designed natural and mixed-mode ventilation.
