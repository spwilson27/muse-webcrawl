The Bactrian camel (Camelus bactrianus) is a large even-toed ungulate native to the steppes of Central Asia. It has two humps on its back, in contrast to the single-humped dromedary camel of Arabia and North Africa. Its population of around two million exists mainly in domesticated form; a small, genetically distinct population of wild Bactrian camels (Camelus ferus) survives in remote deserts of Mongolia and China and is critically endangered.

Adaptations

Bactrian camels are superbly adapted to continental desert climates that swing from bitter cold to intense heat. A thick, shaggy winter coat is shed in summer; two rows of long eyelashes and sealable nostrils protect against blowing sand; and the animal can tolerate large losses of body water, drinking huge quantities when water is available. Contrary to popular belief, the humps store fat, not water, and shrink when food is scarce.

Domestication and use

Domesticated probably around 2500 BCE in Central Asia, the Bactrian camel was the essential pack animal of the Silk Road, carrying goods between China and the West across the deserts of the Tarim Basin and Mongolia. It remains used for transport, milk, meat, wool, and tourism in Mongolia, Kazakhstan, and northern China.

Name

The species is named after Bactria, the ancient region around present-day northern Afghanistan and Central Asia.
