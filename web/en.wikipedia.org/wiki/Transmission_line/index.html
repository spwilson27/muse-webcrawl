Transmission line
TypesCoaxial cable, twisted pair, microstrip, stripline, waveguide
Key parameterCharacteristic impedance (Z0), typically 50 Ω or 75 Ω
ModelTelegrapher's equations; distributed R, L, G, C

In electrical engineering, a transmission line is a specialised cable or other structure designed to conduct electromagnetic waves in a contained manner. The term applies when the conductors are long enough that the wave nature of the propagation must be taken into account — that is, when the line length is a significant fraction of the wavelength. Transmission lines are used for purposes such as connecting radio transmitters and receivers with their antennas (feed lines), distributing cable television signals, trunklines routing calls between telephone switching centres, and computer network connections.

Theory

A transmission line is modelled as a distributed network of series resistance and inductance and shunt conductance and capacitance per unit length, described by the telegrapher's equations, first formulated by Oliver Heaviside. Signals travel as waves; at any discontinuity in the characteristic impedance Z0 part of the wave is reflected, quantified by the reflection coefficient and the standing wave ratio. Terminating a line in its characteristic impedance (impedance matching) prevents reflections and maximises power transfer.

Common types

Coaxial cable (50 Ω for RF, 75 Ω for video/cable TV) and balanced lines such as twisted pair are the most familiar. On printed circuit boards, microstrip and stripline traces act as transmission lines for high-speed digital and RF signals. At microwave frequencies, hollow waveguides carry signals with low loss. The Smith chart is the classic graphical tool for solving transmission-line matching problems.
