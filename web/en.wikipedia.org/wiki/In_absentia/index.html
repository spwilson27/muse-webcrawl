In absentia

In absentia is a Latin legal term meaning "in the absence of". A trial in absentia is a criminal proceeding in a court of law in which the person who is subject to it is not physically present at those proceedings. In many legal systems, the right to be present at one's trial is considered a fundamental right, closely connected with the right to a fair trial and the right to confront one's accusers.

Legal status

Under the European Convention on Human Rights, trials in absentia are permissible only in limited circumstances, such as when the accused has been duly notified and has unequivocally waived the right to appear, or when a retrial is available upon the person's return. The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights likewise protects the right of an accused to be tried in his or her presence.

In the United States, the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure generally prohibit beginning a trial in the defendant's absence for serious offences, though a defendant who flees after trial has begun may be tried in absentia. Some civil-law jurisdictions, including France and Italy, have historically made broader use of trials in absentia, particularly for fugitives.

Notable cases

Trials in absentia have been used in international criminal justice, for example by the Special Tribunal for Lebanon, whose statute expressly permits them. They remain controversial: supporters argue they prevent fugitives from obstructing justice indefinitely, while critics contend they weaken defence rights and produce verdicts that are difficult to enforce.
