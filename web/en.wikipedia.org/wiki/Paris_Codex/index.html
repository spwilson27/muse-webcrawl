Paris Codex

Paris Codex
Also known as: Codex Pérez, Codex Peresianus
Date: Postclassic Maya, c. 15th century
Place of origin: Yucatán, Mexico
Material: Amatl (fig bark) paper
Size: 24 pages (fragments), 140 cm long
Location: Bibliothèque nationale de France, Paris

The Paris Codex (also known as the Codex Peresianus or Codex Pérez) is one of only four surviving pre-Columbian Maya codices, the others being the Dresden, Madrid, and Grolier (Maya Codex of Mexico) codices. It is held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris, where it was rediscovered in 1859 by the French scholar Léon de Rosny, who found it neglected in a basket of old papers in a corner of the library.

Description

The codex is a fragmentary screenfold manuscript made of amatl paper, measuring about 1.4 metres long and 23.5 centimetres high, folded into pages of roughly 12.5 by 23.5 centimetres. Only 11 pages survive substantially intact of an original manuscript thought to have had more; the document is badly damaged, with much of the surface worn away. It is generally dated to the Late Postclassic period, probably the 15th century, shortly before the Spanish conquest.

Contents

The recto of the codex concerns katun prophecies: a series of thirteen katun periods (each about 20 years), each associated with a seated deity figure, ceremonies, and prognostications — this section parallels material in the colonial Books of Chilam Balam. The verso contains a zodiacal almanac with a sequence of animals (including a scorpion and other creatures interpreted as constellations) suspended from an ecliptic band, together with astronomical and calendrical tables, New Year ceremonies, and a possible eclipse table. The zodiac pages are unique among Maya codices in depicting a zodiac-like series of constellations along the ecliptic.

History

The codex's name "Peresianus" derives from the word "Pérez" visible on the paper wrapping in which it was found, which has led to speculation connecting it with a manuscript once owned by the 18th-century Maya scholar Juan Pío Pérez. How it reached Paris is unknown. Because of its poor condition it has been less studied than the Dresden Codex, but it remains a vital source for Postclassic Maya religion, astronomy, and prophecy.
