Saho is a Cushitic language spoken in south-central Eritrea and the Tigray Region of northern Ethiopia. It is the native language of the Saho people, and of the Irob people of northeastern Tigray. Saho forms a dialect cluster with the closely related Afar language, the two together comprising the Saho–Afar branch of Lowland East Cushitic.

Geographic distribution

Saho is spoken in the Debub (Southern) and Northern Red Sea regions of Eritrea, inland from the coast between the Gulf of Zula and the Ethiopian border, and in adjoining districts of Tigray, particularly among the Irob. The principal dialect divisions traditionally recognised are Toroa, Hazo, Assaorta, and Minifere, named for clan groupings of the Saho.

Writing system

Saho has been written in the Ge'ez (Ethiopic) script, especially for religious material produced in monasteries among the Christian Irob. In Eritrea, following independence, a Latin-based orthography was adopted for Saho as for most Eritrean languages, and the language is used in primary education and in government-supported media, including radio broadcasts and a newspaper column. Bible translations and a growing body of schoolbooks exist in the language.

Phonology and grammar

Like other Cushitic languages, Saho is a verb-final (SOV) language with postpositional case marking expressed largely through suffixes and tone/stress distinctions. It possesses a typical East Cushitic consonant inventory including ejective and pharyngeal consonants, and its verbs conjugate with prefixes inherited from Afro-Asiatic — a feature shared with its close relative Afar. Nouns mark gender, and definiteness and case interact with the placement of word stress, which in Saho plays a grammatical role comparable to that documented for Afar.
