X-ray binary

An X-ray binary is a class of binary star system that is luminous in X-rays. The X-rays are produced by matter falling from one component, the donor star, onto the other component, a compact object such as a neutron star or black hole. As the material spirals inward through an accretion disk, it is heated to tens of millions of degrees and radiates most of the gravitational energy released in the X-ray band.

Classification

X-ray binaries are classified by the mass of the donor star:

  • High-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs) – the donor is a young, massive O or B star. Matter is transferred mainly by a strong stellar wind or by overflow of the star's Roche lobe. Examples include Cygnus X-1, the first widely accepted black hole candidate, and Vela X-1.
  • Low-mass X-ray binaries (LMXBs) – the donor is an older, low-mass star that fills its Roche lobe and streams matter onto the compact object. Examples include Scorpius X-1, the brightest extrasolar X-ray source in the sky, and Her X-1.

Behaviour

Many X-ray binaries are variable and transient: LMXBs may spend years in quiescence before erupting in outbursts lasting weeks or months as the accretion rate surges. Some systems produce periodic X-ray bursts, caused by unstable nuclear burning of accumulated material on a neutron star's surface, and some neutron stars in binaries are observed as accretion-powered X-ray pulsars. Microquasars are X-ray binaries that launch relativistic jets resembling those of distant quasars.

Significance

X-ray binaries are key laboratories for studying accretion physics and strong gravity, and they provide some of the best evidence for the existence of stellar-mass black holes, whose masses can be measured from the orbital motion of the companion star.
