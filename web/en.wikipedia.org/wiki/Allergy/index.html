Allergy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An allergy is a disorder of the Immune system in which normally harmless substances (allergens) provoke an exaggerated immune response. Common allergens include pollens, dust mites, animal dander, foods (peanuts, tree nuts, milk, egg, shellfish), insect stings, medicines and latex. Reactions range from sneezing and itchy eyes to the potentially fatal whole-body reaction anaphylaxis.

The classic mechanism is IgE-mediated: on re-exposure, allergen cross-links IgE antibodies on mast cells, which release histamine and other mediators within minutes. Allergists diagnose by history, skin-prick and blood tests, and manage by avoidance, antihistamines, corticosteroids, allergen immunotherapy, and adrenaline (epinephrine) auto-injectors for anaphylaxis.

Conditions

Allergic rhinitis (hay fever), Asthma, atopic eczema, food allergy, drug allergy and venom allergy form the atopic family, which clusters in families and has risen in prevalence in industrialised societies — explanations include the hygiene hypothesis and changed microbiomes.

Food allergy

Food allergies affect a notable minority of children, many of whom outgrow milk and egg allergy; peanut allergy tends to persist. Early-introduction studies (notably LEAP, 2015) reversed older advice — feeding peanut early in infancy prevents most peanut allergy — and reshaped infant-feeding guidelines.
