Yang–Mills theory is a gauge theory based on a non-abelian Lie group, introduced in 1954 by physicists Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills. It generalises the gauge principle of electromagnetism: whereas quantum electrodynamics is based on the abelian group U(1), Yang–Mills theories use groups such as SU(2) or SU(3), whose gauge bosons carry the "charge" themselves and interact with one another. Yang–Mills theories form the mathematical backbone of the Standard Model of particle physics.

Formulation

The theory starts from a Lagrangian built from the field strength tensor of a connection (gauge potential) valued in a Lie algebra. Because the group is non-abelian, the field strength contains a commutator term, producing three- and four-point self-interactions of the gauge fields. The equations of motion generalise Maxwell's equations; their non-linear character makes the classical theory already rich — it admits instanton solutions — and the quantum theory strongly coupled at low energies.

Applications

Quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong interaction, is an SU(3) Yang–Mills theory coupled to quarks; it explains asymptotic freedom — the weakening of the force at short distances, discovered in 1973 — and is believed to produce colour confinement at long distances. The electroweak sector of the Standard Model is an SU(2)×U(1) gauge theory in which the Yang–Mills bosons acquire mass through the Higgs mechanism.

Millennium problem

The "Yang–Mills existence and mass gap" problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems of the Clay Mathematics Institute, asks for a rigorous mathematical construction of quantum Yang–Mills theory in four dimensions and a proof that the theory has a mass gap — that its lightest excitation is massive, as confinement suggests. A one-million-dollar prize awaits the solution.
