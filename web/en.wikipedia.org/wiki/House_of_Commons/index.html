House of Commons

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The House of Commons is the lower house of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. Like the upper house, the House of Lords, it meets in the Palace of Westminster in London, England. Its 650 members (Members of Parliament, MPs) are elected by first-past-the-post in single-member constituencies for terms of up to five years. The Commons is the dominant chamber of Parliament: the government of the day is formed from it and accountable to it, and it holds supremacy over legislation, subject since the Parliament Acts (1911, 1949) to only a suspensive veto by the Lords.

Role and proceedings

Most legislation originates in the Commons; money bills are exclusively its preserve. The government's survival depends on retaining the Commons' confidence — convention, rather than statute, fuses executive and legislature in Bagehot's "efficient secret" of the constitution. Daily proceedings — questions (including Prime Minister's Questions), opposition days, backbench debates, select committees shadowing each department — blend adversarial ritual (the chamber's two sword-lengths between government and opposition benches) with detailed scrutiny. The Speaker enforces order through Erskine May's parliamentary procedure; the whips enforce party. Constituency casework makes MPs local ombudsmen as much as legislators. Debates about the chamber's power relative to the executive ("elective dictatorship", in Lord Hailsham's 1976 phrase) recur whenever strong majorities meet strong party discipline.
