History of Argentina

The history of Argentina spans the pre-Columbian cultures of the Southern Cone, three centuries of Spanish colonial rule, independence in the early 19th century, civil wars and national organization, an era of export-led prosperity, and the turbulent politics of the 20th and 21st centuries.

Colonial era and independence

Indigenous peoples including the Diaguita, Guaraní, and Mapuche inhabited the region before Spanish expeditions reached the Río de la Plata in 1516. Buenos Aires was permanently founded in 1580, and in 1776 became capital of the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata. The May Revolution of 1810 began the independence struggle; formal independence was declared at Tucumán on 9 July 1816, and José de San Martín's Army of the Andes liberated Chile (1817) and proclaimed Peru's independence (1821).

Nation building and prosperity

Decades of civil war between unitarios and federales, dominated by Buenos Aires governor Juan Manuel de Rosas (1829–1852), preceded national organization under the 1853 constitution. From about 1880 to 1930, massive European immigration, railways, and refrigerated beef and grain exports made Argentina one of the world's wealthiest countries per capita, ruled by a conservative oligarchy until the 1912 Sáenz Peña electoral reform.

Twentieth century

The 1930 coup opened decades of alternating civilian and military rule. Juan Domingo Perón (president 1946–1955, and again 1973–1974) reshaped politics with his labor-based movement, Peronism. After cycles of instability, the 1976–1983 military dictatorship waged the "Dirty War" against dissidents and lost the Falklands War (1982), leading to the restoration of democracy in 1983 under Raúl Alfonsín.

Recent decades

Argentina endured hyperinflation in 1989, the convertibility era of Carlos Menem, the collapse and default of 2001–2002, the Kirchner-era governments (2003–2015), and continuing debt and inflation crises into the 2020s, including the libertarian presidency of Javier Milei from December 2023.
