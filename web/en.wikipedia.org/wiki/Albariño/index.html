Albariño (Spanish pronunciation: [alβaˈɾiɲo]) is a variety of white wine grape grown in Galicia (northwest Spain) and in northwest Portugal, where it is known as Alvarinho. It is used to make varietal white wines noted for their aromatic intensity, high acidity and flavors of stone fruit, citrus and white flowers.

History

The grape has been cultivated in the Iberian northwest for centuries. A popular legend holds that Cistercian monks from Cluny brought the variety along the pilgrimage routes to Santiago de Compostela in the 12th century, but DNA profiling and ampelographic evidence suggest Albariño is native to the region, closely related to other local Portuguese varieties such as Loureiro, Arinto and Cainho Branco.

Viticulture

Albariño vines are vigorous and are traditionally trained on pergolas (parras) raised above the ground, a system adapted to the humid Atlantic climate that reduces rot and allows air circulation. The berries are small, thick-skinned and golden when ripe; thick skins help the grape resist fungal disease in the damp coastal environment.

Wine regions

In Spain, Albariño is the principal grape of the Rías Baixas DO, established as a denomination in 1988, where it produces crisp, aromatic dry white wines. In Portugal it is a key component of Vinho Verde, often blended with Loureiro and Trajadura, though single-varietal Alvarinho bottlings from the Monção e Melgaço subregion are highly regarded. The grape has also been planted in California, Oregon, Australia, New Zealand and Uruguay.

Wine style and food pairing

Albariño wines are typically light-bodied, with pronounced aromatics of apricot, peach, grapefruit and honeysuckle, brisk acidity and sometimes a saline, mineral note reflecting the Atlantic influence. They are generally made without oak and drunk young. The wines are a classic match for seafood, particularly shellfish, ceviche and grilled fish.
