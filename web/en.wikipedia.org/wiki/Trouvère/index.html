Trouvère

Trouvère
Region:Northern France
Period:12th–14th centuries
Language:Old French (langue d'oïl)
Related:Troubadour, Minnesang

Trouvère was the name given to the poet-composers of northern France in the High Middle Ages who wrote lyric poetry in the Old French langue d'oïl. The trouvères were the northern counterparts of the troubadours of southern France, who wrote in Occitan (the langue d'oc). The word derives from the Old French verb trover, "to find" or "to compose".

History

The trouvère tradition flourished from about 1150 to 1350, beginning somewhat later than the troubadour tradition, which it imitated and adapted. Where troubadour verse centred on courtly love (fin'amor), trouvère song included a wider range of genres: the grand chant courtois, the pastourelle, the chanson de toile, the aube (dawn song), crusade songs, and dance songs such as the rondeau. Northern courts, including those of Champagne under Marie de Champagne and of Artois, were major centres of patronage.

Notable trouvères

Some 2130 trouvère poems survive, with melodies preserved for roughly two-thirds of them. Well-known trouvères include Chrétien de Troyes, better known for his Arthurian romances; Gace Brulé; the Châtelain de Couci; Conon de Béthune; Blondel de Nesle; Colin Muset; and Adam de la Halle, whose Jeu de Robin et de Marion is considered an early form of comic opera. The last major trouvère is often taken to be Guillaume de Machaut, who carried the tradition into the ars nova.

Music

Trouvère melodies are generally syllabic, in the church modes, and were notated without rhythm in most manuscripts, leaving modern performers to infer rhythmic interpretation. Songs were usually sung by the trouvère himself or by a professional minstrel (jongleur), accompanied by instruments such as the vielle.
