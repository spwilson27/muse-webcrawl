Ambapali (Pali: Ambapālī; also written Amrapali) was a celebrated courtesan of the republican city-state of Vaishali in ancient India, traditionally dated to around 600–500 BCE. Renowned for her beauty and artistry, she is remembered in Buddhist texts for hosting the Buddha at her mango grove, donating the grove to the monastic community (Sangha), and in later life becoming a Buddhist nun (bhikkhuni) who attained enlightenment. Verses attributed to her survive in the Therigatha, the collection of poems by early Buddhist nuns.

Life in the texts

According to tradition, Ambapali was found as an infant at the foot of a mango tree in a royal garden at Vaishali — hence her name, from amba (mango). Raised by the gardener, she grew into a woman of such beauty that, to end the rivalry of the Licchavi princes over her, the city's assembly declared her nagarvadhu, "bride of the city" — the state courtesan, an honoured and highly accomplished public role.

Meeting with the Buddha

The Mahāparinibbāna Sutta and commentaries recount that when the Buddha visited Vaishali he stayed in Ambapali's mango grove. She invited him and his monks to a meal, an invitation he accepted even over a rival offer from the Licchavi nobles, whom he gently directed to her house as well. After the meal Ambapali donated the grove itself to the Sangha. In the commentarial accounts she later renounced her former life, was ordained, and — reflecting, in her Therigatha verses, on the impermanence of her once-celebrated beauty — became an arhat. She is also linked in tradition to King Bimbisara of Magadha, by whom she is said to have had a son, Vimala Kondanna, who became a monk.

Legacy

Ambapali became an enduring figure in Indian literature, theatre, and film — a symbol both of worldly splendour and of the possibility of liberation open to all — retold in novels such as Acharya Chatursen's Vaishali ki Nagarvadhu (1948) and in numerous stage and screen adaptations. The mango-grove gift remains a standard exemplar in Buddhist teaching of generosity and the rejection of social stigma.
