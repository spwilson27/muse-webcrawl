Ghillie suit

A ghillie suit is a type of camouflage clothing designed to resemble the background environment — typically dense foliage, grassland, or snow — and to break up the human outline. It is constructed from a base garment or netting covered with loose strips of cloth, twine, burlap (hessian), and jute, often supplemented with natural vegetation gathered on site. Ghillie suits are used principally by snipers, spotters, military personnel, hunters, and wildlife photographers who need to remain undetected.

History

The name derives from the Scottish Gaelic gille, meaning a servant or lad — gamekeepers and deer stalkers in the Scottish Highlands developed such camouflage for approaching game. The British Army's Lovat Scouts, raised during the Second Boer War and commanded by Lord Lovat, are credited with introducing the ghillie suit to military use; the unit became the British Army's first sniper formation in the First World War, and the concept spread to other armies.

Construction and use

A typical homemade suit is built by sewing netting onto a uniform, jacket, or poncho and attaching hundreds of strips of dyed jute or cloth in colours matched to the operating environment; wearers then "mud up" the suit by dragging it through dirt to remove sheen and add natural odour and texture. Natural vegetation is woven in immediately before use so that the camouflage matches the immediate surroundings. A matching veil or hood covers the head and the rifle may be wrapped in similar material.

Disadvantages

Ghillie suits are hot, heavy — especially when wet — and restrict movement and peripheral vision. The traditional materials are flammable, a risk mitigated in modern suits by flame-resistant synthetic fibres, and the suits can snag on vegetation. Despite these drawbacks, the suit's three-dimensional texture, which defeats the shape-and-shine cues the eye uses to detect a human figure, keeps it in service with sniper teams worldwide.
