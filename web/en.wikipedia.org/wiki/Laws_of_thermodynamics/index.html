Laws of thermodynamics

Thermodynamics arose from the study of steam engines.

The laws of thermodynamics are a set of scientific principles that define fundamental physical quantities — temperature, energy, and entropy — and characterise thermodynamic systems in equilibrium. They are among the most universally applicable laws in physics.

Zeroth law

If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in equilibrium with each other. This establishes temperature as a well-defined, transitive property.

First law

Energy is conserved: the change in a system's internal energy equals the heat supplied to it minus the work it does, ΔU = Q − W. It rules out perpetual motion machines of the first kind.

Second law

The total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold (Clausius), and no engine operating in a cycle can convert heat entirely into work (Kelvin–Planck). This law gives time its thermodynamic arrow and caps the efficiency of heat engines at the Carnot limit.

Third law

As temperature approaches absolute zero, the entropy of a system approaches a constant minimum (Nernst's theorem); absolute zero itself is unattainable in finitely many steps.

History

The laws emerged in the 19th century from the work of Sadi Carnot, Rudolf Clausius, William Thomson (Lord Kelvin), and Walther Nernst, and were grounded microscopically by Maxwell and Boltzmann's statistical mechanics.
