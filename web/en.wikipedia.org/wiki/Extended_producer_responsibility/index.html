Extended producer responsibility

Extended producer responsibility (EPR) is a strategy to promote the integration of environmental costs associated with goods throughout their life cycles into the market price of the products. Extended producer responsibility legislation is a driving force behind the adoption of remanufacturing initiatives because it "focuses on the end-of-use treatment of consumer products and has the primary aim to increase the amount and degree of product recovery and to minimize the environmental impact of waste materials."

EPR uses financial incentives to encourage manufacturers to design products that are easier to recycle, contain fewer hazardous materials, and produce less waste. It shifts responsibility for end-of-life management from municipalities and consumers to the producers who place products on the market.

Implementation

EPR schemes commonly take the form of take-back requirements, deposit-refund schemes, advance recycling fees, and collective producer responsibility organizations that manage collection and recycling on behalf of member companies. Products covered typically include packaging, electronics (e-waste), batteries, tires, vehicles, and paint.

History and adoption

The concept was formalized in the early 1990s, notably by Swedish academic Thomas Lindhqvist, and was adopted in Germany's 1991 Packaging Ordinance, which created the Green Dot system. The European Union has since adopted EPR directives for packaging, waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE), batteries, and end-of-life vehicles. Many U.S. states, Canadian provinces, and Asian countries have enacted EPR laws, especially for packaging.

Criticism

Critics argue that EPR fees can raise consumer prices, that collective schemes may weaken individual design incentives, and that poorly designed programs can entrench monopolies in recycling services. Supporters counter that EPR improves recycling rates and funds infrastructure that voluntary programs cannot sustain.
