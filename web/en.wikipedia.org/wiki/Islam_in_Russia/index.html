Islam in Russia

Islam in Russia is the country's second-largest religion after Christianity. Russia has one of the largest Muslim populations in Europe, commonly estimated at between 14 and 20 million people, or roughly 10–15% of the total population. Islam is considered one of Russia's traditional religions, legally recognised under the 1997 law "On Freedom of Conscience and on Religious Associations" alongside Christianity, Judaism and Buddhism.

History

Islam first reached the territory of modern Russia in the seventh and eighth centuries, when Arab armies advanced into the Caucasus and took Derbent in Dagestan, often described as the oldest centre of Islam in Russia. In 922, the Volga Bulgars formally adopted Islam after the mission of Ibn Fadlan from the Abbasid Caliphate, making the Volga region one of the earliest Muslim areas of Eastern Europe.

Under the Mongol Golden Horde, Islam spread widely among the Turkic peoples of the steppe. After the Russian conquest of the Khanates of Kazan (1552) and Astrakhan (1556), Muslims became subjects of an expanding Orthodox Christian empire. Catherine the Great adopted a policy of pragmatic toleration, founding the Orenburg Muslim Spiritual Assembly in 1788, the first state-recognised Muslim administration in Russia.

During the Soviet period, Islam was subjected to severe repression: mosques were closed or destroyed, religious schools were shut, and the Arabic script was replaced first by Latin and then by Cyrillic for Turkic languages. Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, Russia experienced an Islamic revival, with thousands of new mosques built across the country.

Demographics

Muslims are concentrated in the North Caucasus republics — Chechnya, Dagestan, Ingushetia, Kabardino-Balkaria, Karachay-Cherkessia and Adygea — where they form large majorities, and in the Volga-Ural republics of Tatarstan and Bashkortostan. Large Muslim communities also live in Moscow, Saint Petersburg and other major cities, including many labour migrants from Central Asia. Ethnically, Russian Muslims include Tatars, Bashkirs, Chechens, Avars, Azerbaijanis, Kazakhs, Uzbeks and many others. The overwhelming majority are Sunni, mostly of the Hanafi school in the Volga and Ural regions and the Shafi'i school in the North Caucasus, with a significant Shia minority, mainly among Azerbaijanis.

Organisation

Islam in Russia is administered by several rival muftiates, including the Council of Muftis of Russia, the Central Spiritual Administration of Muslims of Russia based in Ufa, and regional bodies such as the Spiritual Administration of Muslims of the Republic of Tatarstan. The Grand Mufti is an honorific held by several figures; Sheikh Ravil Gaynutdin has chaired the Council of Muftis since the 1990s, while Talgat Tadzhuddin heads the older central administration. Major institutions include the Moscow Cathedral Mosque, reopened in 2015 after reconstruction, and the Kul Sharif Mosque in Kazan.
