16th (Irish) Division

The 16th (Irish) Division was an infantry division of the British Army raised during the First World War as part of Kitchener's New Armies. Recruited largely from Irish volunteers — many drawn from the National Volunteers associated with John Redmond's Irish Parliamentary Party — it served on the Western Front from 1916 and fought at the Somme, Messines and Passchendaele.

Active1914–1919
CountryUnited Kingdom
TypeInfantry division
EngagementsLoos, Somme (Guillemont, Ginchy), Messines, Passchendaele, German spring offensive

Formation

Formed in Ireland in September 1914 from the 47th, 48th and 49th Brigades, the division's battalions came from Irish regiments including the Royal Irish Regiment, the Royal Irish Fusiliers, the Connaught Rangers, the Leinster Regiment and the Royal Munster Fusiliers. Its first commander was Major-General Lawrence Parsons. The division trained in Ireland and England, and the 1916 Easter Rising at home — and the executions that followed — badly damaged the nationalist recruitment that had sustained it.

Service

The division landed in France in December 1915 and first served around Loos. At the Somme in September 1916 it captured Guillemont and Ginchy at heavy cost. In June 1917, at the Battle of Messines, it attacked alongside the Protestant-recruited 36th (Ulster) Division — a pairing later invested with great symbolic significance in Ireland — and both divisions' dead are commemorated at the Island of Ireland Peace Park at Messines. The 16th suffered grievously at Passchendaele and was nearly destroyed in the German spring offensive of March 1918; rebuilt, it finished the war in the advance to victory.

Legacy

The division's service was long overshadowed in independent Ireland, but its soldiers are now commemorated alongside the Ulster Division as symbols of the island's shared wartime sacrifice.
