A glory is an optical phenomenon consisting of one or more concentric, coloured rings surrounding the shadow of an observer's head, appearing on clouds or fog below the observer. It is centred on the antisolar point — the point directly opposite the Sun — and is frequently seen by mountaineers and by passengers in aircraft, where the rings encircle the shadow of the plane on cloud tops.

Formation

Glories are produced when sunlight is backscattered toward the observer by tiny, uniform water droplets in cloud or fog. Unlike a rainbow, which is formed by refraction in large raindrops at about 42° from the antisolar point, a glory is only a few degrees across, and its colours and ring spacing depend on the droplet size. A full explanation requires Mie scattering theory and involves light waves travelling along the surfaces of the droplets; the phenomenon was described mathematically by Hendrik van de Hulst in 1947.

Associated phenomena

A glory seen from a mountain summit often surrounds a Brocken spectre, the observer's magnified shadow cast onto mist. In Chinese tradition, glories observed at Mount Emei are known as "Buddha's light".
