DNA gyrase

DNA gyrase, or simply gyrase, is an enzyme within the class of topoisomerases (specifically, a type II topoisomerase) that reduces topological strain in DNA while double-stranded DNA is being unwound by elongating RNA polymerase or by helicase in front of a progressing replication fork. It does so by introducing negative supercoils into DNA, or by relaxing positive supercoils, in a reaction coupled to ATP hydrolysis.

Mechanism

Gyrase is a tetramer composed of two GyrA and two GyrB subunits. It binds a segment of DNA, introduces a transient double-strand break, passes another segment of the same DNA molecule through the break, and reseals it, changing the linking number by two in each cycle. This activity is essential in bacteria for DNA replication, transcription and chromosome segregation.

Clinical significance

Because gyrase is found in bacteria but not in the same form in humans, it is an important target for antibiotics. The fluoroquinolone antibiotics (such as ciprofloxacin) trap gyrase–DNA cleavage complexes and thereby kill bacterial cells, while aminocoumarins such as novobiocin inhibit the ATPase activity of the GyrB subunit. Mutations in the gyrA and gyrB genes are a common route to quinolone resistance.
