The pectinate muscles (Latin: musculi pectinati) are parallel ridges of cardiac muscle in the walls of the atria of the heart, named for their resemblance to the teeth of a comb (pecten is Latin for comb). They are prominent on the inner surface of the right auricle (right atrial appendage) and, to a much lesser degree, in the left atrial appendage.

Structure

Behind the ridge of the crista terminalis, the inner surface of the right atrium is smooth (forming the sinus venarum), but in front of it the wall is thrown into a series of transverse muscular ridges, the pectinate muscles, which spring from the crista and run forward into the atrial appendage. In the left atrium, pectinate muscles are confined to the interior of the small left atrial appendage, whose narrow, ridged lumen contrasts with the smooth-walled remainder of the chamber. The contrast between the two appendages is a reliable anatomical landmark: the right appendage is broad and triangular with extensive pectinate muscles, whereas the left is narrow and finger-like.

Clinical significance

The pectinate muscles and appendages are clinically important because blood stasis among the ridges — especially in the left atrial appendage during atrial fibrillation — predisposes to thrombus formation and embolic stroke; this is the rationale for left atrial appendage occlusion devices. On echocardiography, prominent pectinate muscles can occasionally be mistaken for intracardiac masses or thrombi. The extent of pectinate muscle onto the crista terminalis can also influence the anatomy relevant to catheter ablation of atrial arrhythmias.
