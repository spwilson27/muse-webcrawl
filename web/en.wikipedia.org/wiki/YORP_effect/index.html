YORP effect

YORP effect
Thermal re-radiation from an irregular body produces torque
FieldPlanetary science
Named forYarkovsky, O'Keefe, Radzievskii, Paddack
AffectsAsteroid rotation rate and axial tilt

The YORP effect (Yarkovsky–O'Keefe–Radzievskii–Paddack effect) is a small torque exerted on an irregularly shaped body, such as an asteroid, by sunlight. Solar radiation that is absorbed and re-emitted as heat does not act symmetrically on an asymmetric object; the resulting reaction force, integrated over the surface, slowly changes the body's rotation rate and the tilt of its spin axis over millions of years.

Mechanism

Sunlight heats an asteroid's surface; the warmed surface re-radiates in the infrared. For a spherical, smooth body the forces cancel, but bumps, facets and craters make the emission asymmetric, and photons carry momentum, so the body experiences a net torque. Depending on shape, the torque can spin the object up or down and drive its obliquity toward 0°, 90° or 180°. The effect scales strongly with size and distance from the Sun, being significant mainly for bodies smaller than a few tens of kilometres over timescales of millions of years.

Observations

The effect was proposed in stages by Ivan Yarkovsky (who described the related orbital Yarkovsky effect), John O'Keefe, V. V. Radzievskii and Stephen Paddack. In 2007 two teams directly measured YORP acceleration of the small asteroid 54509 YORP (the namesake of the acronym), detecting a steady increase in its rotation rate. Similar spin changes have since been measured for asteroids such as (1620) Geographos and Bennu, whose spin was observed to accelerate during the OSIRIS-REx mission.

Consequences

YORP spin-up can rotate a rubble-pile asteroid so fast that material migrates to the equator, producing a "spinning top" shape with an equatorial ridge (as seen at Bennu and Ryugu), shedding mass, or even fissioning the body into a binary asteroid pair. YORP is thus a major driver of the rotational evolution and lifetimes of small asteroids, alongside collisions and the Yarkovsky drift.
