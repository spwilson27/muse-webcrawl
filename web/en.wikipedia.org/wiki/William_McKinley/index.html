William McKinley (January 29, 1843 – September 14, 1901) was the 25th president of the United States, serving from 1897 until his assassination in 1901. A Republican, he led the nation during the Spanish–American War, raised protective tariffs to promote American industry, and maintained the nation's gold standard. Under McKinley the United States annexed Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, emerging as an overseas imperial power.

Early life and Civil War service

McKinley was born in Niles, Ohio. He enlisted in the Union Army during the American Civil War and rose from private to brevet major, serving under future president Rutherford B. Hayes. After the war he settled in Canton, Ohio, married Ida Saxton, and built a legal and political career, serving in Congress from 1877 and as governor of Ohio from 1892 to 1896. He became known as a leading advocate of protective tariffs, authoring the McKinley Tariff of 1890.

Presidency

McKinley defeated Democrat William Jennings Bryan in the elections of 1896 and 1900. His presidency saw rapid economic growth, the passage of the Dingley Tariff of 1897 and the Gold Standard Act of 1900, and victory in the Spanish–American War of 1898, which ended Spanish colonial rule in the Americas and the Pacific. He also ordered the annexation of the Republic of Hawaii and asserted the Open Door Policy toward China.

Assassination

On September 6, 1901, while attending the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York, McKinley was shot by Leon Czolgosz, an anarchist. He died eight days later, on September 14, and was succeeded by his vice president, Theodore Roosevelt. McKinley was the third American president to be assassinated, following Abraham Lincoln and James A. Garfield.

Legacy

Historians generally rank McKinley as an above-average president, crediting him with realigning American politics around Republican dominance that lasted until the 1930s. North America's highest peak was named Mount McKinley in his honour in 1896; it was officially renamed Denali in 2015.
