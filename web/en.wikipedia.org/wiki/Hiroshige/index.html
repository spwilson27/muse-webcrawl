Hiroshige

Utagawa Hiroshige
Born: 1797, Edo, Japan
Died: 12 October 1858, Edo
Known for: ukiyo-e woodblock prints

Utagawa Hiroshige (Japanese: 歌川 広重; 1797 – 12 October 1858), born Andō Tokutarō, was a Japanese ukiyo-e artist, considered the last great master of the Japanese woodblock print tradition. He is best known for his horizontal-format landscape series such as The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō and One Hundred Famous Views of Edo.

Career

Born in Edo (now Tokyo) to a samurai fire warden, Hiroshige entered the studio of Utagawa Toyohiro in 1811. After early work in figure prints, he turned to landscapes; his 1833–34 series of the Tōkaidō road stations was an enormous popular success and established him as the leading landscape print designer of the day, alongside his older contemporary Hokusai.

Style and influence

Hiroshige's prints are noted for their lyrical composition, atmospheric weather effects — sudden rain, snow, mist — and striking use of colour, including imported Prussian blue. His work profoundly influenced European artists of the late 19th century: Van Gogh copied two of his One Hundred Famous Views of Edo designs in oil, and the Japonisme movement drew heavily on his cropped, diagonal compositions.

Death and legacy

Hiroshige died in the 1858 Edo cholera epidemic, leaving a body of work estimated at more than 8,000 designs. His final series, One Hundred Famous Views of Edo (1856–58), was completed posthumously by his pupil Hiroshige II.
