Japanese martial arts are the styles and traditions of combat practised in Japan. The native term bugei (武芸) refers to the martial techniques of the samurai class, while budō (武道, "martial way") denotes the philosophical and character-developing systems that developed from the late nineteenth century onward. They span armed disciplines (kendo, kyūdō, naginata) and unarmed ones (judo, karate, aikido), as well as the professional sport of sumo.

History

Formal martial traditions date to the Heian and Kamakura periods, when mounted archery (yabusame) and swordsmanship defined the samurai. In the long peace of the Tokugawa era, hundreds of koryū (classical schools) systematised techniques. The Meiji Restoration (1868) abolished the samurai class and briefly suppressed the arts, but educators such as Jigoro Kano (judo, 1882) reframed them as sports and moral education. Karate, developed in the Ryukyu Islands with Chinese influences, entered mainland Japan in the 1920s through Gichin Funakoshi.

Major disciplines

Judo ("gentle way") emphasises throws and grappling; it has been an Olympic sport since 1964. Kendo uses bamboo swords (shinai) and armour, descended from samurai swordsmanship. Karate ("empty hand") is primarily striking, with kata forms and sparring. Aikido, founded by Morihei Ueshiba, redirects an attacker's energy through joint locks and throws and stresses harmony rather than aggression. Kyūdō is the contemplative way of the bow. Sumo, the ritualised wrestling of Japan, preserves Shinto ceremonial traditions stretching back centuries.

Ranking and philosophy

Most budō disciplines use the kyū/dan ranking system, marked by coloured and then black belts, and award teaching licences (shōgō). Philosophically they draw on Zen Buddhism and Confucian ethics: the dō ("way") framing emphasises self-cultivation, courtesy (reigi), and lifelong practice over mere combative effectiveness.
