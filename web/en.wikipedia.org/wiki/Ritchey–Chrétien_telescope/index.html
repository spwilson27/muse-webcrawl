Ritchey–Chrétien telescope

A Ritchey–Chrétien telescope (RCT) is a specialized variant of the Cassegrain reflector in which both the primary and secondary mirrors are hyperbolic. Invented in the early 1910s by American astronomer George Willis Ritchey and French astronomer Henri Chrétien, the design eliminates both spherical aberration and off-axis coma, giving sharp images over a much wider field than the classical Cassegrain.

Design and use

The two hyperbolic surfaces are harder to figure and test than simple conics, which long restricted the design to professional instruments. It is the standard for large research telescopes: the Hubble Space Telescope, the Keck telescopes, the Very Large Telescope, and most observatories built since the 1980s use Ritchey–Chrétien optics (Hubble's spherical-aberration mishap arose from a testing error in figuring its primary). Smaller RCTs are popular with advanced amateur astrophotographers.

Residual aberrations include astigmatism and field curvature, typically corrected with a field flattener lens in imaging systems.
