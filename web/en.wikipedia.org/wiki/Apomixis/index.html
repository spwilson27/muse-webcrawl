Apomixis

Apomixis is asexual reproduction in plants through seeds: seeds are formed without fertilisation, so the embryos are genetic clones of the mother plant. The term (from Greek apo-, "away from", and mixis, "mingling") was introduced by Hans Winkler in 1908. Apomixis occurs in more than 400 plant genera across dozens of families, including familiar examples such as dandelions, blackberries, hawthorns, and many grasses and citrus.

Mechanisms

In gametophytic apomixis, an unreduced embryo sac is produced either from the megaspore mother cell without proper meiosis (diplospory) or from a somatic cell of the nucellus (apospory). The unreduced egg then develops into an embryo without fertilisation (parthenogenesis). In sporophytic apomixis (adventive embryony), embryos arise directly from somatic tissue of the ovule alongside a normal sexual embryo, as in many citrus fruits, which is why a single citrus seed may sprout several seedlings.

Many apomicts still require pollination to form the endosperm that nourishes the seed — a phenomenon called pseudogamy — even though the pollen contributes nothing to the embryo's genetics.

Significance

Because apomictic offspring are clones, apomixis can fix a favourable genotype — including hybrid vigour — indefinitely through seed. Plant breeders have long sought to introduce apomixis into major crops such as maize and rice, which would let farmers replant seed from hybrids without losing their qualities. In nature, apomixis is frequently associated with polyploidy and hybrid origin, and it complicates plant taxonomy: apomictic lineages in groups like Taraxacum (dandelions) and Rubus (blackberries) fragment into hundreds of recognised microspecies.
