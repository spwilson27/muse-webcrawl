Augustus II (12 May 1670 – 1 February 1733), commonly known as Augustus the Strong, was Elector of Saxony from 1694 as Frederick Augustus I, and King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania from 1697 to 1706 and again from 1709 until his death in 1733. His sobriquet came from his reputed physical strength — he was said to break horseshoes with his bare hands.

Early life and election

Augustus was the second son of John George III, Elector of Saxony. He succeeded his elder brother as elector in 1694. On the death of John III Sobieski of Poland, Augustus converted to Catholicism and, with Russian support and lavish bribery, secured election to the Polish throne in 1697, founding the personal union between Saxony and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth that lasted until 1763.

Great Northern War

Hoping to recover Livonia from Sweden, Augustus joined Russia and Denmark in attacking Sweden, precipitating the Great Northern War (1700–1721). Charles XII of Sweden defeated him and in 1706 forced him, by the Treaty of Altranstädt, to abdicate the Polish crown in favour of Stanisław Leszczyński. After Charles's defeat at Poltava in 1709, Augustus regained the throne with Russian help — cementing Russia's growing dominance over Polish affairs.

Patronage

Augustus transformed Dresden into a major cultural centre — the "Florence on the Elbe". He commissioned the Zwinger palace, rebuilt the Frauenkirche, and assembled great collections of art and treasures, including the Grünes Gewölbe (Green Vault). He founded the Meissen porcelain manufactory in 1710, the first European producer of hard-paste porcelain, after confining the alchemist Johann Friedrich Böttger to perfect the process.

Death and legacy

Augustus died in Warsaw in 1733 and was succeeded in both Saxony and Poland by his son Augustus III. He is credited with numerous illegitimate children — legend says several hundred — and his reign is remembered for dazzling court culture alongside political decline in Poland.
