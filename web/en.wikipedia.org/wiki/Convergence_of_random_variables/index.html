Convergence of random variables

Convergence of sample means to the expectation

In probability theory, there exist several different notions of convergence of random variables. The convergence of sequences of random variables to some limit random variable is an important concept in probability theory, and its applications to statistics and stochastic processes. The same concepts are known in more general mathematics as stochastic convergence.

Convergence in distribution

A sequence of random variables X1, X2, … converges in distribution to X if their cumulative distribution functions converge at every continuity point of the limit:

lim Fn(x) = F(x)

Convergence in distribution underlies the central limit theorem: the distribution of a normalised sample mean approaches the normal distribution as the sample size grows.

Convergence in probability

Xn converges in probability to X if for every ε > 0, P(|Xn − X| > ε) → 0. This notion underlies the weak law of large numbers: the sample mean converges in probability to the expected value.

Almost sure and mean-square convergence

Convergence almost surely (or with probability 1) requires P(lim Xn = X) = 1, and underlies the strong law of large numbers. Convergence in mean square requires E[(Xn − X)2] → 0. The implications between the notions form a hierarchy: mean-square and almost sure convergence each imply convergence in probability, which in turn implies convergence in distribution; none of the reverse implications hold in general.
