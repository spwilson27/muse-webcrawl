Literal (mathematical logic)

Literal
DefinitionAn atomic formula or its negation
Positive literalAn atom, e.g. P
Negative literalA negated atom, e.g. ¬P

In mathematical logic, a literal is an atomic formula (an atom) or its negation. A literal is called positive if it is an atom and negative if it is the negation of an atom. For example, in propositional logic with atoms P and Q, the formulas P and ¬Q are literals, the first positive and the second negative.

Concepts

The complement of a literal is the literal obtained by adding or removing a negation: the complement of P is ¬P and vice versa. Two literals are complementary if one is the negation of the other. A literal that consists of a single propositional variable is sometimes called a propositional literal, while one built from a predicate applied to terms is a first-order literal; a first-order literal with no variables is a ground literal.

Uses

Literals are the building blocks of clauses: a clause is a disjunction of literals, and conjunctive normal form expresses a formula as a conjunction of such clauses. This representation is fundamental to resolution theorem proving, SAT solving and logic programming, where definite clauses in Prolog consist of literals with at most one positive literal (Horn clauses) in the corresponding clause form.
