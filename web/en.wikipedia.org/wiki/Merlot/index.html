Merlot is a dark blue–coloured wine grape variety used both as a blending grape and for varietal wines. The name is thought to be a diminutive of merle, the French word for blackbird, a reference either to the colour of the grape or to the bird's fondness for the fruit. Its softness, early ripening and fleshy texture make it a popular blending partner for the sterner, later-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon. DNA profiling in the 1990s established that Merlot is the offspring of Cabernet Franc and the obscure Magdeleine Noire des Charentes, making it a half-sibling of Cabernet Sauvignon and Carménère.

History

The earliest recorded mention of Merlot dates from 1784, in notes by a Bordeaux official who praised it as one of the finest grapes of the Libournais. By the 19th century it was widely planted on the right bank of the Gironde, in Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, where clay soils suit it well, and as a blending component across Bordeaux. In the late 20th century Merlot became one of the most planted varieties in the world, driven by demand for soft, approachable varietal wines, especially from California and Chile.

Viticulture

Merlot is an early- to mid-ripening variety with thin skins and loose bunches, which makes it susceptible to rot and mildew in wet conditions but also gives softer tannins than Cabernet Sauvignon. It performs best on cooler clay and limestone soils that retain moisture; on such soils, notably in Pomerol, it can produce wines of great depth and longevity, exemplified by Château Pétrus, which is made almost entirely from Merlot.

Wine styles

Merlot wines are typically medium- to full-bodied with flavours of plum, black cherry, raspberry, chocolate and herbs, moderate acidity and soft tannin. Two broad styles exist: an "international" style of late-harvested, heavily oaked, inky wines, and a more traditional Bordeaux style emphasising freshness and structure. Merlot is also a major component of Super Tuscan blends in Italy, of wines from Washington State, and of Chilean wines (some of which were long mislabelled Carménère plantings).

The "Sideways effect"

Merlot sales in the United States dipped noticeably after the 2004 film Sideways, in which the protagonist famously disparages the variety while praising Pinot Noir. Economists have debated the size and duration of the effect, but plantings and sales data suggest at least a short-term impact on American consumption patterns.
