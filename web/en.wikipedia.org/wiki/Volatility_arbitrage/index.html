Volatility arbitrage

Volatility arbitrage (or vol arb) is a financial arbitrage technique that takes positions in options and their underlying securities in order to profit from differences between the implied volatility of an option and a forecast of the future realised volatility of the option's underlying asset.

Overview

In volatility arbitrage, rather than betting on the direction of the underlying asset's price, the trader speculates on the difference in price movement — the volatility — of the asset. Roughly speaking, an option's price increases with expected volatility, so if the trader believes the asset will be more volatile than the market's implied volatility suggests, they buy the option and hedge the directional exposure with the underlying; if they believe it will be less volatile, they sell the option and hedge.

Delta hedging

The classic implementation is the delta-neutral portfolio: longs and shorts in options are balanced with positions in the underlying so that small price movements do not change the portfolio's value. The position is rebalanced ("gamma scalping") as the underlying moves, and profit accumulates if realised volatility differs from implied volatility in the direction the trader predicted.

Risks

Volatility arbitrage is exposed to model risk, liquidity risk, and "jump" risk from sudden large price moves. Perhaps its most famous failure involved Long-Term Capital Management, whose convergence and volatility trades suffered catastrophic losses in 1998 during the Russian financial crisis, when volatility spiked far beyond model expectations.

Related strategies

Related approaches include dispersion trading (trading index options against options on the index's constituents), variance swaps, and trading the volatility smile or skew.
