An honorary degree is an academic degree for which a university (or other degree-awarding institution) has waived all of the usual requirements. It is also known by the Latin phrases honoris causa ("for the sake of the honour") or ad eundem gradum ("to the same step"). The degree is often conferred as a way of honouring a distinguished visitor's contributions to a specific field or to society in general.

History

The practice dates to the Middle Ages. The earliest recorded honorary degree was granted by the University of Oxford in the 1470s to Lionel Woodville, brother-in-law of Edward IV and later Bishop of Salisbury. By the late 16th century honorary degrees had become common at Oxford and Cambridge, though their conferral has periodically attracted controversy when perceived as rewards for donations or public relations rather than achievement.

Usage and conventions

Honorary degrees are most often doctorates (D.Litt., D.Sc., D.Mus., LL.D., D.D., and so on), though honorary master's degrees and fellowships also exist. By convention, recipients of honorary doctorates do not generally style themselves "Doctor" in formal usage, though practice varies by country and institution, and the degree is listed separately from earned degrees. Recipients commonly deliver the commencement address at the ceremony. Universities typically do not award honorary degrees to their own serving faculty or current political officeholders of their own country (rules vary), and some institutions — MIT and Stanford among them — do not award honorary degrees at all.

Revocation and controversy

Honorary degrees have sometimes been revoked (or offered to be returned) when recipients later fall into disgrace; universities rescinded honours given to figures such as Robert Mugabe (University of Edinburgh, 2007) and have debated many others. Critics argue honorary doctorates can devalue earned qualifications, while universities defend them as a legitimate means of recognising service, philanthropy, artistic achievement, and public life.
