Barnes maze

The Barnes maze is a laboratory tool used in psychological laboratory experiments to measure spatial learning and memory in rodents, particularly rats and mice. It was developed by Carol Barnes in 1979 as an alternative to the radial arm maze and the Morris water maze.

Description

The apparatus consists of a large circular platform, typically about 1.2 metres in diameter for rats, with a series of holes (commonly 18 to 20) evenly spaced around its perimeter. Beneath one hole is a dark escape box; the other holes lead to shallow false bottoms. Visual cues are placed around the room or on the maze walls so the animal can orient itself.

Procedure and measures

Rodents naturally prefer dark, enclosed spaces and are motivated by bright light or open exposure on the platform to find the escape hole. Over repeated trials the animal learns the location of the target hole using spatial cues. Researchers measure latency to find the target, the number of errors (visits to non-target holes), and the search strategy used. Performance is sensitive to hippocampal damage, aging, and genetic manipulations affecting memory, making the Barnes maze a standard dry-land test of spatial cognition that avoids the stress of swimming in the water maze.
