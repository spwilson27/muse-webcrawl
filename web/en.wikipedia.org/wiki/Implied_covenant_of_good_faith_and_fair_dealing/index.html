Implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing

The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is a legal doctrine in United States contract law under which every contract is understood to impose on each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its performance and enforcement. The covenant is implied by law: the parties need not write it into their agreement, and in most contexts it cannot be used to override a contract's express terms.

Doctrine

The doctrine received its classic statement in Kirke La Shelle Co. v. Paul Armstrong Co. (1933), where the New York Court of Appeals held that in every contract there is "an implied covenant that neither party shall do anything which will have the effect of destroying or injuring the right of the other party to receive the fruits of the contract". The Restatement (Second) of Contracts §205 states flatly: "Every contract imposes upon each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its performance and its enforcement." The Uniform Commercial Code likewise imposes the obligation in contracts for the sale of goods and related commercial transactions.

Typical applications include: exercising contractual discretion arbitrarily or to recapture opportunities forgone at contracting; evading the spirit of the bargain; willfully failing to cooperate so that the other party can perform; and manipulating conditions — for example, an output or requirements contract manipulated in bad faith, or a satisfaction clause abused to escape an unwanted deal. The covenant does not, however, furnish a free-standing cause of action in most states, and cannot add obligations inconsistent with express terms: a party exercising an unqualified termination right exactly as written, for instance, generally does not breach the covenant under New York law.

Employment and insurance

Two contexts have stretched the doctrine. In employment, a few states — California prominently, in cases such as Cleary v. American Airlines (1980) — allowed the covenant to limit at-will discharge, though later decisions, especially Foley v. Interactive Data Corp. (1988), confined bad-faith claims to contract remedies and rejected tort damages. In insurance, by contrast, many states treat the covenant as sounding in tort: an insurer that unreasonably refuses to defend its insured, settle within policy limits, or pay a valid claim may be liable for all resulting damages, including punitive damages, because of the special relationship and the insured's vulnerability. The insurance bad-faith tort usually survives the contract-only limit because of the fiduciary-like character of insurance.

Delaware and corporate contexts

Delaware law recognizes the implied covenant as a gap-filling tool in contracts and in the LLC and partnership statutes, where it is frequently litigated in alternative-entity disputes. Delaware courts stress that the covenant applies only where the contract is truly silent on the issue, and that sophisticated parties are held to their written allocations of risk; it is, in the Court of Chancery's phrase, a "limited and extraordinary" remedy.

Comparison

Analogous duties exist in many systems: the civil law's general good-faith clauses are broader, while English law reaches similar results in fragments — through implied terms, estoppel, and doctrines policing discretionary powers — without a general covenant. Canadian law, since Bhasin v. Hrynew (2014), recognizes a duty of honest performance applicable to all contracts.
