Generation of 1914

The generation of 1914 is a term for the young Europeans who came of age during, and were decisively shaped by, the First World War. Millions of men of this cohort were killed, wounded, or psychologically scarred by trench warfare, while the survivors returned to societies transformed by war, revolution, and economic upheaval. The phrase is closely associated with historian Robert Wohl's 1979 study The Generation of 1914, which examined writers and intellectuals in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and England.

The war generation

For front soldiers — the German Frontgeneration — the experience of industrialized mass warfare created a generational identity distinct from both the prewar establishment and younger cohorts who missed combat. In Germany, front experience fed both pacifist literature, such as Erich Maria Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front (1929), and the militant nationalism of writers like Ernst Jünger, contributing to interwar political polarization. In Britain, the war poets and the image of a "lost generation" of public-school officers shaped national memory; in France, veterans' organizations (anciens combattants) became a major political force.

Intellectuals and politics

Wohl argued that a shared generational consciousness among European intellectuals born roughly between 1880 and 1900 Transcended national boundaries: many rejected liberal bourgeois values associated with their fathers and sought new orders through communism, fascism, or cultural modernism. Spanish usage also applies "generación de 1914" to the group around philosopher José Ortega y Gasset, who promoted Europeanization and founded the League of Political Education in 1914 — a meaning largely independent of the war, since Spain remained neutral.

Legacy

The concept influenced the broader sociology of generations derived from Karl Mannheim's 1928 essay "The Problem of Generations." Demographers note the cohort's lasting marks: skewed sex ratios, mass bereavement, and, in several countries, a generation of political leaders — from Adolf Hitler to Charles de Gaulle — whose outlooks were forged between 1914 and 1918.
