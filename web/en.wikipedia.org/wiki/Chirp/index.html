Chirp

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A chirp is a signal in which the frequency increases (up-chirp) or decreases (down-chirp) with time. In some sources, the term chirp is used interchangeably with sweep signal. It is commonly applied to sonar, radar, and laser systems, and to other applications such as in spread-spectrum communications. It is also produced by some animals, most notably crickets, birds, dolphins and bats.

Types

A linear chirp has an instantaneous frequency that varies linearly with time. An exponential chirp varies geometrically: the rate of change of frequency is proportional to the frequency itself. Chirps may also be described as sinusoidal (following sine or cosine sweeps) or hyperbolic.

Applications

Chirp signals are used in radar and sonar pulse compression, where a long swept pulse gives good range resolution while keeping peak power low. Chirp spread spectrum is used in low-power wireless communications such as LoRa. In optics, chirped pulses are the basis of chirped pulse amplification, a technique that earned the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physics. In acoustics, chirps are used to measure room responses and in bioacoustics to study animal vocalizations.

Chirpyness

A signal is said to have chirpyness if its instantaneous frequency varies over the duration of the signal; quantization of chirpyness is used in some signal processing and seismology contexts.
