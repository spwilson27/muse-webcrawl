The Crossing of the Andes (Spanish: Cruce de los Andes) was one of the most important feats in the Spanish American wars of independence. Carried out in January 1817 by the Army of the Andes under General José de San Martín, it moved an army from the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata (present-day Argentina) across the Andes into royalist-held Chile, opening the campaign that liberated Chile and later Peru.

The crossing is often compared to Hannibal's and Napoleon's passages of the Alps. San Martín's columns traversed passes above 4,000 metres in summer, losing many animals and a significant number of men to altitude sickness, cold, and exhaustion, yet arrived in condition to defeat the royalists at the Battle of Chacabuco on 12 February 1817.

Background

After the patriot government of Chile was destroyed by royalist forces at the Battle of Rancagua in 1814 (the end of the "Patria Vieja"), surviving Chilean leaders such as Bernardo O'Higgins fled to Mendoza in Cuyo, Argentina. San Martín, governor of Cuyo from 1814, concluded that Spanish power in South America could only be broken by liberating Chile, then sailing to Peru — the royalist stronghold — rather than by the repeatedly defeated overland route through Upper Peru. With support from Supreme Director Juan Martín de Pueyrredón, he built the Army of the Andes in Mendoza over two years.

Preparation

The preparations were famously thorough. San Martín organized arms workshops, powder mills, and hospitals; raised funds and supplies from the population of Cuyo; and conducted an elaborate deception campaign, spreading false intelligence about his intended route (the so-called "war of zapa") through double agents, conferences with Pehuenche peoples, and planted correspondence. Provisions were pre-positioned along the routes, and the army carried dried food, wine, brandy (against the cold), garlic and onions (against altitude sickness), and warm clothing.

The crossing

The army moved in six columns through different passes to confuse the royalists and converge in Chile. The two main columns crossed by the Los Patos pass (San Martín with the bulk of the infantry) and the Uspallata pass (Juan Gregorio de las Heras with the artillery). Smaller columns crossed further north and south — including the columns of Freire and Cabot — to magnify the apparent threat. Soler's column followed as rearguard.

The main effort, some 5,000 soldiers with thousands of mules and horses and disassembled artillery carried piece by piece, climbed passes over 4,000 m (the Paso de la Cumbre exceeds 3,800 m on the Uspallata route). Men suffered soroche (altitude sickness) and freezing nights; of the livestock taken, roughly half or more of the mules and horses died. Nevertheless the columns descended into Chile in early February and united near the Aconcagua valley.

Aftermath

On 12 February 1817 the Army of the Andes defeated the royalists at Chacabuco, opening the road to Santiago and restoring Chilean independence (formally declared a year later, after the victory at Maipú in April 1818). The crossing thus made possible San Martín's later maritime expedition to Peru in 1820.

Legacy

The Crossing of the Andes is remembered in Argentina and Chile as one of the great military marches in history and a founding epic of both nations. It is commemorated by monuments at the passes (including the Christ the Redeemer of the Andes statue erected in 1904 at the Uspallata pass), by annual reenactment marches in San Martín and Mendoza, and in paintings such as those of Pedro Subercaseaux and Martín Tovar y Tovar.
