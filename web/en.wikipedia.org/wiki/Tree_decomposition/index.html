Tree decomposition

In graph theory, a tree decomposition is a mapping of a graph into a tree that can be used to reason about the graph's structure and to speed up the solution of certain computational problems on it. The concept was introduced in work by Rudolf Halin and developed extensively by Neil Robertson and Paul Seymour in their graph minors project. Intuitively, a tree decomposition represents the vertices of a graph as subtrees of a tree, so that adjacent vertices in the graph appear together in some subtree.

Definition

Formally, a tree decomposition of a graph G consists of a tree T together with a collection of subsets of vertices of G (called bags), one for each node of T, such that: every vertex of G appears in at least one bag; for every edge of G, some bag contains both of its endpoints; and for each vertex, the nodes of T whose bags contain it form a connected subtree. The width of a decomposition is the size of its largest bag minus one, and the treewidth of G is the minimum width over all its tree decompositions.

Applications

Many NP-hard problems, such as Hamiltonian path and graph coloring, become tractable — solvable in linear time for fixed treewidth — by dynamic programming over a tree decomposition when the treewidth is small. Tree decompositions also underpin the Robertson–Seymour theorem and have close connections to chordal graphs, junction trees in graphical models, and the rank-width and tree-depth parameters.
