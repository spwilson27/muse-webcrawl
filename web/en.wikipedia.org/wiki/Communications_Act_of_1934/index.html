The Communications Act of 1934 is a United States federal law signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 19, 1934, which replaced the Federal Radio Commission with the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). It also transferred the responsibility for regulating telephone services from the Interstate Commerce Commission to the new FCC.

Provisions

The Act is divided into titles covering general provisions, common carriers, provisions relating to radio, procedural and administrative provisions, penal provisions, and cable communications. It charges the FCC with regulating interstate and foreign commerce in communication by wire and radio "so as to make available, so far as possible, to all the people of the United States a rapid, efficient, nationwide, and worldwide wire and radio communication service with adequate facilities at reasonable charges."

Section 230 of the Act, added by the Telecommunications Act of 1996, provides immunity from liability for providers and users of an "interactive computer service" for third-party content — a provision of central importance to the modern internet.

Legacy

The Communications Act remains the foundational statute of United States communications law, though substantially amended, most comprehensively by the Telecommunications Act of 1996.
