Neal Koblitz

Neal I. Koblitz (born December 24, 1948) is an American mathematician and professor at the University of Washington. He is best known as one of the two independent inventors of elliptic curve cryptography (ECC), proposed in 1985 alongside Victor S. Miller's independent work, and for his textbooks on number theory, p-adic analysis, and cryptography. He also created the Kovalevskaya Prize to support women mathematicians in developing countries.

Education and career

Koblitz studied at Harvard University, where he was a Putnam Fellow in 1967, and received his PhD in 1974 under the supervision of Nick Katz. He has been a professor of mathematics at the University of Washington since the late 1970s. As a student he spent time in the Soviet Union, learned Russian, and developed a lasting interest in the history and politics of science; he later wrote critically on the misuse of mathematics and on intelligence-agency influence in cryptography standards.

Contributions

In his 1987 paper "Elliptic curve cryptosystems" (Mathematics of Computation), following his 1985 proposal, Koblitz showed how the group of points on an elliptic curve over a finite field could serve as the basis for public-key cryptosystems analogous to Diffie–Hellman and ElGamal, with much shorter keys than RSA for equivalent security. He subsequently developed hyperelliptic curve cryptography. His textbooks include p-adic Numbers, p-adic Analysis, and Zeta-Functions (1977), A Course in Number Theory and Cryptography (1987), and Algebraic Aspects of Cryptography (1998). In 2007, Koblitz co-authored "Another look at 'provable security'" and wrote a widely discussed critique of the level of rigour in parts of theoretical cryptography.

Kovalevskaya Prize

With his wife, historian Ann Hibner Koblitz, he established the Kovalevskaya Prize, named for Sofia Kovalevskaya, which is awarded to women scientists in developing countries; Ann Koblitz's biography of Kovalevskaya helped revive interest in her life and work.
