Common Sense is a 47-page pamphlet written by Thomas Paine in 1775–1776 advocating independence for the Thirteen Colonies from Great Britain to people in America. Published anonymously on 10 January 1776 in Philadelphia, it became an immediate sensation: it remains, in proportion to population, among the best-selling and most widely circulated books in American history, and it is credited with crystallising public opinion in favour of independence months before the Declaration of Independence.

Content

Paine wrote in plain, forceful language accessible to ordinary readers, drawing on biblical references and Enlightenment ideas. The pamphlet is organised into four sections: "Of the Origin and Design of Government in General", "Of Monarchy and Hereditary Succession", "Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs", and "Of the Present Ability of America".

"These are the times that try men's souls." — though this famous line is from Paine's later work The American Crisis, it captures the rhetoric that made Common Sense famous.

Paine attacked monarchy and hereditary succession as absurd and corrupt, argued that an island could not naturally govern a continent, and contended that reconciliation with Britain — which had responded to petitions with violence — would only prolong suffering. He presented independence as both inevitable and an opportunity to build a republican government founded on natural rights.

Publication and impact

Printer Robert Bell published the first edition; after a dispute, Paine expanded the text and had it reprinted by William and Thomas Bradford. Paine donated his royalties to buy mittens for Continental troops. George Washington praised its "sound doctrine and unanswerable reasoning". Loyalists responded with pamphlets such as James Chalmers's Plain Truth, but Paine's arguments helped shift colonial sentiment decisively toward separation in the spring of 1776.
