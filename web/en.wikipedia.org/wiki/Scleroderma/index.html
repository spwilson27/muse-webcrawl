Scleroderma


Specialty: Rheumatology
Type: Autoimmune disease group
Symptoms: Skin hardening and tightening; internal organ fibrosis (in systemic forms)

Scleroderma is a group of autoimmune diseases that may result in changes to the skin, blood vessels, muscles, and internal organs. The disease can be localized to the skin or involve other organs as well. The name derives from the Greek skleros ("hard") and derma ("skin"), describing the hallmark hardening and tightening of the skin caused by excess collagen deposition and fibrosis.

Types

Localized scleroderma, including morphea and linear scleroderma, is limited to the skin and underlying tissues and is more common in children. Systemic scleroderma, or systemic sclerosis, affects internal organs as well. Its two main subtypes are limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis (long associated with the former acronym CREST syndrome: calcinosis, Raynaud's phenomenon, esophageal dysfunction, sclerodactyly, and telangiectasia) and diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis, which progresses more rapidly and may involve the lungs, kidneys, heart, and gastrointestinal tract.

Cause and treatment

The cause is unknown, though genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers are implicated; it is more common in women than in men. There is no cure, and treatment is directed at symptoms and complications — for example immunosuppressants for interstitial lung disease, vasodilators for Raynaud's, and ACE inhibitors, which transformed the prognosis of scleroderma renal crisis. Prognosis varies widely by subtype and organ involvement.
