Hugo Marie de Vries (Dutch: [ˈɦyɣoː də ˈvris]; 16 February 1848 – 21 May 1935) was a Dutch botanist and one of the first geneticists. He is known chiefly for suggesting the concept of genes, for rediscovering the laws of heredity in the 1890s while apparently unaware of Gregor Mendel's work, and for his theory of evolution by sudden mutation.

Rediscovery of Mendel

Working with the evening primrose (Oenothera lamarckiana) and other plants, de Vries arrived at the same segregation ratios Mendel had reported in 1866; in 1900 he published his results, and in the same year Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak independently "rediscovered" Mendel's paper as well. The episode launched the science of genetics. De Vries initially omitted Mendel's priority in his French paper, acknowledging it after Correns's intervention.

Mutation theory

In Die Mutationstheorie (1901–03), de Vries argued that new species arise by sudden, large mutations rather than by Darwin's gradual selection of small variations. His primrose "mutations" turned out later to be mostly chromosomal rearrangements and polyploidy — Oenothera is cytologically peculiar — but the theory introduced the word mutation into biology and drove decades of experimental work. De Vries also proposed hereditary units he called "pangenes" (1889), a term later shortened to "gene" by Wilhelm Johannsen.

Career

De Vries became professor of botany at the University of Amsterdam in 1878, where he also did early plant-physiology work on plasmolysis and osmotic pressure, and directed the Amsterdam botanical garden, developing it into a major research institution. He retired to Lunteren, remaining scientifically active until his death.
