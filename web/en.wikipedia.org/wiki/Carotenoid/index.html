Carotenoids are yellow, orange and red organic pigments produced by plants, algae and photosynthetic bacteria, as well as some non-photosynthetic bacteria and fungi. More than 1,100 carotenoids are known. They give the characteristic colour to carrots, pumpkins, sweet potatoes, tomatoes and autumn leaves, and are responsible for the pink colour of flamingos and salmon, which obtain them from their diet. Animals cannot synthesise carotenoids and must ingest them.

Structure and classes

Carotenoids are tetraterpenoids built from eight isoprene units, giving a 40-carbon skeleton with a system of conjugated double bonds that absorbs blue and green light. They divide into the pure hydrocarbons called carotenes (such as β-carotene and lycopene) and their oxygenated derivatives, the xanthophylls (such as lutein and zeaxanthin).

Functions

In photosynthesis carotenoids act as accessory pigments, extending the range of light harvested, and protect the photosynthetic apparatus by quenching excess energy and scavenging free radicals. In humans, several carotenoids — above all β-carotene — are converted to vitamin A (retinol). Lutein and zeaxanthin accumulate in the retina and are associated with eye health. Carotenoids are widely used as food colourings (annatto, paprika extract) and dietary supplements, and act as antioxidants.
