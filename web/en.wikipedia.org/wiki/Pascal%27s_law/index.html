Pascal's law

Pascal's law (also Pascal's principle) states that a pressure change occurring anywhere in a confined incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished throughout the fluid, so that the same change occurs everywhere. The principle was established by French mathematician and physicist Blaise Pascal in 1653 and published posthumously in his treatise on the equilibrium of liquids.

Applications

The law is the working principle of the hydraulic press and hydraulic brakes, lifts and jacks: a small force applied to a small piston creates pressure that acts on a larger piston to produce a proportionally larger force, multiplying the input force by the ratio of the piston areas.

Pascal's barrel

Pascal illustrated the principle with the "barrel experiment": a long thin vertical tube attached to a sealed barrel of water. Filling the tube raised the water level only modestly in length terms, but the resulting hydrostatic pressure burst the barrel — demonstrating that pressure depends on the height of the fluid column, not its total volume.

Hydrostatic pressure

In a fluid at rest under gravity, pressure increases with depth according to Δp = ρgΔh; combined with Pascal's law this explains the behaviour of communicating vessels and manometers.
