Fascia

A fascia (plural fasciae or fascias) is a generic term for macroscopic membranes of dense irregular connective tissue that interpenetrate and surround the body's organs, muscles, bones, nerves, and blood vessels, forming a continuous three-dimensional structural network. Fasciae have varying compositions and can include sheets, bands, cords, or capsules.

Classification

Fascia is commonly classified into superficial fascia, lying just beneath the skin and containing fat and cutaneous vessels and nerves; deep fascia, dense fibrous sheets investing muscles and forming compartments; and visceral (parietal) fascia, suspending organs within their cavities. The plantar fascia of the foot and the thoracolumbar fascia of the back are well-known examples.

Function

Fascia provides structural support and protection, separates and connects muscles and organs, transmits mechanical forces generated by muscular activity, and serves as a matrix for nerves and blood vessels. Fascia contains sensory nerve endings, including proprioceptors and nociceptors, and has become a focus of research in musculoskeletal pain and manual therapy.

Clinical significance

Fascial disorders include plantar fasciitis, a common cause of heel pain; necrotizing fasciitis, a rapidly progressing bacterial infection; and compartment syndrome, in which pressure rises within an inelastic fascial compartment. Surgical release of fascia (fasciotomy) is used to treat compartment syndrome.
