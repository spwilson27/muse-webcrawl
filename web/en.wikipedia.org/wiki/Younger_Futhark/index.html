Younger Futhark

Script type: Alphabet (runic)
Period: c. 800–1100 AD (Viking Age)
Languages: Old Norse
Characters: 16 runes
Preceded by: Elder Futhark (24 runes)

The Younger Futhark, also called Scandinavian runes, is a runic alphabet that developed from the Elder Futhark during the 8th century and was the principal writing system of Viking Age Scandinavia. Paradoxically, as the Old Norse language was gaining sounds, the alphabet shrank from 24 characters to only 16, so that most runes had to represent several different phonemes — a simplification whose causes remain debated among runologists.

The sixteen runes

The row runs: fé (f/v), úr (u/o/ö/v), þurs (þ/ð), ą́ss (ą/o), reið (r), kaun (k/g), hagall (h), nauðr (n), íss (i/e), ár (a), sól (s), týr (t/d), bjarkan (b/p), maðr (m), lögr (l), and ýr (ʀ, later r). Two regional variants emerged: the Danish "long-branch" runes and the Swedish-Norwegian "short-twig" runes, the latter a kind of shorthand that appears widely on Swedish runestones.

Use and inscriptions

Thousands of inscriptions survive, above all on the great commemorative runestones of 10th- and 11th-century Sweden, recording deaths, inheritance, bridge-building, and Christian devotion, as well as everyday messages carved on wood and bone from trading towns such as Hedeby and Bergen. Icelandic medieval manuscripts preserve rune poems explaining each character's name and meaning.

Successors

With the coming of Christianity and Latin literacy, the Younger Futhark was expanded with dotted (stung) runes in the medieval runic alphabet to distinguish sounds such as e, g, and d, and runes continued in everyday Scandinavian use — calendars, messages, and ownership marks — for centuries after the Viking Age ended.
