Politics of France

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The politics of France take place within the framework of the semi-presidential Constitution of the Fifth French Republic (1958): a directly elected president with substantial powers and a prime minister and government responsible to the National Assembly, elected by two-round voting in single-member constituencies (which traditionally reinforced bipolar blocs of left and right). France is a unitary, decentralised state (regions, departments, communes) with an assertive administrative-law tradition (the Conseil d'État) and a Constitutional Council that has grown into a rights court.

The post-war party system (Gaullists, socialists, communists, centrists) was disrupted in 2017 when Emmanuel Macron's centrist movement won the presidency and assembly, dissolving the old alternation; the far-right National Rally (formerly National Front) grew into one of the largest forces, and the 2024 snap election produced a hung assembly and minority governments — a standing demonstration of the system's strains under cohabitation-like conditions without formal cohabitation. French politics retains distinctive institutions and habits: the cumul des mandats (restricted since 2014/17), the elite grands corps, the strong presidency (“republican monarchy” in critics' phrase), and a protest repertoire — general strikes, the gilets jaunes (2018–19) — that treats the street as a parallel legislature.
