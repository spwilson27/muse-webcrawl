Trachea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The trachea, commonly called the windpipe, is the airway tube that connects the larynx to the two main bronchi of the lungs. About 10–12 cm long and 2 cm wide in adults, it is held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage that are incomplete at the back, where it touches the Esophagus.

Structure and function

The trachea is lined with mucus-producing cells and ciliated epithelium — the mucociliary escalator — which sweeps trapped dust and microbes up to the throat to be swallowed or coughed out. At its lower end it divides at the carina into the right and left main bronchi, one to each lung.

Clinical significance

A blocked trachea is immediately life-threatening; the Heimlich manoeuvre and emergency tracheotomy address obstruction. In intensive care, breathing tubes are placed through the mouth into the trachea (intubation) or through a surgical opening (tracheostomy). Inflammation of the trachea (tracheitis) and, in children, swelling around the upper airway (croup) can dangerously narrow it.
