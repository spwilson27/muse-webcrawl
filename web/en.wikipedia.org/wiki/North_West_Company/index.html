The North West Company was a fur-trading business headquartered in Montreal from 1779 to 1821. It fiercely competed with the London-based Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) for the fur trade of western Canada, and at its height controlled trade across a territory stretching from the Great Lakes to the Arctic and the Pacific.

Formation and structure

After the British conquest of New France, independent Montreal merchants — many of them Scots — pooled resources to trade into the northwest. The agreements of 1779 and 1783–84 united firms such as those of Simon McTavish and the Frobisher brothers. The company was a partnership, not a chartered corporation: Montreal agents supplied goods and sold furs, while "wintering partners" lived year-round at interior posts — an incentive structure that made its men famously aggressive and mobile compared with the salaried servants of the HBC.

Operations and exploration

Each summer, brigades of voyageurs paddled canoes from Montreal and from the depot at Grand Portage (later Fort William, Ontario) to the rendezvous, exchanging goods for furs gathered at posts across the pays d'en haut. Company explorers mapped much of western Canada: Alexander Mackenzie reached the Arctic Ocean in 1789 and the Pacific in 1793 (the first recorded transcontinental crossing north of Mexico); Simon Fraser descended the Fraser River in 1808; and David Thompson surveyed the Columbia River system and the Canada–U.S. border.

Rivalry and merger

Competition with the HBC turned violent in the Red River Colony, culminating in the Battle of Seven Oaks (1816), where Métis under Cuthbert Grant killed Governor Robert Semple and 20 settlers. Exhausted by the rivalry and pressed by the British government, the partners agreed in 1821 to merge into the Hudson's Bay Company, which kept its own name and charter. Many Nor'Westers, and much of the company's Métis workforce, shaped the communities that later clashed with Canada in the North-West Rebellion of 1885.

Legacy

The North West Company is remembered for opening the continental interior, for its francophone voyageur culture, and as a formative institution of Canada. The modern retail firm The North West Company, operator of Northern stores in northern Canada, traces its lineage to the fur trade through the HBC's northern stores division.
