Social cognitive theory

Triadic reciprocal determinism: behaviour, personal factors and environment
Key figureAlbert Bandura
FieldPsychology, education, health behaviour
Key conceptsSelf-efficacy, observational learning, reciprocal determinism, outcome expectations
Seminal workSocial Foundations of Thought and Action (1986)

Social cognitive theory (SCT) is a theory of learning and social behaviour developed by the Canadian-American psychologist Albert Bandura. It proposes that people learn by observing others within social contexts, and that behaviour, personal factors (such as beliefs and expectations) and the environment influence one another reciprocally — a model Bandura called triadic reciprocal determinism. The theory grew out of Bandura's earlier social learning theory but placed much greater emphasis on cognition.

Key concepts

Observational learning (modelling). People acquire new behaviours by watching models and the consequences those models experience, without having to perform the behaviour themselves. Bandura's famous Bobo doll experiments (1961–63) showed that children imitated aggressive behaviour they had seen an adult perform, especially when the adult was rewarded.

Self-efficacy. The centrepiece of the theory: a person's belief in their capability to organise and execute the actions required to manage prospective situations. Self-efficacy is built from four sources — mastery experiences, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological and emotional states — and strongly predicts whether people attempt challenging tasks and persist at them.

Outcome expectations and incentives. People are more likely to perform a behaviour when they expect it to produce valued outcomes. Behaviour is also regulated by self-reinforcement and self-set standards, giving individuals agency rather than making them passive products of their environment.

Applications

Social cognitive theory is one of the most widely used frameworks in health promotion, informing interventions on smoking cessation, exercise, diet and disease self-management, typically by targeting self-efficacy and observational learning through peer models. It is also influential in education (modelling, mastery learning), media psychology, organisational behaviour and the design of behaviour-change programmes.
