Qawwali (Urdu: قوّالی) is a form of Sufi Islamic devotional singing that originated in the Indian subcontinent. Performed by a party of musicians (a qawwal party, or humnava) led by one or two lead singers, qawwali sets mystical and devotional poetry — the praise of God, the Prophet Muhammad, Ali, and Sufi saints, and verses of divine love — to powerful, rhythmically intensifying music. Its purpose within Sufism is sama, spiritual listening intended to induce ecstasy and closeness to God. From its shrine setting, qawwali became one of South Asia's most popular concert and recording genres.

History

Qawwali developed in the Chishti Sufi order's hospices around Delhi in the late 13th century. Tradition credits Amir Khusrau (1253–1325), poet, musician and disciple of Nizamuddin Auliya, with fusing Persian, Arabic, Turkic and Indian musical elements into forms ancestral to qawwali (and also to khyal and tarana). Hereditary qawwal families attached to shrines such as Nizamuddin Dargah in Delhi and Ajmer Sharif preserved the repertoire — hamd, naat, manqabat, ghazal and kafi — across centuries.

Performance

A party typically seats a lead singer (or two), chorus singers who repeat lines and clap the rhythm, a harmonium (a 19th-century addition that displaced the sarangi), tabla and dholak. A performance builds slowly: after an instrumental prelude and devotional invocations, the main song unfolds through repetition and improvisation of key verses, accelerating in tempo and intensity — sometimes for well over fifteen minutes on record and much longer at shrines — as singers respond to the audience's spiritual state and donations. The aesthetic aim is wajd, ecstatic transport.

Modern popularity

Recording, film and world-music circuits made qawwali global. Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (1948–1997), from a centuries-old qawwal family of Faisalabad, became its international emblem — collaborating with Peter Gabriel and Massive Attack and filling concert halls worldwide — while the Sabri Brothers, Aziz Mian and Abida Parveen commanded mass audiences in Pakistan and the diaspora. Purists sometimes distinguish shrine qawwali from its concert and pop derivatives, but the form's intensity and its poetry of longing remain continuous across settings.
