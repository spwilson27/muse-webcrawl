The Temple of Hephaestus, or Hephaisteion, is a remarkably well-preserved ancient Greek temple on the north-west side of the Agora of Athens, atop the Kolonos Agoraios hill. A Doric peripteral temple with six columns by thirteen, it was dedicated to Hephaestus, god of fire and metalworking, and to Athena in her aspect as patroness of crafts.

History

Construction began around 449 BC, in the building programme of Periclean Athens, though work proceeded slowly and the temple was completed only decades later. The surrounding district housed metalworkers' and potters' workshops, fitting its patron deities. Its metopes and Ionic frieze depict labours of Heracles and of Theseus — which long led antiquarians to call it the "Theseion" and suppose it housed the hero's bones. The temple owes its exceptional preservation to its conversion into a Christian church, dedicated to St George, probably in the seventh century AD; it served as a Greek Orthodox church until the 1830s, when it was briefly used as a museum.

Architecture

Built of Pentelic marble, the temple measures about 13.7 by 31.8 metres. Unusually, sculptural decoration covered the eastern metopes, parts of the northern and southern ones, and the pronaos friezes showing the battle of Theseus against the Pallantidae and a Centauromachy. A garden with pomegranate and myrtle plantings, attested by planting pits around the temple, has been re-created in modern times.
