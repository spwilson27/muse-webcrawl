Constitutio Antoniniana

[Hero image placeholder: Giessen papyrus fragment preserving the edict's text]

The Constitutio Antoniniana (Latin for "Constitution [or Edict] of Antoninus"), also called the Edict of Caracalla or the Antonine Constitution, was an edict issued in AD 212 by the Roman emperor Caracalla. It declared that all free men in the Roman Empire were to be given full Roman citizenship and all free women the same rights as Roman women, with the exception of the dediticii, people who had become subject to Rome through surrender in war, and freed slaves.

Background and motives

Before the edict, Roman citizenship had spread progressively through grants to individuals, communities and whole provinces, but large numbers of free inhabitants remained peregrini (non-citizen subjects). The historian Cassius Dio suggested that Caracalla's motive was fiscal: extending citizenship made more people liable to inheritance and manumission taxes from which citizens alone paid. Others emphasise administrative simplification and the emperor's desire for a unifying act of piety toward the gods on behalf of all his subjects.

Text

The edict survives in a Greek translation on a papyrus held at the University of Giessen (Papyrus Gissensis 40). In it, Caracalla grants citizenship to "all the inhabitants of the world" while preserving the rights of existing communities, excluding the dediticii.

Consequences

The edict effectively ended the legal distinction between Italians and provincials, and between citizens and subjects, within the free population. In practice, however, a new social divide between the honestiores (upper classes) and humiliores (lower classes) sharpened in criminal law. After 212, millions of new citizens took the emperor's nomen Aurelius, which floods the epigraphic record of the third century.
