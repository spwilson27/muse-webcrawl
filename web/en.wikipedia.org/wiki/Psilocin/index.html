Psilocin

Psilocin (also known as 4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, 4-HO-DMT, or psilocine) is a substituted tryptamine alkaloid and a classic psychedelic drug. It is the pharmacologically active metabolite of psilocybin and is present, together with psilocybin, in most psilocybin ("magic") mushrooms. Psilocin was isolated and synthesised by Albert Hofmann in 1958, working from Psilocybe mexicana specimens.

Chemistry and pharmacology

Psilocybin ingested orally is rapidly dephosphorylated to psilocin, which acts as a partial agonist at serotonin receptors, principally the 5-HT2A receptor — the mechanism thought to underlie its psychedelic effects. Psilocin is structurally very similar to serotonin itself and to the psychedelic DMT. Unlike psilocybin it is chemically unstable, oxidising on exposure to air and light (responsible for the blue bruising of some mushrooms).

Effects

Effects begin within about 20–40 minutes of ingestion and last roughly 3–6 hours. They include altered perception, visual patterns, euphoria, changes in the sense of time, and, at higher doses, profound alterations of self-experience; anxiety and panic can also occur. Psilocin has low physiological toxicity and is not considered addictive. Typical doses are in the range of 10–50 mg of psilocybin equivalent content in dried mushrooms varies widely.

Legal status and research

Psilocin and psilocybin are listed in Schedule I of the UN 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances and are controlled in most countries, though some jurisdictions have decriminalised possession of psilocybin mushrooms. Since the 2000s, clinical research has investigated psilocybin (acting through psilocin) as a treatment for depression, end-of-life anxiety, and addiction, with promising trial results and breakthrough-therapy designations from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration.
