Sleep deprivation

Sleep deprivation, also known as sleep insufficiency or sleeplessness, is the condition of not having adequate duration and/or quality of sleep to support decent alertness, performance, and health. It can be either chronic or acute and may vary widely in severity. A chronic sleepless state can cause fatigue, daytime sleepiness, clumsiness, and weight loss or weight gain. It adversely affects the brain and cognitive function; in serious cases prolonged total sleep deprivation in laboratory animals is fatal.

Effects

Even modest sleep restriction impairs attention, working memory and decision-making. After 24 hours without sleep, cognitive performance is often compared to that of a person with a blood alcohol concentration of about 0.1%. Microsleeps — involuntary seconds-long lapses into sleep — make sleep deprivation a major factor in transport and industrial accidents. Longer-term partial deprivation is associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, weakened immune function and mood disorders including depression and anxiety.

Causes

Causes include sleep disorders such as insomnia and sleep apnoea, work demands and shift work, medical and psychiatric conditions, and lifestyle factors. Sleep deprivation is also used deliberately, historically as a form of torture and interrogation, and clinically in "wake therapy" for depression.

Studies

In 1959 Peter Tripp stayed awake 201 hours for charity, and in 1964 Randy Gardner set a widely publicised record of 264 hours (11 days) without sleep, monitored by sleep researcher William Dement. Guinness World Records later stopped accepting attempts on health grounds.
