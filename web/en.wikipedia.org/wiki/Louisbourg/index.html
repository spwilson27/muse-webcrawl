Louisbourg is an unincorporated community and former town in Nova Scotia, Canada. It is situated on the south-eastern coast of Cape Breton Island, within the Cape Breton Regional Municipality. The community is best known as the site of the Fortress of Louisbourg, one of the largest historical reconstructions in North America.

History

Founded by the French in 1713 after the Treaty of Utrecht ceded Acadia and Newfoundland to Britain, Louisbourg was named for King Louis XIV. It became a major fishing port — its cod exports dried for the European market were a mainstay of the French Atlantic economy — and a powerful fortress guarding the approaches to the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the St. Lawrence River.

The fortress was besieged and captured twice. In 1745, during King George's War, a New England colonial force under William Pepperrell, supported by a Royal Navy squadron, took Louisbourg in the first siege. It was returned to France by the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748, to colonial outrage. In 1758, during the Seven Years' War, British forces under Jeffery Amherst and James Wolfe captured it again in the second siege, and its fortifications were systematically demolished in 1760 so they could never again threaten British control of the region. The fall of Louisbourg opened the way to the British attack on Quebec the following year.

The fortress today

Beginning in 1961, the Government of Canada undertook a partial reconstruction of the town and fortress — about one quarter of the original — on its original foundations, employing local workers from a community hit by the decline of coal mining and fishing. The Fortress of Louisbourg National Historic Site is now staffed by costumed interpreters portraying 18th-century soldiers, merchants and townspeople, and is one of Nova Scotia's leading tourist attractions. The modern community of Louisbourg, a working fishing port, lies adjacent to the site.
