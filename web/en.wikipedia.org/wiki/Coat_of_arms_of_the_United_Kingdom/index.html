Coat of arms of the United Kingdom

The coat of arms of the United Kingdom, more precisely the royal coat of arms of the United Kingdom, is the arms of dominion of the British monarch and, by extension, the official arms of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland. Variants of the arms are used by other members of the royal family, by the British government, and by courts and officials throughout the United Kingdom and the Commonwealth realms. The arms in their present form date from the accession of Queen Victoria in 1837.

Description

The shield is quartered. The first and fourth quarters bear the arms of England — three gold lions (leopards) passant guardant on a red field. The second quarter bears the arms of Scotland — a red lion rampant within a double tressure flory-counter-flory on gold. The third quarter bears the arms of Ireland — a gold harp with silver strings on blue. Around the shield is the garter of the Order of the Garter, inscribed with the motto Honi soit qui mal y pense ("Shame on him who thinks evil of it").

The shield is supported by a crowned golden lion on the dexter side, representing England, and a silver unicorn on the sinister side, representing Scotland; the unicorn is chained, as medieval legend held the free unicorn to be a dangerous beast. Above the shield is the royal helm and crest — a crowned lion standing on the Crown. Below is a compartment of the national floral emblems (the Tudor rose, the thistle, and the shamrock) and a scroll bearing the sovereign's motto Dieu et mon droit ("God and my right"), used by English kings since Richard I asserted his claim to the French throne.

Scottish version

In Scotland a distinct version is used, in which the Scottish quarter takes precedence (first and fourth quarters), the supporters swap places with the unicorn on the dexter side, both supporters stand higher, and the motto of the Order of the Thistle, Nemo me impune lacessit ("No one provokes me with impunity"), appears. This Scottish form is used by the monarch when in Scotland and by the Scotland Office and Scottish courts.

History

The arms evolved from the arms of the kings of England, which from 1198 featured the three lions of Richard I. Successive monarchs quartered them with the arms of France (dropped in 1801), Scotland (after the Union of the Crowns in 1603), and Ireland (after 1801). The Hanoverian arms occupied an inescutcheon from 1714 until 1837, when Victoria's accession — she could not inherit Hanover under Salic law — removed them, fixing the design used ever since.

Use

The royal arms appear on British passports, government buildings, Acts of Parliament, courtrooms, military colours, and coinage. The government uses a version without the helm and crest in many contexts. All judges in the United Kingdom sit under the royal arms, symbolising that justice flows from the Crown.
