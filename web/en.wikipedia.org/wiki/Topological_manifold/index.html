Topological manifold

In topology, a branch of mathematics, a topological manifold is a topological space that locally resembles real n-dimensional Euclidean space. Topological manifolds are an important class of topological spaces, with applications throughout geometry and physics. The other concepts of manifold — differentiable manifolds, smooth manifolds, and so on — are specializations built on top of the topological manifold concept.

Formal definition

A topological space X is called locally Euclidean if there is a non-negative integer n such that every point in X has a neighbourhood that is homeomorphic to real n-space Rn.

A topological manifold is a locally Euclidean Hausdorff space. It is common to place additional requirements on topological manifolds; in particular, many authors define them to be paracompact or second-countable. In this article the word "manifold" means topological manifold unless otherwise stated.

Every manifold has a topological dimension, the value of n for which it is locally Euclidean. Connected manifolds are locally Euclidean of a unique dimension; non-connected manifolds may have components of differing dimensions.

Charts and atlases

A chart on a topological space X is a pair (U, φ) where U is an open subset of X and φ : U → V is a homeomorphism onto an open subset V of Rn. A collection of charts covering X is called an atlas.

Examples

  • Real n-dimensional Euclidean space Rn is an n-manifold.
  • Any discrete space is a 0-dimensional manifold.
  • A circle is a compact 1-manifold; a torus is a compact 2-manifold.
  • Real and complex projective spaces, Grassmannians and spheres are important families of manifolds.
  • Any open subset of a manifold is itself a manifold of the same dimension.

Properties

Single points in manifolds are closed sets. Manifolds may be connected or disconnected. The product of two manifolds is a manifold, whose dimension is the sum of the dimensions of the factors. Every topological manifold can be embedded in some Euclidean space (a weak form of the Whitney embedding theorem holds in the topological category thanks to the work of various authors).

Classification

In low dimensions, manifolds are well understood: 1-manifolds are disjoint unions of circles and lines/intervals; compact 2-manifolds are classified by genus and orientability. In dimension 3 the Poincaré conjecture, proved by Grigori Perelman, resolves the classification of simply connected closed 3-manifolds. In dimension 4 and higher, a full classification is impossible in general.
