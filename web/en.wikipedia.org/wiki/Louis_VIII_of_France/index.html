Louis VIII (5 September 1187 – 8 November 1226), known as Louis the Lion (Louis le Lion), was King of France from 1223 to 1226. As the son and successor of Philip II (Philip Augustus), he continued his father's expansion of the Capetian royal domain, and as a prince he had intervened in England during the First Barons' War, where he was briefly proclaimed (though never crowned) King of England.

Prince Louis in England

Invited by rebel barons opposed to King John, Louis landed in Kent in May 1216 and was proclaimed king in London, controlling much of eastern England at his peak. John's death in October 1216 undercut support for Louis; after defeats at Lincoln and Sandwich in 1217, he renounced his claim under the Treaty of Lambeth and returned to France with a payment.

Reign

Crowned at Reims in August 1223, Louis revoked his father's concessions regarding the Jews, issued ordinances stabilising coinage and royal administration, and in 1224 seized Poitou and much of the remaining Angevin territory in south-western France. In 1226 he led the royal crusade against the Cathars of Languedoc — the Albigensian Crusade — capturing Avignon after a long siege and receiving the submission of much of the south. On the return journey he died of dysentery at Montpensier in November 1226, leaving the throne to his twelve-year-old son, the future Saint Louis (Louis IX), under the regency of his widow Blanche of Castile.
