Buddhist temples in Japan

Buddhist temples in Japan are, together with Shinto shrines, among the most numerous and important religious buildings in the country. The Japanese word for a Buddhist temple is tera (寺), often suffixed as -ji in temple names (for example Kiyomizu-dera, Tōdai-ji). Buddhism was officially introduced to Japan in the 6th century CE from Baekje, and temples have been central to Japanese art, architecture and culture ever since.

Layout

A typical temple precinct (garan) may include:

  • Sanmon — the main gate;
  • Hondō (main hall) — housing the temple's principal image of worship;
  • Pagoda (tō) — a multi-storeyed tower functioning as a reliquary, deriving from the Indian stupa and Chinese tower;
  • Kōdō — lecture hall;
  • Shōrō — belfry housing the bonshō bell;
  • Kyōzō — sutra repository;
  • Cemetery (hakaba) — most Japanese funerals and ancestral memorial services are Buddhist.

History

The earliest temples, such as Asuka-dera (596) and Hōryū-ji (7th century, containing some of the world's oldest wooden buildings), adopted continental styles from China and Korea. In the Nara period (710–794) the state built a network of provincial temples (kokubunji) and the great Tōdai-ji with its Great Buddha. The Heian period saw the growth of esoteric Tendai and Shingon Buddhism; the Kamakura period brought Zen from China, with its distinctive gardens and architecture. Under the Tokugawa shogunate, the danka system bound every household to a temple. During the early Meiji period, the anti-Buddhist haibutsu kishaku movement destroyed or closed many temples, but Buddhism recovered and today there are over 75,000 temples in Japan.

Notable temples

  • Tōdai-ji, Nara — Great Buddha Hall
  • Kiyomizu-dera, Kyoto — wooden stage temple
  • Sensō-ji, Tokyo — Tokyo's oldest temple
  • Eihei-ji, Fukui — head temple of Sōtō Zen
  • Enryaku-ji, Mount Hiei — head temple of Tendai
  • Kōya-san (Mount Kōya) temple complex, Wakayama
