Freediving
Also known asApnea diving, breath-hold diving
Governing bodiesAIDA International, CMAS
Main disciplinesConstant weight, free immersion, static apnea, dynamic apnea, no limits

Freediving, free-diving, free diving, breath-hold diving, or skin diving, is a mode of underwater diving that relies on breath-holding until resurfacing rather than the use of breathing apparatus such as scuba gear. Besides the physiological limits of breath-hold, freediving is constrained by the pressure changes at depth and the body's response to them.

History

Breath-hold diving is ancient: Japanese ama and Korean haenyeo divers have harvested shellfish and pearls for centuries, and sponge divers worked in the Mediterranean. Competitive freediving developed in the mid-twentieth century, with pioneers such as Enzo Maiorca and Jacques Mayol pushing depth records and popularising the sport — a rivalry dramatised in Luc Besson's 1988 film The Big Blue. Governing bodies AIDA (founded 1992) and CMAS now sanction competitions and ratify records.

Disciplines

  • Static apnea (STA) — holding the breath for time while floating face-down in a pool.
  • Dynamic apnea (DYN/DNF) — swimming horizontally underwater for distance, with or without fins.
  • Constant weight (CWT) — descending and ascending under one's own power, with fins or without, without changing ballast.
  • Free immersion (FIM) — pulling oneself down and up a vertical rope.
  • Variable weight (VWT) and No limits (NLT) — using a weighted sled for descent; no-limits, using a lift bag for ascent, is the deepest and most dangerous discipline and is no longer widely sanctioned. Herbert Nitsch reached 214 metres in a no-limits dive in 2007.

Physiology and risks

Freedivers rely on the mammalian dive response — bradycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction and blood shift — to conserve oxygen. The most serious hazard is hypoxic blackout (often called shallow-water blackout), in which a diver loses consciousness near the surface from falling oxygen partial pressure; hyperventilating before a dive increases the risk by suppressing the urge to breathe. Freediving should never be practised alone, and barotrauma of the ears and sinuses and lung squeeze at depth are additional risks.
