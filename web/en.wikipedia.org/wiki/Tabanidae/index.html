Tabanidae is a family of true flies (order Diptera) commonly known as horse flies, deer flies, and gadflies. The family contains roughly 4,500 described species worldwide, including the genera Tabanus (horse flies), Chrysops (deer flies), and Haematopota (clegs).

Biology

Adult tabanids are stout-bodied flies, often with large, brightly patterned or iridescent eyes; in males the eyes meet on top of the head, while in females they are separated. Females of most species are blood feeders: their blade-like mouthparts slash the skin of mammals (including livestock and humans) and they lap up the pooling blood, producing a painful bite. Males do not bite and feed on nectar and plant juices, as do females when not seeking a blood meal for egg development.

Larvae are typically aquatic or semi-aquatic, living in mud and wet soil near ponds, marshes, and streams, where many are predators of other invertebrates.

Medical and veterinary importance

Tabanids are significant pests of livestock and can mechanically transmit pathogens, including the bacteria causing tularemia and anthrax and the parasitic worm Loa loa in parts of Africa (transmitted by Chrysops species). Their persistent attacks can reduce grazing and weight gain in cattle. They are strong, fast fliers and are considered among the fastest flying insects.
