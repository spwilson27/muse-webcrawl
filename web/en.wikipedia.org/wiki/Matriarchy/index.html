Matriarchy

Women elders in the matrilineal Minangkabau society of West Sumatra.

Matriarchy is a social system in which women hold the primary positions of political leadership, moral authority, social privilege and control of property. The term is often contrasted with patriarchy, and is distinct from related concepts such as matrilineality (descent traced through the female line) and matrilocality (residence with the wife's family), neither of which necessarily implies female rule.

History of the concept

The idea entered modern scholarship through Johann Jakob Bachofen's Das Mutterrecht (1861), which proposed that human societies had passed through an early stage of "mother right" before the advent of patriarchy. Lewis Henry Morgan's work on the matrilineal Iroquois and Friedrich Engels' The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884) further popularised the notion of a primordial matriarchal stage. Twentieth-century anthropologists largely abandoned the theory of a universal matriarchal stage, and whether any society, past or present, qualifies as a matriarchy in the strict sense remains debated.

Matrilineal societies

Several well-documented societies are matrilineal and matrilocal, with substantial female authority in kinship and property, including the Minangkabau of Indonesia — the largest matrilineal society in the world — the Mosuo of southwestern China, the Khasi of Meghalaya, India, and historically the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), whose clan mothers selected and could remove chiefs. Scholars disagree on whether such systems amount to matriarchy or are better described as gender-egalitarian or matrilineal.

In feminist thought

Matriarchy has been a recurring theme in feminist theory and literature, both as a historical hypothesis about prehistory and as an imagined alternative social order, explored in works ranging from anthropological studies to utopian fiction such as Charlotte Perkins Gilman's Herland.
