Brennivín

Type: Aquavit / schnapps

Alcohol by volume: 37.5–40%

Country of origin: Iceland

Brennivín (Icelandic pronunciation: [ˈprɛnːɪˌviːn]) is an Icelandic distilled spirit made from fermented grain or potato mash and flavoured with caraway. It is considered Iceland's signature drink and is a form of akvavit. Its name literally means "burning wine" (i.e., distilled liquor, cognate with "brandy").

History

Brennivín in its modern form was introduced in 1935, when prohibition in Iceland was partially repealed and the state alcohol monopoly (ÁTVR) needed a cheap domestic spirit; the stark black label was designed in part to make the bottle unattractive and discourage consumption. The label, bearing a white skull-like design in early versions, earned the drink the nickname "Black Death" (svarti dauði). After the end of beer prohibition in 1989, Brennivín's role changed, but it remains a fixture of Icelandic tradition.

Consumption

Brennivín is traditionally served ice cold in a shot glass, especially during the midwinter festival of Þorrablót, where it accompanies traditional foods such as hákarl (fermented shark), which it is said to help wash down. It is also consumed at midsummer and other celebrations and is popular with visitors as a souvenir. The drink is produced by Icelandic distillers from grain spirit, water, and caraway seed, and bottled at about 37.5–40% alcohol by volume.

In popular culture

Brennivín has been promoted internationally through Icelandic music and tourism and is frequently cited as a rite-of-passage drink for visitors to Iceland, often paired with hákarl.
