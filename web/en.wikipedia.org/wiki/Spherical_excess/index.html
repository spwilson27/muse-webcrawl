Spherical excess

In spherical geometry, the spherical excess of a spherical triangle is the amount by which the sum of its three interior angles exceeds 180 degrees (π radians). Unlike plane geometry, where the angles of a triangle always sum to exactly π, on a sphere the sum is always greater, and the excess is directly proportional to the area of the triangle.

Definition

For a spherical triangle with interior angles A, B, and C (measured in radians), the spherical excess E is defined as

E = A + B + C − π.

By Girard's theorem (also attributed to Albert Girard, 1629), the area T of the triangle on a sphere of radius R is

T = E · R2.

Thus the spherical excess equals the area of the triangle divided by the square of the sphere's radius; it is sometimes described as the solid angle subtended by the triangle at the centre of the sphere, measured in steradians.

Properties

The spherical excess is always positive for a genuine spherical triangle, and it ranges from 0 (for a triangle so small it is nearly planar) up to nearly 3π in degenerate limiting cases. The result generalises to spherical polygons: the excess of an n-sided spherical polygon is the sum of its interior angles minus (n − 2)π.

Girard's theorem can be proved using lunes: each angle of the triangle defines a lune whose area is proportional to the angle, and the three lunes together cover the sphere with the triangle and its antipodal copy counted three times.

Applications

Spherical excess was historically important in geodesy and surveying: for a large geodetic triangle on the Earth's surface, measuring the excess allowed surveyors to infer the Earth's radius. It also appears in astronomy, navigation, and the theory of polyhedra, where the related concept of angular defect governs the curvature of polyhedral surfaces.
