In projective geometry, Desargues's theorem, named after Girard Desargues, states:

Two triangles are in perspective from a point if and only if they are in perspective from a line.

That is, given triangles ABC and A′B′C′, the lines AA′, BB′ and CC′ are concurrent (meet at a single point) if and only if the intersection points of corresponding sides — BC with B′C′, CA with C′A′, and AB with A′B′ — are collinear, lying on a single line called the axis of perspectivity.

History

Desargues published the theorem in his 1639 work Brouillon project d'une atteinte aux événemens des rencontres du cône avec un plan, a founding text of projective geometry. The theorem was largely forgotten until the 19th century, when projective geometry was revived by Poncelet and others; Desargues's manuscript was rediscovered in 1845.

Proof and status

The theorem is easily proved in three dimensions: if two triangles lie in different planes and are in perspective from a point, the intersections of corresponding sides lie on the line of intersection of the two planes. The planar case then follows by embedding the plane in three-dimensional space. However, the theorem cannot be proved from the axioms of the projective plane alone: there exist non-Desarguesian planes, such as the Moulton plane, in which it fails. A projective plane is Desarguesian if and only if it can be coordinatised by a division ring, a result connected with Hilbert's work on the foundations of geometry.

Configuration

The figure of the theorem — ten points and ten lines, each point on three lines and each line through three points — is the classic Desargues configuration, written 103.
