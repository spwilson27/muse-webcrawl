Cass Gilbert (November 24, 1859 – May 17, 1934) was an American architect. An early proponent of the skyscraper, in a largely Beaux-Arts style, he is best known for the Woolworth Building in New York City (1913), then the tallest building in the world, and for the United States Supreme Court Building in Washington, D.C. (1935).

Career

Gilbert was raised in Saint Paul, Minnesota, studied briefly at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, worked in the New York office of McKim, Mead & White, and then returned to Minnesota. His design for the Minnesota State Capitol (1896–1905), with its self-supporting marble dome modelled on St. Peter's Basilica, made his national reputation. He went on to design the state capitols of West Virginia and Arkansas, the St. Louis Public Library and Saint Louis Art Museum (built for the 1904 Louisiana Purchase Exposition), the Detroit Public Library, and the New York Life Building.

The Woolworth Building

Commissioned by retailer Frank W. Woolworth and paid for in cash, the 57-storey neo-Gothic Woolworth Building was dubbed the "Cathedral of Commerce" on its opening in 1913. Clad in limestone-coloured architectural terracotta, it remained the world's tallest building until 1930 and demonstrated that a skyscraper could be both profitable and a civic monument.

Legacy

Gilbert also completed the United States Supreme Court Building, finished after his death, and the Alexander Hamilton U.S. Custom House in New York. He served as president of the American Institute of Architects and of the National Academy of Design.
