Sogdia

Sogdia (Sogdian: Suγδ; Persian: سغد) or Sogdiana was an ancient East Iranian civilisation that existed between the 6th century BC and the 11th century AD in present-day Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. Its heartland lay in the valleys of the Zeravshan and Kashka Darya rivers, between the Oxus (Amu Darya) and the Jaxartes (Syr Darya).

Geography

Sogdiana was listed as the second of the "good lands and countries" created by Ahura Mazda in the Zoroastrian Vendidad. North of Bactria and separated from it by the Oxus, it formed a chain of fertile oasis city-states rather than a unified empire, each ruled by its own prince under loose Persian, Greek, Kushan or Hephthalite overlordship.

History

Achaemenid and Hellenistic periods

Sogdia was a satrapy of the Achaemenid Empire. Alexander the Great met fierce resistance there in 329–327 BC, notably from Spitamenes, and founded Alexandria Eschate on the Jaxartes. The region later belonged to the Seleucid Empire and the Greco-Bactrian Kingdom.

The Silk Road merchants

From about the 4th century AD Sogdian merchants dominated the overland trade of the Silk Road. Their language became the lingua franca of Central Asian commerce, and Sogdian colonies stretched from Samarkand to Chang'an. The Sogdian Ancient Letters, found near Dunhuang, record this network around 313 AD.

Arab conquest and decline

Muslim armies under Qutayba ibn Muslim conquered the Sogdian cities in the early 8th century. The Battle of Talas (751) consolidated Arab control. Under the Samanids the population gradually adopted New Persian, and the Sogdian language died out except for its descendant Yaghnobi, still spoken in a remote Tajik valley.

Culture

Sogdian art, exemplified by the murals of Penjikent and Afrasiab, blended Iranian, Indian, Chinese and local motifs. Sogdians transmitted religions eastward: many early translators of Buddhist texts into Chinese were Sogdians, and Manichaean and Nestorian communities flourished in the oases.
