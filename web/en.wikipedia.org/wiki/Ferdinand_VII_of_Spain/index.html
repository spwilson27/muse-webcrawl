Ferdinand VII (14 October 1784 – 29 September 1833) was King of Spain in 1808 and again from 1813 until his death. His reign was dominated by the Peninsular War, the loss of Spain's mainland American colonies, and his oscillation between absolutism and reluctant constitutionalism; Spanish liberals remembered him as "the Felon King", while his supporters had hailed him as el Deseado, "the Desired One".

Early life and first reign

The eldest surviving son of Charles IV, Ferdinand intrigued against his father's favourite Manuel Godoy. After the Tumult of Aranjuez (1808) Charles abdicated in his favour, but Napoleon intervened: at Bayonne both Bourbons were forced to surrender the crown, and Joseph Bonaparte was installed as king. Ferdinand spent the Peninsular War as a prisoner at Valençay.

Restoration and the Liberal Triennium

Restored in 1813–14, Ferdinand annulled the liberal Constitution of 1812 and ruled as an absolute monarch, persecuting liberals. In 1820 Rafael del Riego's military pronunciamiento forced him to swear to the constitution, beginning the Liberal Triennium (1820–1823). In 1823 a French army — the "Hundred Thousand Sons of Saint Louis", authorised by the Congress of Verona — restored his absolute power, and a decade of reaction (the "Ominous Decade") followed.

American independence and succession

During his reign Spain lost nearly all of its American empire as the Spanish American wars of independence created new republics; by 1825 only Cuba and Puerto Rico remained in the Americas. In 1830 Ferdinand issued the Pragmatic Sanction, setting aside the Salic law so that his daughter Isabella could succeed him. His brother Don Carlos refused to accept this, and Ferdinand's death in 1833 triggered the First Carlist War.
