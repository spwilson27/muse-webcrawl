In the oldest texts of Buddhism, jhāna (Pali: झान) or dhyāna (Sanskrit: ध्यान) is the training of the mind, commonly translated as meditation, to withdraw the mind from the automatic responses to sense-impressions and sustain a state of lucid, absorbed awareness. In the Buddhist tradition, jhāna is central to the practice of right concentration (sammā-samādhi), the eighth limb of the Noble Eightfold Path.

The four jhānas

The canonical texts describe a progression of four form (rūpa) jhānas:

  1. First jhāna — secluded from sensual pleasures and unwholesome states, the meditator enters absorption accompanied by applied and sustained thought (vitakka and vicāra), with rapture (pīti) and pleasure (sukha) born of seclusion.
  2. Second jhāna — with the stilling of thought, inner tranquillity and unification of mind arise, with rapture and pleasure born of concentration.
  3. Third jhāna — rapture fades; the meditator dwells equanimous, mindful, and clearly aware, experiencing pleasure with the body.
  4. Fourth jhāna — having abandoned pleasure and pain, the meditator abides in purity of mindfulness and equanimity, with neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling.

The formless attainments

Beyond the four jhānas lie four formless (arūpa) attainments: the base of boundless space, the base of boundless consciousness, the base of nothingness, and the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. Together with the four jhānas these form the eight meditative absorptions.

Interpretation

Scholars and modern teachers debate the depth of absorption implied by the texts: commentarial tradition (notably the Visuddhimagga) describes deep absorption in which sense perception ceases entirely, while some modern interpreters argue for a lighter, embodied understanding of jhāna in the earliest suttas.
