Shannon limit

The Shannon limit or Shannon capacity of a communication channel is the theoretical maximum rate at which information can be transmitted over the channel with an arbitrarily low probability of error. It is a central result of information theory, established by Claude Shannon in his landmark 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication".

Statement

For a channel of bandwidth B hertz subject to additive white Gaussian noise, the capacity C in bits per second is given by the Shannon–Hartley theorem:

C = B log2(1 + S/N)

where S is the average signal power and N is the average noise power. The ratio S/N is the signal-to-noise ratio. No coding scheme, however sophisticated, can transmit reliably above this rate; below it, codes exist that achieve arbitrarily small error probabilities as the code length grows.

Implications

The Shannon limit sets an absolute benchmark against which practical modulation and error-correcting codes are judged. Early systems operated far from the limit, but the development of turbo codes (1993), low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes and polar codes brought practical performance within a fraction of a decibel of the bound. The limit also connects energy efficiency to bandwidth: as bandwidth grows without bound, the minimum energy per bit approaches −1.59 dB of Eb/N0, the ultimate Shannon limit.
