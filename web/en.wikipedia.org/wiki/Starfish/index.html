Starfish, or sea stars, are marine invertebrates of the class Asteroidea within the phylum Echinodermata, which also includes sea urchins, brittle stars, and sea cucumbers. About 1,900 species are known, living on seabeds throughout the world's oceans, from tropical reefs to polar waters and from the intertidal zone to abyssal depths.

Anatomy

Most starfish have a central disc and five arms (rays), though some species have many more — the sunflower sea star can have over twenty. Beneath each arm run rows of hydraulic tube feet, part of a water vascular system, which the animal uses to move, grip surfaces, and handle prey. The mouth is on the underside; the anus and the madreporite (the intake for the water vascular system) are on the upper surface. Many species can regenerate lost arms, and some can regenerate a whole body from a single arm.

Feeding

Most starfish are generalist predators. The common starfish feeds on mussels and oysters by prising the shell open with its tube feet and everting part of its stomach into the shell to digest the prey externally. Others graze algae, scavenge detritus, or filter plankton. Species such as the crown-of-thorns starfish, which feeds on coral, can cause serious reef damage during population outbreaks.

Life cycle and ecology

Starfish typically reproduce sexually by broadcast spawning, releasing eggs and sperm into the water; larvae are planktonic before settling and metamorphosing. Some species also reproduce asexually by fission. As abundant predators — notably the ochre star Pisaster ochraceus, the original example of a keystone species — starfish strongly influence intertidal communities. Since 2013, sea star wasting disease has caused mass mortality of many species along the Pacific coast of North America.
