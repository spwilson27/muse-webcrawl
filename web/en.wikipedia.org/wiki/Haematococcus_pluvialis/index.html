Haematococcus pluvialis is a freshwater species of green algae in the class Chlorophyceae. It is famous as the richest known natural source of the carotenoid astaxanthin, a powerful antioxidant pigment that the alga accumulates in large amounts under stress, turning its resting cells blood-red — the source of the genus name ("blood berry") and of pink coloration in some bird baths, rock pools, and even rare "blood rain" reports.

Life cycle

Under favourable conditions the alga grows as green, biflagellate, swimming cells. When conditions deteriorate — intense light, nutrient starvation, high salinity — the cells lose their flagella and transform into thick-walled resting cysts (aplanospores), filling with astaxanthin esters stored in cytoplasmic lipid droplets. The pigment shields the cell's DNA and photosynthetic machinery from light damage during dormancy. When conditions improve, the cysts germinate and release new green motile cells.

Distribution

H. pluvialis has a worldwide distribution in temperate regions, inhabiting temporary freshwater habitats: rock pools, gutters, bird baths, water butts, and artificial containers. Its cysts persist in dried habitat crusts until rains refill them.

Commercial production

Because synthetic feed sources of colour were less favoured for salmon farming and health supplements, H. pluvialis is cultivated commercially (in closed photobioreactors and open ponds, notably in Sweden, Israel, Hawaii, and China) as the leading natural source of astaxanthin. A two-stage process is used: a "green stage" growing biomass, followed by a "red stage" in which stress induces pigment accumulation to as much as 3–5% of dry weight. Astaxanthin from the alga is used in aquaculture feeds (giving farmed salmon and shrimp their pink flesh), nutraceuticals, and cosmetics.
