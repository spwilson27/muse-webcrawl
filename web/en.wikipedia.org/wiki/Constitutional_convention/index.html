Constitutional convention

Constitutional convention may refer to two distinct concepts: (1) a gathering for the purpose of writing or amending a constitution, and (2) in Commonwealth constitutional law, an unwritten rule of political practice (a convention of the constitution) that is considered binding on political actors but is not enforceable by courts.

Constitution-making assemblies

Famous examples include the United States Constitutional Convention of 1787 in Philadelphia, which drafted the U.S. Constitution; the German Parlamentarischer Rat (1948–49); India's Constituent Assembly (1946–49); and South Africa's Constitutional Assembly (1994–96). Such bodies may be elected specifically for the purpose or formed by legislatures.

Conventions as unwritten rules

In the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and other Westminster systems, conventions govern matters such as: the monarch or governor general appointing as prime minister the person able to command the confidence of the lower house; ministerial responsibility; and the head of state acting on ministerial advice. A. V. Dicey distinguished these "conventions" from enforceable constitutional law; the Supreme Court of Canada in the Patriation Reference (1981) held conventions are politically but not legally binding.

See Constitution of Canada.

Constitutional convention (political custom) tests

Sir Ivor Jennings proposed three questions to identify a convention: Are there precedents? Did actors believe they were bound? Is there a constitutional reason for the rule?
