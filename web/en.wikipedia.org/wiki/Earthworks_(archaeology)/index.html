Earthworks (archaeology)

In archaeology, an earthwork is an artificial change in land level, typically made from piles of artificially placed or scooped rock and soil. Earthworks can themselves be archaeological features—banks, ditches, mounds and platforms—or the remains of structures, and they are among the most durable traces of past human activity in the landscape.

The ramparts of an Iron Age hillfort are classic defensive earthworks.

Types

  • Funerary monuments: burial mounds and barrows (round and long barrows), cairns.
  • Defensive works: hillforts, ringforts, ramparts, defensive ditches and linear boundaries such as Offa's Dyke.
  • Ceremonial monuments: henges, causewayed enclosures, cursus monuments, and the vast mound complexes of the Mississippian culture such as Cahokia.
  • Settlement and agricultural remains: house platforms, lynchets, ridge-and-furrow, deserted medieval village earthworks, fishponds and moated sites.
  • Industrial earthworks: mine pits, quarry scoops, spoil heaps and hollow ways.

Study

Earthworks are studied through field survey and measured earthwork plans, and increasingly by remote sensing: aerial photography reveals them as cropmarks or in low sunlight, while lidar can strip away woodland canopy to expose ground relief. Because they often survive where all other evidence has been ploughed away, earthworks are a primary source for reconstructing past landscapes and settlement patterns.
