Snow Country (novel)

Author: Yasunari Kawabata
Original title: 雪国 (Yukiguni)
Language: Japanese
Published: 1935–1937 (serial); 1948 (book)
Translator: Edward G. Seidensticker (1956)

Snow Country (雪国, Yukiguni) is a novel by Japanese writer Yasunari Kawabata, generally regarded as his masterpiece. Written and revised over more than a decade beginning in 1935, it tells of a doomed affair between a wealthy Tokyo dilettante and a provincial geisha in a mountain hot-spring town. The novel was among the works cited when Kawabata received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1968.

Plot

Shimamura, a wealthy, idle Tokyo man, travels by train to a remote onsen town in Japan's "snow country," the region along the Sea of Japan coast famed for its extreme snowfall. There he forms an attachment to Komako, a young woman working as a geisha after having been trained in Tokyo. Over a series of visits across the seasons, their relationship deepens but is shadowed by its hopelessness — Shimamura cannot commit to her, and Komako's devotion is wasted on a man who treats life as an aesthetic spectator. A second woman, Yoko, whose beautiful voice Shimamura hears on the train, becomes the focus of the novel's climactic scene, in which a fire breaks out at a cocoon warehouse being used as a cinema and Yoko falls from a balcony, cradled by Komako as the Milky Way pours into Shimamura's sight.

Style and themes

The novel is written in Kawabata's characteristic haiku-like prose: spare, imagistic, and elliptical, with narrative gaps the reader must fill. Its opening — "The train came out of the long tunnel into the snow country" — is one of the most famous lines in Japanese literature. Themes include wasted beauty and effort, the contrast between cold purity and warm sensuality, and mono no aware, the gentle sadness of impermanence. Edward Seidensticker's 1956 English translation introduced the novel to Western readers and remains the standard version.
