Doolittle Raid

The Doolittle Raid, also known as the Tokyo Raid, was an air raid on 18 April 1942 by the United States on the Japanese capital Tokyo and other places on Honshu during the Second World War. Sixteen B-25B Mitchell medium bombers were launched, without fighter escort, from the aircraft carrier USS Hornet deep in the Western Pacific. The raid was planned and led by Lieutenant Colonel James "Jimmy" Doolittle.

Background

The raid was conceived as retaliation for the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor and as a means of raising American morale. Because B-25s could not land back on a carrier, the plan was for the bombers to strike Japan and continue to airfields in unoccupied China.

The raid

On the morning of 18 April, the task force was sighted by a Japanese picket boat, forcing an early launch some 170 miles farther from Japan than planned. All sixteen aircraft bombed targets in Tokyo, Yokohama, Yokosuka, Nagoya, Kobe and Osaka, causing modest material damage but little serious harm. Running short of fuel, the raiders flew on toward China; fifteen aircraft crashed or were abandoned in China or off its coast, and one landed intact at Vladivostok in the Soviet Union, where its crew was interned.

Aftermath

Most of the 80 airmen survived with Chinese assistance, although three were executed after capture by the Japanese and one died in captivity. Japanese forces launched the Zhejiang-Jiangxi campaign against the Chinese regions that had helped the raiders, killing large numbers of Chinese civilians. Strategically, the raid caused negligible damage, but it proved Japan's home islands were vulnerable, boosted American morale enormously, and contributed to the Japanese decision to extend their defensive perimeter, leading toward the Battle of Midway. Doolittle was promoted and awarded the Medal of Honor.
