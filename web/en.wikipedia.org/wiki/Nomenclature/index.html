Nomenclature

Nomenclature is a system of names or terms, and the rules for forming them, used in a particular field of art or science. The word derives from the Latin nomen ("name") and calare ("to call"); in ancient Rome a nomenclator was a slave who announced the names of visitors and reminded his master of the names of people he met. In modern usage, nomenclature denotes both the act of naming and the body of names within a discipline, as distinct from a full terminology or vocabulary.

Scientific nomenclature

The best-known systems govern the naming of organisms. Carl Linnaeus established binomial nomenclature in the eighteenth century, giving each species a two-part Latin name consisting of a genus and a specific epithet. Today these names are regulated by international codes: the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature for animals, the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants, and corresponding codes for bacteria and viruses.

In chemistry, IUPAC nomenclature provides systematic names for chemical compounds, from simple salts to complex organic molecules, complementing common and trade names. In astronomy, systems of nomenclature govern the naming of planets, stars (such as Bayer designations), and surface features of celestial bodies under the authority of the International Astronomical Union.

Other fields

Medicine uses nomenclatures such as the Terminologia Anatomica for anatomical structures; commerce and engineering use standardised product and parts nomenclatures; and fields from linguistics to musicology maintain their own conventions. Well-designed nomenclatures aim for uniqueness, stability, and unambiguous communication within their discipline.
