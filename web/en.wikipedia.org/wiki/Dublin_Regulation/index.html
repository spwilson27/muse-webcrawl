Dublin Regulation

The Dublin Regulation (Regulation (EU) No 604/2013, commonly called Dublin III) is a European Union law that determines which EU member state is responsible for examining an application for asylum. Its core principle is that the state through which an asylum seeker first entered the EU is normally responsible for the claim, allowing other states to transfer applicants back to that country. The system also applies, through association agreements, to Iceland, Norway, Switzerland, and Liechtenstein.

History

The system began with the Dublin Convention, signed in Dublin in 1990 and in force from 1997, which was replaced by the Dublin II Regulation in 2003 and by Dublin III in 2013. The Eurodac fingerprint database supports the system by recording fingerprints of asylum applicants so that prior applications and irregular entries can be detected.

Criteria

Responsibility is assigned by a hierarchy of criteria: family unity first, then possession of a visa or residence permit, then irregular entry into the territory, with the first country of entry the default. Unaccompanied minors are generally assigned to the state where a family member is present, or where they apply.

Criticism and reform

The regulation has been widely criticised for placing disproportionate burdens on frontline states such as Greece and Italy, and transfers have repeatedly been suspended by courts where reception conditions violated fundamental rights — the European Court of Human Rights' 2011 judgment in M.S.S. v Belgium and Greece being a landmark. During the 2015 European migrant crisis the system effectively broke down. The EU's New Pact on Migration and Asylum, adopted in 2024, replaces Dublin III with the Asylum and Migration Management Regulation, introducing a mandatory solidarity mechanism while retaining first-entry responsibility in modified form.
