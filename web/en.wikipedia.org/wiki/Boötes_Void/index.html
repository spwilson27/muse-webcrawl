The Boötes Void (sometimes called the Great Nothing) is an enormous, approximately spherical region of space in the constellation Boötes containing very few galaxies. At nearly 330 million light-years in diameter, it is one of the largest known voids in the universe.

Discovery

The void was reported in 1981 by Robert Kirshner, Augustus Oemler Jr., Paul Schechter and Stephen Shectman as part of a redshift survey of galaxies. A region of comparable volume elsewhere would be expected to contain thousands of galaxies; surveys of the Boötes Void have confirmed only about 60, many of them lying in a tube-like structure running through it.

Formation and significance

Voids are a natural product of the growth of cosmic structure: gravity draws matter into filaments and walls, leaving expanding underdense regions between them. The Boötes Void may have formed from the merger of smaller voids. Its size and emptiness have occasionally been invoked in popular speculation — including unfounded claims about artificial structures — but astronomers regard it as consistent with the standard ΛCDM model of cosmology, albeit an extreme example.
