Lamed Vav Tzadikim

The Lamed Vav Tzadikim (Hebrew: ל״ו צדיקים, "the 36 righteous ones"), often called the Lamed Vavniks or simply the Thirty-Six, are, according to Jewish mystical tradition, thirty-six righteous people living in every generation for whose sake the world continues to exist, even in the worst of times. They are usually hidden: neither they nor others know who they are, and they typically live modest, anonymous lives — as pedlars, water-carriers, shoemakers or labourers. The name derives from the Hebrew letters lamed (30) and vav (6), whose numerical values in gematria sum to 36.

Sources

The concept has roots in the Talmud. In tractate Sanhedrin (97b) and Sukkah (45b), the sage Abbaye teaches that in every generation there are no fewer than 36 righteous people who are privileged to greet the Divine Presence (Shekhinah), usually connected with the verse in Isaiah 30:18, "happy are all they that wait for him [lo]" — the word lo being written with the letters lamed and vav, totalling 36. Medieval kabbalists and later Hasidic tradition developed the idea of the hidden righteous (tzadikim nistarim): if even one of the 36 were missing, or if their merit failed, the world would revert to chaos, so their concealed existence sustains humanity. Some traditions hold that a Lamed Vavnik who becomes aware of his status immediately dies, being unable to bear the knowledge, and another takes his place; when the need is desperate, one of them may reveal himself to intercede for the community.

In literature

The legend entered modern literature through André Schwarz-Bart's novel The Last of the Just (1959), which traces a family producing one of the 36 in each generation down to the Holocaust; variations appear in stories by Jorge Luis Borges, in Nikos Kazantzakis's writings, and in the film The Thirty-Six (also known as The 36). The motif of the righteous hidden few whose merit preserves the world remains one of the best-known elements of Jewish folklore.
