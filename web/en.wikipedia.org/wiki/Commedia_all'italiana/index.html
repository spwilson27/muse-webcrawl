Commedia all'italiana

Commedia all'italiana ("Italian-style comedy") is an Italian film genre recognised by film historians, critics and the industry as one of the defining movements of Italian cinema alongside neorealism. Born in the late 1950s, it blended the social observation of neorealism with farce and satire, treating serious themes — divorce, adultery, organised crime, corruption, the economic miracle, the condition of women — with bitter irony. The genre's founding film is generally considered Mario Monicelli's Big Deal on Madonna Street (1958), and its very name derives from Pietro Germi's Divorce Italian Style (1961).

Characteristics

Where neorealism had documented postwar poverty, the commedia all'italiana chronicled Italy's transformation during the economic boom of the 1960s: consumerism, emigration, the decline of the peasant world, the hypocrisy of the bourgeoisie and the Church. Its humour was characteristically "bitter" — comic situations frequently ended in failure, disgrace or death for their protagonists. Screenwriters such as Age and Scarpelli and Suso Cecchi d'Amico gave the genre unusually sharp scripts, and its ensemble of comic actors — Sordi, Gassman, Tognazzi, Manfredi and Mastroianni — embodied the vices of the average Italian.

Key films

Major works include Monicelli's The Great War (1959) and The Organizer (1963); Dino Risi's A Difficult Life (1961) and Il Sorpasso (1962); Germi's Seduced and Abandoned (1964); Comencini's Everybody Go Home (1960); and Ettore Scola's We All Loved Each Other So Much (1974) and A Special Day (1977). Divorce Italian Style won the Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay. The genre faded in the late 1970s as Italian cinema entered crisis, though its influence on later Italian comedy remains profound.
