Modern English

English is now a global lingua franca

Modern English is the form of the English language spoken from roughly the end of the 17th century to the present. It is conventionally divided into Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700), the language of Shakespeare and the King James Bible, and Late Modern English (c. 1700 onwards), from which the present-day language descends.

Development

The transition from Middle English was marked by the Great Vowel Shift, the loss of most inflectional endings, and the standardising influence of printing, which spread from the late 15th century. During the Early Modern period the language absorbed large numbers of words from Latin and Greek, spelling became increasingly fixed, and grammarians began to prescribe rules.

From the 18th century, Britain's industrial and colonial expansion carried English worldwide. New regional standards — American, Australian, Indian, Nigerian and many others — developed, differing in pronunciation, vocabulary and some grammar, while remaining broadly mutually intelligible. Today English has well over a billion speakers, the majority of them second-language users, and serves as the dominant language of science, aviation, diplomacy and the internet.

Features

Modern English has a largely analytic grammar: word order rather than case endings signals grammatical relations, and the pronoun system retains only remnants of the old case system. Its vocabulary is unusually large, combining a Germanic core with heavy Romance and learned borrowings.
