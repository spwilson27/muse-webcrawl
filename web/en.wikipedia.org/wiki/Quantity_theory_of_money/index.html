Quantity theory of money

Placeholder: across countries and decades, money growth and inflation are strongly correlated.

The quantity theory of money is a theory within monetary economics that states that the general price level of goods and services is directly proportional to the amount of money in circulation, or money supply. It is one of the oldest theories in economics, with antecedents in the work of Copernicus, Martín de Azpilcueta, and Jean Bodin, who linked the sixteenth-century European price revolution to inflows of American silver.

Equation of exchange

The theory is expressed in Irving Fisher's equation of exchange:

MV = PY

where M is the money supply, V is the velocity of money (the average number of times a unit of money is spent in a period), P is the price level, and Y is real output. If velocity and real output are taken as determined independently of the money supply, then changes in M translate proportionally into changes in P — money is neutral, and sustained inflation is, in Milton Friedman's famous phrase, "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon".

Cambridge version and Keynesian critique

The Cambridge economists Marshall and Pigou reformulated the theory in terms of money demand: M = kPY, where k is the fraction of nominal income people wish to hold as cash balances. Keynes attacked the classical version in the General Theory (1936), arguing that velocity is unstable and that money demand depends on the interest rate (liquidity preference), so monetary expansion need not raise prices when there is slack in the economy. The theory was revived by monetarism in the 1950s–70s: Friedman restated it as a theory of the demand for money with a stable (if predictable) velocity, underpinning proposals for steady money-growth rules.

Empirical evidence

Cross-country evidence over long horizons shows a strong correlation between money growth and inflation, and every hyperinflation on record has accompanied rapid money creation. Over short horizons, however, velocity shifts — notably during financial crises and periods of quantitative easing — weaken the mechanical link, and most central banks have abandoned monetary-aggregate targets in favour of interest-rate rules.
