Buddhist economics is an approach to economics inspired by Buddhist principles, emphasising human well-being, moderation and non-violence rather than the maximisation of production and consumption. The term was popularised by the British economist E. F. Schumacher in his 1973 book Small Is Beautiful, which included a chapter entitled "Buddhist Economics" based on his experience as an adviser in Burma.

Principles

Schumacher argued that from a Buddhist point of view, work is not merely a means to output but serves the functions of developing human faculties, overcoming ego-centredness through joining with others in a common task, and producing goods and services needed for a becoming existence. Consumption is a means to well-being rather than an end; production should favour local resources for local needs, renewable materials and non-violence toward people and nature.

Influence

Buddhist economics influenced the development of ecological and degrowth economics, Bhutan's Gross National Happiness framework, and the sufficiency economy philosophy promoted in Thailand. Critics note that traditional Buddhist societies were not market economies, so Buddhist economics is best read as a modern ethical critique of consumer capitalism rather than a historical system.
