In pharmacology and cell biology, desensitization (also called refractoriness or substrate sensitivity) is the decrease in the response of a cell, tissue or organism to a drug or other stimulus after continuous or repeated exposure to it. It is a fundamental protective mechanism by which cells avoid excessive, potentially harmful stimulation.

Mechanisms

Desensitization can occur through several mechanisms. In G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), agonist binding leads to phosphorylation of the receptor by G protein-coupled receptor kinases (GRKs), recruitment of β-arrestin, and uncoupling of the receptor from its G protein; the receptor may then be internalised into endosomes, from which it is either recycled (resensitization) or degraded (downregulation). Ligand-gated ion channels such as the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor enter a long-lived closed, desensitized state while agonist remains bound. Other mechanisms include depletion of signalling molecules or second messengers, induction of metabolising enzymes, and changes in gene expression.

Related concepts

Desensitization is related to, but distinct from, tolerance — the broader need for increasing doses of a drug to achieve the same effect — and from tachyphylaxis, a rapid, acute decrease in response after administration of a drug, often due to depletion of a mediator (as with indirect sympathomimetics such as ephedrine). Desensitization may be homologous (limited to the stimulated receptor) or heterologous (affecting other receptors sharing the same signalling pathway).

Clinical relevance

Receptor desensitization underlies diminishing responses to drugs such as opioids, β-adrenergic agonists used in asthma, and insulin, and is one reason dosing regimens are designed with drug holidays or escalating doses. Separately, "drug desensitization" in clinical allergy refers to protocols that temporarily induce tolerance to a drug in an allergic patient — a different use of the term.
