The Tenth Amendment (Amendment X) to the United States Constitution, the final amendment of the Bill of Rights, was ratified on December 15, 1791. It expresses the principle of federalism: that any power neither delegated to the federal government by the Constitution nor prohibited to the states is reserved to the states or to the people.

Origins

Anti-Federalists had demanded explicit reservations of state power as the price of ratifying the Constitution, echoing Article II of the Articles of Confederation. James Madison drafted the amendment in the First Congress, though the House and Senate rejected his proposal to insert state-protective language elsewhere in the constitutional text. Notably, unlike the Articles, the amendment omits the word "expressly" before "delegated"—a deliberate choice, as Madison argued, since it is impossible to enumerate every implied federal power.

Interpretation

For much of the nineteenth century the amendment framed dual federalism but rarely invalidated federal action. In United States v. Darby (1941) the Supreme Court dismissed it as a "truism." It regained force in the Rehnquist Court's federalism revival: New York v. United States (1992) and Printz v. United States (1997) held that Congress may not "commandeer" state legislatures or executive officials to implement federal programs, and NFIB v. Sebelius (2012) limited Congress's spending-power coercion of the states. The amendment remains a touchstone for arguments about the reserved police powers of the states and a rallying point for the modern "Tenther" and nullification movements, whose stronger claims the courts have consistently rejected.
