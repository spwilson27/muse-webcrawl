The Dobsonian telescope is an altazimuth-mounted Newtonian telescope design introduced in 1965 and popularized by John Dobson and the San Francisco Sidewalk Astronomers. It is notable for its simple, portable, and inexpensive design, which made very large apertures accessible to amateur astronomers. Dobsonians are prominent in amateur telescope making and are among the most common instruments at star parties.

Design

The design pairs a standard Newtonian reflector optical tube with a radically simplified mount: a "rocker box", usually of plywood, in which the tube's altitude bearings ride on Teflon pads, while the box itself rotates in azimuth on a similar low-friction bearing. The result is a mount that is cheap, rigid, and moves smoothly by hand, but which cannot track the sky automatically in its basic form; the observer nudges the tube to follow objects as the Earth rotates.

Advantages and limitations

Because nearly all of the cost is in the optics, a Dobsonian delivers far more aperture per unit cost than equatorial designs, and large mirrors gather the light needed for faint deep-sky objects. The mount is easy to build at home, aligning with Dobson's philosophy that instruments should be made from recycled and inexpensive materials. Drawbacks include manual tracking (mitigated by later equatorial platforms and "GoTo" drive conversions), bulk in solid-tube form, and the need for collimation and cool-down time for large mirrors. Truss-tube designs, in which the tube collapses into poles for transport, allow apertures of a metre or more to be carried in a car.

Influence

Dobson's "sidewalk astronomy" outreach and the design's spread through magazines and telescope-making clubs transformed amateur astronomy in the 1980s, shifting its culture toward large home-built reflectors. Commercial manufacturers adopted the design widely, and it remains the standard recommendation for visual observers seeking maximum aperture on a budget.
