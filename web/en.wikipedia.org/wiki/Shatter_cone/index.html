Shatter cone

Shatter cones are rare geological features: conical, distinctly striated fractures found only in bedrock beneath meteorite impact craters or underground nuclear explosion test sites. They are the only macroscopic shock-metamorphic effect visible to the naked eye and are considered uniquely diagnostic evidence of an impact structure.

Characteristics

Shatter cones range in size from less than a centimetre to several metres. They display curved, branching grooves (striae) that radiate from the apex of the cone in a characteristic "horsetail" pattern. The apexes generally point toward the centre of the impact — toward the source of the shock wave — though orientation can vary in complex craters. They form in many rock types, most strikingly in fine-grained limestones, dolomites, shales and quartzites.

Formation

Shatter cones are produced by the passage of a high-pressure shock wave, at pressures of roughly 2 to 30 gigapascals. The precise mechanism remains debated; proposed explanations include interference of the shock front with heterogeneities in the rock and scattering of the wave as it passes through the target. They were first described from the Steinheim Basin in Germany in the early 20th century, initially misattributed to volcanic or tectonic causes, and were recognized as impact indicators only in the 1960s.

Significance

Because they can survive erosion that removes a crater's surface expression, shatter cones have been used to confirm ancient, deeply buried structures such as the Vredefort and Sudbury impact structures in South Africa and Canada.
