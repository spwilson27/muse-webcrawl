Operation Compass was the first large British military operation of the Western Desert campaign (1940–1943) during the Second World War. British, Empire, and Commonwealth forces attacked Italian forces of the 10th Army in western Egypt and eastern Libya from December 1940 to February 1941, in what began as a five-day raid and became one of the most one-sided victories of the war.

Background

Italy invaded Egypt from Libya in September 1940, but the 10th Army halted at Sidi Barrani and built a chain of fortified camps. General Archibald Wavell planned a limited counter-raid by Major-General Richard O'Connor's Western Desert Force — about 36,000 men — through a gap between the Italian camps.

The offensive

The attack opened on 9 December 1940; the Italian camps were overrun in sequence, Sidi Barrani was taken, and the operation was extended into a full offensive. Bardia fell in early January 1941, Tobruk on 22 January, and a daring armoured dash across the desert interior cut the coast road south of Benghazi, trapping the retreating Italians at the Battle of Beda Fomm (5–7 February). In two months the Western Desert Force advanced some 800 km (500 miles), destroyed the Italian 10th Army, and took roughly 130,000 prisoners, 400 tanks, and over 1,200 guns, at a cost of fewer than 2,000 casualties.

Aftermath

The advance halted at El Agheila, partly because troops were diverted to Greece. Germany responded by sending the Afrika Korps under Erwin Rommel, whose counter-offensive in March–April 1941 retook most of the ground except Tobruk — beginning the long see-saw of the desert war.
