Lancefield grouping

Lancefield grouping is a system of serologic classification of beta-hemolytic streptococci based on the carbohydrate composition of bacterial cell wall antigens. It was devised in 1933 by the American microbiologist Rebecca Lancefield, working at the Rockefeller Institute, and remains a standard first step in the clinical identification of streptococcal pathogens.

The system

Lancefield extracted the group-specific "C carbohydrate" from bacterial cell walls using hot acid and showed, by precipitin reactions with rabbit antisera, that strains fell into distinct serological groups, designated by capital letters (A, B, C, D, and onward through the alphabet, eventually from A to W with some letters omitted). The antigen's chemical identity varies by group — rhamnose-based polysaccharides in group A, for example.

Clinical significance

  • Group A (Streptococcus pyogenes) — causes strep throat, scarlet fever, impetigo, cellulitis, necrotizing fasciitis and the post-infectious sequelae rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis.
  • Group B (Streptococcus agalactiae) — a leading cause of sepsis and meningitis in newborns; pregnant women are screened for colonisation before delivery.
  • Group C and G streptococci — cause pharyngitis and invasive infections resembling group A disease.
  • Group D — historically included enterococci, which were later reclassified into their own genus Enterococcus.

Not all pathogenic streptococci fit the scheme: Streptococcus pneumoniae is alpha-hemolytic and not Lancefield groupable. Modern laboratories still use latex agglutination kits based on Lancefield antigens for rapid grouping, supplemented by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and sequencing.
