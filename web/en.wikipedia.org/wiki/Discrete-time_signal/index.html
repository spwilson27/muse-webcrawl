Discrete-time signal

A discrete-time signal is a sequence of values that correspond to particular instants in time, defined only at discrete times rather than continuously. It is typically written x[n], where the integer index n enumerates successive samples. Discrete-time signals are the foundation of digital signal processing.

Obtaining discrete-time signals

Most discrete-time signals arise by sampling a continuous-time signal at a uniform sampling interval T, so that x[n] = xc(nT). The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that if the continuous signal contains no frequencies above half the sampling rate, it can be reconstructed exactly from its samples; otherwise aliasing corrupts the representation. Other discrete-time signals, such as daily stock prices or digital data streams, are inherently discrete.

Basic sequences

  • The unit sample (discrete impulse) δ[n], equal to 1 at n = 0 and 0 elsewhere.
  • The unit step u[n], equal to 1 for n ≥ 0.
  • Sinusoids and complex exponentials ejωn, whose frequency ω is periodic with period 2π — a key difference from continuous time.

Operations and analysis

Discrete-time systems transform input sequences into output sequences; linear time-invariant systems are completely characterized by their impulse response, and their action is computed by convolution. Frequency-domain analysis uses the discrete-time Fourier transform and the Z-transform. Note that "discrete-time" refers only to the time axis: sample values may still be continuous in amplitude, unlike a digital signal, which is both discrete-time and quantized.
