The Roanoke Colony was an enterprise financed and organised by Sir Walter Raleigh in the late 16th century to establish a permanent English settlement in the Virginia Colony. The colony was established on Roanoke Island in what is now Dare County, North Carolina. A first settlement in 1585–86 was abandoned; a second group of colonists, led by governor John White, arrived in 1587. When White finally returned from England in 1590, delayed by the Anglo-Spanish War, he found the settlement deserted — giving Roanoke its enduring name, the Lost Colony.

The 1585 colony

An expedition led by Sir Richard Grenville established a settlement of about 108 men under governor Ralph Lane in 1585. Relations with the local Secotan people deteriorated over food supplies and perceived slights, ending in violence. Short of supplies, the colonists accepted a ride home with Sir Francis Drake in June 1586. A small holding party left by Grenville also disappeared.

The 1587 colony and disappearance

In 1587 White returned with around 115 colonists, including his daughter Eleanor Dare, whose daughter Virginia Dare, born on 18 August 1587, was the first English child born in the Americas. White sailed back to England for supplies later that year, but the Spanish Armada crisis prevented relief ships from sailing. When he reached Roanoke on 18 August 1590, the site was abandoned and fortified. The only clues were the word "CROATOAN" carved into a post of the fort and "CRO" carved into a tree — the name of a nearby island and its Native American inhabitants, suggesting the colonists may have moved there or been absorbed into local tribes.

Theories and legacy

The fate of the colonists remains unknown. Proposed explanations include integration with the Croatoan or other Algonquian peoples, starvation, disease, a move towards Chesapeake Bay followed by massacre (as reported in later Jamestown accounts), and Spanish attack. Archaeological work at sites on Hatteras Island and near the western shore of Albemarle Sound has produced English artefacts consistent with, but not proving, the colonists' survival and assimilation. The mystery has inspired extensive literature, drama (notably Paul Green's 1937 outdoor play The Lost Colony, still performed at Manteo), and fiction.
