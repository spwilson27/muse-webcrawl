Shellfish poisoning

Shellfish poisoning includes a group of foodborne illnesses caused by toxins produced by marine algae (dinoflagellates and diatoms) that accumulate in filter-feeding shellfish such as mussels, clams, oysters, and scallops. The toxins are heat-stable, so cooking the shellfish does not reliably destroy them. Poisonings typically follow harmful algal blooms (often called "red tides"), and harvesting closures are used to prevent outbreaks.

Paralytic shellfish poisoning

PSP is the most dangerous form. Saxitoxin blocks voltage-gated sodium channels, producing numbness and tingling that begin around the lips, then weakness and, in severe cases, respiratory paralysis. There is no antidote; treatment is supportive, including mechanical ventilation. Symptoms usually appear within minutes to a few hours of eating contaminated shellfish.

Amnesic shellfish poisoning

ASP was first recognised in a 1987 outbreak on Prince Edward Island, Canada, traced to cultured blue mussels contaminated with domoic acid produced by the diatom Pseudo-nitzschia. Domoic acid is an excitatory amino acid analogue that can damage the hippocampus, causing short-term memory loss — the feature that gave the syndrome its name.

Prevention

Monitoring programmes measure toxin levels in shellfish and phytoplankton, and commercial and recreational harvesting is closed when levels exceed regulatory limits. Consumers are advised to obtain shellfish from approved sources and to heed local closure notices, since the toxins cannot be detected by taste or removed by cooking.
