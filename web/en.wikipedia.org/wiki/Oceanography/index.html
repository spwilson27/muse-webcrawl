Oceanography

Oceanography (from Greek ōkeanós, "ocean", and gráphō, "to write"), also called oceanology or marine science, is the study of the world's oceans — their physics, chemistry, biology and geology, and their interactions with the atmosphere, coasts and seafloor. It is an interdisciplinary Earth science drawing on physics, chemistry, biology, geology, meteorology and engineering.

Branches

  • Physical oceanography — currents, waves, tides, thermohaline circulation, and ocean–atmosphere interaction (including El Niño).
  • Chemical oceanography — seawater composition, salinity, dissolved gases, and the ocean's role in the carbon cycle (see nutrient cycle).
  • Biological oceanography — marine organisms, plankton, productivity and ecosystems from surface waters to hydrothermal vents.
  • Geological oceanography — the seafloor, plate tectonics, sediments and the history recorded in ocean basins.

History

Early knowledge came from navigators such as Captain Cook's scientific voyages and Matthew Fontaine Maury's wind and current charts (1840s–50s). The science is usually dated from the Challenger expedition (1872–1876), whose reports founded modern oceanography. The 20th century added echo sounding, the bathyscaphe, scuba-based research, oceanographic institutions such as Scripps and Woods Hole, and satellite altimetry; the theory of plate tectonics was confirmed largely by marine geophysics (magnetic striping, seafloor spreading). Today autonomous floats (the Argo programme, with thousands of profiling floats), gliders, moorings and satellites monitor the ocean continuously.

Importance

The oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface, store most of its water and roughly 50 times more carbon than the atmosphere, produce about half of global oxygen through phytoplankton photosynthesis, and drive weather and climate. Oceanography underpins weather and climate prediction, fisheries management, shipping, offshore energy, coastal protection and understanding of sea-level rise and ocean acidification.
