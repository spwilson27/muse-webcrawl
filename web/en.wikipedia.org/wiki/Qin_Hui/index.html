Qin Hui

Qin Hui (Chinese: 秦檜; 1090–1155) was a chancellor of the Southern Song dynasty of China, remembered in Chinese tradition as a corrupt traitor who persecuted his political opponents and engineered the execution of the celebrated general Yue Fei. He dominated the court of Emperor Gaozong for nearly two decades and championed a policy of peace with the Jurchen Jin dynasty.

Career

Qin Hui passed the imperial examinations in 1115 and served the Northern Song. When the Jin captured Kaifeng in 1127 he was taken north, but he returned south in 1130 under circumstances that led many contemporaries to suspect he had become a Jin agent. Backed by Emperor Gaozong, he rose to chancellor and, after a brief dismissal, held the office continuously from 1138 until his death.

Peace with the Jin and the fall of Yue Fei

Qin Hui advocated negotiating a settlement recognising Jin control of northern China. The Treaty of Shaoxing (1141) fixed the border at the Huai River and required the Song to pay annual tribute. To secure the peace, the war faction had to be broken: the general Yue Fei, whose armies had recovered territory from the Jin, was recalled, arrested on charges widely regarded as fabricated — summed up by the phrase mò xū yǒu (莫須有, "perhaps there is", i.e. possibly guilty) attributed to Qin — and executed in prison in 1142.

Legacy

Historiography has treated Qin Hui as one of the archetypal villains of Chinese history. Iron statues of Qin, his wife Lady Wang and two accomplices, kneeling in disgrace before Yue Fei's tomb in Hangzhou, have for centuries been cursed, spat upon and struck by visitors, and have been recast many times. Some modern commentators have argued that the decision for peace was principally Emperor Gaozong's and that Qin served as its scapegoat, but public veneration of Yue Fei and vilification of Qin remain deeply entrenched.
