Photoresistor

A photoresistor (also known as a light-dependent resistor, LDR, or photoconductive cell) is a passive electronic component whose electrical resistance decreases when exposed to increasing light intensity. Photoresistors are made of a high-resistance semiconductor; photons absorbed by the material free electrons into the conduction band, increasing conductivity.

A typical cadmium sulfide photoresistor.

Operation

In darkness, a photoresistor can have a resistance of several megaohms; under illumination it may fall to a few hundred ohms. The response is slow — tens of milliseconds or more — and depends on the previous lighting history, but the devices are inexpensive and simple to interface. The most common material is cadmium sulfide (CdS), with a spectral response similar to that of the human eye peaking in the green; lead sulfide and lead selenide devices are used for infrared sensing. Environmental restrictions on cadmium have encouraged alternatives in some markets.

Applications

Photoresistors are used in streetlights that switch on at dusk, camera light meters, alarm systems, night lights, and as light sensors in consumer electronics and toys. They are often combined with a fixed resistor as a voltage divider feeding a comparator or an analogue input.
