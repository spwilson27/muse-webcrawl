Face on Mars

The Face on Mars is a distinctive surface feature in the Cydonia region of Mars, roughly 1.5 km across, which in a 1976 photograph by the Viking 1 orbiter appeared to resemble a human face. It became a famous example of pareidolia — the tendency to perceive meaningful patterns, especially faces, in random stimuli.

Viking imagery

The image (frame 035A72) was taken on 25 July 1976. NASA released it with a caption noting the face-like appearance was caused by shadows and lighting; a trick of light and image defects (including missing data bits that formed a "nostril") enhanced the illusion. Project scientist Gerry Soffen famously dismissed it as a trick of light.

Later imaging

Higher-resolution images from the Mars Global Surveyor (1998, 2001) and Mars Express showed the feature to be a natural mesa, heavily eroded, with no face-like appearance under different lighting — confirming the pareidolia explanation.

Cultural impact

Before the later imaging, the "Face" inspired books and speculation about artificial Martian monuments, notably by Richard C. Hoagland, and appeared widely in popular culture as a symbol of alleged NASA cover-ups.
