Roman à clef

Roman à clef (French for "novel with a key") is a novel about real people and events, but presented under fictitious names and details. The "key" — the mapping between characters and their real-life counterparts — is often understood by contemporary readers, and sometimes circulated literally as a list identifying who is who.

Origins

The form emerged in 17th-century France, notably in the works of Madeleine de Scudéry, whose vast romances such as Artamène, ou le Grand Cyrus portrayed courtiers and public figures under classical disguises. The disguise gave authors plausible deniability to satirize or comment on powerful people.

Notable examples

Classic examples include Aldous Huxley's Point Counter Point, Thomas Mann's Buddenbrooks, Sylvia Plath's The Bell Jar (Esther Greenwood standing in for Plath), Jack Kerouac's works, and political novels such as Primary Colors, published anonymously and based on Bill Clinton's 1992 presidential campaign. The roman à clef remains a device for exploring recent history and celebrity culture while sheltering authors from libel claims.
