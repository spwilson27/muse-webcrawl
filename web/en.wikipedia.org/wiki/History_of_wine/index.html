The history of wine spans thousands of years. Wine, the fermented juice of grapes, was first produced in the Neolithic period and spread from the Caucasus and Near East across the Mediterranean world and eventually to every continent.

Origins

The earliest archaeological evidence of winemaking comes from Georgia around 6000 BCE, with later finds in Iran and Armenia, including the Areni-1 winery (c. 4100 BCE). Wine cultivation spread to Egypt, where it was associated with royalty and religion, and to the Aegean world.

Greek and Roman eras

Greeks spread viticulture through their colonies and honored the wine god Dionysus. The Romans systematized winemaking, spread vines across their empire—including Gaul, Iberia, and the Rhine—and developed techniques such as aging in wooden barrels.

Medieval and early modern periods

Monasteries preserved and refined viticulture after Rome's fall, especially in Burgundy and the Rhine. Spanish and Portuguese colonists carried vines to the Americas from the 16th century. The introduction of glass bottles and corks in the 17th century enabled long aging and the modern wine trade.

Phylloxera and the modern era

In the late 19th century the phylloxera louse devastated European vineyards; recovery came through grafting European vines onto resistant American rootstocks. The 20th century brought appellation systems, temperature-controlled fermentation, and the rise of New World producers in the Americas, Australia, and South Africa.
