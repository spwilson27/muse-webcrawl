Moscone–Milk assassinations

The Moscone–Milk assassinations were the killings of San Francisco mayor George Moscone and supervisor Harvey Milk on November 27, 1978, by Dan White, a former city supervisor who had recently resigned his seat. White shot both men multiple times inside San Francisco City Hall. The murders shocked the city and the nation, cutting short the careers of two of California's most prominent progressive politicians.

Background

George Moscone, elected mayor in 1975, had appointed Harvey Milk, the first openly gay man elected to major public office in California, to a city commission. Milk won election to the Board of Supervisors in 1977. Dan White, a conservative former firefighter and police officer, clashed with both men over policy and sought reappointment after resigning his seat in November 1978; Moscone, influenced by Milk and others, refused.

The shootings

On the morning of November 27, 1978, White entered City Hall through a basement window to avoid metal detectors, carrying a loaded revolver. He first shot Moscone several times in the mayor's office, then walked to Milk's office and shot him five times. White fled and later surrendered to police.

Trial and aftermath

White was convicted of voluntary manslaughter rather than first-degree murder, after his defense argued diminished capacity — an argument popularly (and somewhat inaccurately) remembered as the "Twinkie defense". The verdict sparked the White Night riots of May 21, 1979, in which gay protesters clashed with police. White was paroled in 1984 and died by suicide in 1985. Dianne Feinstein, president of the Board of Supervisors, succeeded Moscone as mayor.
