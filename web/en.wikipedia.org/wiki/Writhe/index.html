Writhe

In knot theory and the geometry of curves, writhe is a measure of the local non-planarity or "coiling" of a curve in three-dimensional space. It was introduced in the study of the topology of closed ribbons and DNA by F. Brock Fuller and others, building on work by Gheorghe Călugăreanu.

Definition

For a closed curve, writhe is defined by a double integral over pairs of points on the curve, averaging a directional crossing number over all viewing directions. Unlike the linking number, writhe is not a topological invariant: it depends on the actual geometry of the curve and changes continuously as the curve is deformed.

Relation to twist and linking

For a closed ribbon, the Călugăreanu–White–Fuller theorem states that the linking number of the ribbon's two edges equals the sum of the ribbon's total twist and the writhe of its centreline: Lk = Tw + Wr. This relation is fundamental in the study of DNA supercoiling, where enzymes that change the linking number of DNA (topoisomerases) partition the change between twist and writhe.

Applications

Writhe appears in the physics of elastic rods, magnetic flux tubes in plasma physics, vortex filaments in fluid dynamics, and polymer science. Numerical algorithms that compute writhe for polygonal curves are used in simulations of supercoiled DNA and knotted proteins.
