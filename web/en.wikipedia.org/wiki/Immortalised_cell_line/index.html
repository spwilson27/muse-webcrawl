Immortalised cell line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: HeLa cells in culture]

An immortalised cell line is a population of cells from a multicellular organism that, through mutation or deliberate modification, has evaded normal cellular senescence and can be grown indefinitely in culture. Immortalised lines are a cornerstone of cell biology, providing a renewable, standardised model in place of primary cells, which die after a limited number of divisions (the Hayflick limit).

How lines arise

Immortalisation can occur spontaneously in cultured cells, especially in cancer-derived material: the first and most famous line, HeLa (1951), came from a cervical carcinoma of Henrietta Lacks. Lines can also be created deliberately by introducing viral oncogenes (e.g. SV40 large T antigen), expressing telomerase (hTERT), or disrupting tumour suppressors such as p53 and Rb. Common lines include HEK 293, CHO, MDCK and Vero cells.

Uses and caveats

Immortalised lines are used in drug screening, vaccine production, toxicity testing and basic research, and in the manufacture of therapeutic proteins. Because they are genetically abnormal and drift over generations — and because cross-contamination (notably by HeLa) has corrupted many supposedly distinct lines — results require authentication by DNA profiling and caution when extrapolating to cells in the body.
