Jacob Wetterling Crimes Against Children and Sexually Violent Offender Registration Act

Jacob Wetterling Act

Enacted: September 13, 1994, as part of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act

Named for: Jacob Wetterling (1978–1989)

Purpose: Required states to establish sex-offender registries

Succeeded/amended by: Megan's Law (1996); Adam Walsh Act (2006)

The Jacob Wetterling Crimes Against Children and Sexually Violent Offender Registration Act, commonly the Jacob Wetterling Act, is a United States federal law enacted in 1994 that required every state to establish a registry of people convicted of sex offenses and offenses against children. It was the first comprehensive federal sex-offender registration law and laid the groundwork for later measures including Megan's Law and the Adam Walsh Child Protection and Safety Act.

Background

The act is named for Jacob Wetterling, an 11-year-old boy from St. Joseph, Minnesota, who was abducted at gunpoint by a masked man on October 22, 1989, while biking home with his brother and a friend; his fate remained unknown for nearly 27 years. His parents, Jerry and Patty Wetterling, founded the Jacob Wetterling Foundation and became national advocates for missing-children legislation. In 2016, Danny Heinrich confessed to abducting and murdering Jacob and led authorities to his remains.

Provisions

Passed as part of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, the Wetterling Act required states, on pain of losing federal crime-fighting funds, to maintain registries of offenders convicted of sexually violent offenses or crimes against children. Registrants had to provide their addresses, verify them annually (or every 90 days for the most serious "sexually violent predators"), and update the registry when moving. The original act made registry information available to law enforcement; the 1996 Megan's Law amendment required public notification to communities.

Legacy

All fifty states built registries under the act's framework, and the federal role expanded with the Pam Lychner Act (1996), which created a national database, and the Adam Walsh Act of 2006, whose Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act (SORNA) superseded the Wetterling standards with a tiered federal scheme.
