Fuse (explosives)

In an explosive, pyrotechnic device, or military munition, a fuse (or fuze) is the part of the device that initiates function. In common usage, the word fuse is used indiscriminately. However, when being specific (and in particular in a military context), the term fuse describes a simple pyrotechnic initiating device, like the cord on a firecracker, whereas the term fuze is used for more complicated electronic or mechanical devices in modern munitions.

Historically, a fuse was a length of slow-burning material — often gunpowder encased in paper or textile — used to transmit flame to an explosive charge after a predictable delay, allowing the user to retire to a safe distance.

Safety fuse

The safety fuse, invented by William Bickford in 1831, consists of a core of black powder wrapped in textile fibres and waterproofed coating. It burns at a steady rate of roughly 30 seconds per foot (about 100 seconds per metre), and revolutionised mining and demolition by making blasting far more predictable and safer.

Detonating cord

Detonating cord (detcord) is a flexible cord with a core of pentaerythritol tetranitrate (PETN) that detonates — rather than burns — along its length at several kilometres per second, transmitting a detonation almost instantaneously between charges.

Fuzes in munitions

Modern artillery, mortar and bomb fuzes may function on impact, after a time delay, in proximity to a target (proximity fuze), or by command. They incorporate safety and arming mechanisms so the munition remains safe during handling and transport and arms only after firing.
