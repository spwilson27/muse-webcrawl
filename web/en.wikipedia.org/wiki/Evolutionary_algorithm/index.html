Evolutionary algorithm

An evolutionary algorithm (EA) is a population-based metaheuristic optimisation algorithm inspired by biological evolution. Candidate solutions to a problem are treated as individuals in a population; a fitness function measures their quality, and new generations are produced by selecting fitter individuals and varying them through recombination (crossover) and mutation. Over successive generations the population tends to evolve toward better solutions. EAs are members of the wider family of evolutionary computation methods within artificial intelligence and are used when a problem has no known efficient exact method, or when its objective landscape is discontinuous, noisy or poorly understood.

Types

The classical branches of the field are genetic algorithms (developed by John Holland, typically operating on encoded strings), evolution strategies (developed in Germany by Ingo Rechenberg and Hans-Paul Schwefel, operating on real-valued vectors with self-adapting mutation), evolutionary programming, and genetic programming, in which the candidates are computer programs, popularised by John Koza. Modern variants include CMA-ES for continuous optimisation, differential evolution, and multi-objective algorithms such as NSGA-II that optimise several conflicting criteria at once.

Applications

Evolutionary algorithms have been applied to antenna and circuit design — including an evolved antenna flown on a NASA spacecraft — scheduling, engineering design, machine-learning hyperparameter tuning, automatic programme synthesis, finance and the generation of art and music. Their advantages are generality and ease of parallelisation; their weaknesses include heavy computation and no guarantee of finding a global optimum.
