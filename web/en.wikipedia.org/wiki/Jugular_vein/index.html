Jugular vein

The jugular veins are veins that take blood from the head back to the heart. They are the principal venous drainage of the head and neck, returning deoxygenated blood toward the superior vena cava. On each side there are three main jugular veins: the internal, external, and anterior jugular veins.

Internal jugular vein

The internal jugular vein is the largest of the jugular veins. It begins at the jugular foramen in the base of the skull as the continuation of the sigmoid dural venous sinus, descends within the carotid sheath alongside the internal and common carotid arteries and the vagus nerve, and ends behind the sternoclavicular joint by joining the subclavian vein to form the brachiocephalic vein. It drains the brain, much of the face and neck, and receives tributaries including the inferior petrosal sinus, facial, lingual, pharyngeal, and superior and middle thyroid veins.

External and anterior jugular veins

The external jugular vein runs superficially down the neck, crossing the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and drains blood from the scalp and face into the subclavian vein; it is often visible through the skin. The anterior jugular vein is a smaller, variable vessel descending near the midline of the front of the neck, draining into the external jugular or subclavian vein.

Clinical significance

The internal jugular vein is a common site for central venous catheterization, providing access for measuring central venous pressure, administering fluids and medications, and hemodialysis. Assessment of the jugular venous pulse and pressure in the neck veins is a standard part of the cardiovascular examination, giving information about right-heart function; a raised jugular venous pressure is a sign of conditions such as right ventricular failure and fluid overload. Jugular vein distension and the hepatojugular reflux are classic bedside findings.
