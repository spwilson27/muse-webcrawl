Central venous pressure

Central venous pressure (CVP) is the blood pressure in the venae cavae, near the right atrium of the heart. CVP reflects the amount of blood returning to the heart and the ability of the heart to pump the blood back into the arterial system. It is often used as an approximation of right ventricular preload, though it is increasingly recognised to be an imperfect guide to fluid responsiveness.

Measurement

CVP is measured using a central venous catheter whose tip lies in the superior vena cava near the right atrium. The catheter is connected to a pressure transducer (or, historically, a water manometer), zeroed at the level of the right atrium (the phlebostatic axis). Normal CVP in a healthy person is usually quoted as 2–8 mmHg (roughly 3–11 cmH₂O), though values vary with posture, ventilation, and intra-abdominal pressure.

Factors affecting CVP

CVP is determined by the interaction of venous return and cardiac function. It is raised by:

  • Increased blood volume (hypervolaemia) or fluid overload
  • Decreased cardiac output / right ventricular failure
  • Venoconstriction
  • Forced exhalation and positive-pressure ventilation (including PEEP)
  • Cardiac tamponade and constrictive pericarditis
  • Tricuspid stenosis or regurgitation

It is lowered by hypovolaemia, venodilation, and deep inhalation (negative intrathoracic pressure).

Waveform

The CVP trace shows characteristic waves similar to those in the jugular venous pulse: the a wave (atrial contraction), the c wave (tricuspid valve closure bulging into the atrium), the x descent, the v wave (venous filling of the atrium against the closed tricuspid valve), and the y descent (tricuspid opening and ventricular filling). Abnormalities of the waveform aid diagnosis: for example, giant v waves occur in tricuspid regurgitation, and "cannon" a waves occur when the atrium contracts against a closed tricuspid valve, as in complete heart block.

Clinical use

CVP monitoring was long used to guide fluid therapy in shock and sepsis, with targets (e.g. 8–12 mmHg in early goal-directed therapy) built into management protocols. Later evidence showed that a static CVP value is a poor predictor of whether a patient will respond to fluids, and contemporary practice emphasises dynamic measures instead, although CVP remains useful for assessing right heart function, venous congestion, and trends over time.
