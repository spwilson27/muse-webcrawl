The Peloponnese is the great hand-shaped peninsula of southern Greece, joined to the mainland by the Isthmus of Corinth.

The Peloponnese (Greek: Πελοπόννησος, Pelopónnisos) is a peninsula and geographic region in southern Greece, connected to the central part of the country by the Isthmus of Corinth, which was cut by the Corinth Canal in 1893. Its name means "Island of Pelops", after the mythical king. Administratively, most of it forms the Peloponnese Region, while its northwestern and northeastern parts belong to West Greece and Attica respectively.

Geography

The peninsula is mountainous, with ranges including Taygetus (2,404 m) in the south, and indented coasts forming the Messenian, Laconian and Argolic gulfs. Its seven regional units correspond closely to the famous ancient territories: Corinthia, Argolis, Arcadia, Laconia (Sparta), Messenia, Elis (Olympia) and Achaea.

History

The Peloponnese was a heartland of Mycenaean civilisation (Mycenae, Pylos, Tiryns) and later of classical city-states: Sparta, Corinth, Argos and Elis, where the Olympic Games were held. The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) between Athens and Sparta takes its name from the peninsula. In the medieval period it was known as the Morea; it was a principal theatre of the Greek War of Independence in the 1820s, including the Battle of Navarino (1827).
