Machicolation

A machicolation (French: mâchicoulis) is a floor opening between the supporting corbels of a battlement or parapet, through which defenders could drop stones, boiling water, hot sand, quicklime or other objects on attackers at the base of a defending wall. Machicolations are a common feature of medieval castles and fortified manor houses, particularly in France, and are the stone equivalent of a wooden hoarding.

Description

Machicolations project outward from the wall face on a series of stone corbels, creating a gallery whose floor is pierced by slots or openings. Unlike arrowslits, which allowed horizontal fire along the wall, machicolations allowed defenders to attack enemies directly below without exposing themselves. A continuous row of machicolations is sometimes called a machicolated parapet; a small machicolation over a doorway may be called a "murder hole" in later usage, though the term properly refers to a ceiling opening inside a gatehouse.

History

The feature developed in the crusader castles of the Levant — early examples appear at Krak des Chevaliers in Syria — and spread through southern France and the rest of Europe in the 13th and 14th centuries. Because they remained visible from outside and conveyed status, machicolations were retained as decorative, symbolic features on later châteaux and even on 19th-century Gothic Revival buildings, long after they had lost military value.
