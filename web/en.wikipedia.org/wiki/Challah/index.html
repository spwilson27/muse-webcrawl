Challah

Challah (Hebrew: חַלָּה, plural challot) is a special bread in Jewish cuisine, usually braided and typically eaten on ceremonial occasions such as Shabbat and major Jewish holidays (other than Passover). Ritually acceptable challah is made from dough from which a small portion has been set aside as an offering (hafrashat challah). The word derives from the biblical Hebrew term for a kind of loaf or cake; in modern usage it most often refers to the rich, golden braided loaf of Ashkenazi tradition made with eggs, flour, water, yeast, sugar and salt.

Religious significance

Two loaves of challah are traditionally placed on the Shabbat and festival table, recalling the double portion of manna that fell on Fridays for the Israelites in the desert. The blessing HaMotzi is recited over the loaves before the meal. The term challah also refers to the portion of dough separated with a blessing before baking, in memory of the tithe given to the priests (kohanim) in the time of the Temple; historically this portion was burned.

Preparation and shapes

The dough is enriched with eggs and oil, giving a soft, golden crumb, and is usually braided from three, four or six strands; braiding is both decorative and symbolic, variously said to represent love, unity, or the manna's woven appearance. For Rosh Hashanah, the Jewish New Year, challah is often baked in a round or spiral shape symbolising the cycle of the year, and may be sweetened with raisins and honey. Sephardi and Mizrahi traditions include flatter or differently seasoned loaves, and "water challah" — made without eggs — is prepared in some communities, particularly for the meal ending Yom Kippur.

Variations

Some bakers top challah with poppy or sesame seeds. Leftover challah is popular for French toast and bread pudding, and the bread is a staple of Jewish bakeries and delicatessens worldwide. Modern adaptations include whole-wheat, gluten-free and vegan versions, and sweet chocolate or cinnamon varieties.
