Byzantine aristocracy and bureaucracy

Placeholder image: Byzantine courtiers in ceremonial dress.

The Byzantine Empire had a complex system of aristocracy and bureaucracy inherited from late Roman practice and refined over a thousand years. Unlike the hereditary feudal nobility of Western Europe, Byzantine status flowed primarily from the emperor: rank was expressed through a graded hierarchy of court titles and offices, recorded in precedence lists called taktika.

Titles and offices

Originally a distinction existed between titles (dignities conferred by insignia, such as patrikios, magistros and the exalted Kaisar) and offices (functional posts such as the logothetes who ran the fiscal and postal departments, the eparch of Constantinople, and the domestics commanding the armies). By the middle Byzantine period many dignities had inflated and new ones were created above them; titles could be purchased and carried a salary (roga) paid in a formal ceremony.

The aristocracy

Powerful military families — the Phokades, Skleroi, Komnenoi and Doukai — emerged from the Anatolian frontier from the 9th century, intermarrying with the imperial house and at times seizing the throne. Under the Komnenian emperors from 1081, government was reorganised around an extended imperial clan, with new titles (sebastokrator, panhypersebastos) created for the emperor's relatives.

Bureaucracy

The central administration in Constantinople comprised fiscal bureaux (sekreta) staffed by career officials and supervised by logothetes, a service famous — and later notorious — for its elaborate ceremonial, paperwork and continuity through political upheavals.
