The American Railway Union (ARU) was briefly among the largest labor unions in the United States. Founded in Chicago on June 20, 1893, by railway workers led by Eugene V. Debs, it was an industrial union open to all railway employees regardless of craft or race (in practice, its constitution barred Black workers, a compromise Debs opposed). It grew rapidly to about 150,000 members within a year.

Pullman Strike

In 1894 the ARU won an early victory against the Great Northern Railway. That spring, workers at the Pullman Palace Car Company struck over wage cuts; in June the ARU voted to support them with a nationwide boycott of trains hauling Pullman cars. The boycott escalated into the Pullman Strike, disrupting rail traffic across the country. A federal injunction, the intervention of federal troops ordered by President Grover Cleveland, and the arrest of Debs and other leaders broke the strike. Debs served six months in prison, where he read socialist literature and emerged committed to socialism.

Aftermath

The ARU never recovered from the strike's defeat. In June 1897 its remaining members converted the organization into the Social Democracy of America, a political movement that was a forerunner of the Socialist Party of America, which Debs would lead as its five-time presidential candidate.
