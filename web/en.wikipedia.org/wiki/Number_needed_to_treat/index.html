Number needed to treat

The number needed to treat (NNT) is an epidemiological measure used in communicating the effectiveness of a health-care intervention, typically a medication. It is the inverse of the absolute risk reduction: the average number of patients who need to be treated over a specified period for one additional patient to benefit compared with a control treatment.


Pictogram illustrating an NNT of 10: one in ten treated patients benefits.

Calculation

If a treatment reduces the event rate from 40% in the control group to 30% in the treatment group, the absolute risk reduction is 10%, and the NNT is 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10: ten patients must be treated for one extra patient to avoid the event. NNT was introduced by Laupacis, Sackett, and Roberts in a 1988 paper in the New England Journal of Medicine. The ideal NNT is 1, meaning every treated patient benefits; in practice, values for effective preventive therapies commonly range from the single digits into the hundreds.

Interpretation

NNT depends on the baseline risk of the population studied and on the duration of follow-up, so it should always be quoted with its time horizon. The reciprocal concept, the number needed to harm (NNH), expresses the frequency of adverse effects and is used alongside NNT to weigh benefit against risk. Confidence intervals for NNT are awkward because the scale passes through infinity when the risk difference crosses zero — a feature that has prompted some criticism of the measure, though it remains widely used in evidence-based medicine and patient decision aids.
