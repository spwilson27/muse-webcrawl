Creole cuisine

Louisiana Creole cuisine is a style of cooking originating in Louisiana that blends French, Spanish, African, Native American, Caribbean, and other influences among the Louisiana Creole people. It is closely related to, but distinct from, Cajun cuisine: Creole cooking developed largely in New Orleans as the food of a cosmopolitan, urban, often aristocratic society with access to imported ingredients and classically trained cooks, whereas Cajun cuisine arose among the rural French-speaking Acadian settlers of southwestern Louisiana. A common aphorism holds that Creole food is "city food" and Cajun food is "country food".

Foundations

Creole dishes frequently begin with the "holy trinity" of diced onion, celery, and bell pepper. Many classic dishes show direct European antecedents refracted through local ingredients: gumbo (with African okra, French roux, and Choctaw filé powder), jambalaya (related to Spanish paella), étouffée, shrimp Creole, grillades, and sauces such as remoulade and meunière. Creole cooking makes generous use of butter and cream, tomatoes (a point of contrast with many Cajun versions of the same dishes), seafood from the Gulf of Mexico, and refined French technique. Famous Creole restaurants in the French Quarter of New Orleans include Antoine's (founded 1840), Galatoire's, Arnaud's, and Brennan's, the last the origin of bananas Foster. The tradition was codified in cookbooks such as The Picayune's Creole Cook Book (1901) and carried forward by chefs such as Leah Chase of Dooky Chase's Restaurant and Emeril Lagasse.

Other Creole cuisines

The term "Creole cuisine" is also used for the blended cooking of other Creole cultures around the Caribbean, the Indian Ocean (Mauritius, Réunion, Seychelles), and West Africa, each combining European colonizers' techniques with African, Asian, and indigenous ingredients.
