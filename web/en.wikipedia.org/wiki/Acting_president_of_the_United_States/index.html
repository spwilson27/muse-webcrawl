Acting president of the United States

An acting president of the United States is a person who lawfully exercises the powers and duties of the President of the United States without themselves holding the office. The acting president is usually the vice president, acting during a president's temporary incapacity; officials in the line of succession under the Presidential Succession Act may also act in that capacity if the presidency and vice presidency are both unable to serve.

An acting president discharges the powers and duties of the office from the White House during the president's incapacity.

Constitutional basis

Article II of the Constitution provided that presidential "powers and duties" devolve on the vice president in cases of inability, and the Twenty-fifth Amendment (1967) clarified the mechanism. Under Section 3, a president may voluntarily transfer power by written declaration — typically during medical procedures under anesthesia — and resume it by a further declaration. Under Section 4, the vice president and a majority of the Cabinet may declare the president unable, a provision never formally invoked.

Instances

The first formal Section 3 transfer occurred on 13 July 1985, when Vice President George H. W. Bush served as acting president for about eight hours while Ronald Reagan underwent surgery (Reagan's letter disclaimed the intent to set a binding precedent, but it is treated as the first use). Dick Cheney acted for George W. Bush twice (2002 and 2007) during colonoscopies. On 19 November 2021, Kamala Harris acted for Joe Biden for about 85 minutes, becoming the first woman to hold presidential power in U.S. history.

Succession context

If both the presidency and vice presidency are vacant or their holders unable, the Presidential Succession Act of 1947 designates the Speaker of the House, then the president pro tempore of the Senate, then Cabinet officers, to act as president. No one below the vice presidency has ever done so.
