Latency (engineering)

Latency, from a general point of view, is a time delay between the cause and the effect of some physical change in the system being observed. In engineering, latency is the time interval between stimulation and response, or, in communication systems, the time it takes for a packet of data to travel from source to destination.

Communication latency

In computer networking, latency is an expression of how much time it takes for a packet of data to get from one designated point to another. It is commonly measured as a round-trip delay — the time from sending a request to receiving a response — and is often reported via the ping utility. Contributors include propagation delay (limited by the speed of light in the medium), transmission delay, queueing delay and processing delay. Latency differs from bandwidth (throughput): a connection can have high bandwidth but also high latency, as with geostationary satellite links (roughly 240 ms one-way propagation).

Audio latency

In audio systems, latency is the delay between an input signal and its audible output. Musicians performing with digital audio workstations generally need round-trip latency below about 10–20 milliseconds to avoid perceptible delay; buffer size is the main trade-off against processing dropouts.

Mechanical and human latency

In mechanical systems, latency appears as dead time in control loops. In human perception, reaction times — simple reaction time to a visual stimulus is roughly 200–250 ms — set lower bounds for interface design, gaming and simulation.
