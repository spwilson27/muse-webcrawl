The Balkan Wars were two conflicts that took place in the Balkan states in 1912 and 1913. In the First Balkan War, the Balkan League — Serbia, Greece, Montenegro and Bulgaria — defeated the Ottoman Empire and divided its remaining European territories among themselves. In the Second Balkan War, Bulgaria attacked its former allies Serbia and Greece, and was in turn defeated by a coalition including Romania and the Ottomans. The wars redrew the map of southeastern Europe and set the stage for the First World War.

First Balkan War (1912–1913)

Encouraged by Ottoman weakness after the Italo-Turkish War, the Balkan League declared war in October 1912. League armies won rapid victories: the Serbs at Kumanovo, the Bulgarians at Kirk Kilisse and Lüleburgaz, and the Greeks advanced into Macedonia and took Salonika (Thessaloniki). The Ottomans sued for peace; the Treaty of London (May 1913) ceded nearly all Ottoman land west of the Enos–Midia line to the allies, and an independent Albania was created under great-power pressure, frustrating Serbia's hope of an Adriatic port.

Second Balkan War (1913)

Quarrels over the division of Macedonia led Bulgaria to attack Serbian and Greek positions in June 1913. Romania invaded from the north and the Ottomans retook Adrianople (Edirne). Defeated on all fronts, Bulgaria signed the Treaty of Bucharest (August 1913): Serbia and Greece kept most of Macedonia, Romania gained Southern Dobruja, and the Ottomans recovered eastern Thrace by the separate Treaty of Constantinople.

Consequences

The wars roughly doubled Serbia's territory, intensified Austro-Serbian antagonism, and left Bulgaria revisionist and aggrieved — factors that contributed to the Sarajevo crisis of 1914. The conflicts were marked by large-scale atrocities against civilians, documented by an international Carnegie Endowment commission, and by mass expulsions that reshaped the region's ethnic geography.
