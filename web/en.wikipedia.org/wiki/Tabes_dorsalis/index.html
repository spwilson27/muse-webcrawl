Tabes dorsalis

Tabes dorsalis
SpecialtyNeurology, infectious disease
SymptomsSensory ataxia, lightning pains, loss of proprioception, Argyll Robertson pupils
CausesUntreated syphilis (Treponema pallidum)
OnsetTypically 15–30 years after initial infection

Tabes dorsalis, also known as syphilitic myelopathy, is a late manifestation of untreated syphilis characterized by slow degeneration of the posterior (dorsal) columns of the spinal cord and the dorsal nerve roots. It is a form of neurosyphilis and of tertiary syphilis. The name derives from the Latin tabes, meaning "wasting," and refers to the withering of the dorsal spinal cord visible at autopsy. Tabes dorsalis was first described in the mid-19th century by Guillaume-Benjamin Duchenne, who called it progressive locomotor ataxia.

Signs and symptoms

Degeneration of the dorsal columns destroys proprioceptive input — the sense of limb position — producing the hallmark sensory ataxia: an unsteady, stamping gait in which patients watch their feet and slap them down to compensate for lost position sense. The gait worsens dramatically in the dark or with eyes closed (a positive Romberg sign). Other characteristic features include:

  • Lightning pains — brief, stabbing pains, often in the legs, caused by involvement of the dorsal roots
  • Loss of vibration and position sense, diminished or absent deep tendon reflexes
  • Argyll Robertson pupils — small pupils that constrict to near focus but not to light ("light-near dissociation")
  • Charcot joints — painless destructive arthropathy from repeated unperceived injury
  • Visceral crises — attacks of severe gastric or laryngeal pain
  • Bladder dysfunction and optic atrophy in some patients

Cause and pathophysiology

Tabes dorsalis results from infection with the spirochete Treponema pallidum subspecies pallidum, usually appearing 15 to 30 years after the initial infection in individuals who never received adequate treatment. Inflammation and demyelination concentrate on the dorsal roots and posterior columns, particularly in the lumbosacral cord. Unlike meningovascular neurosyphilis, organisms are sparse in the affected tissue, and the damage is thought to be substantially inflammatory.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture together with serological testing for syphilis and examination of the cerebrospinal fluid, which typically shows lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein; CSF-VDRL is specific but may be negative in a substantial minority of cases. Treatment is intravenous aqueous penicillin G, which halts progression but cannot reverse established neurological damage. Pain is managed with agents such as carbamazepine or gabapentin. Because of effective antibiotic treatment of early syphilis, tabes dorsalis has become rare in developed countries, though it still occurs, including in association with HIV infection.
