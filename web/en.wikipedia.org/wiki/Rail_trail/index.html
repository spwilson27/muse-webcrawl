Rail trail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rail trail is a shared-use path converted from a disused railway corridor. Because railways were built with gentle gradients and broad curves, their routes make nearly level trails suitable for walking, cycling, horse riding and other non-motorised recreation, often passing over original bridges and through tunnels.

Development

The movement began in the United States in the 1960s — the Elroy–Sparta State Trail in Wisconsin (1967) is often cited as the first — and spread as railway abandonment accelerated. The Rails-to-Trails Conservancy, founded in 1986, promotes "railbanking", in which corridors are preserved for possible future rail use while serving as trails in the meantime. There are now tens of thousands of miles of rail trails in the US, and comparable networks in Britain (many on routes closed by the Beeching cuts), Germany, Australia and elsewhere. Long examples include Missouri's Katy Trail, at about 240 miles one of the longest in the United States.

Issues

Conversions sometimes face opposition from adjoining landowners over privacy, trespass and property rights, and legal questions about whether the original railway easements permit trail use. Trails are typically managed by park agencies or local authorities.
