Andrei Tarkovsky

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Andrei Tarkovsky on the set of Stalker (1979), in the Zone's waterlogged ruins, conferring with his actors]

Andrei Arsenyevich Tarkovsky (4 April 1932 – 29 December 1986) was a Russian film director and screenwriter, widely regarded as one of the greatest and most influential directors in cinema history. In seven features across 27 years he forged a cinema of duration and memory — long takes, water, earth, faith — that stands as the principal alternative to montage in film art.

His films are Ivan's Childhood (1962, Golden Lion at Venice), Andrei Rublev (1966, released 1969), Solaris (1972), Mirror (1975), Stalker (1979), Nostalghia (1983, made in Italy) and The Sacrifice (1986, Sweden). His book Sculpting in Time (1986) sets out his aesthetics: the shot's inner time-pressure, not editing, as cinema's essence.

Under Soviet constraint

The son of the poet Arseny Tarkovsky (whose verse haunts the films), Andrei studied at the VGIK under Mikhail Romm. Soviet authorities shelved, cut and grudgingly exported his films; each release was a negotiation. Mirror — an autobiographical collage of memory, newsreel and dream — was nearly suppressed; Stalker, remade after its first footage was destroyed, acquired the aura of a parable made under curse.

Exile and legacy

Filming Nostalghia in Italy, Tarkovsky announced in 1984 that he would not return to the USSR; The Sacrifice, shot on Gotland with Bergman's collaborators (cinematographer Sven Nykvist, actor Erland Josephson), won the Grand Prix at Cannes while its director was dying of lung cancer in Paris, aged 54. His influence — on slow cinema, on directors from von Trier to Ceylan — grows yearly; the 'Tarkovskian' long take is contemporary art cinema's default poetry.
