Tess of the d'Urbervilles: A Pure Woman Faithfully Presented is a novel by Thomas Hardy, first published in serialised form in the British illustrated newspaper The Graphic in 1891 and in book form the same year. It is now considered one of the great English novels of the 19th century, though it initially met with mixed reviews because it challenged the sexual morality of Victorian England.

Plot

Tess Durbeyfield, the daughter of a poor Wessex family, is sent to claim kinship with the wealthy d'Urberville family after her father learns they may be descendants of an ancient noble line. There she meets the manipulative Alec d'Urberville, who seduces — in the novel's ambiguous treatment, effectively rapes — her, and she returns home pregnant; the child dies in infancy. Later, working as a dairymaid at Talbothays, Tess falls in love with Angel Clare, a parson's son learning farming. They marry, but when Tess confesses her past on their wedding night, Angel — despite his own earlier indiscretion — rejects her and leaves for Brazil. Destitute, Tess is eventually forced back into Alec's keeping. When Angel returns, repentant, Tess murders Alec and flees with Angel; she is captured at Stonehenge, tried, and hanged at Wintoncester.

Themes

The novel is a sustained attack on Victorian double standards of sexual morality: Hardy subtitles Tess "A Pure Woman" in defiance of readers who would condemn her. It also depicts the destruction of the rural peasantry by industrialisation and mechanised agriculture, and is pervaded by Hardy's pessimistic view of fate and an indifferent universe — the famous closing phrase speaks of "the President of the Immortals" ending his sport with Tess.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted many times for stage and screen, including Roman Polanski's film Tess (1979) and BBC television serials in 1998 and 2008. Hardy's fictional Wessex maps closely onto Dorset, and Tess's story is closely tied to real locations such as Dorchester and Stonehenge.
