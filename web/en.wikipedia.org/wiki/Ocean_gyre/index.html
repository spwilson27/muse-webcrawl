An ocean gyre is a large system of circulating ocean currents, driven principally by global wind patterns and the Coriolis effect. The five great subtropical gyres — in the North and South Pacific, the North and South Atlantic, and the Indian Ocean — dominate surface circulation in the open oceans, rotating clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and anticlockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.

Formation

Trade winds and westerlies exert stress on the sea surface at different latitudes. Through Ekman transport — the net movement of surface water at right angles to the wind — water converges toward the centre of each subtropical gyre, piling up a broad, low mound of water. Geostrophic balance between the pressure gradient and the Coriolis effect then drives a steady circulation around the mound. Friction and the variation of the Coriolis effect with latitude intensify currents on the western side of each basin, producing swift western boundary currents such as the Gulf Stream and the Kuroshio.

Types of gyre

  • Subtropical gyres — the five great gyres centred near 30° latitude in each ocean basin.
  • Subpolar gyres — smaller circulations at high latitudes, such as the Weddell and Ross gyres near Antarctica and the gyres of the North Pacific and North Atlantic.
  • Tropical gyres — circulations near the equator shaped by the trade winds.

The five major gyres

The North Pacific Gyre, the largest, is bounded by the Kuroshio, North Pacific, California and North Equatorial currents. Its Atlantic counterpart is bounded by the Gulf Stream, North Atlantic, Canary and North Equatorial currents and encloses the Sargasso Sea. The Indian Ocean Gyre rotates anticlockwise and includes the Agulhas and West Australian currents.

Ecology and debris accumulation

Convergence at gyre centres concentrates floating material. Downwelling in these regions produces some of the clearest, most nutrient-poor (oligotrophic) waters on Earth, sometimes called "ocean deserts", yet they support specialised ecosystems. The same convergence accumulates floating plastic debris, most notoriously the Great Pacific garbage patch in the North Pacific Gyre.
