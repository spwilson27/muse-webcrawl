Triplane

A triplane is a fixed-wing aircraft equipped with three vertically stacked wing planes. The arrangement allowed a large wing area — and thus lift — within a short wingspan, giving good manoeuvrability and rate of climb while keeping the structure light enough for the low-powered engines of the pioneer and First World War eras. Triplanes enjoyed a brief vogue, chiefly in 1916–1917, before being superseded by more powerful biplanes and monoplanes.


The Fokker Dr.I, the most famous triplane

Famous triplanes

The British Sopwith Triplane of 1916 impressed with its climb and agility and directly inspired the German Fokker Dr.I, the machine associated with Manfred von Richthofen, the "Red Baron", who scored his final 19 victories in it. Other examples include the Sopwith Camel's triplane prototypes, the large Caproni Ca.4 bomber and passenger types, and experimental Curtiss and Nieuport designs. Outside the war, A. V. Roe's Roe I and Roe II triplanes were among the earliest British aircraft, and the 1909 Roe triplane was the first all-British aircraft to fly.

Advantages and disadvantages

Stacking three short-chord wings reduced span for a given area — aiding roll rate and structural stiffness — but interference between the wings increased drag and the configuration gave a restricted view for the pilot in some designs. As engine power rose, the simpler biplane achieved the same performance with less drag, and the triplane disappeared from front-line service by 1918.
