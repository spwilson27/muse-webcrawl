Bernard Bolzano

Bernard Bolzano (full name Bernardus Placidus Johann Nepomuk Bolzano; 5 October 1781 – 18 December 1848) was a Bohemian mathematician, logician, philosopher, theologian and Catholic priest of Italian extraction, known for his contributions to mathematical analysis and the philosophy of mathematics. Writing mostly in German and largely unrecognised in his lifetime, he anticipated ideas later credited to Weierstrass, Cantor and others.

Life

Bolzano studied at Charles University in Prague, was ordained a priest in 1805, and took the newly created chair of philosophy of religion there. His liberal sermons and teaching brought him into conflict with the Austrian authorities; he was dismissed from his chair in 1819 and placed under restrictions, spending much of his later life in retirement under the protection of friends, writing prodigiously.

Mathematics

In his 1817 pamphlet Rein analytischer Beweis ("Purely analytic proof"), Bolzano gave the first rigorous, purely arithmetic proof of the intermediate value theorem, and he developed the epsilon-style definitions of limit and continuity later associated with Cauchy and Weierstrass. His name attaches to the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem (every bounded infinite sequence in Rn has a convergent subsequence). He also constructed an early example of a continuous nowhere-differentiable function, and his posthumous Paradoxes of the Infinite (1851) explored the properties of infinite sets decades before Cantor.

Philosophy

Bolzano's magnum opus, the Wissenschaftslehre (Theory of Science, 1837), developed a theory of "propositions in themselves" (Sätze an sich) — objective abstract contents independent of being thought — and a rigorous account of logical consequence and ground-consequence relations that influenced Husserl and the analytic tradition, partly through the mediation of his student Robert Zimmermann and the philosopher Franz Brentano's circle.
