Amoraim

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Amoraim (Aramaic: אמוראים, singular Amora) were Jewish scholars of the period from about 200 to 500 CE, who "said" or "told over" the teachings of the Oral Torah. They were principally concerned with elucidating the Mishnah, the compilation of Rabbi Judah the Prince, and their discussions form the body of the Gemara, which together with the Mishnah constitutes the Talmud.

Periods and schools

The Amoraim worked in two centres: the Land of Israel (Jerusalem, Tiberias, Sepphoris, and Caesarea) and Babylonia (Nehardea, Sura, and Pumbedita). Their era is conventionally divided into generations — five in Babylonia and seven or eight in Israel. Famous Amoraim include Rav and Shmuel, founders of the Babylonian academies of Nehardea and Sura; Rav Huna; Rabba and Rav Yosef of Pumbedita; Abaye and Rava, whose dialectical debates (Havayot de-Abaye ve-Rava) are a hallmark of the Babylonian Talmud; and in Israel, Rabbi Yochanan and Resh Lakish.

Role of the Amora

The term originally described the scholar's spokesman or interpreter, who voiced the rabbi's lecture to the audience in a loud, clear form. Over time it came to denote the sages themselves. The Amoraim were succeeded by the Savoraim, who gave the Talmud its final form.
