Living Constitution

In United States constitutional law, the Living Constitution (or loose constructionism) is the claim that the Constitution has a dynamic meaning and that contemporary society should be taken into account when interpreting key constitutional provisions. The theory is commonly associated with the Warren Court era and with the view that judges may interpret open-textured clauses — such as the Equal Protection Clause and the Due Process Clauses — in light of evolving social conditions and values.

Overview

Proponents argue that the framers deliberately used broad language capable of growth, and that constitutional interpretation must adapt to circumstances the framers could not have foreseen, such as new technologies and changing social mores. The phrase is often credited to Howard Lee McBain's 1927 book The Living Constitution, though the idea predates the term; Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. wrote in Missouri v. Holland (1920) that the Constitution should be considered "in the light of our whole experience and not merely in that of what was said a hundred years ago."

Contrast with originalism

The Living Constitution is most often contrasted with originalism, which holds that the Constitution's meaning is fixed at the time of ratification, and with textualism more generally. Critics of the living-constitution approach, such as Justice Antonin Scalia, argued that it licenses judges to impose their own policy preferences and that the proper mechanism for constitutional change is the amendment process under Article V. Defenders reply that some degree of adaptation is unavoidable, pointing to decisions such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which struck down racial segregation in public schools.

"We must never forget that it is a constitution we are expounding... a constitution intended to endure for ages to come, and consequently to be adapted to the various crises of human affairs." — Chief Justice John Marshall, McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)
