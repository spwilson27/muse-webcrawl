No-ball

In cricket, a no-ball is a delivery which does not count as one of the bowler's six legal deliveries in an over, because it infringes the Laws of the game. The batting side is awarded one run (an extra) — two in some limited-overs variants — and the ball must be bowled again. A batter cannot be dismissed off a no-ball except by being run out, handling the ball, hitting the ball twice, or obstructing the field.

Types of no-ball

The most common infringement is the front-foot no-ball, where the bowler's front foot lands wholly beyond the popping crease with no part behind it. Others include:

  • The back foot landing on or outside the return crease.
  • The bowler throwing (chucking) rather than bowling the ball.
  • A full toss passing above the batter's waist height, or a short ball passing above head height.
  • The ball pitching more than twice before reaching the batter, or rolling along the ground.
  • Fielding-side infringements, such as having too many fielders behind square on the leg side or breaking the fielding restrictions in limited-overs cricket.
  • The wicket-keeper encroaching in front of the stumps before the ball is played or passes.

Free hit

In One Day Internationals and Twenty20 matches, a foot-fault no-ball is followed by a free hit: the next delivery cannot dismiss the batter except by run out (and certain other rare methods), allowing an unrestricted swing. The rule was introduced by the ICC in 2007 and adopted in Test cricket's shorter formats to penalise foot faults more severely.

Signalling and adjudication

The umpire calls "no-ball" and signals by extending one arm horizontally. Front-foot no-balls in televised internationals are now checked automatically by the TV umpire under ICC playing conditions.
