Qui tam

Qui tam is a provision of law allowing a private individual, called a relator, to bring a lawsuit on behalf of a government against persons or companies that have defrauded it, and to share in any recovery. The term abbreviates the Latin qui tam pro domino rege quam pro se ipso in hac parte sequitur — "who sues in this matter for the king as well as for himself".

In the United States

The principal modern vehicle is the False Claims Act, originally enacted in 1863 ("Lincoln's Law") during the Civil War to combat fraud by contractors supplying the Union Army, and significantly strengthened in 1986, 2009 and 2010. A relator files the complaint under seal; the Department of Justice investigates and decides whether to intervene. Successful relators typically receive 15–30 percent of the recovery, and defendants face treble damages and per-claim penalties. Billions of dollars, especially in healthcare and defence procurement fraud cases, have been recovered.

History and other jurisdictions

Qui tam actions existed in medieval English law and colonial America, fell into disuse in England (abolished for most purposes in 1951), but flourished in the United States. Analogous informer-reward mechanisms appear in securities (SEC), tax (IRS) and other whistleblower programmes.
