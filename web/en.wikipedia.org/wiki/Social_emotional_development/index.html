Social emotional development

Social emotional development represents a specific domain of child development. It is a gradual, integrative process through which children acquire the capacity to understand, experience, express, and manage emotions and to develop meaningful relationships with others. As such, social emotional development encompasses a large range of skills and constructs, including self-awareness, joint attention, play, theory of mind, self-esteem, emotion regulation, friendships, and identity development.

Infancy and attachment

Social emotional development emerges in the earliest exchanges between infants and caregivers. Secure attachment, described by John Bowlby and measured by Mary Ainsworth, provides a foundation for later relationships and emotional regulation. Infants progress from primary intersubjectivity — reciprocal face-to-face interaction — to joint attention and social referencing by the end of the first year.

Early childhood

In toddlerhood and the preschool years children develop self-conscious emotions such as pride, shame, and guilt, expand their emotional vocabulary, and begin pretend play and early friendships. Erik Erikson's stages describe the crises of autonomy versus shame and doubt, and initiative versus guilt, during this period.

Middle childhood and adolescence

School-age children refine emotion-regulation strategies, perspective-taking, and peer competence. Adolescence adds identity formation, more complex peer hierarchies, and romantic relationships, alongside heightened sensitivity to social evaluation.

Assessment and intervention

Social-emotional learning (SEL) programs in schools aim to teach self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making. Meta-analyses associate participation in SEL programs with improved academic achievement and reduced conduct problems.
