The stroboscopic effect is a visual phenomenon caused by aliasing that occurs when continuous rotational or repetitive motion is represented by a series of short or instantaneous samples—as by a stroboscopic light or a camera shutter—at a sampling rate close to the period of the motion. Under the right conditions, moving objects appear slowed, frozen, or even reversed.

Explanation

If an object rotates once per second and is illuminated only in brief flashes once per second, it is always lit at the same point in its cycle and appears stationary. Flashing slightly faster makes the object appear to drift slowly backwards; flashing slightly slower makes it drift forwards. The same effect makes wagon wheels and aircraft propellers in films appear to rotate slowly or in reverse (the "wagon-wheel effect"), because the camera samples at a fixed frame rate.

Applications

The stroboscope—an instrument producing adjustable regular flashes—exploits the effect to measure rotation speed and to study vibrating machinery, vocal cords (videostroboscopy in medicine), and fluid motion without contact. Stroboscopic tachometers and timing lights for engine ignition are standard engineering tools. The principle also underlies nineteenth-century motion toys such as the phenakistoscope and zoetrope, and hence animation and cinema.

Hazards

In industrial settings lit by flickering discharge lamps, rotating machinery can appear stationary or slow when its motion synchronises with the light's flicker, creating a serious safety hazard; regulations on lighting address this. Flashing lights in certain frequency ranges (roughly 3–30 Hz) can also trigger seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy, which informs broadcast and web-content guidelines.
