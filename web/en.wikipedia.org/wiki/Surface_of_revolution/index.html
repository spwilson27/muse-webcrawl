Surface of revolution

Surface of revolution
Generating a surface of revolution from a plane curve
FieldGeometry, calculus
Generated byRotating a plane curve (generatrix) about an axis in its plane
ExamplesSphere, cylinder, cone, torus, hyperboloid, catenoid

A surface of revolution is a surface in Euclidean space created by rotating a curve (the generatrix) around an axis of rotation in the same plane. Familiar examples include the sphere (from rotating a semicircle about its diameter), the right circular cylinder and cone (from a line parallel to, or meeting, the axis), the torus (from a circle rotated about an external axis), and the hyperboloid of one sheet.

Parametrisation

If the generating curve is given in the xz-plane by x = φ(t), z = ψ(t), rotation about the z-axis yields the parametrisation x = φ(t) sin θ, y = φ(t) cos θ, z = ψ(t), where θ ranges over an angle of rotation. The parallels (circles traced by points of the curve) and meridians (copies of the generating curve) form a natural coordinate grid on the surface. Such surfaces are central to the calculus of areas and volumes taught via the disk, washer and shell methods.

Area and volume

The classical Pappus centroid theorems state that the surface area generated by rotating a plane curve about an external axis equals the product of the arc length of the curve and the distance travelled by its centroid, and that the volume of the resulting solid of revolution equals the product of the generating area and the distance travelled by its centroid. In integral form, the area of a surface generated by rotating y = f(x) about the x-axis is 2π ∫ f(x) √(1 + (f′(x))²) dx. Gabriel's horn — generated by rotating y = 1/x for x ≥ 1 — famously has finite volume but infinite surface area.

Properties and applications

Surfaces of revolution have rotational symmetry, constant Gaussian curvature along parallels, and their geodesics obey Clairaut's relation. Minimal surfaces of revolution (other than the plane) are exactly the catenoids. Rotationally symmetric shapes are ubiquitous in engineering because they are what a lathe produces; pressure vessels, bells, lenses, nozzles and cooling towers (hyperboloids) are all surfaces of revolution.
