Flight attendant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A flight attendant in uniform welcoming passengers at an aircraft door]
Cabin crew greeting passengers.

A flight attendant (cabin crew, historically steward/stewardess or air hostess) is a member of an airliner\'s crew employed to ensure passenger safety and comfort. Their primary legal duty is safety: conducting demonstrations, securing the cabin, and commanding evacuations — cabin crew must be able to evacuate a full aircraft in 90 seconds in certification conditions. The first flight attendant was Ellen Church, a nurse hired by United Air Lines (Boeing Air Transport) in 1930.

Role and training

Training covers evacuation on land and water (ditching), fire fighting, first aid, dangerous goods, security and survival, with recurrent checks. On board, crew brief, check emergency equipment, serve passengers, and manage disruptions; senior pursers lead the cabin team and liaise with the flight deck.

History

Early \'stewards\' were often cabin boys from ships; Ellen Church persuaded Boeing to hire nurses in 1930, and the profession was long female, glamourised and restricted by marriage and age rules until lawsuits and unions dismantled them in the 1960s–80s. The term \'flight attendant\' became standard in the 1970s.

Working life

Crews work to strict flight-time limitations, bid for rosters by seniority, and endure jet lag and time-zone disruption. Unionisation is high in North America and Europe; minimum crew numbers are set by regulation (typically one attendant per 50 passenger seats).
