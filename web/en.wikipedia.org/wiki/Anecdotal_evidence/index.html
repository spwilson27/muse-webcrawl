Anecdotal evidence

"Anecdotal evidence" is based on individual accounts rather than systematic data.

Anecdotal evidence is evidence based only on personal observation, collected in a casual or non-systematic manner. The term is often used in contrast to scientific evidence, such as evidence from controlled experiments or statistical samples. Anecdotes may be true and even useful for generating hypotheses, but they cannot by themselves establish general claims, because a small, self-selected sample is subject to chance, bias, and confounding factors.

Characteristics

An anecdote is a short account of a particular incident or event. When offered as proof — "my grandfather smoked all his life and lived to 95" — it illustrates several known weaknesses: the sample size is one; memorable cases are more likely to be reported (availability bias); and no comparison group exists. In law, by contrast, testimony about personal experience is a formal category of evidence, judged by standards of credibility rather than statistics. In science communication, critics of pseudoscience object that testimonials are used to support claims about alternative medicine, paranormal phenomena, and extraordinary events where controlled evidence is lacking.

Related concepts

The phrase "the plural of anecdote is not data" is commonly attributed in various forms to demographer and pollster Frank Luntz's predecessors and to political scientist Raymond Wolfinger. Anecdotal evidence is related to the informal fallacy of hasty generalization, in which a conclusion is drawn from an unrepresentative sample, and to cherry picking, the selection of favourable cases only.
