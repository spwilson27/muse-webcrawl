John Milton Hay (8 October 1838 – 1 July 1905) was an American statesman and official whose government career stretched over half a century. Beginning as private secretary to President Abraham Lincoln, he rose to become Secretary of State under Presidents William McKinley and Theodore Roosevelt, where he authored the Open Door Policy toward China and negotiated the treaty that made the Panama Canal possible. He was also a noted man of letters, co-author of the standard early biography of Lincoln.

Early life and the Lincoln White House

Hay was born in Salem, Indiana, and raised in Warsaw, Illinois. A brilliant student at Brown University (class of 1858), he read law in Springfield in the office next to Abraham Lincoln's. When Lincoln won the presidency in 1860, Hay went to Washington as his assistant private secretary, sharing with John G. Nicolay the daily management of the President's office and observing the Civil War at the closest quarters. His wartime letters and diaries are a prime source for Lincoln scholarship. Briefly, Hay also served as a Union army officer in Florida and South Carolina.

Diplomat and author

After Lincoln's assassination Hay held diplomatic posts in Paris, Vienna and Madrid, then returned to journalism and literature in New York. His Pike County Ballads (1871) made his name as a dialect poet, and his novel The Bread-Winners (1883) appeared anonymously. With Nicolay he wrote the monumental ten-volume Abraham Lincoln: A History (1890), based on Lincoln's papers entrusted to them by Robert Todd Lincoln. He served as Assistant Secretary of State (1879–81) and as Ambassador to Britain (1897–98), famously preaching Anglo-American rapprochement.

Secretary of State

Appointed Secretary of State by McKinley in 1898, Hay's signature achievement was the Open Door notes of 1899–1900, declaring that the powers with spheres of influence in China should preserve equal trading rights and Chinese territorial integrity — a doctrine that anchored American China policy for decades. During the Boxer Rebellion he helped keep the settlement moderate. He negotiated the Hay–Pauncefote Treaty (1901) clearing the way for an American-built canal, won ratification of the Hay–Herrán Treaty and then the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty (1903) with the new republic of Panama, and helped settle the Alaska boundary dispute. He died in office on 1 July 1905.
