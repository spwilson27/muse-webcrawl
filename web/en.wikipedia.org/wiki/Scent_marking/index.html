Scent marking

Scent marking, also known as territorial marking or simply marking, is a behaviour used by animals to identify and advertise their territory. It is a form of olfactory communication in which chemical signals — in urine, faeces or secretions from specialised scent glands — are deposited on objects in the environment.

Mechanisms

Many mammals possess specialised scent glands, such as the anal glands of carnivores, the preorbital glands of deer, and the pedal (foot) glands of various ungulates. Marking behaviours include urine spraying, as in domestic cats and many canids; rubbing the face or body against trees and rocks, as in bears and big cats; scraping the ground with the hind feet; and depositing dung in prominent middens, as do hippopotamuses and some antelopes.

Functions

Scent marks may advertise the identity, sex, reproductive condition, age and social status of the marker. For territorial species, marks help neighbours recognise boundaries and can reduce the need for costly fights — the so-called "dear enemy" effect. Marks may also function in mate attraction and in coordinating group movements. Because scent persists after the animal has left, marking allows communication across time as well as space, and many species counter-mark or over-mark the marks of rivals.

Examples

Wolves urinate on raised objects at territory edges; male ring-tailed lemurs engage in "stink fights", anointing their tails with wrist and shoulder gland secretions; and giant pandas perform handstand urinations to place marks high on tree trunks.
