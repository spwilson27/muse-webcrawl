The electrical telegraph is a point-to-point text messaging system, now historical, in which electrical signals sent over wires coded messages — most famously in Morse code — between telegraph offices. First commercialised in the late 1830s and 1840s, it was the first practical use of electricity for communication and the first technology to separate communication from physical transport, allowing messages to cross continents in minutes. It laid the foundation for the telephone, radio and the internet, and shaped news, finance, railways and warfare for over a century.

Development

Early experiments by Pavel Schilling, Carl Friedrich Gauss and Wilhelm Weber preceded the commercial systems. In Britain, William Fothergill Cooke and Charles Wheatstone patented a needle telegraph in 1837, adopted by railways from 1839. In the United States, Samuel Morse, with Alfred Vail and Leonard Gale, demonstrated his recording telegraph and code; the federally funded Washington–Baltimore line opened in 1844 with the message "WHAT HATH GOD WROUGHT". Submarine cables followed: the Dover–Calais cable of 1851 and, after failures, the durable transatlantic cable of 1866, which cut London–New York communication from weeks to minutes.

Operation and decline

Operators tapped out messages on a key, later superseded by printing telegraphs, stock tickers, the telex network and automated systems. Telegraph companies such as Western Union built vast networks; standardised time, wire-service journalism and terse "telegraphic" prose were among its cultural effects. The telephone, then telex, fax, email and mobile messaging displaced it: Western Union sent its last telegram in 2006 and India's state service, once the world's largest, closed in 2013.
