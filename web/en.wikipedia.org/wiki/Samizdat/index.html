Samizdat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Samizdat (Russian: самиздат, “self-publishing”) was the clandestine copying and distribution of literature and documents banned by the state in the Soviet Union and the Eastern Bloc: works were typed with carbon paper (each typing yielding a handful of legible copies), photographed or mimeographed, and passed hand to hand, with each reader potentially becoming a reproducer. Its catalogue ranged from poetry (Akhmatova's Requiem) and novels (The Gulag Archipelago, circulated 1968–73 before Paris publication) to human-rights bulletins (the Moscow Chronicle of Current Events, 1968–82) and the Czech and Polish underground editions that kept Václav Havel, Charter 77 and Solidarity's literature alive.

Samizdat was slow, dangerous (possession could mean prosecution for “anti-Soviet agitation”) and socially transformative: it created the readership and trust networks of the dissident movements and a parallel public sphere that historians credit with hollowing out regime legitimacy before 1989. Its variants named the binding and channel — tamizdat (“published over there”, smuggled back), magnitizdat (tape-recorded songs) — and its ethos is routinely claimed as an ancestor of internet file-sharing and dissident publishing worldwide.
