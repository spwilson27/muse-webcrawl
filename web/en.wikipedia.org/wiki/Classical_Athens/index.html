Classical Athens

Classical Athens was the Greek city-state of Athens during the Classical period, roughly from 508 BC, when Cleisthenes established democracy, to 323 BC, the death of Alexander the Great. It was the leading cultural, naval and intellectual power of the Greek world.

History

After the reforms of Cleisthenes, Athens developed a radical democracy in which adult male citizens voted directly in the Assembly (Ekklesia) on the Pnyx. During the Greco-Persian Wars Athens led the Delian League, whose treasury it gradually converted into an Athenian empire.

The age of Pericles saw the building of the Parthenon and the flowering of drama, philosophy and historiography — Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Aristophanes, Thucydides, Socrates and Plato were all associated with the city. The long Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) against Sparta ended in defeat, plague and the loss of empire, though Athens recovered a democracy and remained Greece's cultural capital into the Hellenistic age.

Government and society

Citizens served by lot on the Council of 500 (Boule), in juries, and as magistrates; generals (strategoi) were elected. Women, slaves and resident foreigners (metics) were excluded from politics, though metics were central to trade and craft. The economy rested on Attic silver from Laurion, maritime trade through Piraeus, and tribute.

Culture

Festivals such as the Panathenaea and the City Dionysia structured civic life. Athenian pottery, sculpture and architecture set standards imitated across the Mediterranean.
