Pot-au-feu

Pot-au-feu ("pot on the fire") is a classic French boiled beef dish, in which tough cuts of beef are simmered for hours with root vegetables to produce two courses: a rich, clear broth served first, followed by the sliced meat and vegetables, traditionally accompanied by coarse salt, mustard, cornichons, and horseradish. Chef Raymond Blanc has called it "the quintessence of French family cuisine" and arguably France's national dish.

Preparation

A proper pot-au-feu uses several contrasting cuts — lean, fatty, and gelatinous (such as shin, brisket, plate, oxtail, or cheek) — plus marrow bones, whose marrow is scooped onto toast with salt as a delicacy. Vegetables (leeks tied in bundles, carrots, turnips, celery, and an onion studded with cloves and charred for color) are added at staggered times so each is correctly cooked; potatoes are often boiled separately. The liquid is never allowed to boil hard, keeping the broth clear.

History

The dish descends from medieval and peasant cookery, where a pot perpetually on the fire received whatever was available — King Henri IV's wish that every laborer might have a "poule au pot" (chicken in the pot) on Sundays expresses the same ideal. Regional and bourgeois versions developed across France, with variants using chicken, pork, or sausage.

Cultural significance

Pot-au-feu anchors French Sunday family meals and appears throughout French literature, from seventeenth-century letters to modern memoirs. Comparable boiled dinners worldwide — Italian bollito misto, Spanish cocido, and the New England boiled dinner — reflect the same technique. The broth also serves as a base for soups and for the next day's leftovers.
