Computational theory of mind

The computational theory of mind (CTM) is a family of views in philosophy of mind holding that the human mind is an information-processing system and that cognition and consciousness are forms of computation. On this view, mental states are computational states, and thinking is the manipulation of symbols according to rules.

History

The theory emerged in the 1960s with the rise of cognitive science and early artificial intelligence. Hilary Putnam proposed an early version by analogy with Turing machines, and Jerry Fodor developed the influential representational theory of mind, which posits a "language of thought" (Mentalese): mental representations with syntactic structure over which computational processes operate. CTM underpins classical cognitivism and functionalism.

Criticisms

Critics include John Searle, whose Chinese room argument contends that symbol manipulation alone cannot produce understanding or intentionality. Connectionists argued that cognition is better modelled by parallel neural networks than by serial symbol processing, while embodied and enactive approaches stress the role of the body and environment. Defenders respond with refined versions of the theory or hybrid models.
