A joint or articulation (or articular surface) is the connection made between bones, ossicles, or other hard structures in the body which link an animal's skeletal system into a functional whole. Joints are constructed to allow for different degrees and types of movement. Some joints, such as the knee, elbow and shoulder, are self-lubricating and almost frictionless, and can withstand compression and heavy loads while still executing smooth and precise movements. The scientific study of joints is arthrology; the medical treatment of joint disorders is mainly the province of rheumatology and orthopaedics.

Classification

Structural classification

Joints are classified structurally by the connective tissue that binds them:

  • Fibrous joints — bound by dense connective tissue, allowing little or no movement (e.g. the sutures of the skull, the gomphosis anchoring a tooth in its socket).
  • Cartilaginous joints — bound by cartilage; these include synchondroses (hyaline cartilage, e.g. growth plates) and symphyses (fibrocartilage, e.g. the pubic symphysis and intervertebral discs).
  • Synovial joints — the bones are separated by a cavity filled with synovial fluid enclosed in a joint capsule; these are the most common and most movable joints.

Functional classification

Functionally, joints are grouped by their range of motion: synarthroses (immovable), amphiarthroses (slightly movable) and diarthroses (freely movable, comprising the synovial joints).

Synovial joints

Synovial joints have a joint cavity filled with synovial fluid, articular (hyaline) cartilage covering the bone ends, and a fibrous capsule lined by a synovial membrane; many also contain ligaments, menisci, bursae and fat pads. They are subdivided by shape and movement: ball-and-socket (shoulder, hip), hinge (elbow, knee), pivot (atlanto-axial), condyloid, saddle (thumb carpometacarpal) and plane (gliding) joints.

Clinical significance

Common joint disorders include osteoarthritis (degenerative cartilage loss), rheumatoid arthritis and other autoimmune arthritides, gout and septic arthritis; injuries include sprains, dislocations and meniscal tears. Severely damaged joints may be replaced by prostheses in arthroplasty, most commonly at the hip and knee. The number of joints in the adult human body is often given as around 360, depending on how sesamoid bones and skull sutures are counted.
