Two-body problem

In classical mechanics, the two-body problem is to determine the motion of two point particles that interact only with each other, typically through a central force such as gravity. Unlike the three-body problem, it is completely solvable: the motion reduces to that of a single particle of reduced mass moving in a central potential, and for inverse-square forces the orbits are conic sections — the content of Kepler's laws.

Two-body problem
FieldClassical mechanics, celestial mechanics
Key conceptReduced mass; barycentre
SolutionConic-section orbits (Kepler problem)

Reduction to one body

The centre of mass of the two bodies moves in a straight line at constant velocity. Subtracting the centre-of-mass motion leaves a single equation for the relative position vector r, with the two masses replaced by the reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂). Angular momentum conservation confines the motion to a plane, and energy conservation further restricts it.

The Kepler problem

When the force is gravitational (or electrostatic), the relative orbit is a conic section with the centre of mass at one focus: an ellipse for bound systems, a parabola or hyperbola for unbound encounters. Each body traces a scaled copy of the same orbit about the barycentre. This solution underlies the analysis of binary stars, planetary orbits and spacecraft trajectories, while quantum mechanics applies the same reduction to the hydrogen atom, whose spectrum it predicts exactly.
