Composting toilet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A waterless composting toilet installation

A composting toilet is a type of dry toilet that treats human waste by a biological process called composting. This process leads to the decomposition of organic matter and turns human waste into compost-like material. It is carried out by microorganisms (mainly bacteria and fungi) under aerobic conditions. Most composting toilets use no water for flushing, or very little, and are therefore a type of dry toilet — unlike flush toilets, they are not connected to a sewer system or septic tank.

How it works

Waste falls into a composting chamber containing a carbon-rich bulking material such as sawdust, coconut coir, or peat moss, which balances the nitrogen-rich waste, absorbs liquid, and allows air to circulate. Ventilation — often with a small fan — removes odours and excess moisture and helps maintain aerobic conditions. Many designs separate urine from faeces (urine diversion), which reduces odour and keeps the composting mass from becoming too wet. Over weeks to months, microorganisms break the material down; finished compost is periodically removed from a separate finishing drawer or chamber. Properly managed, the process destroys most pathogens, though guidance in many jurisdictions is to use the end product only on ornamental plants rather than food crops.

Applications

Composting toilets are used where water is scarce or where sewerage is unavailable or impractical: in cabins, boats, recreational vehicles, roadside rest areas, national parks, and off-grid homes, and as part of ecological sanitation (EcoSan) projects in developing countries. Commercial systems range from small self-contained units under the seat to large central systems (such as the Swedish Clivus Multrum, developed in the 1930s–1960s) serving whole buildings through chutes.

Advantages and challenges

Advantages include large water savings, no need for sewer infrastructure, and recovery of nutrients as a soil conditioner. Challenges include the need for regular user maintenance (adding bulking material, emptying finished compost), managing moisture balance, ensuring adequate pathogen reduction, and complying with local building and health codes, which in some places restrict or do not recognize composting toilets.
