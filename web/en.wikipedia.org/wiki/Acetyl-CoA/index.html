Acetyl-CoA

Acetyl-CoA
NamesAcetyl coenzyme A
FormulaC23H38N7O17P3S
Molar mass809.57 g/mol
RoleCentral metabolite; acetyl-group carrier

Acetyl-CoA (acetyl coenzyme A) is a molecule that participates in many biochemical reactions in protein, carbohydrate, and lipid metabolism. Its main function is to deliver the acetyl group to the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) to be oxidized for energy production. It is one of the most central hub metabolites in the cell, linking catabolic pathways to biosynthesis.

Structure

Acetyl-CoA consists of an acetyl group (CH3–C(=O)–) attached through a high-energy thioester bond to the thiol of coenzyme A. Coenzyme A itself is built from pantothenic acid (vitamin B5), cysteine, and an ADP moiety. The thioester bond is energy-rich: its hydrolysis releases about as much free energy as the hydrolysis of ATP.

Production

Acetyl-CoA is generated by several routes:

  • Oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, the bridge between glycolysis and the citric acid cycle.
  • Beta-oxidation of fatty acids in the mitochondria and peroxisomes.
  • Catabolism of certain amino acids and of ethanol.
  • In the cytosol, cleavage of citrate by ATP citrate lyase, supplying acetyl units for fatty-acid and cholesterol synthesis.

Uses in the cell

Beyond oxidation in the citric acid cycle, acetyl-CoA is the building block for the synthesis of fatty acids and cholesterol, a precursor of ketone bodies in the liver during fasting, and the acetyl donor in acetylation reactions, including the acetylation of histones that regulates gene expression.

History

Coenzyme A was discovered by Fritz Lipmann in the 1940s as the heat-stable factor required for acetylation reactions; Lipmann shared the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in part for this work.
