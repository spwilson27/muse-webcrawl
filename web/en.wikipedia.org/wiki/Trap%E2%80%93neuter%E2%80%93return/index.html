Trap–neuter–return

Trap–neuter–return (TNR), also known as trap–neuter–release, is a humane method of managing populations of feral cats. Cats are humanely trapped, evaluated, spayed or neutered by a veterinarian, vaccinated, and then returned to the outdoor location where they were found, where caretakers may provide food and monitoring. Kittens and friendly adult cats are typically removed for adoption.

Practice

TNR programmes commonly mark treated cats by "ear tipping" — the painless removal of the tip of one ear under anaesthesia — so that already-neutered cats can be identified at a distance. Supporters argue that stabilised colonies decline over time through natural attrition, that neutered cats are less noisy and less prone to roaming and fighting, and that TNR avoids the continual repopulation ("vacuum effect") that follows removal-only approaches.

Debate

The effectiveness and acceptability of TNR are debated. Wildlife and conservation organisations have criticised it because free-roaming cats prey on birds and other wildlife, and some studies question whether typical programmes sterilise enough cats to reduce populations. Animal welfare organisations counter that TNR is the only humane and practical long-term approach. The method originated in Europe, with schemes in the United Kingdom from the 1950s and Denmark in the 1970s, and spread to the United States from the 1980s onward.
