Child discipline is the system of methods parents and caregivers use to teach children self-control, acceptable behaviour, and respect for rules and social norms. The word derives from the Latin disciplina, "teaching". Modern developmental psychology distinguishes discipline — guiding and teaching — from punishment, and research generally favours consistent, warm, explanation-based ("authoritative") approaches over harsh or purely punitive ones.

Methods

Common non-physical methods include positive reinforcement of desired behaviour (praise and rewards), setting clear rules and routines, redirecting young children, time-outs (brief removal from a stimulating situation), and natural or logical consequences related to the misbehaviour. Parenting styles research, originating with Diana Baumrind, contrasts authoritative (high warmth, high control), authoritarian (low warmth, high control), permissive, and neglectful styles, finding authoritative parenting associated with the most favourable outcomes in most studies.

Corporal punishment

Physical punishment such as spanking was historically near-universal but has become increasingly contested. Numerous studies associate corporal punishment with increased aggression and poorer mental health outcomes, and major paediatric bodies advise against it. As of the 2020s, more than 60 countries — beginning with Sweden in 1979 — have prohibited all corporal punishment of children, including by parents in the home, while it remains lawful in the home in many other jurisdictions. School corporal punishment is banned in most countries but persists in parts of the United States and elsewhere.

Cultural and legal variation

Norms about discipline vary widely across cultures and have shifted substantially over the past century toward less physical and more communicative methods. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child obliges states to protect children from all forms of physical or mental violence.
