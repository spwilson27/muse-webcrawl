George Arthur Akerlof (born 17 June 1940) is an American economist and university professor at Georgetown University and the University of California, Berkeley. He shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz for analyses of markets with asymmetric information.

His 1970 paper 'The Market for Lemons: Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism' — rejected by three journals before the Quarterly Journal of Economics published it — showed how asymmetric information between buyers and sellers can unravel a market entirely, and became one of the most cited economics papers of the century. Akerlof's later work brought identity, norms, fairness and 'animal spirits' (with Robert Shiller, 2009) into macroeconomics, challenging purely rational-agent models.

The lemons problem

If sellers know a used car's quality but buyers cannot distinguish good cars from 'lemons', buyers will pay only an average price — driving good cars out of the market, lowering the average further, and potentially collapsing trade altogether. The analysis explains real institutions — warranties, brands, licensing, disclosure laws — as responses to information asymmetry, and applies to insurance, credit and labour markets.
