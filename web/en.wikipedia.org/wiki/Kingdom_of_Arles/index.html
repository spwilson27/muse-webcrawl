The Kingdom of Arles (also called the Kingdom of Burgundy or, in Latin, the Regnum Arelatense) was a medieval kingdom formed in 933 by the union of the kingdoms of Upper and Lower Burgundy. Centred on the city of Arles in Provence, it comprised much of south-eastern France and western Switzerland between the Rhône, the Alps and the Mediterranean. From 1033 it formed one of the constituent kingdoms of the Holy Roman Empire, alongside Germany and Italy, though imperial authority in it steadily weakened and its lands were gradually absorbed by France.

Formation

After the fragmentation of the Carolingian Empire, two Burgundian kingdoms emerged: Upper Burgundy (Transjurane Burgundy, ruled from Saint-Maurice) and Lower Burgundy (Provence and Cisjurane Burgundy). In 933 Hugh of Arles, king of Italy and effective ruler of Lower Burgundy, ceded Lower Burgundy to Rudolph II of Upper Burgundy in exchange for Rudolph's withdrawal from Italy — creating the united kingdom, named after its principal city and coronation site, Arles.

Imperial period

In 1032 the last independent king, Rudolph III, died childless, having named the Emperor Conrad II as his heir. After a succession war, Conrad was crowned king of Burgundy at Payerne in 1033, and the kingdom passed to the Holy Roman Emperors, who were represented locally by a rector of Burgundy. In practice power lay with regional magnates — the counts of Provence and Savoy, the dauphins of Viennois, the counts of Burgundy (Franche-Comté) and the bishops of the Rhône cities.

Frederick Barbarossa sought to revive imperial authority and had himself crowned at Arles in 1178. Charles IV was the last emperor crowned king of Arles there, in 1365. In 1378 Charles IV made the Dauphin of France imperial vicar in the kingdom, effectively conceding it to French influence; Provence passed to the French crown in 1481, and the kingdom's title survived only in the imperial style.
