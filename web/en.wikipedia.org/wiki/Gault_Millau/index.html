Gault & Millau (French: Gault et Millau) is a French restaurant guide founded by the food critics Henri Gault and Christian Millau. One of the most influential guides in France — second in prestige only to the Michelin Guide — it is credited with launching and popularizing the nouvelle cuisine movement in the 1970s.

History

Gault and Millau began writing restaurant criticism together in the 1960s, founding a monthly magazine in 1965 and publishing their first guide in 1972. In 1973 they codified the "ten commandments" of nouvelle cuisine — shorter cooking times, fresh market ingredients, lighter sauces, and inventive combinations — championing young chefs such as Paul Bocuse, Michel Guérard, and the Troisgros brothers against classical haute cuisine.

Rating system

Unlike Michelin's stars, Gault & Millau grades restaurants on a scale of 20 points and awards one to five toques (chef's hats) to the best. Historically it never awarded a full 20/20, holding it unattainable; Marc Veyrat became the first chef to receive 20 in 2003 (a score also later given to Arnaud Lallement). The guide has never awarded its top mark based on food alone, publishing lengthy written reviews alongside scores. The company has expanded internationally, publishing guides in numerous countries, and was sold to new owners in 2011 and again to a Russian-backed group in 2019, with the guide continuing under the Gault & Millau name.
