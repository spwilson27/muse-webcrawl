Atta (genus)

Atta is a genus of New World ants of the subfamily Myrmicinae. It contains some of the best-known leafcutter ants, along with the related genus Acromyrmex. Atta ants are fungus growers: they cut fragments of leaves and other vegetation and carry them back to their nests, where the material is used as a substrate for cultivating fungi of the family Agaricaceae, on which the ants feed.

Scientific classification

Kingdom: Animalia

Phylum: Arthropoda

Class: Insecta

Order: Hymenoptera

Family: Formicidae

Subfamily: Myrmicinae

Tribe: Attini

Genus: Atta Fabricius, 1804

Description

Atta workers are strongly polymorphic, ranging from tiny minims that tend the fungus gardens to large majors (soldiers) with powerful jaws capable of cutting through leather. Colonies are among the largest and most complex of any ant society, numbering up to several million workers in a single nest, with vast subterranean chambers excavated over many years. A mature Atta nest can occupy a large area, with conspicuous mounds of excavated soil and well-worn foraging trails.

Species and distribution

The genus contains around 15 described species, distributed from the southern United States (Louisiana and Texas) through Central America to Argentina. Well-known species include Atta cephalotes, Atta colombica, Atta sexdens, Atta laevigata, and Atta texana.

Ecology and impact

Leafcutter ants are dominant herbivores in Neotropical ecosystems, harvesting a significant fraction of vegetation and profoundly influencing soil turnover and nutrient cycling. They are also major agricultural pests, defoliating crops and ornamental plants, and their nests can undermine roads and buildings. The mutualism between Atta and its fungus is ancient, originating tens of millions of years ago, and involves additional symbionts, including bacteria that produce antibiotics protecting the gardens from parasitic fungi.
