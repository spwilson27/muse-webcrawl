Cynethryth

Cynethryth (died after 798) was a queen of Mercia, wife of King Offa (reigned 757–796). She is the only Anglo-Saxon queen certainly known to have had coins issued in her name, a rare mark of status modelled on the practice of Byzantine empresses. After Offa's death she became abbess of Cookham and was involved in disputes with the archbishop of Canterbury over control of minsters.

Origins and marriage

Cynethryth's origins are unknown. Her name echoes those of earlier Mercian royal women, and she may have been related to the Mercian royal house. She married Offa by about 770, when she first appears witnessing charters as queen (regina). She appears regularly in charters and acted as a witness alongside the king and their son Ecgfrith.

Coinage

Offa's mint issued a distinctive penny type bearing Cynethryth's name and portrait, styled Cynethryth Regina — the only coinage for an early medieval English queen. The issue is usually dated to the 790s and may be connected with the consecration of her son Ecgfrith as king in 787. The portrait derives ultimately from Roman and Byzantine imperial models, advertising the imperial ambitions of Offa's regime.

Later reputation

Later medieval writers, notably in the St Albans tradition, blamed Cynethryth for the murder of Æthelberht II of East Anglia, who was beheaded at Offa's court in 794; contemporary evidence does not support the charge. After Offa's death in 796 and Ecgfrith's death a few months later, Cynethryth became abbess of Cookham in Berkshire, and her correspondence with Archbishop Æthelheard over disputed minsters survives in letters into 798.
