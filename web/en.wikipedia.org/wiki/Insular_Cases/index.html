Insular Cases

The Insular Cases are a series of opinions by the Supreme Court of the United States in 1901 about the status of U.S. territories acquired in the Spanish–American War. The cases were De Lima v. Bidwell, Goetze v. United States, Dooley v. United States, Armstrong v. United States, and Downes v. Bidwell. Even though the name 'Insular' specifically suggests the cases decided in 1901, the Supreme Court later relied on the opinions and principles in various cases for many years, eventually including cases about the Panama Canal Zone and other overseas areas acquired later.

Background

Under the Treaty of Paris (1898), Spain ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States. Congress imposed tariffs on goods from Puerto Rico under the Foraker Act, raising the question whether such duties violated the Constitution's Uniformity Clause, which requires duties to be "uniform throughout the United States." The cases asked, in effect, whether the Constitution "follows the flag."

Decisions

The Court held that the new possessions were territories "appurtenant to" but not part of the United States for all constitutional purposes. In Downes v. Bidwell, Justice Edward Douglass White's concurrence introduced the doctrine of territorial incorporation: territories destined for statehood are "incorporated," while others are "unincorporated," and in unincorporated territories only fundamental constitutional rights apply automatically, with Congress deciding the rest. Justice Henry Billings Brown's opinion for the Court described the islands as "inhabited by alien races" differing in customs and modes of thought — language widely criticized today.

Later history and criticism

Late twentieth-century cases on birthright citizenship in American Samoa and benefits equality for Puerto Rico residents have kept the framework alive. Judges and scholars have increasingly condemned the Insular Cases as products of racial prejudice; Justice Neil Gorsuch in 2022 wrote that they "rest on a rotten foundation" and "deserve no place in our law," and the Justice Department in 2024 stated it would no longer defend their core racial reasoning. As of the mid-2020s they have not been formally overruled, and the distinction between incorporated and unincorporated territories — uniquely, the United States still calls Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, the Northern Mariana Islands, and American Samoa unincorporated territories — continues to affect the rights of millions of U.S. citizens and nationals.
