Adjacent channel interference

Adjacent channel interference (ACI) is interference in telecommunications caused by extraneous power from a signal in an adjacent frequency channel. It arises from imperfect filtering — unwanted emissions (out-of-band emissions and spectral side lobes) from a transmitter on one channel leaking into the passband of a receiver tuned to a neighbouring channel — and from inadequate frequency selectivity in receivers.

FieldTelecommunications, radio engineering
CausesFilter roll-off, out-of-band emissions, non-linear amplification
MitigationsGuard bands, emission masks, channel planning

Mechanism

No practical filter can cut off perfectly at a channel edge, and modulated signals have spectral content beyond their nominal bandwidth. If a strong adjacent-channel transmitter is much closer or more powerful than the wanted signal, its leaked energy can overwhelm the receiver, a situation measured by the adjacent-channel leakage ratio (ACLR) of the transmitter and the adjacent-channel selectivity (ACS) of the receiver; together these give the adjacent channel interference ratio (ACIR).

Management

Regulators and standards bodies control ACI by specifying emission masks, requiring filtering, placing guard bands between channels, and planning channel assignments geographically (as in broadcast radio and television allotments). In cellular systems, careful frequency reuse planning and power control limit ACI, and ACI grows in importance when operators in adjacent bands deploy dissimilar technologies — a recurring issue in spectrum refarming and in disputes over new services next to GPS, radio altimeter and satellite bands.

Related concepts

ACI is distinguished from co-channel interference, where the interfering signal occupies the same channel, and from intermodulation, where non-linear mixing creates new interfering frequencies.
