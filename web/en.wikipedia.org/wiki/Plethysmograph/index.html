Plethysmograph

A plethysmograph is an instrument for measuring changes in volume within an organ or the whole body (usually resulting from fluctuations in the amount of blood or air it contains). The word derives from the Greek plethysmos ("increase") and graphein ("to write").

Types and uses

Pulmonary (body) plethysmography

In respiratory medicine, the patient sits inside a sealed, transparent chamber resembling a small telephone booth. By measuring pressure changes as the patient breathes against a shutter, the technique calculates lung volumes that spirometry alone cannot measure, including total lung capacity, functional residual capacity, and residual volume, as well as airway resistance. It is considered a reference standard in pulmonary function testing, based on Boyle's law.

Venous occlusion plethysmography

Used to study blood flow in limbs: a cuff briefly blocks venous return while arterial inflow continues, and the rate of limb swelling—measured by strain gauges, air cuffs, or water displacement—gives arterial blood flow. It has long been used in vascular physiology and pharmacology research, for example to test endothelial function.

Photoplethysmography

An optical technique in which light (often infrared or green) is shone into tissue and the reflected or transmitted intensity, which varies with the blood volume in each pulse, is recorded. Photoplethysmography (PPG) is the basis of pulse oximeter sensors, fingertip pulse monitors, and the heart-rate sensors in smartwatches and fitness trackers.

Other types

Impedance plethysmography infers volume changes from electrical impedance and has been used to screen for deep-vein thrombosis. Penile plethysmography measures changes in penile volume or circumference in response to stimuli; it has been used, controversially, in the assessment and treatment of sex offenders and in forensic contexts, and its reliability and ethics remain debated.

History

Plethysmographic methods date to the nineteenth century, when physiologists such as Angelo Mosso used water-filled devices to study circulation. The body box for lung volumes was developed in the mid-twentieth century and remains a cornerstone of respiratory laboratories.
