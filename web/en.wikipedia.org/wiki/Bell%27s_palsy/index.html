Bell's palsy

SpecialtyNeurology
Named afterSir Charles Bell

Bell's palsy is a type of facial paralysis of unknown cause that results in a temporary inability to control the facial muscles on the affected side. It is the most common cause of one-sided facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) paralysis and is named after the Scottish anatomist Sir Charles Bell, who described the facial nerve's function.

Signs and symptoms

Onset is usually sudden, reaching maximum severity within 48 to 72 hours. Symptoms include weakness or paralysis of one side of the face, drooping of the mouth, inability to close the eye on the affected side, drooling, dry eye and mouth, altered taste, and increased sensitivity to sound (hyperacusis). Pain around the jaw or behind the ear may occur. A presumed association with reactivation of herpes simplex virus within the geniculate ganglion is widely held, though unproven.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical and by exclusion of other causes of facial weakness such as stroke (which typically spares the forehead), Lyme disease, tumours and Ramsay Hunt syndrome. Most people begin to recover within a few weeks; about 70% recover completely without treatment. Corticosteroids such as prednisolone, started within 72 hours, significantly improve the chance of full recovery; antiviral drugs may add modest benefit. Protecting the eye with lubrication and patching is important when closure is incomplete.
