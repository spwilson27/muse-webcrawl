Pogo (comic strip)

Pogo was an American comic strip created by cartoonist Walt Kelly (1913–1973), featuring anthropomorphic animal characters living in the Okefenokee Swamp of Georgia. The title character is Pogo Possum, a gentle, unassuming opossum who acts as an everyman amid a cast of eccentric swamp creatures. The strip ran in newspapers from 1948 (as a syndicated strip from 1949) until 1975, and combined lush, expressive artwork with dialect humour, wordplay, and some of the sharpest political and social satire in American comics.

Characters

  • Pogo Possum — the modest, good-natured protagonist, often reluctantly drafted into swamp "politics".
  • Albert the Alligator — Pogo's blustering, cigar-chomping best friend.
  • Howland Owl — a pompous, pseudo-intellectual "perfesser" figure.
  • Churchy LaFemme — a superstitious turtle given to songs and portents.
  • Beauregard Bugleboy, Porkypine, Miz Beaver, Deacon Mushrat, and the villainous Simple J. Malarkey, a bobcat caricature of Senator Joseph McCarthy.

Satire and language

Kelly used the swamp as a microcosm of American public life. Pogo was repeatedly "nominated" for president in mock campaigns (beginning in 1952 with the slogan "I Go Pogo"), and the strip satirised McCarthyism, communism, segregation, and pollution. The characters speak a richly invented swamp dialect full of malapropisms and fractured quotations. The strip's most famous line — adapted from Commodore Oliver Hazard Perry's "We have met the enemy and they are ours" — appeared in full on a poster for the first Earth Day in 1970 and as the title of a 1971 collection:

We have met the enemy and he is us.

Origins and legacy

Pogo and Albert first appeared in comic books published by Dell in the 1940s, including Animal Comics (1942). After a brief run in the short-lived New York Star in 1948, the strip was syndicated nationally from 1949 and at its height appeared in nearly 500 newspapers. Kelly died in 1973; the strip continued under others until 1975, and was briefly revived in 1989–1993 as Walt Kelly's Pogo. Kelly's influence on later cartoonists — from Jim Henson to Bill Watterson and Garry Trudeau — is widely acknowledged.
