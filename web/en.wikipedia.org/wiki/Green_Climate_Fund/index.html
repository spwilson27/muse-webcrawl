The Green Climate Fund (GCF) is a fund established within the framework of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) as an operating entity of the Financial Mechanism to assist developing countries in adaptation and mitigation practices to counter climate change. The GCF is based in Incheon, South Korea. It is governed by a Board of 24 members and supported by a Secretariat.

The objective of the Green Climate Fund is to "support projects, programmes, policies and other activities in developing country Parties using thematic funding windows". It is intended that the Green Climate Fund be the centrepiece of efforts to raise climate finance under the UNFCCC.

History

The Green Climate Fund was first mentioned in the Copenhagen Accord, negotiated at COP 15 in Copenhagen in December 2009. Formally, the GCF was established during the 2010 United Nations Climate Change Conference in Cancún and in 2011 in Durban, South Africa, by 194 governments. Its governing instrument was adopted at COP 17 in Durban, and Incheon, South Korea, was selected to host the Fund in 2012.

The Fund's first replenishment (GCF-1) mobilized over US$10 billion, and its second replenishment (GCF-2), concluded in 2023, raised about US$12.8 billion in pledges from more than thirty countries.

Structure

The GCF is governed by a Board comprising 24 members, composed of an equal number of members from developing and developed countries. The Board is co-chaired by one member from a developing country Party and one from a developed country Party. The World Bank initially served as interim trustee of the Fund.

Activities

The Fund allocates resources through accredited entities, including multilateral development banks, UN agencies, and national and regional institutions. It aims for a balanced allocation between adaptation and mitigation, with at least half of adaptation funding directed to particularly vulnerable countries, including the least developed countries, small island developing States and African States.

Criticism

The Fund has faced criticism over slow disbursement, the accreditation of large commercial banks, and decisions on fossil-fuel-related projects. The United States pledged US$3 billion under President Barack Obama, of which US$1 billion was transferred before the administration of Donald Trump announced it would not fulfil the remainder; the pledge was later renewed under President Joe Biden.
