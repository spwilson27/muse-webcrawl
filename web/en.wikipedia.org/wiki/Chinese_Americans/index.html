Chinese Americans

San Francisco's Chinatown, established in the 1850s

Chinese Americans are Americans of full or partial Chinese ancestry. They constitute one of the largest overseas Chinese communities and the largest ethnic group of Asian Americans, numbering over 5 million people, roughly 1.5 percent of the U.S. population. The Chinese American community includes immigrants and descendants from mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan, and Southeast Asia.

History

Significant Chinese immigration to the United States began in the mid-19th century, drawn by the California Gold Rush and later employed to build the transcontinental railroad, completed in 1869. Anti-Chinese sentiment led to the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the first U.S. law to bar immigration based on nationality, which was not repealed until 1943. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 reopened large-scale immigration, and later waves included students and professionals from Taiwan, Hong Kong, and, after 1978, mainland China.

Demographics

Large Chinese American communities exist in New York City, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Houston, Chicago, and Boston. Chinese Americans speak varieties of Chinese including Mandarin, Cantonese, and Hokkien, alongside English. The community has high average levels of education and income, though outcomes vary widely by origin and generation.

Culture

Chinese American culture blends Chinese traditions—such as Lunar New Year celebrations—with distinctly American creations, including American Chinese cuisine, whose dishes such as chop suey, fortune cookies, and General Tso's chicken developed in the United States. Chinatowns remain cultural and commercial centers in many cities.
