ACVR1 (activin A receptor type 1), also known as ALK2, is a protein-coding gene in humans that encodes a type I receptor of the TGF-β superfamily. The ACVR1 receptor transmits signals from bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and related ligands, activating intracellular SMAD1/5/8 signalling that controls skeletal development, iron metabolism and many other processes.

Function

ACVR1 sits in the cell membrane as part of a receptor complex with type II receptors. On ligand binding, the type II receptor phosphorylates the glycine-serine-rich (GS) domain of ACVR1, activating its kinase. FKBP12 (FKBP1A) binds the inactive receptor and restrains leaky signalling. Activated ACVR1 phosphorylates receptor-regulated SMADs, which combine with SMAD4 and enter the nucleus to regulate target genes, including the hepcidin gene HAMP in the liver — a pathway central to systemic iron homeostasis.

Clinical significance

Heterozygous gain-of-function mutations in ACVR1 cause fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP), an ultra-rare disorder in which soft connective tissues progressively turn to bone (heterotopic ossification), gradually immobilising the body. In 2006 a team led by Frederick Kaplan and Eileen Shore identified the recurrent mutation c.617G>A (p.Arg206His, R206H) in the GS domain in nearly all classic FOP cases. The mutant receptor is mildly overactive and, crucially, responds abnormally to activin A — normally an antagonist of ACVR1 — treating it as an activating ligand.

Somatic ACVR1 mutations also occur in roughly a quarter of diffuse intrinsic pontine gliomas (diffuse midline gliomas, H3 K27-altered), a lethal childhood brain tumour, often alongside histone H3 mutations. Drug development targeting ACVR1 includes small-molecule kinase inhibitors and ligand traps; the retinoid agonist palovarotene, which reduces heterotopic ossification, was approved for FOP in 2023.
