Don't repeat yourself

Don't repeat yourself (DRY) is a principle of software development aimed at reducing repetition of information and code. It was formulated by Andy Hunt and Dave Thomas in their book The Pragmatic Programmer (1999): "Every piece of knowledge must have a single, unambiguous, authoritative representation within a system."

Rationale

When the same knowledge is duplicated, a change must be made consistently in every copy, and any copy missed becomes a bug. Applying DRY means encoding knowledge once — in code, schemas, documentation or build configuration — and deriving other representations from it. Violations of DRY are sometimes called WET ("write everything twice").

Caveats

Excessive abstraction to eliminate superficial similarity can couple unrelated concepts ("wrong abstraction"); DRY applies to duplication of knowledge, not merely of text, so coincidentally similar code embodying different rules may be left separate.
