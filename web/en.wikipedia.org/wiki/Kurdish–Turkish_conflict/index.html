Kurdish–Turkish conflict

The Kurdish–Turkish conflict is an armed conflict between the Republic of Turkey and various Kurdish insurgent groups, chiefly the Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK), which have demanded either separation from Turkey to create an independent Kurdistan, or autonomy and greater political and cultural rights for Kurds in Turkey.

Background

Following the collapse of the Ottoman Empire and the founding of the Turkish Republic in 1923, Kurdish identity and language were suppressed under policies of assimilation. Kurdish rebellions — including the Sheikh Said rebellion of 1925 and the Dersim revolt of 1937–38 — were put down by force. The PKK was founded in 1978 by Abdullah Öcalan on Marxist-Leninist lines and launched an armed campaign on 15 August 1984 with attacks on Eruh and Şemdinli.

Human cost

The conflict has claimed more than 40,000 lives by most estimates, including soldiers, militants and civilians, and has involved sustained counterinsurgency operations in south-eastern Turkey. The 1990s saw the evacuation or destruction of thousands of Kurdish villages and mass displacement.

Peace process and resumption

Öcalan was captured in Kenya in 1999 and sentenced to life imprisonment. Ceasefires were declared several times, and between 2013 and 2015 a formal peace process raised hopes of a negotiated settlement; it collapsed in July 2015 amid renewed fighting and urban warfare in cities such as Diyarbakır's Sur district, Cizre and Nusaybin. Turkey has also carried out cross-border operations against Kurdish groups in northern Iraq and northern Syria, the latter targeting the YPG, which Ankara regards as the PKK's Syrian affiliate.
