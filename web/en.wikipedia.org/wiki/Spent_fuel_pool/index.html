Spent fuel pool

A spent fuel pool (also called an SFP, spent fuel pond, or fuel cooling pond) is a storage pool for spent nuclear fuel from a nuclear reactor, typically 40 or more feet (12 m) deep, with the bottom 14 feet equipped with storage racks designed to hold fuel assemblies removed from the reactor core. The water in the pool serves two purposes: it shields workers and the environment from the intense radiation emitted by the spent fuel, and it removes the decay heat that the fuel continues to generate after shutdown.

Operation

Freshly discharged fuel assemblies produce substantial heat — around 6 per cent of full reactor power immediately after shutdown, falling to below 1 per cent within a day and continuing to decline. Assemblies are moved underwater from the reactor vessel to the pool through a flooded transfer canal, preserving continuous shielding. Pool water is circulated through heat exchangers to keep temperatures low, and the fuel typically remains in the pool for several years (often five to ten) until its decay heat has fallen enough for transfer to air-cooled dry cask storage.

Storage racks keep assemblies separated, both for cooling and to prevent criticality; modern high-density racks incorporate neutron-absorbing materials such as borated stainless steel or boron carbide composites. At many reactors, pools originally designed for short-term storage have been "re-racked" to hold much larger inventories as permanent geological repositories were delayed.

Safety concerns

Because spent fuel remains intensely radioactive, loss of pool cooling or water inventory is a serious accident scenario. If cooling is lost, pool water heats and eventually boils; a large loss of water could expose fuel assemblies, allowing zirconium cladding to overheat and potentially ignite in air, dispersing radionuclides. Such scenarios received intense scrutiny after the 2011 Fukushima Daiichi accident, where the spent fuel pool of the damaged Unit 4 became a major focus of concern. Studies by regulators and the US National Academies have examined the comparative risks of dense pool storage versus expedited transfer to dry casks, and plants have added backup spray systems, additional instrumentation, and portable pumping equipment (such as the US FLEX programme).

Alternatives

After sufficient cooling, spent fuel can be transferred to dry cask storage — sealed metal or concrete containers cooled passively by air — or, in countries that reprocess, shipped to a reprocessing plant. Some countries operate centralised wet storage facilities away from reactors, such as Sweden's CLAB.
