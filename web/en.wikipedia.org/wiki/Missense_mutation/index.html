Missense mutation

A missense mutation is a point mutation in which a single nucleotide change results in a codon that codes for a different amino acid in the resulting protein. It is a type of nonsynonymous substitution, in contrast to a synonymous ("silent") mutation, which changes the codon but not the amino acid, and a nonsense mutation, which creates a premature stop codon.

Effects

The consequences of a missense mutation range from none to severe, depending on how chemically similar the substituted amino acid is (a "conservative" versus "non-conservative" change) and on the position of the residue in the protein. A well-known example is sickle-cell disease, caused by a single missense mutation in the β-globin gene (HBB) that changes glutamic acid to valine at position 6, making haemoglobin polymerise under low oxygen. Other missense mutations cause diseases such as phenylketonuria (in some cases), cystic fibrosis (less commonly than the ΔF508 deletion), and many cancers through oncogene activation, for example in RAS genes.

Classification

Missense variants found in clinical sequencing are classified as benign, likely benign, of uncertain significance (VUS), likely pathogenic, or pathogenic, using population frequency data, computational predictions (such as SIFT and PolyPhen), and functional evidence.
