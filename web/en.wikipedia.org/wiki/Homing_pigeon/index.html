Homing pigeon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The homing pigeon is a variety of domestic pigeon (Columba livia domestica) selectively bred for its ability to find its way home over extremely long distances. Because of this skill, trained homing pigeons were used for centuries to carry messages — a service known as pigeon post — and are still kept today for the sport of pigeon racing.

Homing ability

Homing pigeons can return to their loft from release points hundreds of kilometres away, at average speeds of 60–80 km/h and over distances exceeding 1,500 km in racing. Research indicates they navigate using a combination of cues: a magnetic sense, probably based on magnetite in the beak and on light-dependent mechanisms in the eye, which provides a compass and possibly a map; the position of the sun; visual landmarks; low-frequency infrasound; and a keen sense of smell.

History

Pigeons were used to carry messages in ancient Egypt and Persia, and by the Roman Empire. Sultan Nur ad-Din established a pigeon post in the 12th century, and Genghis Khan is said to have maintained a relay network across Asia. In the 19th century, news agencies such as Reuters began with pigeon services carrying stock prices between cities not yet linked by telegraph. During both world wars, military pigeons carried messages where radio was unavailable or unsafe; celebrated birds such as Cher Ami, which saved the "Lost Battalion" in 1918, and G.I. Joe were decorated for their service. Some armies retained pigeon units into the late 20th century.

Pigeon racing

In modern pigeon racing, birds are transported to a release point and timed on their return to their home lofts; the winner is the bird with the highest average velocity. The sport is especially popular in Belgium, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, China and Taiwan, and successful racing pigeons can change hands for very large sums.
