Historicism (art)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Revival styles such as Neo-Gothic and Neo-Renaissance are hallmarks of historicism.

Historicism or historism (German: Historismus) comprises artistic styles that draw their inspiration from recreating historic styles or imitating the work of historic artists. In architecture and the applied arts of the 19th century, it denotes the deliberate revival and recombination of earlier styles — Neo-Gothic, Neo-Renaissance, Neo-Baroque, Neo-Romanesque and others — often several within a single building or interior.

Development

After the classicism of the early 19th century, architects and designers increasingly treated history as a catalogue of styles from which to choose according to a building's purpose: Gothic for churches and parliaments, Renaissance for banks and museums, Baroque for theatres and opera houses. In Britain the movement overlapped with the Gothic Revival; in Germany it produced the Rundbogenstil and the monumental Ringstrasse developments in Vienna; in France it is associated with the Beaux-Arts tradition and the eclectic interiors of the Second Empire.

Historicism was driven by new historical scholarship, archaeological publication, and industrial techniques that made carved ornament and period detail cheap to reproduce. It dominated public and bourgeois building from roughly the 1830s until the turn of the 20th century.

Criticism and decline

From the 1890s, reform movements — Art Nouveau, the Arts and Crafts movement, and later modernism — attacked historicism as inauthentic imitation lacking a style of its own. Twentieth-century modernist historiography long treated it as mere eclecticism, though later scholarship has rehabilitated it as a serious engagement with history and national identity, closely tied to the rise of museums, preservation, and nation-building.
