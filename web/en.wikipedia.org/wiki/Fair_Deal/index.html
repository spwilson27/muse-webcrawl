Fair Deal

The Fair Deal was a set of domestic policy proposals by United States President Harry S. Truman, announced in his 1949 State of the Union address. Building on Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal, the Fair Deal called for universal health insurance, a higher minimum wage, expanded Social Security, federal aid to education, civil rights legislation, and public housing.

Program

Truman's proposals included repeal of the Taft–Hartley Act, a national health insurance system, federal assistance for education, expanded public housing and slum clearance, increased farm supports, new public-power projects, and strong civil-rights measures including anti-lynching and fair-employment laws.

Outcome

A conservative coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats blocked much of the programme. Significant achievements nonetheless included the Housing Act of 1949, the Social Security Amendments of 1950 (which greatly expanded coverage), and the raising of the minimum wage from 40 to 75 cents under the Fair Labor Standards Amendments of 1949. National health insurance and most civil-rights bills failed, though Truman advanced civil rights by executive order, including desegregating the armed forces in 1948.

Legacy

The Fair Deal set the agenda for American liberalism into the 1960s, when parts of it were realised in Lyndon B. Johnson's Great Society, including Medicare and federal aid to education.
