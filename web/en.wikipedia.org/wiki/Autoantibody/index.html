An autoantibody is an antibody produced by the immune system that is directed against one or more of the individual's own proteins (self-antigens). Autoantibodies are a hallmark of autoimmune diseases, in which the normal mechanisms of self-tolerance fail; in some diseases they directly cause tissue damage, while in others they are primarily diagnostic markers. Low levels of certain autoantibodies also occur in healthy people and increase with age.

Mechanisms of harm

Autoantibodies can injure the body by binding cell-surface receptors and stimulating or blocking them (as in Graves' disease and myasthenia gravis), by forming immune complexes that deposit in tissues and provoke inflammation (as in systemic lupus erythematosus), by opsonising blood cells for destruction (autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, immune thrombocytopenia), or by interfering with clotting factors and phospholipids (antiphospholipid syndrome).

Detection and significance

Laboratories detect autoantibodies by indirect immunofluorescence on cell substrates (for example HEp-2 cells for ANA patterns), enzyme immunoassays, and line blots. A positive test is not by itself diagnostic — titres, patterns, and the clinical picture together determine whether an autoantibody signals present disease, predicts future disease (as islet autoantibodies do for type 1 diabetes), or is an incidental finding.
