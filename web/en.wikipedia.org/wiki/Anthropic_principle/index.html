Anthropic principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Illustration of a range of universes with different constants, only a narrow band allowing stars and observers]
Only a narrow range of constants permits observers to exist.

The anthropic principle, also known as the 'observation selection effect', is the hypothesis that the range of possible observations that could be made about the universe is limited by the fact that observations can only happen in a universe capable of developing intelligent life. In its weak form it is a tautology about selection bias: what we observe must be compatible with our existence. In its strong form — invoked with the Multiverse — it becomes an explanatory strategy for otherwise puzzling coincidences, such as the values of physical constants.

The term was coined by Brandon Carter in 1973 (at a Kraków symposium honouring Copernicus, as a corrective to excessive Copernican humility). Its most consequential scientific use is Steven Weinberg's 1987 anthropic bound on the Cosmological constant: a much larger Λ would have prevented galaxy formation, so observers should expect a small nonzero value of the order Weinberg estimated — roughly what was measured a decade later. Fred Hoyle's 1953 prediction of a carbon-12 resonance (needed for stellar nucleosynthesis, hence for carbon-based observers) is the classic retrospective example. Critics charge that anthropic reasoning explains nothing and forestalls the search for deeper laws; defenders reply that in a multiverse it is ordinary statistics.

Debate

The principle sits at the boundary of physics and philosophy: as a reminder of selection effects it is unassailable and practically important (it invalidates many would-be improbability arguments); as a multiverse-based explanation it is only as good as the multiverse framework and its unresolved measure problem. Roger Penrose and others regard the required fine-tunings (e.g. the early universe's low entropy) as far too extreme for anthropic selection to carry.
