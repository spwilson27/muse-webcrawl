Sharing economy

[Image: a homeowner handing house keys to a guest using a home-sharing platform]

The sharing economy (collaborative consumption, access economy) is an economic model in which individuals share access to goods, services and space — often idle capacity — coordinated by digital platforms: ride-hailing (Uber, Lyft), home-sharing (Airbnb), car-sharing, co-working and skill marketplaces. The term was popularised in the 2010s as the model scaled.

Economics and criticism

Platforms cut transaction costs — search, trust (via ratings and verified identity) and payment — that previously kept such exchanges within small circles, unlocking supply from underused assets: the spare room, the parked car. Economists study their pricing (surge pricing as Supply and demand in action), network effects and two-sided market design, and their blurring of consumer/producer and employee/contractor lines (see gig economy). Critics argue much of the sector is ordinary rental or labour brokerage rebranded, with regulatory arbitrage on hotels, taxis and tenant protection; incumbents and cities have responded with caps and licensing. Measurement is difficult, but the model's platform logic now extends from transport and lodging to tools, clothing and finance.
