Kurrent

Kurrent is an old form of German-language handwriting based on late medieval cursive writing, also known as Kurrentschrift, deutsche Schrift ("German script") or Spitzschrift. For centuries it was the standard form of handwriting in German-speaking lands for everyday writing, in contrast to the printed blackletter typefaces (Fraktur) used for books.

History

Kurrent developed from late Gothic cursive scripts in the early 16th century. Its letters are characterised by sharp angles, narrow forms and distinctive loops; several letters differ markedly from Latin cursive — notably the lowercase e (resembling an n), h, and the long s (ſ) versus round s distinction. Regional and chronological variants existed, making old documents challenging for modern readers.

In 1911 the Prussian graphic designer Ludwig Sütterlin created the Sütterlin script, a standardised, more rounded form of Kurrent, which was taught in German schools from 1915. In 1941, the Nazi regime — which had earlier promoted Fraktur as quintessentially German — abruptly abolished blackletter and Kurrent by the Normalschrifterlass, declaring them "Jewish letters" (Schwabacher Judenlettern) and mandating Latin script (lateinische Ausgangsschrift) instead. After the Second World War, Kurrent rapidly fell out of use, though Sütterlin continued to be taught in some West German schools into the 1970s.

Letter forms

Characteristic features include the tall, narrow lowercase letters, the e that looks like a modern n, hooked forms of a and g, and the use of umlaut marks originally written as a small e above the vowel. Capital letters are ornate and can be difficult to distinguish, a feature shared with related scripts such as the Danish-Norwegian gotisk skrift.

Use today

Kurrent is now studied mainly by historians, genealogists and archivists who need to read German manuscripts, church registers and family documents written before the mid-20th century.
