Sage (philosophy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sage (Ancient Greek: sophos), in classical philosophy, is someone who has attained the wisdom which a philosopher seeks. The figure of the sage functions as an ideal of perfected human reason and virtue against which the ordinary person — and even the philosopher, who merely loves wisdom — is measured.

Stoicism

The concept is most fully developed in Stoicism, where the sage is the person who lives perfectly according to nature and reason: free from destructive passions, infallible in judgment, self-sufficient, and happy even under torture. The Stoics conceded that an actual sage was exceedingly rare — "rarer than the phoenix" — but maintained the ideal as the standard of moral progress, dividing humanity sharply into sages and fools.

Other traditions

In Confucianism, the sage (shengren) is the highest exemplar of humanity, embodied by the legendary sage-kings Yao and Shun and approached by Confucius; Daoism likewise honours the zhenren and the sage who acts by wu wei. Hindu traditions revere the rishi, seer-sages to whom the Vedas were revealed. In common usage, "sage" has come to mean any profoundly wise person, and the Seven Sages of Greece were remembered for their pithy maxims of practical wisdom.
