McKinley Tariff

The Tariff Act of 1890, commonly called the McKinley Tariff, was an act of the United States Congress, sponsored by Representative (and future President) William McKinley, then Chairman of the House Ways and Means Committee. It was designed to protect domestic industries from foreign competition and raised the average duty on imports to almost 50 percent.

Provisions

The tariff raised the average rate of duty on dutiable imports from about 38 percent to nearly 50 percent. Among its notable provisions:

  • Raw sugar was placed on the free list, while domestic sugar producers received a bounty of two cents per pound.
  • Duties on woolen goods, steel rails, and many manufactured goods were increased.
  • A system of reciprocity allowed the President to impose penalties on countries that maintained "unreasonably" high duties on American goods.
  • The tariff included a provision exempting the Hawaiian Islands' sugar under reciprocity arrangements, with major consequences for Hawaiian politics.

Political impact

The tariff was extremely unpopular with consumers, who saw prices rise on many goods, including tinware and woolens. It contributed heavily to the Republican Party's disastrous defeat in the 1890 midterm elections, in which the party lost nearly 100 seats in the House of Representatives. McKinley himself lost his House seat in 1890, though he was elected Governor of Ohio the following year and President in 1896.

The Wilson–Gorman Tariff Act of 1894 lowered many of the McKinley rates, but the Dingley Act of 1897 raised them again to even higher levels.
