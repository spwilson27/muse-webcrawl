Nostradamus

Michel de Nostredame (December 1503 – 2 July 1566), usually Latinised as Nostradamus, was a French physician, astrologer and reputed seer best known for his book Les Prophéties (The Prophecies), a collection of 942 poetic quatrains first published in 1555. The ambiguous verses have been credited — almost always retrospectively — with predicting major world events, making Nostradamus one of the most famous figures in the history of prophecy literature.

Life

Nostredame was born in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence to a family of converted Jewish ancestry. He studied at the University of Avignon and the University of Montpellier, becoming a physician who treated plague victims in southern France and Italy; he was noted for methods such as his "rose pill" and for hygiene measures, though he lost his first wife and children, apparently to plague. He later settled in Salon-de-Provence, married a wealthy widow, and turned increasingly to astrology and the occult, publishing popular almanacs from 1550.

Les Prophéties made him famous. Catherine de' Medici summoned him to court, and he served as counsellor and physician-in-ordinary to King Charles IX. He died in Salon in 1566 and was buried in the church of Saint-Laurent there.

The Prophecies

The quatrains are written in a mixture of French, Latin, Greek and Provençal, filled with anagrams, archaic spellings and astrological references, arranged in ten "centuries" of 100 verses (the seventh being incomplete). Followers have matched verses to events from the Great Fire of London to the rise of Hitler and the attacks of 11 September 2001. Scholars and sceptics note that the matches rely on loose translations, mistranslations and vague imagery that can be fitted to almost any event after the fact, and that mistranslated "quatrains" circulate widely as hoaxes.

Sources and legacy

Modern academic research shows Nostradamus drew heavily on earlier printed collections of prophecies and world histories — such as the Mirabilis Liber (1522) — and used techniques of "bibliomancy" and historical projection, adapting past events as templates for the future. His cultural afterlife is enormous: he remained in print for centuries, was exploited by propagandists in the Second World War, and remains a staple of popular occult literature.
