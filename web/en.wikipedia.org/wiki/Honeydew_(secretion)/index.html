Honeydew (secretion)

Honeydew is a sugar-rich sticky liquid secreted by aphids, some scale insects, whiteflies, and other plant-sucking insects in the order Hemiptera as they feed on plant sap. Because phloem sap is high in sugar but low in amino acids, these insects must process large volumes of it to obtain enough protein, and the excess sugar and water are excreted as honeydew.

Ecological role

Honeydew is a keystone resource in many ecosystems. Ants avidly collect it, and many ant species actively tend aphid colonies—protecting them from predators and even moving them to fresh feeding sites—in a mutualistic relationship sometimes described as aphid "farming." Bees collect honeydew in some regions to produce dark, strong-flavoured "forest honey" or honeydew honey, prized in parts of Europe and New Zealand. Birds, wasps, and other insects also feed on it.

Problems

Where it coats leaves, honeydew supports the growth of black sooty mould fungi, which block sunlight and reduce photosynthesis. On cars, pavements, and garden furniture beneath infested trees it forms a sticky nuisance. Honeydew production is therefore one of the main ways heavy aphid or scale infestations damage ornamental plants, citrus groves, and crops, beyond the direct harm of sap feeding.

Other uses of the name

The word "honeydew" is also used for the sugary exudations of some plants themselves, and in New Zealand for the secretion of the native sooty beech scale insect, which sustains endemic birds such as the kākā and tūī and the threatened native ecosystem of beech forests. Ergot-infected grasses can also produce honeydew containing fungal spores.
