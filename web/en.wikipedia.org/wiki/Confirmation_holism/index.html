Confirmation holism, also called epistemological holism, is the claim in the philosophy of science that a scientific theory as a whole can be tested, rather than each of its component statements. The idea is associated with the French physicist and philosopher Pierre Duhem, who wrote in 1906: "A physics experiment can never condemn an isolated hypothesis but only a whole theoretical group."

Duhem's argument

Duhem argued that a prediction is derived from a hypothesis only with the aid of auxiliary assumptions — about instruments, initial conditions, and background theories. If the prediction fails, logic alone cannot say whether the hypothesis or one of the auxiliaries is at fault. Hence no single statement faces the tribunal of experience alone.

Quine's extension

Willard Van Orman Quine, in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), generalised the thesis: any statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system, and conversely no statement is immune to revision. His "web of belief" metaphor presents knowledge as a fabric touching experience only at its edges; recalcitrant experience prompts redistribution of truth values across the interior, guided by pragmatic virtues such as simplicity and conservatism.

Criticisms

Critics argue that confirmation holism is compatible with reasonable scientific practice: Bayesian confirmation theory can distribute confirmation across statements, and working scientists do routinely locate blame in auxiliary assumptions while defending core theories. The thesis nonetheless undermined the logical empiricists' hope of testing hypotheses one by one.
