Atomic theory

Atomic theory is the scientific theory that matter is composed of discrete particles called atoms. It began as a philosophical concept in ancient Greece and India — in the atomism of Democritus and Leucippus — and entered the scientific mainstream in the early 19th century, when discoveries in chemistry showed that matter behaves as though it consists of atoms. The modern atomic theory underlies all of chemistry and much of physics.

Philosophical atomism

The idea that matter is made of indivisible units appears in the works of the pre-Socratic philosophers Leucippus and Democritus (5th century BC) and in Indian schools such as Vaisheshika. These ideas, lacking experimental support, were largely displaced by Aristotelian physics for two millennia.

Dalton and modern chemistry

In 1803–1808 the English chemist John Dalton proposed the first modern atomic theory: elements consist of identical atoms of characteristic weight; compounds form by combination of atoms in simple whole-number ratios; and atoms are indestructible in chemical reactions. Dalton's theory explained the laws of definite and multiple proportions. During the 19th century, work by Avogadro, Cannizzaro, and Mendeleev produced the periodic table and consistent atomic weights, although some scientists, such as Ernst Mach, remained sceptical of atoms' reality.

Subatomic structure

J. J. Thomson's discovery of the electron (1897) showed atoms are divisible. Einstein's 1905 analysis of Brownian motion and Jean Perrin's experiments provided near-conclusive evidence for atoms. Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1909–1911) revealed the atomic nucleus; Bohr's model (1913) introduced quantised electron orbits; and quantum mechanics in the 1920s produced the modern picture of electrons in orbitals around a nucleus of protons and neutrons (the neutron was identified by Chadwick in 1932).

Modern atomic theory

Today atoms are known to consist of a dense nucleus surrounded by a cloud of electrons described by quantum mechanics; nuclei themselves consist of nucleons, which are made of quarks. Isotopes, ions, and chemical bonding are all described within the framework that grew out of atomic theory.
