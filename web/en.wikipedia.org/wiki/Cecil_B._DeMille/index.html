Cecil Blount DeMille (August 12, 1881 – January 21, 1959) was an American film director and producer. Between 1913 and 1956 he made 70 feature films, both silent and sound, and is widely regarded as a founding father of American cinema and one of the most commercially successful filmmakers of all time. He was famed for epic spectacles, showmanship, and an authoritarian directing style performed with riding boots, a megaphone and a chair-borne platform on set.

Early life and career

DeMille was born in Ashfield, Massachusetts, to playwright Henry Churchill de Mille and Matilda Beatrice (Samuel) DeMille, whose family ran a playwriting agency. He began his career as a stage actor and later wrote and directed plays with his brother William C. deMille. In 1913 he co-founded the Jesse L. Lasky Feature Play Company with Jesse L. Lasky and Samuel Goldwyn; its first film, The Squaw Man (1914), co-directed with Oscar Apfel, was shot in a rented barn in Hollywood and became one of the first feature films made there.

Epic filmmaking

After society dramas and comedies with stars such as Gloria Swanson, DeMille pioneered the biblical epic with the silent The Ten Commandments (1923) and The King of Kings (1927). His sound-era successes included The Sign of the Cross (1932), Cleopatra (1934), The Crusades (1935), Union Pacific (1939), Reap the Wild Wind (1942) and Samson and Delilah (1949). The Greatest Show on Earth (1952), a circus drama, won the Academy Award for Best Picture. His final film, a lavish remake of The Ten Commandments (1956) starring Charlton Heston as Moses, was one of the biggest box-office hits of its era.

Legacy

DeMille was a co-founder of Paramount Pictures' predecessor and of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences. The Golden Globe Awards' Cecil B. DeMille Award for lifetime achievement is named in his honour; he received its first presentation in 1952. His name became a byword for the Hollywood spectacle film, and his catchphrase reputation — crystallised in Sunset Boulevard's line "I'm ready for my close-up" directed at his former star Swanson — endures in popular culture.
