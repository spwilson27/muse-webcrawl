Extended technique

Image placeholder: Extended piano technique — playing the strings directly

Extended technique is a term used in music to describe the use of unconventional, unorthodox, or non-traditional methods of singing or playing musical instruments in order to obtain unusual sounds or timbres. The term is most associated with contemporary classical music and experimental music, but techniques once considered extended — such as col legno bowing for strings — illustrate how the boundary shifts over time.

Examples

Common extended techniques include the prepared piano, in which objects are placed on or between the strings (developed by John Cage); string effects such as bowing behind the bridge, harmonics, and Bartók pizzicato; wind techniques including multiphonics, flutter-tonguing, key clicks, and circular breathing; and vocal techniques such as overtone singing, screaming, and sprechstimme. Guitarists employ tapping, prepared guitar, and playing with objects or electronics.

History

Composers of the 20th century — including Henry Cowell, John Cage, and later generations of the avant-garde — greatly expanded the palette of instrumental sounds, and performers and composers have developed specialised notation to specify the new techniques. Many extended techniques have since entered mainstream practice in film scores, jazz, rock, and electronic music.
