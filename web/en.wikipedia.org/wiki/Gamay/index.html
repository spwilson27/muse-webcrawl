Gamay

Gamay is a purple-colored grape variety used to make red wines, most notably grown in Beaujolais and in the Loire Valley around Tours. Its full name is Gamay noir à jus blanc. It is a very old variety, mentioned as long ago as the 15th century. It has been often cultivated because it makes for abundant production; however, it can produce wines of distinction when planted on acidic soils, which help to soften the grape's naturally high acidity.

History

The Gamay grape is thought to have appeared in the village of Gamay, south of Beaune, in the 1360s. The grape brought relief to the village growers following the decline of the Black Death. In contrast to the Pinot noir variety, Gamay ripened two weeks earlier and was less difficult to cultivate. It also produced a strong, fruity wine in larger quantities.

In July 1395, the Duke of Burgundy Philippe the Bold outlawed the cultivation of the grape in his lands, calling it a "disloyal plant", presumably because it occupied land that could be used for the more elegant Pinot noir. Nevertheless, the grape continued to be cultivated in the region south of the Côte d'Or, in Beaujolais, where it thrived in the granite soils.

Characteristics

Gamay is a very vigorous vine with weak wood growth that tends to produce a large number of small grapes. The compact clusters are cylindrical in shape. The berries are small, spherical, and blackish-blue with a characteristic fragrant, earthy aroma. The grapes are moderately acidic and deeply colored.

Wine regions

As well as Beaujolais, Gamay is grown in the Loire Valley around Tours, and in the Mâconnais. It is also planted in the French departments of Allier and Nièvre. Outside France, it is grown in Switzerland, where it is often blended with Pinot noir, as well as in Canada, Italy, Croatia, and elsewhere.

Synonyms

Gamay is known under a variety of synonyms, including Gamay noir, Gamay Beaujolais (in California), Plant Robert (in the Mâconnais), and numerous local names.
