Hellenistic-era warships

From the 4th century BC onward, the successor kingdoms of Alexander the Great developed warships far larger than the classical trireme. These Hellenistic-era warships were rated by the number of rowers on each vertical bank of oars — quadriremes, quinqueremes, and still larger polyremes up to the enormous "forty" (tessarakonteres) of Ptolemy IV — and served as platforms for marines, artillery, and missile troops as much as for ramming.

A large Hellenistic polyreme, reconstruction

Development

The quadrireme and quinquereme appeared in the late 4th century BC, credited by ancient sources to the Carthaginians and to Dionysius I of Syracuse respectively. Rather than adding extra superimposed banks, designers placed multiple rowers on each oar. During the wars of the Diadochi, Antigonus I and his son Demetrius Poliorcetes, the Ptolemies of Egypt and the Seleucids competed to build ever bigger ships — "sixes", "sevens", up to "thirteens" and beyond — for prestige and as fighting platforms in sieges such as Demetrius' attack on Rhodes (305–304 BC).

Giant ships

The largest known vessel was the tessarakonteres ("forty") built for Ptolemy IV Philopator of Egypt, described by Callixenus of Rhodes (preserved in Athenaeus) as about 128 m (420 ft) long with more than 4,000 rowers and nearly 3,000 marines; it was probably a double-hulled showpiece and of little practical military use. More practical capital ships were the quinqueremes used in fleets by Carthage and Rome in the Punic Wars, while smaller fast craft such as lembos and liburnians were used for scouting and raiding.

Tactics

Large polyremes carried towers, bolt-shooters and stone-throwers, and fought boarding actions with heavy complements of marines; ramming remained possible for the handier classes. As Rome achieved uncontested naval supremacy after the Battle of Actium (31 BC), the arms race ended and fleets reverted to smaller, cheaper types.
