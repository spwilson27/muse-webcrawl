Swinging flashlight test

The swinging flashlight test, also known as the swinging light test, is a clinical examination used to detect a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) by comparing the pupillary light reflex of the two eyes. It is a quick, objective test of the function of the retina and optic nerve of each eye.

Procedure

In a dimly lit room, the examiner shines a bright light into one eye for a few seconds, then quickly swings the light across to the other eye, pausing three to four seconds on each side. Normally, shining the light in either eye causes both pupils to constrict (direct and consensual responses) and the pupils remain constricted when the light swings across. In a positive test — a Marcus Gunn pupil — the pupil of the affected eye dilates paradoxically when the light is swung onto it, because that eye perceives the light as dimmer than the fellow eye did.

Clinical significance

A relative afferent pupillary defect indicates asymmetric disease of the retina or optic nerve, such as optic neuritis, ischaemic optic neuropathy, severe retinal detachment or advanced asymmetric glaucoma. The test does not measure acuity and can be performed even when the pupils are irregular; it cannot detect bilateral symmetric disease because it relies on comparison between the eyes.
