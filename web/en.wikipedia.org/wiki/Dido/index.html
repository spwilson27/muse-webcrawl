Dido supervising the building of Carthage; her flight from Tyre and foundation of the city are among the best-known legends of the ancient Mediterranean.

Dido (also called Elissa) was, according to ancient Greco-Roman sources, the founder and first queen of the Phoenician city-state of Carthage in North Africa. Having fled the tyranny of her brother Pygmalion, king of Tyre, she sailed with a group of followers and founded Carthage, traditionally dated to the 9th century BC.

Legend

According to the legend, Dido's husband Acerbas (Sychaeus) was murdered by Pygmalion for his wealth. Dido escaped Tyre with the treasure and her followers, landing in North Africa, where she bargained with the local ruler Iarbas for as much land as could be covered by an oxhide. Cleverly cutting the hide into thin strips, she enclosed the hill of Byrsa — the citadel of Carthage, whose name was punningly derived from the Greek word for hide. When the city prospered, Iarbas demanded her hand in marriage; in the earlier tradition, Dido immolated herself on a pyre rather than break her vow of fidelity to her dead husband.

In Virgil's Aeneid

Dido is best known from Book 4 of Virgil's Aeneid, in which the shipwrecked Trojan hero Aeneas is welcomed at Carthage and the two fall in love through the machinations of the gods. When Aeneas, reminded of his destiny, departs for Italy, Dido curses him and his descendants — mythically explaining the Punic Wars between Rome and Carthage — and kills herself on a funeral pyre with Aeneas's sword. Her tragedy has inspired countless later works, including Purcell's opera Dido and Aeneas.

Historicity

Whether a historical queen lies behind the legend is uncertain; the name Elissa is Phoenician, and Carthage was indeed a Tyrian foundation, but the detailed narrative is generally treated as legend shaped by Greek and Roman storytellers.
