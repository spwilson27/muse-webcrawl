Solid-state drive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A solid-state drive (SSD) is a type of mass storage device that stores data persistently using integrated circuits, typically NAND flash memory, and contains no moving mechanical components. SSDs have largely replaced hard disk drives (HDDs) as the primary storage in laptops and desktop computers because of their much faster access times, lower latency, resistance to physical shock, and silent operation.

Architecture

An SSD consists of NAND flash memory chips and a controller that manages reading, writing, and wear. Data are stored in cells as electrical charge; modern drives use multi-level cells (MLC), triple-level cells (TLC), or quad-level cells (QLC), trading endurance for density. Because flash blocks must be erased before rewriting, controllers perform wear leveling, garbage collection, and over-provisioning, and expose the TRIM command so operating systems can mark deleted data as free. Many drives include a DRAM cache or use a portion of system memory (host memory buffer) to accelerate mapping-table lookups.

SATA SSDs are limited to about 600 MB/s, whereas PCIe 4.0 NVMe drives exceed 7,000 MB/s and PCIe 5.0 drives surpass 12,000 MB/s sequential throughput.

History

Early solid-state storage used DRAM with battery backup in the 1970s and 1980s. Flash-based products appeared in the 1990s, but cost per gigabyte kept them niche until the late 2000s, when consumer SSDs such as Intel's X25-M (2008) demonstrated the performance advantage. Through the 2010s, falling NAND prices drove mass adoption; by the early 2020s SSDs were standard in nearly all new personal computers, while HDDs remained in use for bulk, low-cost archival storage.

Comparison with hard disk drives

SSDs offer random access times measured in tens of microseconds versus milliseconds for HDDs, and far higher input/output operations per second. They consume less power at idle and are immune to magnetic fields and head crashes. Their disadvantages include a higher price per terabyte and a finite write endurance, specified in terabytes written (TBW), though for typical consumer workloads endurance ratings exceed the useful life of the drive.
