English verbs

English verbs are notable for their relatively small number of inflected forms compared with most European languages, combined with a rich system of auxiliary verbs used to express tense, aspect, mood and voice. A typical lexical verb has at most five forms: the plain form (walk), the third-person singular present (walks), the past tense (walked), the past participle (walked) and the -ing participle (walking). The verb be is the most irregular, with eight forms: be, am, is, are, was, were, being, been.

Regular and irregular verbs

Most English verbs are regular ("weak"), forming the past tense and past participle with the suffix -ed. About 200 verbs are irregular ("strong" or otherwise), surviving from older Germanic patterns of vowel change (ablaut), as in sing–sang–sung and drive–drove–driven, or showing other changes such as teach–taught, or no change at all (cut–cut–cut). Many of the most frequent verbs in the language are irregular.

Tense, aspect and mood

Strictly, English has two morphological tenses, present and past. Future time and finer distinctions are expressed with auxiliaries: the perfect aspect with have (she has eaten), the progressive with be (she is eating), the passive with be (it was broken), and modality with the modal verbs can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would. Combinations of these yield the traditional list of twelve "tenses" taught to learners, from present simple to future perfect continuous.

Auxiliary verbs

The primary auxiliaries be, have and do serve grammatical functions: do is used in questions and negation (Do you know? I do not know), a distinctive feature of English known as do-support. Modal auxiliaries are defective — they lack participles and infinitives — and are followed by the bare infinitive.

Other properties

Verbs differ in transitivity: transitive verbs take a direct object, intransitive verbs do not, and some are ditransitive (give her a book). Multi-word phrasal verbs such as put off, look after and get over are extremely common and often idiomatic. Non-finite forms include the infinitive (to go), the gerund-participle in -ing, and participles used in perfect and passive constructions. The subjunctive survives chiefly in the plain form after verbs of demand (I insist that he go) and in set phrases.
