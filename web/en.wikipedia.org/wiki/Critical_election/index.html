Critical election

Critical elections produce sharp and durable shifts in party coalitions.

A critical election is an election in a party system that dramatically changes the political structure or party alignments of a polity, producing a new, relatively durable governing coalition. The concept was introduced by political scientist V. O. Key Jr. in a 1955 article, describing elections "in which voters are, at least from impressionistic evidence, unusually deeply concerned, in which the extent of electoral involvement is relatively quite high, and in which the decisive results of the voting reveal a sharp alteration of the pre-existing cleavage within the electorate" that persists for several subsequent elections.

Realigning elections in the United States

In American political science the idea underlies the theory of party systems and realignment, developed by Key, Walter Dean Burnham and James Sundquist. Elections commonly identified as critical or realigning include 1800, 1828, 1860, 1896 and 1932 — the last inaugurating the New Deal coalition that dominated American politics for a generation. Some scholars also argue for 1968 or 1980, though others contend that late-20th-century change was gradual ("secular realignment" or dealignment) rather than driven by single elections.

Criticism

Historians and quantitative scholars have questioned whether realignments are as abrupt and uniform as the theory implies, noting regional variation and continuity beneath apparent breaks. David Mayhew's Electoral Realignments: A Critique of an American Genre (2002) argued the framework is empirically shaky, sparking a continuing debate over the concept's usefulness outside — and within — the United States.
