Russian domesticated red fox

The Russian domesticated red fox (also called the domesticated silver fox) is a form of the red fox (Vulpes vulpes) bred at the Institute of Cytology and Genetics in Novosibirsk, Siberia, in a famous long-running experiment on domestication begun in 1959 by the Soviet geneticist Dmitry Belyaev and continued by Lyudmila Trut.

The experiment

Belyaev hypothesised that selecting animals solely for tameness — willingness to approach humans without fear or aggression — would reproduce the changes seen in domesticated species. Starting from silver foxes farmed for fur, breeders each year tested kits for their response to a human hand and bred only the tamest few percent. Within about ten generations many foxes eagerly sought human contact, wagging their tails and whining.

Domestication syndrome

Alongside tameness appeared a suite of unselected changes resembling those in dogs: floppy ears, curled tails, piebald ("star") coat patterns, shorter snouts, altered reproductive timing, and changed levels of stress hormones such as cortisol. This cluster, often called domestication syndrome, has been linked to mild deficits in neural crest cell development. Critics have since noted that the founding foxes came from Canadian fur farms with their own selection history, complicating the strongest interpretations of the experiment.

Legacy

The tame foxes became a celebrated model for the genetics and endocrinology of domestication; after funding crises in the 1990s, some were sold as exotic pets to support the colony. A parallel line bred for aggression provides a comparison population. Trut's account, written with Lee Alan Dugatkin, appeared as How to Tame a Fox (and Build a Dog) (2017).
