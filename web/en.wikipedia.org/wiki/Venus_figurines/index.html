PeriodUpper Paleolithic, ≈ 40,000–11,000 years ago

Venus figurines are small statuettes of women produced during the Upper Paleolithic period, roughly between 40,000 and 11,000 years ago, found across Europe and as far east as Siberia. Typically carved from mammoth ivory, bone, antler or stone — and in a few cases modelled in the earliest known fired ceramics — most are between about 4 and 25 cm tall. The name, applied in the 19th century, is anachronistic: they predate the Roman goddess Venus by tens of thousands of years.

Characteristics

Many figurines share a striking pattern: exaggerated breasts, bellies and hips, with heads, arms, hands, legs and feet reduced or absent, and facial features rarely shown. Examples include the limestone Venus of Willendorf (c. 28,000–25,000 BCE) from Austria, the mammoth-ivory Venus of Lespugue from France, the stone figurines of Dolní Věstonice in Moravia (where fired-clay figures are among the world's oldest ceramics), and the Venus of Hohle Fels from Germany, dated to at least 35,000 years ago and among the earliest undisputed depictions of the human body. Not all figurines fit the voluptuous stereotype; some are slender, and a few are sexless or possibly male.

Interpretation

Their purpose is unknown and much debated. Proposed explanations include fertility symbols or a mother-goddess cult; self-portraits by women depicting their own bodies as seen looking down (which would explain the perspective and absent faces); erotica; good-luck charms or tokens of health and survival in a glacial climate, where fatness signalled success; and markers of identity or exchange networks — the wide distribution of a shared style suggests information moved across Ice Age Europe. Modern scholarship stresses that no single explanation accounts for all the figurines and warns against projecting later ideas of "Venus" onto Paleolithic art.

Discovery

The first to be recognized, the so-called Vénus impudique from Laugerie-Basse in France (found in 1864), set the naming convention. More than 200 figurines and fragments have since been catalogued, from sites including Brassempouy, Willendorf, Kostenki and Mal'ta.
