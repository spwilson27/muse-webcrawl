Tuatara

The tuatara (Sphenodon punctatus) is a reptile endemic to New Zealand. Though resembling most lizards, it is part of a distinct lineage, the order Rhynchocephalia, whose members flourished around 200 million years ago in the age of the dinosaurs. The tuatara is therefore often called a "living fossil". Its name derives from the Māori language and means "peaks on the back".

Anatomy and physiology

Tuatara have a distinctive third "parietal eye" on top of the head, complete with a lens and retina, which is covered by scales in adults and probably senses light levels. They lack external ears, have teeth fused to the jaw bone (acrodont dentition) that are not replaced, and are unusually long-lived—individuals may live well past 100 years. They are active at low body temperatures (16–21 °C), low for a reptile.

Distribution and conservation

Once widespread on New Zealand's main islands, tuatara were wiped out there by introduced Pacific rats and later predators, surviving on about 32 predator-free offshore islands. Captive breeding and island translocations, including releases on the mainland at sanctuaries such as Zealandia in Wellington, have helped secure the species. Two subspecies/populations were long recognised under a second species name (S. guntheri), though most current authorities treat the tuatara as a single species.

Life cycle

Tuatara mature slowly, reaching sexual maturity at 10–20 years. Females lay eggs only every two to five years; incubation takes 12–15 months, among the longest of any reptile, and sex is determined by nest temperature.
