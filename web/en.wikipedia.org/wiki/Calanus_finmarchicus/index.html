Calanus finmarchicus is a species of copepod found in the cold waters of the North Atlantic Ocean and the Arctic. It is one of the most abundant animal species on Earth and is considered a cornerstone of the North Atlantic marine food web.

Ecology

Adults reach 2–4 mm in length and are recognisable by red-orange pigmentation. The species feeds on phytoplankton, especially the spring diatom bloom, converting it into exceptionally energy-rich lipids: a large internal oil sac of wax esters can make up more than half of its body weight. These lipids make C. finmarchicus a vital food for larval and juvenile cod, herring, mackerel, and other fish, for seabirds, and for the endangered North Atlantic right whale, which feeds on dense surface aggregations of the copepod.

Life cycle

The life cycle takes about one year in most of its range. Late-stage juveniles accumulate fat through the summer, then descend to depths of several hundred to over a thousand metres to spend the winter in diapause, a dormant state with suppressed metabolism, before rising in early spring to moult, mate, and spawn in time for the phytoplankton bloom.

Climate change and harvest

As the North Atlantic warms, the range of C. finmarchicus is shifting northward and the species is declining in the North Sea, where it is being replaced by the less nutritious warm-water relative C. helgolandicus — a change linked to poor recruitment of North Sea cod. In Norway, a developing fishery harvests the copepod for its omega-3-rich oil, sold as a nutritional supplement, under regulated quotas.
