R v Dudley and Stephens (1884) 14 QBD 273 is a leading English criminal law case that established the precedent that necessity is no defence to a charge of murder. It arose from an episode of survival cannibalism following the sinking of the yacht Mignonette and remains one of the most famous cases in the common-law world, taught to generations of law students.

Facts

The English yacht Mignonette, en route to Sydney, sank in a storm in the South Atlantic on 5 July 1884, some 1,600 miles from land. Captain Thomas Dudley, Edwin Stephens, Edmund Brooks and the 17-year-old cabin boy Richard Parker escaped in an open dinghy with minimal food and no water, surviving on two tins of turnips and a caught turtle. After some 20 days adrift, with Parker near death from drinking seawater, Dudley — with Stephens's assent and over Brooks's abstention — killed the boy with a penknife. The three men fed on his body and were rescued four days later by the German barque Moctezuma.

Trial and judgment

On their return to Falmouth the men made no secret of what had happened, believing the "custom of the sea" protected them. They were nonetheless prosecuted for murder; a jury at Exeter returned a special verdict stating the facts and leaving the law to the court. A five-judge Queen's Bench court, led by Lord Chief Justice Lord Coleridge, held that no necessity could justify the deliberate killing of an innocent person: "Who is to be the judge of this sort of necessity? By what measure is the comparative value of lives to be measured?" Dudley and Stephens were sentenced to death, but the sentence was commuted to six months' imprisonment; Brooks was not charged.

Legacy

The case settled the law on necessity and murder in England and influenced courts across the common-law world. It also endures in popular culture — the name Richard Parker recurs, likely via this case and an earlier Poe tale, in works such as Yann Martel's Life of Pi.
