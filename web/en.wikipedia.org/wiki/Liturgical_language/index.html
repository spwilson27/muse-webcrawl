Liturgical language

A liturgical language is a language used in religious services and rituals that differs from the everyday vernacular of the worshipping community. Such languages are often archaic forms of a language preserved by tradition, or languages regarded as sacred because scripture was originally composed or revealed in them. The use of a special sacred language can add solemnity, preserve doctrinal texts unchanged, and connect worshippers across regions and centuries.

Examples

  • Latin — used for centuries in the Roman Catholic Church; after the Second Vatican Council (1962–65) most Masses are celebrated in vernacular languages, though Latin remains the Church's official language and is still used in the Traditional Latin Mass.
  • Koine Greek — the language of the New Testament and of the Byzantine liturgy in Greek Orthodox churches.
  • Church Slavonic — used in Slavic Orthodox and some Eastern Catholic churches; derived from the 9th-century translations of Cyril and Methodius.
  • Classical Arabic — the language of the Quran; Islamic prayer (salah) is performed in Arabic worldwide.
  • Biblical Hebrew — retained in Jewish prayer and Torah reading, even after Hebrew ceased to be a vernacular (it was later revived as Modern Hebrew).
  • Sanskrit — the liturgical language of Hinduism; mantras and the Vedas are recited in Sanskrit.
  • Pali — the canonical language of Theravada Buddhism.
  • Ge'ez — the liturgical language of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches, long extinct as a vernacular.
  • Coptic — used in the Coptic Orthodox Church, the final stage of the ancient Egyptian language.
  • Classical Syriac — used in Syriac Christian traditions.
  • Avestan and Old Persian forms — preserved in Zoroastrian ritual.
  • Ecclesiastical Latin, Old Church Slavonic, and Classical Armenian (Grabar) in their respective churches.

Characteristics

Liturgical languages are typically learned formally by clergy and scholars, pronounced according to fixed tradition, and changed only slowly. Because they are not native languages for most worshippers, comprehension may rely on translations, bilingual service books, or memorized responses. Some religious movements — notably the Protestant Reformation and the 20th-century liturgical reforms — have emphasized vernacular worship for accessibility.
