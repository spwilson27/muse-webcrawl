Coil spring

A coil spring is a mechanical device, typically made of spring steel, which is used to store and release energy, absorb shock or maintain a force between contacting surfaces. It is formed by winding wire into a helix. Coil springs are among the most common types of spring and appear in applications ranging from vehicle suspensions to mattresses, ballpoint pens and mechanical watches.

Types

Compression springs

Helical compression springs resist compressive loads. They are usually wound with space between the coils and shorten under load. They are used in car suspension systems, often around a shock absorber, and in mattresses and valves.

Extension springs

Extension (or tension) springs are wound tightly and stretch under a pulling load; their ends usually have loops or hooks. Examples include trampoline springs and garage-door counterbalance springs.

Torsion springs

Torsion springs resist twisting: the coil is wound or unwound by torque applied through legs extending from its ends. The clothespin and the mousetrap use torsion springs.

Spring rate

The spring rate (or spring constant) is the change in force applied divided by the resulting deflection, commonly measured in newtons per millimetre or pounds per inch. For a typical helical compression spring the rate depends on the wire diameter, coil diameter, number of active coils and the shear modulus of the material, following the relations described by Hooke's law for small deflections.

Materials and manufacture

Coil springs are made from music wire, oil-tempered wire, stainless steel and alloy steels. They are formed on coiling machines, then heat-treated, ground (for flat ends), shot-peened and often coated for corrosion protection.
