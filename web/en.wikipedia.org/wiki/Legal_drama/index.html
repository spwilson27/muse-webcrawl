Legal drama
The courtroom is the genre's central stage

A legal drama, also called a courtroom drama, is a genre of film, television and theatre which generally focuses on narratives regarding legal practice and the justice system. It follows the working and personal lives of lawyers, judges, plaintiffs and defendants, and draws its drama from the adversarial contest of the courtroom: the presentation of evidence, cross-examination of witnesses, closing arguments and the verdict. The American legal drama is closely related to the police procedural and the crime drama, but concentrates on the trial rather than the investigation.

Themes

Legal dramas characteristically place their protagonists in a moral conflict between the letter of the law and the demands of justice or conscience. Recurring themes include the presumption of innocence, the ethics of defending clients believed to be guilty, prosecutorial misconduct, perjury, the death penalty, plea bargaining, and the tension between legal technicalities and moral outcomes. Because the trial is structured as a contest with a formal conclusion, the genre lends itself to stories of underdogs, whistleblowers and crusading advocates.

History

Early landmarks in film include 12 Angry Men (1957), set almost entirely in a jury room, To Kill a Mockingbird (1962) and Anatomy of a Murder (1959). John Grisham adaptations such as The Firm (1993) and A Time to Kill (1996) popularised the legal thriller, a suspense-oriented subgenre. On television, Perry Mason (1957–1966) established the template of the defence attorney who invariably unmasks the real culprit in court. Later series such as L.A. Law (1986–1994), Law & Order (1990–2010; revived 2022), The Practice (1997–2004), Ally McBeal (1997–2002), Boston Legal (2004–2008), Suits (2011–2019) and The Good Wife (2009–2016) mixed case-of-the-week stories with serialised character arcs.

Accuracy

Legal dramas are frequently criticised by lawyers for compressing procedure, dramatising objections and surprise witnesses, and presenting trials that resolve far more quickly and decisively than real litigation. Studies have examined the "CSI effect" and similar influences of courtroom fiction on jurors' expectations of evidence and advocacy.
