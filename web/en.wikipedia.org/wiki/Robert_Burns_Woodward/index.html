Robert Burns Woodward

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Robert Burns Woodward (10 April 1917 – 8 July 1979) was an American organic chemist, widely regarded as the leading organic chemist of the twentieth century. He achieved landmark total syntheses of complex natural products — including quinine, cholesterol, cortisone, strychnine, and vitamin B12 — and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1965. With Roald Hoffmann he formulated the Woodward–Hoffmann rules for pericyclic reactions.

Career

Woodward entered MIT at sixteen and, after a brief expulsion and readmission, earned his bachelor's degree in 1936 and his PhD a year later, at age twenty. He joined the Harvard faculty in 1937 and remained there for his entire career. His 1944 synthesis of quinine with William von Eggers Doering made his reputation; a string of syntheses followed, each notable for elegant strategy and the use of physical methods and new reagents.

Major achievements

Woodward's syntheses included cholesterol and cortisone (1951), strychnine (1954), reserpine (1958), chlorophyll (1960, with Martin Streith and others), and cephalosporin C. The culmination was the total synthesis of vitamin B12, completed in 1972 with Albert Eschenmoser's group in Zurich after a collaboration involving about a hundred postdoctoral researchers. The Woodward–Hoffmann rules (1965) grew from an insight during this work, rationalising the stereochemistry of electrocyclic reactions through orbital symmetry conservation.

Legacy

The Woodward–Hoffmann rules reshaped mechanistic organic chemistry, and his approach to synthesis — planning around structure and mechanism at a strategic level — defined the modern discipline. He founded the journals Tetrahedron and Tetrahedron Letters with Robert Robinson, and the Woodward Research Institute in Basel.
