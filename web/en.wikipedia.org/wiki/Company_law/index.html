Company law (or corporate law) is the body of law governing the formation, governance, operation, and dissolution of companies and corporations. It treats the company as a legal person separate from its owners, regulates the rights and duties of shareholders, directors, and creditors, and prescribes how companies raise capital, keep accounts, and may be wound up.

[Image: incorporation documents]

Separate personality and limited liability

The foundational principle, settled in English law by Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897], is that a duly incorporated company is a person distinct from its shareholders. Its corollary is limited liability: shareholders' exposure is generally confined to the amount unpaid on their shares. Exceptions, often described as "piercing the corporate veil," allow courts to disregard the separate personality in cases of fraud or abuse.

Formation and constitution

Incorporation typically requires filing documents — articles of incorporation, a memorandum and articles of association, or equivalent filings — with a state or national registry. The company's constitution allocates power between the board of directors, who manage the business, and the shareholders in general meeting, who elect directors and approve fundamental changes.

Corporate governance

Company law imposes fiduciary duties on directors — duties of loyalty, care, and good faith — and provides mechanisms of accountability: shareholder voting, derivative actions, disclosure and audit requirements, and rules on directors' conflicts of interest. Public companies are additionally subject to securities regulation governing share issues, takeovers, and market disclosure.

Insolvency and dissolution

When a company cannot pay its debts, insolvency law (often integrated with company law) provides for liquidation, in which assets are realized for creditors, or rescue procedures such as administration and restructuring. Dissolution and striking off terminate the company's existence.
