Four causes

The four causes are elements of an influential principle in Aristotelian thought whereby explanations of change or movement are classified into four fundamental types of answer to the question "why?". In his Physics and Metaphysics, Aristotle argues that full knowledge of a thing requires knowing its four causes.

Four causes
MaterialThat out of which a thing comes to be
FormalThe form or essence of the thing
EfficientThe primary source of change
FinalThe end (telos), that for the sake of which

The four causes

Using a statue as an example: its material cause is the bronze from which it is made; its formal cause is its shape or design; its efficient cause is the sculptor who made it; and its final cause is its purpose, e.g. to honour a god or decorate a temple. Aristotle held that natural things, not only artefacts, have final causes: an acorn develops into an oak because that is its end.

Later history

The doctrine was transmitted through medieval scholasticism. With the rise of modern science, explanations in terms of final causes were largely discarded in physics and biology (though teleological language persists heuristically), while efficient and material causes dominated. Philosophers continue to debate the status of teleology and formal explanation.
