Nathan Glazer

Nathan Glazer (February 25, 1923 – January 19, 2019) was an American sociologist and public intellectual. A professor at Harvard University, he was a leading figure among the New York intellectuals and later an influential voice of neoconservatism, though he resisted simple labels. His works include Beyond the Melting Pot (1963, with Daniel Patrick Moynihan), The Lonely Crowd (1950, co-authored), and We Are All Multiculturalists Now (1997).

Early life and education

Glazer was born in New York City to Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe and grew up in East Harlem and the Bronx. At the City College of New York he belonged to the celebrated Alcove No. 1 circle of anti-Stalinist leftist students that included Irving Howe, Daniel Bell and Irving Kristol. After wartime service he earned his PhD in sociology from Columbia University, studied under Robert K. Merton and Paul Lazarsfeld, and worked at Commentary magazine under Elliot Cohen.

Career and ideas

With David Riesman and Reuel Denney, Glazer co-authored The Lonely Crowd (1950), a best-selling study of American character. Beyond the Melting Pot, written with Moynihan, argued that New York's ethnic groups — Blacks, Puerto Ricans, Jews, Italians and Irish — had retained distinct identities rather than assimilating into a common culture, and it reshaped debates about ethnicity in America. Glazer served in the Kennedy and Johnson administrations' housing agencies, but grew sceptical of Great Society social engineering, setting out his doubts in The Limits of Social Policy (1988). He defended affirmative action in the 1970s, writing Affirmative Discrimination (1975), but later revised his views; his 1997 We Are All Multiculturalists Now conceded, ruefully, that multiculturalism had prevailed in American education. He co-edited the journal The Public Interest and taught at Berkeley and, from 1969, at Harvard.

Death and legacy

Glazer died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, on January 19, 2019, aged 95. He is remembered as one of the most empirically grounded and least dogmatic of the New York intellectuals, an analyst of ethnicity, cities and social policy who repeatedly revised his own positions in light of evidence.
