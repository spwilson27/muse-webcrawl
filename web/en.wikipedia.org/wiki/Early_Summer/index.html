Early Summer (Japanese: 麦秋, Bakushū, literally "barley autumn" — the barley-harvest season) is a 1951 Japanese drama film directed by Yasujirō Ozu. It is the middle film of Ozu's informal "Noriko trilogy", preceded by Late Spring (1949) and followed by Tokyo Story (1953), each starring Setsuko Hara as a character named Noriko, though the three Norikos are separate characters in unrelated stories.

Plot

Noriko Mamiya, a 28-year-old department store secretary in Tokyo, lives with her extended family in Kamakura: her parents Shūkichi and Shige, her elder brother Kōichi (a doctor), his wife Fumiko, and their two young sons. The family and Noriko's friends gently press her to marry before she is considered too old, and a promising match is proposed with Mr Satake, a middle-aged businessman and golfing friend of her boss. Noriko, content with her unmarried life, resists without drama. When Kōichi's assistant, Kenkichi Yabe, a widower with a young daughter, is transferred to Akita, Noriko spontaneously decides to marry him and move away — a choice the family at first laments, preferring the grander match. In the closing scenes the parents, left behind in Kamakura, reflect on their children's dispersal, watched over by a field of ripening barley.

Themes and style

The film exemplifies Ozu's mature style: low, static "tatami shot" camera positions, direct cuts and "pillow shots" of landscapes and objects, and an elliptical treatment of major events — the marriage decision itself happens off-screen. Critics have noted the film's cyclical view of family life, its gentle comedy, and the way Noriko's choice subverts the family's plans while fulfilling the social expectation of marriage in an unexpected form. The critic Donald Richie and others rank it among Ozu's finest achievements.

Reception and legacy

Early Summer won the Kinema Junpo award and is regularly placed on lists of the greatest films ever made; it appeared in the British Film Institute's Sight and Sound critics' polls. In 2011 The Criterion Collection released it on Blu-ray alongside the companion Noriko films. The film's Japanese title refers to the season when barley is harvested, roughly early summer — a metaphor for Noriko being "ripe" for marriage.
