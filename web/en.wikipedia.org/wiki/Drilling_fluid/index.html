Drilling fluid

In geotechnical engineering, drilling fluid, also known as drilling mud, is a fluid used to aid the drilling of boreholes into the earth, most notably for oil and gas wells. It is circulated down the drill pipe, through the bit and back up the annulus to the surface, where cuttings are removed and the fluid is reconditioned and reused.

Functions

Drilling fluid carries rock cuttings to the surface, cools and lubricates the bit, balances subsurface pressures to prevent blowouts, supports the borehole wall with a filter cake, and provides hydrostatic pressure that keeps formation fluids out of the wellbore.

Types

The main categories are water-based muds, oil-based muds and synthetic-based muds, along with gaseous fluids such as air and foam for certain shallow drilling. Common additives include bentonite clay, barite for density, and polymers and chemicals that control viscosity, fluid loss and shale stability. The specialists who manage mud properties on site are often called mud engineers.

Environmental issues

Spent mud and cuttings can contain hydrocarbons and heavy metals and must be treated and disposed of according to regulation; oil-based muds face particular restrictions on offshore discharge.
