Bosom of Abraham

The bosom of Abraham (Greek: ὁ κόλπος Ἀβραάμ) is a term in Jewish and early Christian thought for the abode of the righteous dead awaiting resurrection — a place of rest and refreshment within or alongside Sheol/Hades. It is best known from Jesus' parable of the rich man and Lazarus in the Gospel of Luke (16:19–31), where the beggar Lazarus is carried by angels to Abraham's bosom while the rich man is tormented in Hades, a great chasm fixed between them.

[ Image: Medieval miniature of Lazarus resting in Abraham's bosom, shown as a cloth holding the souls of the righteous ]

Background

The imagery draws on the ancient banquet practice of reclining with one's head near the host's chest — the place of the guest of honour (compare John 13:23, the beloved disciple at Jesus' breast). In Second Temple Jewish literature the righteous dead were pictured rejoicing in Abraham's company; to be "in Abraham's bosom" was to share the patriarch's blessedness. The phrase also echoes the biblical idiom of being "gathered to one's people".

In Christian theology

Early church writers variously treated Abraham's bosom as a literal compartment of Hades, the resting place of the just before Christ's resurrection, or as equivalent to Paradise or Heaven itself. Augustine and others debated whether it was a place or a state of rest. In later Catholic theology it was associated with the limbus patrum, the limbo of the fathers, emptied at the Harrowing of Hell. Medieval art commonly depicted Abraham holding small figures — the souls of the blessed — in a fold of cloth across his lap.

In other traditions

The image survived in hymnody and spirituals ("Rock-a my soul in the bosom of Abraham") and as a general idiom for peaceful rest after death.
