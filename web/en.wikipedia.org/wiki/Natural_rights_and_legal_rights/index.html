Natural rights and legal rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Natural rights are rights that people supposedly have inherently, by virtue of their humanity — not granted by any law, custom or government, and in strong versions inalienable and universal. Legal rights, by contrast, are rights conferred and protected by a legal system: created, modified and enforceable through constitutions, statutes and courts. The contrast structures much of rights theory.

History and debate

Natural rights descend from natural law (medieval and scholastic traditions) through Grotius, Locke's life, liberty and estate, and the American Declaration's "unalienable Rights" and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man. Jeremy Bentham dismissed natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts"; legal positivism recast rights as legal artefacts; and the catastrophe-driven 20th century rebuilt the idea internationally as human rights — positive-law instruments (the UDHR, the Covenants) whose rhetoric still invokes inherent dignity. Analytical jurisprudence (Hohfeld's incidents: claims, liberties, powers, immunities) clarifies what any "right" actually contains; live debates include whether animals, nature and future generations hold rights, and whether socio-economic goods are rights in the same sense as freedoms.
