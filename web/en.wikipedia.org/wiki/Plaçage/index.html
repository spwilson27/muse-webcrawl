Plaçage

TypeRecognized extralegal union system
RegionFrench and Spanish colonial Louisiana, esp. New Orleans
Period18th–19th centuries

Plaçage was a recognized extralegal system in French and Spanish colonial Louisiana, by which ethnic European men entered into civil unions with non-European women of African, Native American and mixed-race descent. The term comes from the French placer, "to place with" — the women were known as placées.

History

The system developed in New Orleans and other Gulf Coast settlements where colonial populations had far more European men than European women, and where a large, relatively prosperous class of free people of color (gens de couleur libres) had emerged. A plaçage arrangement was typically formalized by a contract: the man agreed to provide the woman with a house, an allowance, and support and education for their children. The children, often called Creoles of color, could inherit property and were frequently sent to France for education.

The popular legend of glamorous "Quadroon balls", where mothers presented their daughters to prospective protectors, has been romanticized and embellished in later literature; historians note that the historical reality was more varied, that such unions arose partly from a shortage of marriageable women and partly within the constraints of colonial racial law, which barred legal marriage across the color line. Some placées accumulated substantial property and became notable property owners and businesswomen in New Orleans.

Decline

Plaçage declined after the Louisiana Purchase of 1803 as Anglo-American law and custom hardened racial categories, and it largely disappeared after the American Civil War. Well-known families of the New Orleans Creole community, including relatives of figures such as Marie Laveau and the ancestors of Homer Plessy, were formed within this system.
