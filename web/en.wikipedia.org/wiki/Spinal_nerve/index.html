Spinal nerve

SystemPeripheral nervous system
BranchesDorsal ramus, ventral ramus, meningeal branches, rami communicantes

A spinal nerve is a mixed nerve that carries motor, sensory and autonomic signals between the spinal cord and the body. In humans there are 31 pairs of spinal nerves, one on each side of the vertebral column: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal pair. (There are eight cervical pairs although only seven cervical vertebrae, because the first pair emerges above the atlas and the eighth below the seventh cervical vertebra.)

Each spinal nerve is formed by the union of a dorsal (posterior) root, which carries sensory afferent fibres from the body to the spinal cord, and a ventral (anterior) root, which carries motor efferent fibres from the cord to muscles and glands. The cell bodies of the sensory neurons lie in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on the dorsal root just before the two roots join. The nerve exits the vertebral column through an intervertebral foramen and almost immediately divides into a smaller dorsal ramus, supplying the back, and a larger ventral ramus, supplying the limbs and the front and sides of the trunk.

Function and clinical relevance

The ventral rami of the cervical, lumbar and sacral nerves intermingle to form plexuses—the cervical, brachial, lumbar and sacral plexuses—from which the major peripheral nerves of the limbs arise. Each spinal nerve supplies a characteristic area of skin (a dermatome) and a group of muscles (a myotome), so the pattern of sensory loss or weakness following nerve root compression, as in a herniated intervertebral disc, allows clinicians to localize the lesion. Inflammation of a spinal nerve or its root is termed radiculitis; shingles (herpes zoster) results from reactivation of varicella-zoster virus within a dorsal root ganglion, producing a painful rash in the corresponding dermatome.
