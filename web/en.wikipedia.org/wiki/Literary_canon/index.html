Literary canon

The literary canon is the body of books, narratives and other texts considered by critics, scholars and educational institutions to be the most important and influential works in a given tradition, language, or period — the works deemed worthy of study and preservation. The term "canon" derives from the Greek kanōn (a measuring rule or standard) and was transferred from the Biblical canon to secular literature.

The Western canon

The "Western canon" conventionally begins with Homer's Iliad and Odyssey and includes Greek tragedy (Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides), Virgil's Aeneid, Dante's Divine Comedy, Chaucer, Shakespeare — long regarded as the centre of the canon — Cervantes' Don Quixote, Milton, Goethe, Austen, Tolstoy, and modernists such as Joyce, Proust and Kafka.

Canon formation

Canons are not fixed by any single authority. They form gradually through the cumulative judgements of readers, writers, publishers, teachers and anthologists. Works such as Moby-Dick, ignored on publication, entered the canon decades later; others once central have faded. Literary critic Harold Bloom defended the traditional canon in The Western Canon (1994), arguing that aesthetic strength and influence ("strangeness" and originality) explain a work's survival.

Criticism and debate

From the late 20th century, the canon was challenged for over-representing white, male, European authors. Feminist, postcolonial and minority scholars sought to recover neglected writers and widen syllabi — a debate sometimes called the "canon wars" in American universities. Critics of expansion, like Bloom, argued that criteria of representativeness displace aesthetic judgement; reformers replied that the canon had never been politically neutral.

National canons

Many countries maintain informal national canons — for example, Shakespeare and Dickens in England, Goethe and Schiller in Germany, Pushkin in Russia, and Cervantes in Spain — which shape school curricula and national identity.
