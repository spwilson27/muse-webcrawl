Martin Chemnitz

Martin Chemnitz
Born: November 9, 1522, Treuenbrietzen, Margraviate of Brandenburg
Died: April 8, 1586 (aged 63), Braunschweig
Occupation: Lutheran theologian
Known for: Formula of Concord; called the "second Martin"

Martin Chemnitz (November 9, 1522 – April 8, 1586) was an eminent second-generation German Lutheran theologian, reformer, churchman, and confessor. So central was he to the consolidation of Lutheranism after Luther's death that a traditional saying holds: "If Martin [Luther] had not come before, Martin [Chemnitz] would scarcely have come after."

Life

Born in Treuenbrietzen, Chemnitz worked as a weaver's apprentice and Latin schoolteacher before studying at Wittenberg and Königsberg, where he also served as librarian and astrologer to Duke Albert of Prussia. Ordained in 1554, he became superintendent of the church in Braunschweig, a post he held for the rest of his life.

Theological work

Chemnitz was a principal author of the Formula of Concord (1577), which settled the internal doctrinal controversies of early Lutheranism. His Examen Concilii Tridentini (Examination of the Council of Trent, 1565–1573) remains a classic Lutheran response to Roman Catholic doctrine, and his posthumously published Loci Theologici was a standard Lutheran systematic theology for generations.
