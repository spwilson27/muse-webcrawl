The Battle of Hamel was a successful attack by Australian Corps troops, with attached American companies, on the village of Le Hamel in northern France on 4 July 1918, during the First World War. Planned by Lieutenant General John Monash, it is celebrated as a model of combined-arms tactics, achieving all its objectives in 93 minutes — close to the 90 minutes Monash had planned.

Background

The village of Hamel, held by the Germans since the spring offensive, overlooked Australian positions. Monash, newly in command of the Australian Corps, planned a set-piece attack integrating infantry, 60 Mark V tanks, artillery with predicted fire, aircraft for reconnaissance, supply dropping and noise masking, and gas and smoke in the barrage.

Battle

The attack began at 3:02 a.m. on 4 July under a massive creeping barrage. Tanks crushed wire and carried supplies, aircraft drowned out their engine noise, and infantry took Hamel, Vaire Wood and the Pear Trench. Four companies of the U.S. 33rd Division fought alongside the Australians — their first action in the war, on American Independence Day — despite orders from General Pershing that they be withdrawn. By 7:00 a.m. all objectives were secured, with some 1,600 prisoners taken.

Aftermath

Hamel became the template for the great Allied offensive at Amiens a month later. Monash's methods — meticulous planning, all-arms integration and care to minimise casualties — are still studied as a landmark in the development of modern warfare.
