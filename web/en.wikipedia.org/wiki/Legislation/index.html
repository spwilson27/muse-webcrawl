Legislation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legislation is the process or result of enrolling, enacting or promulgating laws by a legislature, parliament or analogous governing body. Legislation in the product sense is synonymous with statutory law (primary legislation), distinguished from delegated or secondary legislation made by executives and agencies under parliamentary authority (regulations, statutory instruments, rules) and from judge-made law.

Process and quality

A bill's journey — drafting (by parliamentary counsel or agency lawyers), readings, committee scrutiny, amendment, passage in both houses where bicameral, assent and promulgation — is designed for deliberation and is bent by party discipline, guillotines and omnibus packaging in practice. Modern legislatures delegate massively (the "Henry VIII clauses" debate in the UK; US non-delegation doctrine's revival) and legislate at volumes their members cannot read, shifting effective lawmaking to executives and courts. Drafting quality is a standing professional concern (plain-language movements, post-legislative scrutiny, sunset and review clauses), as are legislative impact assessments and the empirical evaluation of statutes after enactment.
