Korean New Year

Sebae — the deep New Year bow to elders — is the heart of Seollal

Korean New Year, known as Seollal (Korean: 설날), is one of the most important traditional holidays of Korea, celebrating the first day of the Korean lunar calendar. The holiday normally lasts three days — the day before, the day itself, and the day after — and is a national public holiday in both South and North Korea, occasioning one of the largest annual migrations as families travel to their hometowns.

Customs

On Seollal morning families dress in colourful traditional clothing (hanbok) and perform charye, memorial rites for ancestors at a table laden with food. Afterwards comes sebae: younger members kneel and bow deeply to their elders, offering wishes for health and fortune; elders reply with blessings and give the young money (sebaetdon) in silk or paper pouches. Everyone eats tteokguk, a soup of sliced rice cakes — eating it is traditionally said to add a year to one's age and bring luck, since the white rice cakes symbolise purity and the coin-shaped slices wealth.

Food and games

Other holiday foods include jeon (savoury pancakes), galbijjim (braised short ribs), japchae, and rice cakes and sweets prepared in quantity for guests. Traditional games include yutnori (a board game played by throwing four sticks), kite flying, neolttwigi (standing seesaw jumping, traditionally for women), and jegi chagi (footbag). Television broadcasts special programmes and folk-game contests, and palaces and folk villages hold public celebrations.

History

Seollal descends from ancient lunar new year observances documented in Chinese and Korean records of the Three Kingdoms period, when rituals at the winter new moon are described in the Book of Sui and Korean histories. During the Japanese colonial period the lunar new year was officially discouraged in favour of the solar "shinjeong", but Seollal was restored as a public holiday in 1985 and extended to three days in 1989, reflecting its enduring place at the centre of Korean family life.
