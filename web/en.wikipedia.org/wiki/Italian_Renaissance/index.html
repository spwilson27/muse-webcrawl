The Italian Renaissance (Rinascimento) was the earliest manifestation of the general European Renaissance, a period of great cultural change and achievement that began in Italy during the 14th century and spread through the 15th and 16th. Centred first on Florence, it marked the transition between the Middle Ages and modernity: a revival of classical Greek and Roman learning (humanism) combined with new achievements in painting, architecture, sculpture, literature, science and political thought.

Origins

Wealthy Italian city-states, enriched by Mediterranean trade and banking, provided patronage unavailable elsewhere in Europe, while competition between courts and republics turned art into civic prestige. The Medici bankers of Florence bankrolled artists and scholars; humanists such as Petrarch and later Marsilio Ficino recovered and translated classical texts, many arriving with Byzantine scholars before and after the fall of Constantinople (1453). In 1401 the competition for Florence's baptistery doors, won by Lorenzo Ghiberti over Filippo Brunelleschi, is often taken as a symbolic starting point; Brunelleschi went on to engineer the dome of Florence Cathedral (completed 1436) and to codify linear perspective.

High Renaissance

Around 1495–1527 the movement reached its climax, shifting its centre to Rome under popes Julius II and Leo X. Leonardo da Vinci painted the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper; Michelangelo sculpted the David and painted the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–12); Raphael painted The School of Athens in the Vatican stanze; Bramante designed the new St Peter's Basilica. In literature and politics, Machiavelli's The Prince (written 1513) and Castiglione's Book of the Courtier (1528) defined new models of statecraft and manners.

Decline and spread

The Italian Wars (1494–1559) made the peninsula a battleground for France and Spain; the Sack of Rome in 1527 dispersed artists across Europe. The later, more self-conscious style known as Mannerism succeeded the High Renaissance in Italy, while Renaissance ideas spread northward — carried by print (Italy itself, especially Venice, was an early printing hub), trade and itinerant artists — shaping the Northern Renaissance.
