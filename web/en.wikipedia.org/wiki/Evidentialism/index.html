Evidentialism

Evidentialism is a theory of epistemic justification according to which a belief is justified when it is supported by the evidence possessed by the believer. In its standard formulation by Earl Conee and Richard Feldman, a doxastic attitude (believing, disbelieving, or suspending judgment) is justified for a subject at a time when it "fits" the subject's total evidence at that time.

Overview

Evidentialists hold that justification supervenes on evidence alone: two subjects with the same evidence are equally justified in their beliefs, regardless of other factors. This makes evidentialism an internalist theory, since the justifying factors are accessible to the believer. The view contrasts with reliabilism and other externalist theories that tie justification to the reliability of the belief-forming process.

Ethics of belief

Evidentialism is closely associated with W. K. Clifford's famous 1877 maxim that "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence." Critics such as William James replied that some beliefs may be legitimately held ahead of, or beyond, the evidence.

Criticisms

Objections to evidentialism include the problem of forgotten evidence, cases of unjustified but blameless belief, and the "new evil demon" problem, which evidentialists typically treat as supporting their internalism. Debates also concern whether epistemic justification is purely a matter of evidence or also involves pragmatic considerations.
