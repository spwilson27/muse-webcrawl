Alopecia is the medical term for hair loss — the partial or complete absence of hair from areas of the body where it normally grows. It is a symptom and a group of conditions rather than a single disease: hair loss may be localised or diffuse, scarring or non-scarring, temporary or permanent, and it arises from causes as varied as genetics, autoimmunity, hormonal change, infection, drugs, and physical traction or trauma to the follicle.

Classification and types

Androgenetic alopecia

The most common form worldwide, androgenetic alopecia (male- and female-pattern hair loss) is driven by genetic sensitivity of follicles to dihydrotestosterone. In men it typically produces recession at the temples and thinning at the crown; in women, diffuse thinning over the top of the scalp with a preserved hairline. Roughly half of men show significant loss by age 50.

Alopecia areata

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune disorder in which the immune system attacks hair follicles, producing round, smooth patches of loss on the scalp or beard. It may extend to the whole scalp (alopecia totalis) or the entire body (alopecia universalis). The course is unpredictable; regrowth is common but relapse frequent.

Other forms

  • Telogen effluvium — diffuse shedding triggered by illness, childbirth, stress, or nutritional deficiency, usually reversible.
  • Anagen effluvium — abrupt loss during the growth phase, classically from chemotherapy.
  • Traction alopecia — loss from sustained tension by tight hairstyles.
  • Scarring (cicatricial) alopecias — including lichen planopilaris and discoid lupus, in which the follicle is destroyed and loss is permanent.
  • Tinea capitis — fungal scalp infection, mainly in children.
  • Trichotillomania — compulsive hair pulling.

Causes and mechanism

Healthy hair cycles through growth (anagen), regression (catagen), and rest (telogen) phases. Alopecia results when follicles miniaturise, are attacked by inflammation, are pushed prematurely into shedding, or are destroyed outright. Hormones (especially androgens), immune dysfunction, thyroid disease, iron deficiency, and drugs such as anticoagulants and retinoids are among the systemic contributors evaluated in diagnosis.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the pattern and history of loss, scalp examination and dermoscopy (trichoscopy), a gentle pull test, and, where indicated, blood tests (iron, thyroid, androgens) or scalp biopsy — the key discriminator between scarring and non-scarring disease.

Treatment

Treatment targets the specific type. Topical or oral minoxidil and the antiandrogen finasteride are mainstays for pattern loss; corticosteroids and, since 2022, JAK inhibitors such as baricitinib are used for severe alopecia areata; antifungals treat tinea capitis; traction loss improves if styling tension is removed early. Hair transplantation redistributes follicles for permanent patterned loss. Many people choose no treatment, and psychological support and cosmetic options (wigs, head coverings) are recognised parts of care.

Psychological impact

Because hair carries strong social and identity significance, alopecia — especially areata and totalis — is associated with anxiety, depression, and reduced quality of life disproportionate to its physical severity, and support organisations exist in many countries.
