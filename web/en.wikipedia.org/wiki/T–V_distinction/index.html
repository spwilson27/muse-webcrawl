T–V distinction

The T–V distinction is the use of different pronouns for the second person depending on the social relationship between speaker and addressee — typically a familiar or informal form (T) and a polite or formal form (V). The terms come from the Latin pronouns tu (T) and vos (V), and the distinction was analysed in the influential 1960 paper by Roger Brown and Albert Gilman, "The pronouns of power and solidarity".

Examples

  • French: tu (informal) vs vous (formal/plural).
  • Spanish: tú vs usted; in much of Latin America, vos forms (voseo) are also used.
  • German: du vs Sie.
  • Russian: ty vs vy.
  • Italian: tu vs Lei.
  • Persian, Hindi–Urdu (tū / tum / āp), Bengali, and many other languages have two or even three levels of gradation.

History and usage

The distinction arose in Late Latin when vos began to be used to address the emperor, and spread through the languages of Europe. English once had the distinction too — thou (T) versus ye/you (V) — but by the 18th century you had become the general form, leaving thou in religious, poetic and dialectal use. Brown and Gilman argued that pronoun choice reflects two dimensions: power (non-reciprocal usage between unequals) and solidarity (reciprocal usage within equals). Modern usage in much of Europe has shifted toward the solidarity principle, with the T form expanding into workplaces and public life, a trend accelerated in Sweden (the du reform) and post-Franco Spain.
