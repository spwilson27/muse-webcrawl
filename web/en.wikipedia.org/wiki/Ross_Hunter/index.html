Ross Hunter (born Martin Fuss; May 6, 1916 – March 10, 1996) was an American film and television producer, best known for the glossy melodramas and romantic comedies he produced at Universal Pictures in the 1950s and 1960s. "Ross Hunter movies" became a byword for lavish, escapist entertainment aimed principally at female audiences, featuring sumptuous sets, high-fashion costumes, and lush orchestral scores.

Career

Born in Cleveland, Ohio, Hunter worked as a schoolteacher before moving to Hollywood, where he briefly acted in low-budget films in the 1940s under the name Ross Hunter. He moved into production at Columbia Pictures and then at Universal, where his breakthrough came with the remake Magnificent Obsession (1954), directed by Douglas Sirk and starring Rock Hudson and Jane Wyman. Its success established the Hunter formula and a fruitful collaboration with Sirk that included All That Heaven Allows (1955), Written on the Wind (1956, uncredited involvement varies by account), and Imitation of Life (1959), the latter one of the year's biggest hits.

Hunter also produced the Doris Day–Rock Hudson romantic comedy Pillow Talk (1959), which won an Academy Award for its screenplay, as well as Midnight Lace (1960), Back Street (1961), Tammy Tell Me True (1961), Flower Drum Song (1961), The Thrill of It All (1963), and Madame X (1966) with Lana Turner.

Later work

His biggest commercial success came with Airport (1970), produced for Universal from Arthur Hailey's novel; the film grossed over $100 million and launched the 1970s disaster-film cycle. His subsequent films, including the musical remake Lost Horizon (1973), were less successful, and he moved into television production in the later 1970s.

Hunter's productions have been reassessed by critics as prime examples of the 1950s "woman's picture" and of Sirkian melodrama, and their knowing glamour has made several of them cult favourites. He died in Los Angeles in 1996.
