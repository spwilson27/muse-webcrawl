Quadratic variation

Placeholder: quadratic variation measures the accumulated squared increments of a process.

In mathematics, quadratic variation is used in the analysis of stochastic processes such as Brownian motion and other martingales. Quadratic variation is a type of variation, reporting the accumulated magnitude of a process's fluctuations over time. It plays a central role in stochastic calculus, in particular in Itô's lemma and in the theory of stochastic integration.

Definition

Let Xt be a real-valued stochastic process defined on a probability space. The quadratic variation of X up to time t is defined as the limit, in probability, of sums of squared increments over increasingly fine partitions of [0, t]:

[X]t = lim‖P‖→0 Σk (Xtk+1 − Xtk)²

where P ranges over partitions of [0, t] and ‖P‖ is the mesh of the partition. This construction can be extended to semimartingales, and more generally the covariation of two processes is defined by a polarisation identity.

Properties

A finite-variation process has zero quadratic variation. Standard Brownian motion has quadratic variation [W]t = t. More generally, an Itô process dX = μ dt + σ dW has quadratic variation [X]t = ∫ σ² ds, so only the diffusion term contributes. For a continuous martingale, the quadratic variation is the central object in the Doob–Meyer decomposition and in the definition of the stochastic integral with respect to the process.

Applications

Quadratic variation underlies Itô calculus: Itô's lemma contains a second-order term precisely because Brownian motion has non-zero quadratic variation. In mathematical finance, the realised variance of an asset price is an estimator of its (integrated) quadratic variation, and the quadratic variation of a price process determines the hedging error of discretely rebalanced portfolios. The related concept of total variation generalises the idea to paths measured with other powers; the p-variation framework interpolates between them.
