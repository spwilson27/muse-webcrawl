Siege of Breda (1624)

The siege of Breda of 1624–1625 was one of the most famous sieges of the Eighty Years' War. Spanish forces under Ambrogio Spinola invested the heavily fortified Dutch city of Breda in August 1624 and, after a blockade of more than nine months, compelled its surrender in June 1625. The victory was the last great Spanish triumph of the war in the Netherlands and was celebrated across Catholic Europe; it is commemorated in Diego Velázquez's celebrated painting The Surrender of Breda (Las Lanzas).

Background

After the Twelve Years' Truce expired in 1621, Spain sought to strangle the Dutch Republic economically and to recover key fortresses. Breda, a strong fortress in northern Brabant held by the House of Nassau, was an obvious target. The Dutch stadtholder Maurice of Nassau expected Spinola to attack elsewhere and the investment of Breda in late August 1624 caught the Republic off guard.

The siege

Spinola, rather than assaulting the powerful defences, built an extensive double line of circumvallation and contravallation — tens of kilometres of trenches, forts, and redoubts — and settled down to starve the city out. Dutch relief attempts by Maurice, and after his death in April 1625 by Frederick Henry, failed to break the blockade; a relief effort by Mansfeld's troops also failed. Inside Breda, food ran out and disease spread. With no prospect of relief, the governor Justin of Nassau surrendered on honourable terms on 5 June 1625; the garrison marched out with full military honours, a courtesy Velázquez depicted with famous magnanimity.

Aftermath

The fall of Breda was a propaganda triumph for Spain and the Archduchess Isabella in Brussels, but it exhausted Spanish finances and could not be repeated: the Dutch recaptured Breda in 1637 under Frederick Henry, and it remained Dutch thereafter. The siege became a literary subject too — Calderón de la Barca wrote a play on it, and it forms the climax of Arturo Pérez-Reverte's novel The Sun over Breda.
