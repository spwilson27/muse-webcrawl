Foraging

Foraging is searching for wild food resources. It affects an animal's fitness because it plays an important role in an animal's ability to survive and reproduce. In humans, foraging also describes the gathering of wild plants, fungi, berries and other foods, and by extension the subsistence strategy of hunter-gatherer societies.

Foraging theory

Behavioural ecologists study foraging through optimal foraging theory, which predicts how an animal should allocate time and effort to maximise net energy gain. Key models include the diet-breadth (prey choice) model, patch-use models such as Charnov's marginal value theorem, and central place foraging, in which animals carry food back to a nest or cache.

Influencing factors

Foraging decisions are shaped by predation risk, competition, food distribution and the animal's internal state. Many species use learning and memory — scrub jays, for example, remember the locations and ages of thousands of cached food items.

Human foraging

For most of human history, foraging (hunting and gathering) was the universal subsistence mode; it survives today among some peoples and as a recreational practice of gathering wild foods such as mushrooms and berries.
