Fluoride therapy

Fluoride therapy is the use of fluoride for medical purposes, principally the prevention of dental caries (tooth decay). Fluoride is delivered topically — in toothpastes, mouth rinses, gels and professionally applied varnishes — and systemically, through community water fluoridation, salt or milk fluoridation, and dietary supplements. Fluoride protects teeth by promoting remineralisation of enamel, enhancing the formation of acid-resistant fluorapatite, reducing demineralisation by bacterial acids, and inhibiting bacterial metabolism.

Forms and delivery

Fluoride toothpastes (typically 1,000–1,500 ppm fluoride as sodium fluoride, sodium monofluorophosphate or stannous fluoride) are the most widely used vehicle worldwide and are credited with much of the decline in caries in developed countries since the 1970s. Professional applications include acidulated phosphate fluoride gels, neutral sodium fluoride gels and fluoride varnish (about 5% sodium fluoride), recommended at intervals for people at high risk of decay. Systemic supplements are prescribed in some non-fluoridated areas for children, at doses varying by age and ambient water fluoride.

Effectiveness

Systematic reviews, including Cochrane reviews, find that fluoride toothpaste, varnish, gels and rinses each reduce caries increment compared with no fluoride, with greater benefit at higher concentrations and more frequent application. Community water fluoridation (adjusted to about 0.7 mg/L in the United States) has been called one of the ten great public health achievements of the twentieth century by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Safety

Excessive fluoride intake during tooth development can cause dental fluorosis, usually mild white flecking of enamel; very high chronic doses cause skeletal fluorosis. Acute toxicity requires ingestion of large amounts (the probably toxic dose is about 5 mg/kg body weight), which is why children's toothpaste use is supervised and supplements are dosed carefully. Fluoride compounds were once used systemically to treat osteoporosis, but this use was largely abandoned because of side effects and increased fracture risk at high doses.
