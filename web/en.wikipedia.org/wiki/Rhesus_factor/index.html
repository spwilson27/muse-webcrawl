Rhesus factor (Rh blood group system)

The Rh blood group system is a human blood group system containing proteins carried on the surface of red blood cells. After the ABO system it is the most clinically important of the blood group systems, mainly because of the immunogenicity of its D antigen—the Rhesus (Rh) factor. A person who carries the D antigen is designated Rh-positive (written, e.g., A+); a person who lacks it is Rh-negative (A−). The system is named after the rhesus macaque, in experiments on whose blood the factor was identified.

Discovery

In 1937 Karl Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener reported an antigen in rhesus monkey blood that agglutinated many human red cell samples; by 1940 the related human antibody was linked to transfusion reactions and to haemolytic disease of the newborn. The original monkey antigen turned out to differ from the human D antigen (the monkey antigen is now called LW), but the "Rh" name stuck.

Genetics

The Rh antigens are encoded by two adjacent genes on chromosome 1, RHD and RHCE. Most Rh-negative Europeans carry a complete deletion of RHD. The RHCE protein carries the C/c and E/e antigens; in total more than 50 Rh antigens have been described. Nomenclature follows two systems: the Fisher–Race notation (D, C, E, c, e) and the Wiener notation (Rh).

Clinical significance

  • Transfusion — Rh-negative patients should receive Rh-negative red cells to avoid forming anti-D, which can cause haemolytic transfusion reactions on later exposure.
  • Pregnancy — an Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive fetus may be sensitised by fetal blood entering her circulation, usually at delivery. In subsequent pregnancies her anti-D antibodies can cross the placenta and destroy fetal red cells, causing haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis), which ranges from mild anaemia to fatal hydrops fetalis.
  • Prevention — anti-D immunoglobulin (e.g. RhoGAM), given during pregnancy and after delivery, prevents sensitisation and has made severe Rh disease rare where it is routinely available.

Population distribution

Rh-negative frequencies are highest among Europeans (about 15%) and especially Basques (up to roughly 25–35% in some surveys), lower in Africans (around 5% or less for D-negativity), and very low (under 1%) in East and Southeast Asian populations.
