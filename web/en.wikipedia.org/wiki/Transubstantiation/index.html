Transubstantiation

Transubstantiation
Doctrine of: Roman Catholic Church (also affirmed in some Eastern traditions)
Defined: Fourth Lateran Council 1215; Council of Trent
Subject: Eucharistic bread and wine

Transubstantiation is, according to the teaching of the Catholic Church, 'the change of the whole substance of bread into the substance of the Body of Christ and of the whole substance of wine into the substance of the Blood of Christ', occurring at the consecration in the Mass.

Theology

Using Aristotelian terms, the substance (essential reality) of the bread and wine is converted, while the accidents — appearance, taste, texture — remain unchanged. The term was in use by the 11th–12th centuries (Hildebert of Tours), was affirmed at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215), elaborated by Thomas Aquinas, and defined against Reformation alternatives at the Council of Trent (1551).

Other views

Most Protestant traditions reject transubstantiation, variously teaching consubstantiation (Lutheran), a spiritual (pneumatic) presence (Reformed), or memorialism (Zwinglian). Eastern Orthodoxy affirms the change (metousiosis) without binding itself to the scholastic explanation. See Eucharist and Real presence.
