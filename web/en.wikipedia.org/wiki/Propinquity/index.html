Propinquity

In social psychology, propinquity (from Latin propinquitas, "nearness") is one of the main factors leading to interpersonal attraction. The propinquity effect is the tendency for people to form friendships and romantic relationships with those they encounter often, simply because physical proximity increases the chance of interaction and familiarity.

Research

A classic study by Festinger, Schachter, and Back (1950) examined friendship formation in a married-student housing complex at MIT. Residents were far more likely to be friends with their immediate neighbours than with people living further away, and even small differences in functional distance — such as living near a stairway or mailbox — measurably affected who became friends.

The effect is related to the mere-exposure effect: repeated exposure to a person generally increases liking for them. Propinquity operates in workplaces, classrooms (where seating proximity predicts friendship), and neighbourhoods. Researchers distinguish physical distance from "functional distance" — the likelihood of paths crossing in daily routines — which is often the stronger predictor.

Modern context

With the rise of online communication, some scholars have argued that propinquity now includes virtual proximity: people who interact frequently in the same online spaces experience similar familiarity effects. Nonetheless, physical proximity remains a strong predictor of relationship formation, and most people's closest ties continue to be geographically near.
