The Single UNIX Specification (SUS) is a standard for computer operating systems, compliance with which is required to qualify for using the "UNIX" trademark. It specifies application programming interfaces (APIs), a command-line shell, and utility interfaces for operating systems, and is maintained by the Austin Group, a joint working group of the IEEE, The Open Group, and ISO/IEC. The core of the specification is closely aligned with the POSIX standard.

History

The SUS grew out of the "Unix wars" of the late 1980s and early 1990s, when competing vendors shipped incompatible Unix variants. An industry effort codified the common APIs as "Spec 1170" (named for the roughly 1,170 interfaces it catalogued), which The Open Group adopted in 1994 as the basis of its X/Open portability guides and the UNIX 95 branding program. Subsequent versions — UNIX 98, UNIX 03, and UNIX V7 — tracked revisions of POSIX. In 2001 the SUS and POSIX were merged into a single jointly developed standard, POSIX.1-2001, so that one document serves both as the IEEE standard and as the Single UNIX Specification.

Contents

The specification is organized into volumes covering Base Definitions, System Interfaces, System Headers, Shell and Utilities, and Rationale. It defines the C library and system-call interface, the sh shell language and hundreds of standard utilities (from awk and grep to make and vi), extended curses interfaces, and conformance profiles. Certification involves the VSX test suites and grants the right to use the UNIX mark; certified systems have included macOS, AIX, HP-UX, Solaris, and z/OS.

Relation to Linux

Most Linux distributions are highly POSIX-compatible but are generally not formally certified, largely because of the cost and per-release nature of certification; a small number of enterprise distributions have obtained UNIX or "Inspired by" registrations over the years. The Linux Standard Base drew on the SUS but has been largely dormant since the mid-2010s.

Editions

Major technical editions include Issue 6 (2001, POSIX.1-2001), Issue 7 (2008, POSIX.1-2008, with technical corrigenda), and Issue 8 (2024, POSIX.1-2024), the first new edition in over fifteen years, which added interfaces such as getentropy and aligned utilities with modern practice.
