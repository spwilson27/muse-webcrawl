Bell's spaceship paradox

Bell's spaceship paradox
Field: special relativity
Proposed by: E. Dewan and M. Beran (1959); popularized by John Stewart Bell
Related concepts: length contraction, Born rigidity, Rindler coordinates

Bell's spaceship paradox is a thought experiment in special relativity. It was first described by E. Dewan and M. Beran in 1959 and became widely known after John Stewart Bell included it in his collection of essays on relativity. Two spaceships, initially at rest in an inertial frame and connected by a taut string, accelerate identically and simultaneously in that frame. The question is whether the string breaks.

The paradox

In the initial rest frame, the distance between the ships remains constant because both undergo the same acceleration programme. Naively, one might expect the string to remain unstressed. However, in the instantaneous rest frame of the ships, their separation increases: length contraction applies to the moving string as judged in the launch frame, but the ships' separation does not contract because their acceleration is synchronized in that frame rather than in their own. The string, which "wants" to Lorentz-contract, is stretched beyond its natural length and eventually breaks. Bell reported that many physicists initially answered incorrectly, and he verified the result with colleagues at CERN.

Resolution and analysis

The resolution emphasizes that maintaining a constant separation in the launch frame is incompatible with Born rigidity. In the ships' accelerating frame, described by Rindler coordinates, the rear ship must accelerate harder than the front ship to keep the proper distance constant; identical launch-frame accelerations therefore increase the proper distance. The paradox illustrates that relativistic effects are real physical stresses, not mere appearances, and that "relativistic rigidity" cannot be achieved by simply accelerating all parts of a body identically in one frame.
