Richard Edwin Shope (December 25, 1901 – October 2, 1966) was an American virologist. Working at the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research (later Rockefeller University), he was the first to isolate an influenza virus, the first to vaccinate animals against influenza, and a pioneer in demonstrating that viruses could cause cancer in mammals.

Early life and education

Shope was born in Des Moines, Iowa, in 1901. He studied at Iowa State College and then at the University of Iowa, where he received his medical degree in 1924, originally intending to practise medicine. Instead he was drawn into research on animal diseases, joining the Rockefeller Institute's department of animal pathology at Princeton, New Jersey.

Influenza research

In 1931 Shope isolated the causative agent of swine influenza and showed that the disease resulted from the combined action of a filterable virus—influenza A virus—and the bacterium Haemophilus influenzae suis. This was the first isolation of an influenza virus from any species and laid the groundwork for the isolation of the human influenza virus two years later by Wilson Smith, Christopher Andrewes, and Patrick Laidlaw in England, who used ferrets in experiments modelled on Shope's work. Shope later showed that serum from people who had lived through the 1918 influenza pandemic neutralised the swine virus, supporting the hypothesis that the 1918 pandemic virus was related to the swine strain and that it had persisted in pigs.

Shope papilloma virus

In the early 1930s, following reports from Kansas of cottontail rabbits bearing horn-like growths, Shope demonstrated that the growths (papillomas) were caused by a filterable virus, now called the Shope papilloma virus (cottontail rabbit papilloma virus). With Peyton Rous he went on to show that virus-induced papillomas in domestic rabbits could progress to malignant cancers—among the first clear demonstrations that a virus could cause cancer in a mammal. The rabbit papilloma work became a foundational model for tumour virology and later contributed to the understanding of human papillomaviruses.

Other contributions and honours

Shope also worked on pseudorabies, hog cholera, and the virus of fibroma in rabbits (Shope fibroma virus). He was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and received numerous honours for his contributions to virology. He was married to Helen Shope, and the couple spent much of his career in Princeton, New Jersey, where he died in 1966.
