Bantu peoples

The Bantu peoples are an ethnolinguistic grouping of roughly 400 distinct ethnic groups native to Africa who speak Bantu languages. Numbering around 350 million people in total, they form the majority population of much of central, eastern, and southern Africa, from Cameroon and Gabon in the west to Kenya and Somalia in the east and south to South Africa. The word bantu means "people" in many of the languages themselves, formed from the common root *-ntʊ̀ "person" with the plural class prefix ba-.

The Bantu expansion

Linguistic and archaeological evidence points to an original homeland in the borderlands of present-day Nigeria and Cameroon. Beginning roughly 3,000–4,000 years ago, Bantu-speaking farmers spread south and east in one of the largest demographic movements in prehistory — the Bantu expansion — carrying agriculture, ironworking, and pottery across the Congo Basin and the Great Lakes region, and ultimately to the continent's southeastern coast and interior. By about 1000 CE their languages dominated most of sub-equatorial Africa.

Peoples and cultures

Bantu-speaking peoples include the Zulu, Xhosa, and Sotho-Tswana of South Africa; the Shona of Zimbabwe; the Chewa, Tonga, and Bemba of Malawi and Zambia; the Kikuyu, Luo-adjacent Luhya, and Kamba of Kenya; the Hutu, Tutsi, and Twa of the Great Lakes; the Kongo, Luba, and Mongo of the Congo Basin; and the Swahili of the East African coast. Despite their linguistic kinship, Bantu societies developed highly diverse polities, from the decentralised village groups to kingdoms such as Kongo, Buganda, Luba, and Great Zimbabwe. The Swahili language, a Bantu language enriched by Arabic, became the lingua franca of East Africa and an official language of the African Union.
