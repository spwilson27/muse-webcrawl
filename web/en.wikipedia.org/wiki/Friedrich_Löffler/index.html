Friedrich Löffler

Born: 24 June 1852, Frankfurt (Oder)
Died: 9 April 1915 (aged 62), Berlin
Fields: Bacteriology, virology
Known for: Discovery of the foot-and-mouth disease virus; Löffler's medium

Friedrich August Johannes Löffler (24 June 1852 – 9 April 1915) was a German bacteriologist at the University of Greifswald. Working in the circle of Robert Koch, he made foundational contributions to bacteriology and is regarded as one of the founders of virology.

Career

Löffler studied medicine at the University of Würzburg and in Berlin, served as a military physician, and from 1879 worked at the Imperial Health Office in Berlin alongside Robert Koch. In 1884, together with Wilhelm Schütz, he identified the bacterium that causes glanders. The same year he isolated the diphtheria bacillus, Corynebacterium diphtheriae (also called the Klebs–Löffler bacillus), confirming Edwin Klebs's earlier observations and developing the blood-serum culture medium still known as Löffler's medium. In 1888 he became professor of hygiene at the University of Greifswald.

Foot-and-mouth disease virus

In 1897–1898, working with Paul Frosch, Löffler showed that foot-and-mouth disease of cattle was caused by an agent that passed through filters fine enough to retain the smallest known bacteria — the first demonstration that an animal disease could be caused by a filterable virus, following Dmitri Ivanovsky's and Martinus Beijerinck's work on tobacco mosaic disease of plants. This discovery is a landmark in the birth of virology.

Legacy

In 1910 Löffler founded a research institute on the island of Riems near Greifswald for the study of foot-and-mouth disease — the world's oldest virological research institute — which today bears the name Friedrich-Loeffler-Institut and serves as Germany's federal research institute for animal health.
