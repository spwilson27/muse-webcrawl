Literary criticism

Literary criticism (or literary studies) is the study, evaluation, and interpretation of literature. Modern literary criticism is often influenced by literary theory, the philosophical and methodological discussion of the aims and methods of literature. Although the two activities are closely related, literary critics are not always, and have not always been, theorists.

Whether or not literary criticism should be considered a separate field of enquiry from literary theory is a matter of some controversy. The terms are often used near-interchangeably, with "literary theory" understood as the more abstract, systematic reflection on literature and "criticism" as its practical application to particular texts.

History

Literary criticism in the classical period began with Plato, who in the Republic famously distrusted poetry as imitation removed from truth, and Aristotle, whose Poetics defended poetry and analysed tragedy. Horace's Ars Poetica and Longinus's treatise On the Sublime shaped criticism through the Renaissance and the neoclassical age, when critics such as John Dryden, Alexander Pope and Samuel Johnson judged works by standards of nature, reason and decorum.

Romantic critics, including William Wordsworth in the preface to Lyrical Ballads (1800) and Samuel Taylor Coleridge in Biographia Literaria (1817), shifted attention to imagination and the creative mind. In the 19th century, Matthew Arnold championed criticism as a disinterested effort "to know the best that is known and thought in the world", while historical, biographical and philological approaches flourished in the universities.

Twentieth-century schools

The 20th century saw a succession of influential schools and movements:

  • New Criticism — close reading of the text itself, associated with I. A. Richards, William Empson, John Crowe Ransom and Cleanth Brooks.
  • Russian formalism and structuralism — analysing literature through its devices, structures and sign systems (Viktor Shklovsky, Roman Jakobson, Roland Barthes).
  • Reader-response criticism — focusing on the reader's experience of a work (Stanley Fish, Wolfgang Iser).
  • Deconstruction — associated with Jacques Derrida, exposing instabilities and contradictions within texts.
  • Psychoanalytic criticism — applying Freud, Jung or Lacan to authors, characters and readers.
  • Marxist criticism — reading literature in relation to class, ideology and material conditions.
  • Feminist and gender criticism, postcolonial criticism (Edward Said's Orientalism, 1978), new historicism, ecocriticism and queer theory extended criticism to questions of power, identity and representation.

Criticism today

Contemporary literary criticism is practised both in universities and in journalism — book reviews, essays and cultural commentary. The rise of digital humanities has added computational methods such as "distant reading" (Franco Moretti) to the critic's toolkit, while debates continue over the canon, the value of theory, and the place of aesthetic judgement.
