Preregistration (science)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Preregistration records a study's hypotheses and analysis plan before data are examined.

Preregistration is the practice of registering the scientific methodology and statistical analysis plan of a research study in a public, timestamped repository before the study is conducted or before the data are examined. The purpose is to distinguish confirmatory research—testing clearly stated hypotheses—from exploratory research, in which hypotheses are generated after seeing the results.

Rationale

Preregistration addresses several problems identified during the replication crisis in psychology and other fields. Without a prior record of the analysis plan, researchers may engage in questionable research practices such as p-hacking (trying multiple analyses until one yields a statistically significant result), HARKing (hypothesizing after the results are known), and selective reporting of outcomes. By committing to hypotheses, outcome measures, sample size, and exclusion criteria in advance, researchers make deviations transparent: any change from the registered plan can be identified and must be justified.

Procedure

A typical preregistration document specifies the research questions and hypotheses, the study design, the sampling plan (including the planned sample size and stopping rule), the variables to be measured and how they will be operationalized, and the statistical models to be fitted. Registrations are stored on platforms such as the Open Science Framework (OSF), AsPredicted, or—in clinical medicine—trial registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov. Registrations may be embargoed for a period so that researchers are not scooped before publication.

Registered reports

A related format is the registered report, in which the introduction and methods of a study are peer-reviewed and provisionally accepted by a journal before the data are collected. The journal commits to publishing the results regardless of whether they are statistically significant, provided the approved protocol was followed, thereby reducing publication bias.

Criticism

Critics argue that preregistration may be treated as a bureaucratic formality, that vague preregistrations can still permit analytic flexibility, and that exploratory research—which is valuable for generating hypotheses—may be unfairly devalued. Proponents respond that preregistration does not prohibit exploration; it merely requires that exploratory analyses be labelled as such.
