Anthony the Great (c. 251 – 356 AD) was an Egyptian Christian monk from Egypt, revered since his death as a saint. He is known as the Father of All Monks, as his biography — the Life of Anthony written by Athanasius of Alexandria — helped spread the concept of Christian monasticism, particularly in Western Europe through its Latin translations.

Life

Born to wealthy parents in Middle Egypt, Anthony heard the Gospel command "sell what you possess and give to the poor" and, around the age of 20, gave away his inheritance and withdrew to live an ascetic life. He retreated progressively deeper into the Eastern Desert, living in a tomb, then in an abandoned fort at Pispir for some twenty years, then in the Inner Mountain near the Red Sea, where the Monastery of Saint Anthony still stands.

His struggles with demons and temptation in the desert became one of the most famous themes of Western art, treated by artists from Martin Schongauer and Hieronymus Bosch to Salvador Dalí. Disciples gathered around him, forming colonies of hermits — the Desert Fathers — and he twice travelled to Alexandria, to support martyrs during persecution and to preach against Arianism. He is traditionally said to have lived to 105.

Legacy

Anthony's feast day is 17 January. He is the patron saint of animals, skin diseases, and gravediggers; "St Anthony's fire" was a name given to ergotism and erysipelas. The pig often shown beside him in art relates to the Antonine hospitallers who kept pigs and treated the sick.
