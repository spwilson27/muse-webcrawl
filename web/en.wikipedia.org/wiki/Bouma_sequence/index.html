The Bouma sequence (after Arnold H. Bouma, 1932–2011) describes a classic set of sedimentary structures in turbidite beds deposited by turbidity currents. It was proposed by Bouma in 1962 on the basis of his study of deep-water sediments in the French Maritime Alps and has become the standard idealised model against which individual turbidite beds are compared.

Divisions

A complete Bouma sequence consists of five divisions, lettered Ta to Te from base to top, recording deposition from a turbidity current of waning energy:

  • Ta — massive to normally graded sandstone, deposited rapidly from suspension; often has a scoured base with sole marks such as flute casts.
  • Tb — planar (parallel) lamination in sand, reflecting upper-stage plane-bed flow.
  • Tc — ripple cross-lamination and convolute lamination, from lower-energy traction as the current slows.
  • Td — parallel lamination in silt and fine sand, sometimes called the upper laminated division.
  • Te — mud and silt, the fine "background" sediment that settles last, often bioturbated and containing pelagic microfossils.

Interpretation

Real turbidite beds rarely show all five divisions. Distal, low-energy deposits may consist only of the upper divisions (Tc–Te or Td–Te), a pattern described as a "partial Bouma sequence", while very coarse proximal deposits can be dominated by Ta. The sequence is widely used to interpret ancient deep-marine successions, to recognise turbidites in outcrop and core, and in petroleum geology to characterise deep-water reservoir sandstones.
