Congregational polity

[Image placeholder: historic congregational meeting house]

Congregational polity, often called congregationalism, is a system of ecclesiastical polity in which every local church congregation is independent and ecclesiastically sovereign (autonomous). Authority rests with the gathered members of the local church rather than with bishops or presbyteries. It contrasts with episcopal polity, governed by bishops, and presbyterian polity, governed by assemblies of elders.

Principles

Congregational churches hold that each local congregation, under Christ, has full authority to govern itself: it calls and dismisses its own ministers, admits and disciplines members, and manages its own property and doctrine. Wider associations of churches — such as the Congregational churches that later formed the United Reformed Church in England, or the United Church of Christ in the United States — are voluntary fellowships without coercive authority over member churches.

History

The polity emerged from English Separatism in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, among Puritans who rejected the episcopal structure of the Church of England. Robert Browne, John Robinson and the Pilgrim settlers carried it to New England, where documents such as the Cambridge Platform (1648) codified it. Congregational polity is also characteristic of most Baptist churches, the Disciples of Christ, and many independent evangelical churches.
