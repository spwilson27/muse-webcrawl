Trailing edge

The trailing edge of an aerodynamic surface, such as a wing or propeller blade, is the rear edge of the surface, where the airflow separated by the leading edge rejoins. It is the part of the surface furthest downstream in normal operation, and the point where the flows over the upper and lower surfaces meet and leave the body.

Aerodynamics

The shape of the trailing edge strongly affects lift and drag. Classical aerofoil theory, through the Kutta condition, assumes a sharp trailing edge at which the flow leaves smoothly, fixing the circulation around the wing. Blunt or thickened trailing edges can reduce some manufacturing and structural problems but generally increase base drag, though small amounts of trailing-edge thickness are sometimes used to improve handling or reduce noise.

Control surfaces

Most aircraft control and high-lift devices are mounted at the trailing edge: ailerons on the wings, elevators on the tailplane, rudders on the fin, and trailing-edge flaps that extend and deflect to increase lift at low speed. Helicopter rotor blades, propellers, turbine blades and sails likewise have defined trailing edges.
