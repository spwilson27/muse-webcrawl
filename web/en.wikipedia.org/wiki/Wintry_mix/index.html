Wintry mix

Accumulations of ice pellets and freezing rain during a wintry mix event

A wintry mix is a term used in the United States for a mixture of winter precipitation types occurring during a single weather event, typically including some combination of rain, freezing rain, ice pellets (sleet), and snow. The term is commonly used in weather forecasts when precipitation type is expected to vary — either across a forecast area, over the duration of an event, or both — and forecasters cannot assign a single dominant type.

Formation

Wintry mix precipitation arises from the vertical temperature structure of the atmosphere near a winter storm. Snowflakes forming aloft fall through layers of air of differing temperature. If they fall through a deep layer of above-freezing air, they melt completely into rain; if that rain then passes through a shallow sub-freezing layer near the ground, it may refreeze into ice pellets (sleet), or it may remain supercooled and freeze on contact as freezing rain. If the melting layer is thin and the surface layer is deep and cold, partially melted flakes refreeze into pellets; where the warm layer disappears, precipitation arrives as snow. Small variations in temperature of one or two degrees can flip precipitation type, which is why mixed events are among the most difficult to forecast precisely.

Terminology

In United States National Weather Service usage, "sleet" refers to ice pellets, whereas in the United Kingdom "sleet" usually means a mixture of rain and snow. The phrase "wintry mix" is an informal forecast shorthand rather than a technical precipitation category; METAR weather observation codes report the specific types present, such as SN (snow), PL (ice pellets), FZRA (freezing rain), and RA (rain). In aviation weather reports, the combined code for a mixture of several precipitation types may also appear.

Impacts

Mixed-precipitation events can be particularly disruptive because the hazards are varied: freezing rain accretes as glaze ice on roads, trees, and power lines, causing outages; ice pellets accumulate like coarse snow and are difficult to clear; and precipitation type may change repeatedly, making road treatment and travel decisions difficult. Even small amounts of ice can be more dangerous for travel than substantial snow, because a thin, transparent coating of ice ("black ice") may be nearly invisible.
