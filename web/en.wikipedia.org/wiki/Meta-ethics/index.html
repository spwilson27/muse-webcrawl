Meta-ethics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Meta-ethics is the branch of ethics that studies the nature of ethical thought and language. Rather than asking which actions are right or wrong (the business of normative ethics) or how moral codes should be applied in practice (applied ethics), meta-ethics asks what moral judgments mean, whether they can be true or false, whether moral facts exist, and how moral knowledge is possible. It is traditionally dated from G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903).

Central questions

  • Semantics: What do words like "good", "right" and "ought" mean? Are moral sentences assertions at all?
  • Ontology: Are there moral properties and facts, and if so, are they part of the natural world?
  • Epistemology: How, if at all, can moral beliefs be justified or known?
  • Psychology and motivation: Does sincerely judging an action right necessarily give one some motivation to do it?

Major positions

Cognitivist theories hold that moral sentences express beliefs that can be true or false. They include moral realism (moral facts exist independently of attitudes), divided between ethical naturalism (moral properties are natural properties) and non-naturalism (Moore's position: "good" is a simple, unanalysable, non-natural property — his "naturalistic fallacy" and open-question argument). Error theory (J. L. Mackie) is cognitivist but holds all positive moral claims false. Non-cognitivist theories — emotivism (Ayer, Stevenson), prescriptivism (Hare) and modern expressivism (Gibbard, Blackburn's quasi-realism) — hold that moral utterances express attitudes or prescriptions rather than beliefs. Between them stand subjectivism, relativism and constructivism (Korsgaard, Rawls).
