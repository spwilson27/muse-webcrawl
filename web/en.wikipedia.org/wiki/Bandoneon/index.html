The bandoneon (Spanish: bandoneón) is a type of concertina particularly popular in Argentina and Uruguay, where it is an essential instrument in most tango ensembles. Invented in Germany in the 1840s by Heinrich Band of Krefeld — from whom it takes its name — the instrument was originally intended as a portable substitute for the church organ for religious and popular music.

Description

Like other concertinas, the bandoneon is played by holding both ends and pushing or pulling the bellows while pressing buttons. Most bandoneons are bisonoric: each button produces a different note on the push and on the draw, a system that makes the fingering notoriously illogical but gives the instrument its characteristic phrasing. The standard "Rheinische Tonlage" 142-tone layout used in Argentine tango is purely diatonic in layout yet fully chromatic in range. Its timbre is reedy and melancholic, capable of sharp staccato attacks (marcato) and swelling long tones.

History in tango

German, Italian and Spanish immigrants carried bandoneons to the Río de la Plata in the late 19th century, where the instrument was absorbed into the emerging tango orchestras (orquestas típicas). Players such as Pedro Maffia, Pedro Laurenz and Aníbal Troilo raised it to a soloist's instrument. Astor Piazzolla, who studied with Troilo, brought the bandoneon to the concert stage and made it the voice of his nuevo tango style. The best-known maker, Alfred Arnold of Carlsfeld ("AA" or "Doble A" instruments), ceased production after the Second World War, and vintage instruments are highly prized.
