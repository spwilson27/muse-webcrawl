Capitation (healthcare)

Capitation is a payment model in healthcare in which a provider or organisation receives a fixed amount of money per enrolled person ("per head", from Latin caput) per period of time, in return for delivering, or arranging for, an agreed range of services — regardless of how much care each individual actually uses. It contrasts with fee-for-service payment, in which providers are paid for each item of care delivered.

How it works

Under capitation, a payer such as an insurer or a public system pays a practice or network a monthly sum for each patient on its list. Rates are usually adjusted for age, sex, and health risk. The provider bears financial risk: if patients need less care than expected the provider keeps the surplus; if they need more, the provider absorbs the extra cost within the fixed budget. Partial or "blended" capitation covers only some services, with others billed separately.

Advantages and risks

Capitation is intended to reward prevention and efficient care rather than volume, and gives payers predictable costs. Its critics warn it can create incentives to under-treat, to avoid enrolling expensive patients ("cream-skimming"), or to shift costs elsewhere, so systems using it typically monitor quality indicators and risk-adjust payments carefully. Capitation is a core funding model in the British National Health Service's general practice contracts and in US HMO arrangements.
