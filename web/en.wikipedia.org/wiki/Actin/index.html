Actin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Fluorescence image of actin filaments forming a web inside a cultured cell]
Fluorescence image of actin filaments forming a web inside a cultured cell

Actin is a family of globular, multi-functional proteins that form microfilaments — one of the three components of the Cytoskeleton — and the thin filaments of muscle fibres. Essential in all eukaryotic cells, actin filaments (F-actin), assembled from globular subunits (G-actin), drive cell shape, movement, division and intracellular transport, and partner with Myosin to power Muscle contraction.

Structure and dynamics

Actin monomers polymerise head-to-tail into a double-helical filament that constantly grows and shrinks ('treadmilling'), steered by regulatory proteins — profilin, cofilin, the Arp2/3 complex that builds branched networks for crawling cells. The system is a favourite target of pathogens: cholera and pertussis toxins modify actin regulators, and Listeria bacteria hijack polymerisation to rocket through cells on comet tails.

In muscle and beyond

In striated muscle, actin thin filaments interlock with myosin in the Sarcomere; calcium-regulated troponin and tropomyosin sit on the actin strand. Elsewhere actin underlies microvilli, the contractile ring of cytokinesis, filopodia and the synaptic remodelling thought to store memories. Beta-actin is so fundamental it serves as a 'housekeeping' reference gene in molecular biology.

History

Actin was discovered in 1942 by the Hungarian biochemist Brunó Straub in Albert Szent-Györgyi's Szeged laboratory, during the work that founded modern muscle biochemistry — a wartime Hungarian school that also gave myosin its first characterisation.
