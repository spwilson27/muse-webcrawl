Ming (typeface)

Ming (Chinese: 明朝體, Míngcháotǐ; Japanese: 明朝体, Minchōtai; Korean: 명조체, Myeongjoche) is a category of typeface used for printing Chinese characters, and likewise for Japanese kana and Korean hangul in East Asian typography. It is the East Asian analogue of the serif style in Latin typography: strokes have varying thickness, with thick verticals and thin horizontals, and horizontal strokes end in small triangular serifs (Japanese: uroko, "fish scales").

History

The style developed during the Ming dynasty, when block printing for a growing publishing industry standardized letterforms derived from regular-script calligraphy; horizontal strokes were slimmed, partly to suit carving along the wood grain, while verticals remained heavy. The name reflects this origin. In the twentieth century, Ming/Minchō became the default body-text face for books and newspapers in China, Japan, Taiwan, and Korea, and it remains a standard system font style throughout East Asia.

Terminology

In mainland China the same style is usually called Song (宋体, Sòngtǐ) after its Song-dynasty antecedents, while "Ming" predominates in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Japan. Well-known digital implementations include SimSun, PMingLiU, MS Mincho, and Songti SC. The contrasting East Asian category, heavy uniform strokes without serifs, is called Gothic or Hei (sans-serif).
