In elementary geometry, a polytope is a geometric object with flat sides (faces), existing in any number of dimensions. A polygon is a polytope in two dimensions, a polyhedron in three dimensions, and so on in higher dimensions (such as a 4-polytope in four dimensions). Some theories further generalize the idea to include unbounded objects such as apeirotopes and tessellations, and abstract polytopes.

The term was coined by mathematician Alicia Boole Stott, daughter of logician George Boole. The study of polytopes — especially regular and convex polytopes — connects geometry with combinatorics, group theory, linear programming and optimization.

Definitions

Several inequivalent definitions are in use. A convex polytope may be defined as the convex hull of a finite set of points, or equivalently as a bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces; the equivalence of these two descriptions is a fundamental result sometimes called the Weyl–Minkowski theorem. More general (possibly non-convex or self-intersecting) polytopes can be built by gluing together lower-dimensional faces, formalized in the theory of abstract polytopes as partially ordered sets of faces satisfying certain incidence axioms.

Elements

A polytope consists of elements of different dimensions known collectively as faces. In n dimensions these include the vertices (0-faces), edges (1-faces), polygonal faces (2-faces), and cells (3-faces), up to the facets of dimension n − 1. The whole polytope and the empty set are counted as faces of dimensions n and −1 respectively. The pattern of incidences among faces is recorded in combinatorial invariants such as the f-vector, which counts faces of each dimension.

Regular polytopes

A regular polytope is one whose symmetry group acts transitively on its flags (maximal chains of incident faces). In two dimensions these are the regular polygons; in three dimensions the five Platonic solids; in four dimensions there are six convex regular polychora, including the tesseract and the 120-cell. In five and higher dimensions only three families survive: the simplex, the hypercube and the cross-polytope.

Applications

Convex polytopes appear throughout applied mathematics. The feasible region of a linear program is a convex polyhedron, and the simplex algorithm moves along its edges. Polytopes also arise in algebraic geometry (toric varieties), in the study of crystal structures, and, through the theory of state polytopes and Gröbner bases, in computational algebra.
