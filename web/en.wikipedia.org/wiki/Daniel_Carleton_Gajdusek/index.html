Daniel Carleton Gajdusek

Born: September 9, 1923, Yonkers, New York
Died: December 12, 2008 (aged 85), Tromsø, Norway
Field: Virology, medical research
Award: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1976)

Daniel Carleton Gajdusek (September 9, 1923 – December 12, 2008) was an American physician and medical researcher who shared the 1976 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work on "slow virus" infections — the transmissible spongiform encephalopathies later understood as prion diseases.

Kuru research

From 1957 Gajdusek studied kuru, a fatal degenerative brain disease epidemic among the Fore people of the New Guinea highlands. He and his collaborators showed the disease was transmissible to chimpanzees after long incubation, and the epidemic faded as the Fore abandoned ritual endocannibalism, which had spread the agent. The work reshaped understanding of diseases such as Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease and scrapie.

Later life

Gajdusek headed a laboratory at the National Institutes of Health for decades and travelled widely studying isolated populations. In 1996 he pleaded guilty to child molestation charges arising from allegations involving boys he had brought to the United States from Micronesia and New Guinea, serving a prison sentence before relocating to Europe. The Nobel Foundation does not revoke prizes, and he remained a laureate until his death in Norway in 2008.
