Great Exhibition

The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations, also known as the Great Exhibition or the Crystal Palace Exhibition, was an international exhibition that took place in Hyde Park, London, from 1 May to 15 October 1851. Organised by Henry Cole and Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, husband of Queen Victoria, it was the first in the series of world's fairs that became defining showcases of industrial culture. More than six million people — equivalent to a third of Britain's population — visited its 14,000 exhibits.

The Crystal Palace

Paxton, a gardener-engineer, sketched the building on blotting paper during a railway board meeting: a vast prefabricated structure of cast iron and the largest sheets of glass then made, erected in months around living elm trees in the park. Contemporaries were astonished by its light and speed of assembly; after the exhibition it was re-erected, enlarged, at Sydenham in south London, where it stood until destroyed by fire in 1936.

Exhibits and meaning

Displays ranged from the Koh-i-Noor diamond and great steam engines to Jacquard looms, Colt revolvers, a lump of Canadian timber and a stuffed elephant carrying a howdah. For its organisers the exhibition preached free trade, peace through industry, and design reform; for the crowds, arriving on cheap excursion trains organised by Thomas Cook, it was simply the spectacle of the age — proof, as the Victorian era believed, that British industry led the world, and a source of quiet anxiety that rivals were catching up.
