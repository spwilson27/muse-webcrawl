A Bernal sphere is a type of space habitat intended as a long-term home for permanent residents, first proposed in 1929 by the Irish scientist and crystallographer John Desmond Bernal. In its original form, the Bernal sphere was a hollow non-rotating spherical shell 1.6 kilometres (1 mi) in diameter, filled with air and with a target population of 20,000 to 30,000 people. Bernal imagined it as a giant hollow globe constructed from materials mined from asteroids or other celestial bodies, within which a population could live, work, and reproduce, building further spheres as the population grew.

Original proposal

Bernal described the sphere in his 1929 essay "The World, the Flesh and the Devil". He envisioned a shell perhaps 16 kilometres or more across in later generations, with the interior surface used for habitation and agriculture. Because the original design did not rotate, the interior would be in near-weightlessness, with inhabitants living on structures built inward from the shell or floating within it. Bernal predicted that such habitats would eventually outnumber planetary populations and could spread through the Solar System and beyond.

Later versions

In the 1970s, physicist Gerard K. O'Neill and his students at Princeton developed modified versions of the concept. The "Island One" design was a modified Bernal sphere about 500 metres in diameter that rotated to provide artificial gravity of about 0.9 to 1.0 g at its equator. A later, larger concept — sometimes called the "Island Three" or O'Neill cylinder — scaled the idea up dramatically. The rotating Bernal sphere concept was studied as part of the 1975 NASA Ames/Stanford summer study on space colonization, which examined spheres up to about 16 km in diameter housing up to a million inhabitants in some configurations.

In popular culture

Bernal spheres and related rotating habitats appear frequently in science fiction, and the concept influenced later fictional depictions of space colonies, including the Stanford torus and the cylindrical habitats of works such as Rendezvous with Rama.
