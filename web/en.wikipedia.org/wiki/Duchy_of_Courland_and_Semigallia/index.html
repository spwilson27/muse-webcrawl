Duchy of Courland and Semigallia

Rundāle Palace, built for Duke Ernst Johann von Biron

The Duchy of Courland and Semigallia was a duchy in the Baltic region, existing from 1561 to 1795. It was created from the southern part of the Livonian Order's lands when Gotthard Kettler, the last master of the Order, secularised his state under the Treaty of Vilnius and became the first duke, a vassal of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and later of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. Its capitals were Mitau (Jelgava) and Goldingen (Kuldīga). Despite its small size, the duchy is remarkable for having maintained a merchant fleet and established short-lived colonies in Tobago and at the mouth of the Gambia River in the seventeenth century.

History

Under Gotthard Kettler and his sons the duchy weathered the pressures of the Polish–Swedish wars. Its golden age came under Duke Jacob Kettler (reigned 1642–1682), who promoted shipbuilding, metalworking and textile production, traded with most of Europe, and launched Courland's colonial ventures: a settlement on Tobago (held intermittently from 1654) and Fort Jacob on an island in the Gambia (1651–1661). Jacob's ambitions were curtailed when he was captured by the Swedes during the Second Northern War.

In the eighteenth century the duchy fell increasingly under Russian influence; Ernst Johann von Biron, favourite of Empress Anna of Russia, became duke in 1737 and built the palaces of Rundāle and Jelgava to designs by Bartolomeo Rastrelli. In the Third Partition of Poland (1795) the last duke, Peter von Biron, abdicated and Courland was annexed to the Russian Empire as the Courland Governorate.

Society

The duchy's population consisted mainly of Latvian peasants, ruled by a German-speaking landed nobility and burgher class, with Lutheranism as the dominant confession. Serfdom in Courland was among the harsher forms in Europe. The duchy's legacy survives in Latvian regional identity and in the name of Courland (Kurzeme), one of Latvia's historical regions.
