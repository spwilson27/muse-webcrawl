The siamang (Symphalangus syndactylus) is an arboreal, black-furred gibbon native to the forests of Indonesia, Malaysia, and southern Thailand. It is the largest of the gibbons, being roughly twice the size of other gibbons and reaching 1 m (3.3 ft) in height and weighing up to 14 kg. The siamang is the only species in the genus Symphalangus.

Two features distinguish the siamang from other gibbons. First, two fingers on each foot — the second and third toes — are partially joined by a membrane, giving the species its epithet syndactylus, "united fingers." Second, it possesses a large gular sac (throat pouch), which can be inflated to the size of its head, allowing it to make loud, resonating calls.

Behavior

Siamangs live in small family groups, typically a monogamous pair and their offspring, defending territories in the forest canopy. They are primarily frugivorous but also eat leaves, flowers, and insects. Mated pairs perform elaborate synchronized duets, amplified by the throat sac, that advertise their territory and strengthen the pair bond. Like other gibbons, they move mainly by brachiation, swinging by their long arms.

Threats

The siamang is classified as Endangered, chiefly because of habitat loss from logging and conversion of forest to oil palm plantations, together with capture for the illegal pet trade.
