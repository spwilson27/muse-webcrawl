Bose–Einstein statistics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Illustration of bosons crowding into the lowest energy state compared with classical particles]
Bosons, unlike classical particles or fermions, prefer to share the lowest state.

In quantum statistics, Bose–Einstein statistics describes the distribution of identical, indistinguishable particles with integer spin (bosons) over energy states in thermal equilibrium. Developed by Satyendra Nath Bose for photons (1924) and generalised by Albert Einstein to massive particles, it predicts that bosons — unlike fermions, which obey Fermi–Dirac statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle — can all occupy the same quantum state.

The occupancy of a state of energy E is 1/(e^{(E−μ)/kT} − 1). Applied to photons it yields Planck's law of black-body radiation with no classical input; applied to atoms it predicted the Bose–Einstein condensate; applied to liquid helium-4 it underlies Superfluidity. The contrast with Fermi–Dirac statistics is the statistical expression of the spin–statistics theorem.

Origin

Bose's 1924 derivation counted the ways indistinguishable photons could fill phase-space cells — treating arrangements that differ only by permuting identical photons as the same state. Einstein saw that the same counting for massive particles implied a condensation phenomenon and, in correspondence with Paul Ehrenfest, the bizarre wave-like interference terms that foreshadowed matter waves in gas statistics.
