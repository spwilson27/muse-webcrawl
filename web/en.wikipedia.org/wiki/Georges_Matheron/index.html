Georges Matheron

Georges François Paul Marie Matheron (2 December 1930 – 7 August 2000) was a French mathematician and civil engineer of mines. He is known as the founder of geostatistics and a co-founder, together with Jean Serra, of mathematical morphology. His work provided the mathematical theory underlying methods of estimating mineral resources from sample data, now used throughout mining, petroleum geology, hydrology, and environmental science.

Education and career

Matheron studied at the École Polytechnique and the École des Mines de Paris, training in mathematics, probability theory, and physics. In the 1950s he was assigned to the French Bureau de Recherches Géologiques et Minières (BRGM) and worked in Algeria, where he confronted practical problems of estimating ore grades in mines from limited sampling.

Geostatistics

Building on the empirical work of the South African mining engineer Danie Krige, Matheron developed a rigorous theory of regionalized variables — quantities distributed in space whose values show both random and structured behavior. His theory introduced the variogram as the central tool for characterizing spatial correlation and provided optimal linear estimation procedures. The estimation method he formalized was later named kriging in honor of Krige.

His major treatises, Traité de géostatistique appliquée (1962–1963) and The Theory of Regionalized Variables and Its Applications (1971), laid the foundations of the discipline. In 1968 he founded the Centre de Géostatistique at the École des Mines in Fontainebleau, which became the world center for research and training in the field.

Mathematical morphology

With Jean Serra, Matheron also developed mathematical morphology, a theory for the analysis of spatial structures based on set theory and lattice algebra, which became a standard approach in image analysis. His book Random Sets and Integral Geometry (1975) gave the theory its probabilistic foundations.

Legacy

The Georges Matheron Lectures, awarded by the International Association for Mathematical Geosciences, commemorate his contributions. The kriging methods he developed are now ubiquitous in spatial statistics, from ore reserve estimation to weather mapping and machine learning, where Gaussian process regression is mathematically equivalent.
