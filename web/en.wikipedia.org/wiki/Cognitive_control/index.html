Cognitive control

[Image placeholder: Brain regions of the frontoparietal control network]

Cognitive control is an umbrella term for the mental operations that allow information processing and behaviour to vary adaptively from moment to moment depending on current goals, rather than remaining rigid or automatic. It overlaps substantially with the concept of executive functions, and is associated especially with the prefrontal cortex and the frontoparietal control network.

Processes

Core processes studied under cognitive control include working-memory updating, response inhibition (as in the Stroop and go/no-go tasks), task switching and set shifting, and conflict monitoring, often attributed to the anterior cingulate cortex. Control can be proactive (sustained, goal-driven) or reactive (engaged after conflict is detected) — a distinction formalised in the dual mechanisms of control framework.

Development and disorders

Cognitive control develops through childhood and adolescence as prefrontal circuits mature, and declines in normal ageing. Impairments are implicated in ADHD, schizophrenia, depression and addiction, making control a major target of cognitive neuroscience research.
