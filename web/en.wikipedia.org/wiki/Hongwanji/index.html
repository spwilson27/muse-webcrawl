Hongwanji

The Hongwanji (本願寺, also read Hongan-ji, "Temple of the Primal Vow") is the collective name for the head temples of the Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land) school of Japanese Buddhism, the largest Buddhist tradition in Japan. The name refers to Amida Buddha's Primal Vow to save all beings who entrust themselves to him. Today there are two rival Hongwanji organizations in Kyoto—the Nishi (Western) Hongan-ji and the Higashi (Eastern) Hongan-ji—located only a few hundred metres apart.

History

The original Hongwanji was founded in 1272 in Kyoto by Kakushinni, youngest daughter of Shinran (1173–1263), founder of Jōdo Shinshū, on the site of his mausoleum. Under the eighth head priest, Rennyo (1415–1499), the temple's following grew enormously among peasants and provincial warriors, and the movement built fortified temple-towns. In the sixteenth century the Hongwanji, based at the fortress-cathedral of Ishiyama Hongan-ji in Osaka, fought a decade-long war against the warlord Oda Nobunaga (1570–1580) before surrendering.

The split

In 1602 the shogun Tokugawa Ieyasu, wary of the sect's power, granted land in Kyoto to the rival claimant Kyonyo, dividing the Hongwanji into eastern and western branches—an arrangement that persists today. Nishi Hongan-ji, the western temple, is renowned for masterpieces of Azuchi–Momoyama architecture and art, including the Hiunkaku pavilion and some of Japan's oldest Noh stages; its precinct is a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto. Higashi Hongan-ji's Goeidō (Founder's Hall), rebuilt in 1895, is one of the largest wooden structures in the world.

Modern role

The two Hongwanji remain the spiritual centres for millions of Jōdo Shinshū adherents in Japan and for overseas missions in Hawaii, North and South America, and Europe. The western branch's head temple organization is formally called Jōdo Shinshū Hongwanji-ha.
