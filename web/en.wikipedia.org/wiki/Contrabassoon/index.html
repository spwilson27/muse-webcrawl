Contrabassoon

Contrabassoon
ClassificationWind, woodwind, double reed
RangeOne octave below the bassoon; commonly down to B♭1 (written C2)
Related instrumentsBassoon, bass oboe, heckelphone

The contrabassoon, also known as the double bassoon, is a larger version of the bassoon, sounding an octave lower. Its technique is similar to its smaller cousin, and its repertoire is not extensive, but Arnold Bax, Gustav Holst, Carl Nielsen, Daniel Shostakovich, Charles Ives, and Igor Stravinsky have all written works prominently featuring the instrument, and it is a standard member of the large modern symphony orchestra.

Construction

The contrabassoon's tube is over 5.5 metres (18 feet) long, folded back on itself several times; modern instruments are usually made of maple (sometimes plastic or metal for marching and military use) and stand about 1.3 metres tall on an endpin. It uses a double reed slightly larger than the bassoon's. Leading makers include Heckel, Fox, Moosmann and Amati; the Contraforte, developed by Guntram Wolf and Benedikt Eppelsheim, is a modern redesign with a wider bore and improved intonation.

History and repertoire

Precursors such as the semi-contra and quart bassoon date to the 17th and 18th centuries; Handel, Haydn and Mozart knew early double bassoons, and Beethoven wrote for it in the Ninth Symphony. Verdi, Brahms, Mahler, Strauss and Ravel used it for weight and darkness in the orchestra's bass. Notable solo features include the opening of Ravel's Piano Concerto for the Left Hand and Susan Nigro's extensive catalogue of solo and chamber recordings.

Use in ensembles

Orchestras typically employ one contrabassoonist, often doubling as third bassoon. The instrument reinforces the bass line of the woodwind and strings, and appears in wind bands, film scores and contemporary ensembles. Its low notes are felt as much as heard, giving it a distinctive role in scoring for suspense and grandeur.
