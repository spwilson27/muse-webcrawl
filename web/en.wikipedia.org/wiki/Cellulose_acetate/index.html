Cellulose acetate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biochemistry, cellulose acetate refers to any acetate ester of cellulose, usually cellulose diacetate. It was first prepared in 1865 by Paul Schützenberger. A bioplastic, cellulose acetate is used as a film base in photography, as a component in some coatings, and as a frame material for eyeglasses; it is also used as a synthetic fiber in the manufacture of cigarette filters and playing cards. In photographic film, cellulose acetate film replaced nitrate film in the 1950s, being far less flammable and cheaper to produce.

History

Acetate was first introduced in 1865 by the French chemist Paul Schützenberger, who discovered that cellulose could react with acetic anhydride to form cellulose acetate. In 1894, Charles Frederick Cross and Edward John Bevan patented the first industrial process of manufacturing cellulose acetate in Britain. Commercial production of acetate fiber began in the United States in 1924 by the Celanese Corporation. Cellulose acetate film was introduced in 1934 as a replacement for the unstable and highly flammable cellulose nitrate film used in the motion picture industry.

Production

Until the mid-2010s, cellulose acetate was made from wood pulp processed through several stages. The pulp is treated with acetic acid and acetic anhydride in the presence of a catalyst, typically sulfuric acid, producing cellulose triacetate, which is then partially hydrolyzed to the desired degree of substitution. The resulting flakes are dissolved in solvents such as acetone and extruded or cast into fibers, films, or molded articles.

Uses

  • Fibers: acetate fibers are used in apparel, linings, and home furnishings; triacetate is used for fabrics where wrinkle resistance is valued.
  • Cigarette filters: cellulose acetate tow is the standard filter material for cigarettes.
  • Film: "safety film" base for still photography and motion pictures, and the base for some magnetic tapes.
  • Membranes: used in reverse osmosis and other filtration applications.
  • Eyewear and tools: molded acetate is a common material for spectacle frames, screwdriver handles, and similar goods.

Degradation

Cellulose acetate film is subject to "vinegar syndrome", an autocatalytic degradation in which acetic acid is released as the material deteriorates, causing shrinkage, brittleness, and eventually destruction of the film. Archives store acetate film under cool, dry conditions to slow the process. Cellulose acetate is biodegradable, though the rate depends strongly on the degree of substitution and environmental conditions.
