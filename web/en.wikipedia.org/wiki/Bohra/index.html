Bohra

The Bohras are an ethnic and religious group native to the western Indian states of Gujarat and Maharashtra. The term most commonly refers to the Dawoodi Bohras, a Musta'li Ismaili Shia Muslim community, but it is also applied to the Alavi and Sulaymani Bohras, the Sunni Vohra community, and historically to some groups of Hindu Bohras. The word Bohra is traditionally derived from the Gujarati word vehru or vahoru, meaning "to trade", reflecting the community's mercantile origins.

History

The Bohras trace their origin to the arrival of Ismaili missionaries (da'is) sent from Fatimid Yemen to Gujarat in the 11th century, during the reign of the Fatimid caliph-imam al-Mustansir Billah. The community embraced Tayyibi Musta'li Islam, and after the death of the 21st imam al-Tayyib, authority passed to the Da'i al-Mutlaq, the imam's deputy. Over the centuries the seat of the da'wat moved from Yemen to India, settling first at Sidhpur and later at Surat, and since the mid-20th century in Mumbai.

Factional disputes produced several branches. The Dawoodi Bohras, the largest group, recognise Syedna Mohammed Burhanuddin and his successor Mufaddal Saifuddin as recent da'is. The Alavi Bohras are centred in Vadodara, and the Sulaymani Bohras are numerous in Yemen and Saudi Arabia. A progressive reform movement has also existed since the early 20th century, challenging the authority of the clergy.

Beliefs and practices

Bohras follow the pillars of Islam with distinctively Ismaili interpretations. They pray three times a day, observe Ramadan, pay zakat, and perform the Hajj. Distinctive practices include the misaaq, an oath of allegiance taken at puberty, and communal meals served on a large shared platter called a thaal. The community maintains its own calendar, the Fatimid calendar, and speaks Lisan al-Dawat, a dialect of Gujarati written in Arabic script.

Culture

Bohra men traditionally wear a white three-piece outfit (kurta, saya and a crocheted cap or topi), while women wear the rida, a colourful two-piece garment. The community is noted for its trading networks stretching from East Africa to Southeast Asia, its charitable institutions, and large communal kitchens such as Faiz al-Mawaid al-Burhaniyah, which deliver daily meals to tens of thousands of families.
