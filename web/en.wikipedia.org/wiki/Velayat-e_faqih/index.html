Velayat-e faqih
Guardianship of the Jurist

Velayat-e faqih (Persian: ولایت فقیه, velāyat-e faqīh), usually translated as the Guardianship of the Islamic Jurist, is a doctrine in Twelver Shia Islam holding that, during the occultation of the Mahdi (the Twelfth Imam), qualified Islamic jurists (fuqaha) hold a mandate of guardianship over the community, which in its most developed form extends to political rule. The doctrine forms the constitutional basis of the Islamic Republic of Iran, where the Supreme Leader is the ruling jurist (vali-e faqih).

Development

Elements of the doctrine were discussed by classical Shia scholars, but its modern political form was developed by Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini in a series of lectures delivered in Najaf in 1970 and published as Islamic Government (Hokumat-e Eslami). Khomeini argued that since Islamic law comprehensively governs society, and since the Prophet and the Imams exercised political authority, just jurists must govern in the Imam's absence, and that such governance is an extension of the guardianship jurists already exercised over orphans, the mentally incapable and certain judicial matters.

In Iran

Following the Iranian Revolution of 1979, the doctrine was enshrined in the Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran (articles 5 and 107–112). The Supreme Leader, chosen by the Assembly of Experts, is commander-in-chief of the armed forces, appoints the head of the judiciary and has final authority on major state policies. A 1989 constitutional revision removed the requirement that the Leader be a marja' (the highest rank of jurist) and emphasised political qualifications; Khomeini's successor, Ali Khamenei, was chosen under the revised provisions.

Criticism and debate

The doctrine has been contested within Shia scholarship: quietist clerics, including senior marja's in Najaf and Qom, have held that jurists' guardianship is limited to religious and charitable affairs and that political rule awaits the Mahdi's return. Critics also debate the "absolute" form of the doctrine, under which the ruling jurist may suspend even primary religious ordinances in the interest of the Islamic state.
