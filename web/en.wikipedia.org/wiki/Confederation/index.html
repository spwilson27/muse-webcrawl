Confederation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A confederation is a political union of sovereign states united for purposes of common action. Unlike a federation, in which a central government exercises direct authority over citizens and the component units are constitutionally subordinate in shared fields, a confederation's central institutions are generally weak, act through the member states, and leave sovereignty — including, usually, a right of withdrawal — with the members.

The classic modern example is the United States under the Articles of Confederation (1781–1789), replaced by the federal United States Constitution after the Confederation Congress proved unable to tax, regulate commerce or enforce its decisions. Switzerland retains “Confederation” in its official name (Confoederatio Helvetica) although it has been a federation since 1848. The Confederate States of America (1861–1865) styled themselves a confederation during the American Civil War.

Characteristics

Confederal arrangements usually require unanimity or near-unanimity for major decisions, fund the centre by contributions from members rather than direct taxation, and rely on member states to execute common policies. They are often transitional: history suggests they either dissolve or deepen into federations, because the centre's weakness frustrates common defence, trade and finance.

Modern examples

The European Union is sometimes described as a sui generis entity between confederation and federation: member states retain sovereignty and a legal right of withdrawal, yet Union law has direct effect and supremacy in its fields. The Commonwealth of Independent States and various historical unions (the United Provinces of the Netherlands, the German Confederation of 1815–1866) are more straightforwardly confederal.
