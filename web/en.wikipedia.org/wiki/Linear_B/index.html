Linear B is a syllabic script that was used for writing in Mycenaean Greek, the earliest attested form of the Greek language. It was preceded by Linear A, the script of the Minoan civilisation, from which it was adapted. Linear B was in use mainly on clay tablets and vessels from about 1450 BC until the collapse of Mycenaean palace civilisation around 1200 BC, at sites including Knossos and Chania in Crete and Pylos, Mycenae, Thebes, Tiryns and Midea on the Greek mainland.

Contents of the tablets

The surviving texts are almost entirely administrative: inventories, ration lists, land records, and accounts of livestock, textiles, grain, oil, bronze and personnel kept by palace bureaucracies. The tablets were preserved accidentally, baked hard in the fires that destroyed the palaces. The script comprises about 87 syllabic signs — mostly representing a consonant followed by a vowel — together with more than a hundred logograms (ideograms) for commodities, animals, people and measures, and a decimal numeral system.

Decipherment

Arthur Evans excavated the first tablets at Knossos in 1900 and named the script Linear B to distinguish it from the earlier Linear A. For decades the language behind it was unknown. Alice Kober's systematic analysis in the 1940s demonstrated its inflected character and identified key sign patterns. In 1952 the English architect and amateur linguist Michael Ventris, building on Kober's work, announced that the language was Greek — several centuries older than any previously known Greek text. The philologist John Chadwick collaborated with Ventris to confirm and publish the decipherment, which overturned assumptions that the Mycenaean palaces were non-Greek and gave Greek a written history extending back into the Bronze Age.
