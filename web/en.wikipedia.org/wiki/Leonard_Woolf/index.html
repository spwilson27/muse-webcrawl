Leonard Woolf

Leonard Sidney Woolf (25 November 1880 – 14 August 1969) was a British political theorist, author, publisher, and civil servant. A central figure of the Bloomsbury Group, he married the writer Virginia Stephen — Virginia Woolf — in 1912, and with her founded the Hogarth Press in 1917, which published much of the landmark modernist literature of the interwar years.

Life and career

Educated at St Paul's School and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he was an Apostle alongside Lytton Strachey, John Maynard Keynes, and E. M. Forster, Woolf joined the Ceylon Civil Service in 1904. Seven years in Ceylon — documented in his acclaimed anti-imperial novel The Village in the Jungle (1913) — turned him into a critic of empire. Resigning in 1911, he returned to England and married Virginia Stephen the following year.

Politics and publishing

Woolf became an influential figure in the Labour Party and the Fabian Society, writing extensively on international government, imperialism, and co-operation in works such as International Government (1916), which helped shape thinking behind the League of Nations, and Imperialism and Civilization (1928). He edited the International Review, the Political Quarterly, and the literary pages of the Nation. With Virginia he ran the Hogarth Press from their Richmond home, publishing T. S. Eliot, Katherine Mansfield, and the first English translations of Freud. After Virginia's death in 1941 he wrote a celebrated five-volume autobiography, including Sowing (1960) and Beginning Again (1964).
