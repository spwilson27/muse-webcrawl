Free jazz is a style of avant-garde jazz that developed in the late 1950s and 1960s, in which musicians attempted to change or disregard conventional jazz constraints such as regular meter, fixed chord changes and predetermined form, in favour of collective improvisation, extended techniques and intense emotional expression. The term comes from Ornette Coleman's 1961 album Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation.


Free jazz performance

Development

Early experiments by pianist Cecil Taylor and saxophonist Ornette Coleman loosened the bond between soloist and chord progression; Coleman's 1959 quartet recordings and his double-quartet Free Jazz session announced the "new thing." In the 1960s Albert Ayler brought a raw, spiritual intensity with trios such as the one that recorded Spiritual Unity, while John Coltrane — already the leading post-bop saxophonist — moved toward the style on Ascension (1965) and his late recordings. Sun Ra's Arkestra merged free improvisation with cosmic theatre, and musicians such as Eric Dolphy, Don Cherry and Archie Shepp broadened the idiom.

Characteristics and reception

Free jazz typically features collective improvisation, atonality or fluctuating tonality, extreme registers and timbres (screams, honks, multiphonics), and energy playing driven by rhythm sections that abandon steady swing. It was controversial: some critics hailed liberation, others dismissed it as chaos, and it never found a large audience. In Europe it fed "free improvisation," while in the United States it was intertwined with Black nationalism and the Black Arts movement.

Legacy

Free jazz influenced the AACM in Chicago, the loft jazz scene of 1970s New York, and later musicians from David Murray to John Zorn, and it remains a foundation of avant-garde improvised music worldwide.
