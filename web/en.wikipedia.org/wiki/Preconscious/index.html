Preconscious

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychoanalysis, the preconscious is the locus of mental content that is not currently in conscious awareness but is readily accessible to it — memories, knowledge, and thoughts that can be recalled at will, in contrast to the repressed material of the unconscious proper. Sigmund Freud introduced the term within his topographic model of the mind, which distinguished the conscious, preconscious, and unconscious systems.

Freud's account

For Freud, preconscious contents are governed by the secondary process — logical, reality-oriented thinking — and are separated from the unconscious by a censorship that can be relaxed, as in sleep, allowing disguised material to pass into dreams. In his later structural model (id, ego, superego), the preconscious no longer named a separate system but a quality of mental contents belonging chiefly to the ego.

Later usage

In cognitive psychology the term is used more loosely for information processing that occurs outside awareness but can influence behaviour, overlapping with research on implicit memory and automatic processing. The distinction between preconscious material that is merely unattended and unconscious material that is actively repressed remains a point of discussion between psychoanalytic and cognitive approaches.
