The Peace of Lodi was a peace agreement signed on April 9, 1454, at Lodi in Lombardy between the Duchy of Milan, the Republic of Venice, and the Republic of Florence, ending the Wars in Lombardy, a series of conflicts that had destabilized northern and central Italy for some three decades.

Terms

The treaty recognized Francesco Sforza as Duke of Milan, fixed the Adda River as the boundary between Milanese and Venetian territory in Lombardy, and established Venetian retention of Brescia, Bergamo, and Crema. The signatories agreed to defend the existing territorial order and the independence of the Italian states against external (especially French) intervention.

Italic League

Within months the settlement broadened into the Italic League (Lega Italica), a 25-year alliance joining Milan, Venice, Florence, the Papal States, and the Kingdom of Naples — the five great powers of the peninsula — in a system of mutual defense and balance of power.

Significance

The Peace of Lodi ushered in roughly forty years of relative stability in Italy, during which Renaissance culture flourished and Italian diplomacy — resident ambassadors, alliance systems, and balance-of-power reasoning — reached a maturity often credited as the birthplace of modern European diplomacy. The equilibrium collapsed after the death of Lorenzo de' Medici in 1492 and the French invasion of 1494 that opened the Italian Wars.
