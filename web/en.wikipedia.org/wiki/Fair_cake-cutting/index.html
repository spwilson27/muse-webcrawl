Fair cake-cutting

Fair cake-cutting is a kind of fair division problem in which a heterogeneous resource, the "cake", is to be divided among participants with different preferences over parts of the cake. The continuous nature of the cake means it can be cut into arbitrarily small pieces, unlike indivisible goods in other fair-division problems. The classical model was introduced by Hugo Steinhaus in the 1940s.

Definitions

A division is proportional if each of n participants receives a piece worth at least 1/n of the whole cake by their own valuation. It is envy-free if no participant prefers another's piece to their own. A division that is both is called perfect. Other criteria include equitability, in which all participants receive exactly the same value by their own measures.

Procedures

For two participants, the ancient divide and choose procedure guarantees both proportionality and envy-freeness: one participant cuts the cake into two pieces they value equally, and the other chooses first. For three participants, the Steinhaus–Knaster "lone divider" procedure yields a proportional division. The Selfridge–Conway procedure produces an envy-free division for three participants.

For n participants, envy-free cake-cutting procedures were found by John Selfridge and John Horton Conway for n = 3, and a general bounded procedure was long elusive; in 2016 Aziz and Mackenzie presented the first bounded envy-free protocol for any number of participants.
