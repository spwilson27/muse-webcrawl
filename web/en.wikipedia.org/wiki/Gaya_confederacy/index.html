Gaya confederacy

The Gaya confederacy (Korean: 가야; Hanja: 加倻), also rendered Kaya, was a confederacy of territorial polities in the Nakdong River basin of southern Korea, growing out of the Byeonhan confederacy of the Samhan period. It flourished from around the 1st century AD until it was absorbed by the kingdom of Silla in the 6th century (532–562). Gaya was renowned for its high-quality iron production and its extensive trade with neighbouring states and the Japanese archipelago.

Gaya polities occupied the lower Nakdong valley between Baekje and Silla.

History

According to legend recorded in the Samguk Yusa, six golden eggs descended from heaven in 42 AD and hatched into the first kings of the six Gaya states; the firstborn became Suro, king of Geumgwan Gaya. Early Gaya, led by Geumgwan Gaya near modern Gimhae, prospered through iron exports and maritime trade. From the 4th century, pressure from Goguryeo and Baekje weakened the confederacy, and leadership shifted inland to Daegaya (near modern Goryeong). One by one the Gaya states fell to Silla: Geumgwan Gaya surrendered in 532, and Daegaya, the last holdout, was conquered in 562.

Economy and culture

Gaya sat on rich iron deposits and produced iron ingots that functioned almost as currency in regional trade with Lelang, Wa (Japan) and the neighbouring kingdoms. Its potters produced durable stoneware that influenced Sue ware in Japan. Large tumuli at sites such as Daeseong-dong (Gimhae) and Jisan-dong (Goryeong) contain elaborate burials with iron armour, horse gear and ornate gold crowns; several Gaya tumuli clusters were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.

The Mimana controversy

Older Japanese historiography claimed that Japan (Wa) ruled a colony called Mimana in the Gaya region, based on readings of the Nihon Shoki. Most modern Korean and many international scholars reject this as unsupported by archaeology, instead describing a zone of intensive trade and cultural exchange.
