Carl Zeiss (11 September 1816 – 3 December 1888) was a German scientific instrument maker, optician and businessman who founded the workshop in Jena in 1846 that became the optical company Carl Zeiss AG. His microscopes and lenses made him renowned across Europe, and the firm he founded became one of the world's great optical manufacturers.

Life and work

Apprenticed as a mechanic, Zeiss studied at the University of Jena before opening a workshop for precision mechanics and optics in 1846. Initially building microscopes by empirical trial, he placed design on a scientific basis from 1866 by collaborating with physicist Ernst Abbe, whose theory of image formation (including the Abbe sine condition) revolutionised microscope optics. In 1884 Otto Schott's new optical glasses further expanded the firm's capabilities. Zeiss also pioneered progressive social arrangements for his workers, including an eight-hour day and profit-sharing.

Legacy

After Zeiss's death, Abbe transferred the firm to the Carl-Zeiss-Stiftung (Carl Zeiss Foundation), an unusual ownership structure that persists today. The Zeiss name became famous for camera lenses, planetariums, binoculars and semiconductor lithography optics.
