Mesoamerican pyramids

Mesoamerican pyramids form a prominent part of ancient Mesoamerican architecture. Although similar in name to Egyptian pyramids, Mesoamerican structures are typically stepped pyramids with flat tops, on which temples were built. They served as platforms for temples and rituals — including, among the Aztecs, human sacrifice — rather than as tombs in the Egyptian manner, although burials have been found within some of them.

Form and construction

The typical Mesoamerican pyramid consists of a series of stacked platforms (cuerpos) with sloping or battered walls, sometimes in the talud-tablero style characteristic of Teotihuacan, and a broad central stairway leading to a temple on the summit. Many pyramids were built in successive layers over older cores, so that excavation reveals nested earlier structures — famously at El Castillo in Chichen Itza and the Great Pyramid of Cholula.

Notable examples

  • Great Pyramid of Cholula (Puebla, Mexico) — by volume, the largest pyramid in the world, a massive structure built over centuries by successive cultures.
  • Pyramid of the Sun and Pyramid of the Moon at Teotihuacan — among the largest structures of the ancient Americas.
  • El Castillo (Temple of Kukulkan) at Chichen Itza — famed for the equinox shadow effect resembling a descending serpent.
  • Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan — the great twin-staired pyramid at the center of the Aztec (Mexica) capital.
  • Temple of the Inscriptions at Palenque — a Maya pyramid containing the tomb of K'inich Janaab' Pakal.
  • La Danta at El Mirador, Guatemala — one of the largest Preclassic Maya complexes.

Function

Pyramids were sacred mountains in built form, mediating between the human world and the divine; their summits hosted temples where priests conducted calendrical ceremonies, offerings, and sacrifices. Many were aligned to astronomical events and to sacred features of the landscape.
