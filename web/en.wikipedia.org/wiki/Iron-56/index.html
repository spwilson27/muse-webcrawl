Iron-56

Protons: 26
Neutrons: 30
Natural abundance: ≈ 91.75% of iron
Stability: Stable (observationally)

Iron-56 (56Fe) is the most common isotope of iron, making up about 91.75% of natural iron. With 26 protons and 30 neutrons, it is one of the most tightly bound nuclei per nucleon and is the end product of fusion in massive stars.

Nuclear properties

Iron-56 is often described as the most stable nucleus, though nickel-62 has a marginally higher binding energy per nucleon and iron-58 is also close. Iron-56 has the lowest mass per nucleon of the common nuclei because the greater Coulomb repulsion among its protons outweighs the difference. Strictly, 56Fe is predicted to be very slightly unstable to double electron capture, but with a half-life far exceeding the age of the universe, so it is treated as stable.

Astrophysical significance

Silicon burning in massive stars produces nickel-56, which decays through cobalt-56 to iron-56; this decay chain powers the light curves of supernovae. Because fusion beyond the iron group absorbs energy rather than releasing it, iron accumulates in stellar cores, and the collapse of the resulting iron core triggers core-collapse supernovae. Iron-56 is therefore the pivot between the fusion-powered lives of stars and their explosive deaths.
