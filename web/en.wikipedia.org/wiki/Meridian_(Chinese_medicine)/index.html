In traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), meridians (simplified Chinese: 经络; traditional Chinese: 經絡; pinyin: jīngluò) are the channels or pathways through which qi ("vital energy") and blood are held to circulate in the body, connecting the internal organs (zang-fu) with the surface and with each other. The meridian system underlies acupuncture, acupressure and related therapies: stimulating points (acupoints) along the meridians is believed to regulate the flow of qi and restore balance.

The twelve standard meridians

There are twelve principal meridians, each associated with an organ and running bilaterally:

Yin (zang) organsYang (fu) organs
Lung, Spleen, Heart, Kidney, Pericardium, LiverLarge Intestine, Stomach, Small Intestine, Bladder, Gallbladder, Triple Burner (San Jiao)

Flow follows a fixed daily circuit, beginning with the Lung meridian in the early hours (3–5 a.m.) and passing through each channel in a two-hour period (the "Chinese clock"). In addition there are eight extraordinary vessels, of which the Governing vessel (Du Mai, along the spine) and Conception vessel (Ren Mai, along the front midline) have their own acupoints and are most used clinically.

History

The earliest surviving meridian texts are silk manuscripts excavated from the Mawangdui tombs (sealed 168 BC), which describe eleven vessels treated by moxibustion, before acupuncture needles became standard. The system was codified in the Huangdi Neijing (Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon, c. 2nd–1st century BC) and elaborated by later physicians; Wang Weiyi's illustrated bronze teaching figure (1027 AD) standardised point locations during the Song dynasty.

Scientific assessment

Meridians have no identified anatomical counterpart: dissection studies have not found channel structures matching the classical maps, and qi is not measurable in biomedical terms. Scientific evaluations of acupuncture show modest effects for some pain conditions, but results do not clearly support the specific meridian-acupoint model, and effects are often attributed at least partly to context and placebo. The meridian framework nonetheless remains central to the practice and teaching of TCM worldwide.
