Copyright reform

Copyright reform refers to efforts to change, modernize, or in some proposals abolish the system of copyright law. Debates over reform intensified with the rise of the Internet, which made copying and distribution nearly costless and brought ordinary users into direct contact with copyright restrictions. Reform positions range from incremental adjustments to radical proposals.

Issues

Recurring reform issues include the length of copyright terms (critics such as Lawrence Lessig argue terms have been repeatedly extended retroactively); the treatment of orphan works whose owners cannot be found; exceptions for education, parody, accessibility and text and data mining; the criminalisation of circumvention of digital rights management; and the balance between creators, intermediaries and the public domain. Movements advocating reform include the free culture movement, copyleft and Creative Commons licensing, and political groups such as the Pirate Parties, which first won seats in the European Parliament from Sweden in 2009.

Recent legislation

Major contested reforms include the proposed US bills SOPA and PIPA (2011–12), withdrawn after widespread online protests; the EU Directive on Copyright in the Digital Single Market (2019), whose Article 17 (originally Article 13) on platform liability was strongly debated; and Canada's Copyright Modernization Act (2012), which added fair dealing for education and non-commercial user-generated content.
