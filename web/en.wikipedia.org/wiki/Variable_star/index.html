Variable star

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A light curve showing a star's brightness dipping in a repeating pattern
A light curve showing a star's brightness dipping in a repeating pattern

A variable star is a star whose observed brightness varies with time — intrinsically (pulsation, eruption, rotation) or extrinsically (eclipses by a companion, transits). Variable stars are astronomy's workhorses: pulsating Cepheid variables gave the cosmic distance scale (Leavitt's law), eclipsing binaries give masses and radii, and cataclysmic variables, supernovae and microlensing events mark stellar life and death. Tens of thousands are catalogued, and survey telescopes now count them in the millions.

Classes

Pulsators (Cepheids, RR Lyrae, Miras, δ Scuti) change size and temperature on clockwork periods driven by the κ-mechanism (opacity valve); eruptive variables flare (T Tauri, UV Ceti) or explode (novae); eclipsing and ellipsoidal binaries encode geometry; rotating variables carry starspots. The General Catalogue of Variable Stars organises this zoo, citizen science (AAVSO, founded 1911) still supplies much of its monitoring.

Importance

Beyond distances, variables are laboratories: asteroseismology reads pulsations like earthquakes for stellar interiors, and variable-star archives stretching back a century give astronomy its only time machine for slow change. The discovery space now is the transient sky — supernova surveys and gravitational-wave counterparts are variable-star science at industrial scale.
