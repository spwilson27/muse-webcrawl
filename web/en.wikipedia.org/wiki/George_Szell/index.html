George Szell (Hungarian: Széll György; 7 June 1897 – 30 July 1970) was a Hungarian-born American conductor and composer. He is widely considered one of the twentieth century's great conductors, remembered above all for his twenty-four years as music director of the Cleveland Orchestra, which he transformed into one of the world's finest ensembles.

Early life

Szell was born in Budapest and raised in Vienna, where he was a child prodigy at the piano. At the age of eleven he toured Europe as a pianist, and at seventeen he conducted the Vienna Philharmonic. He worked as an assistant to Richard Strauss in Berlin and held conducting posts in Strasbourg, Prague, Darmstadt, Düsseldorf, Berlin and Glasgow before the Second World War.

Career in America

Szell emigrated to the United States in 1939. He conducted at the Metropolitan Opera in New York and made celebrated guest appearances with the NBC Symphony and other American orchestras. In 1946 he became music director of the Cleveland Orchestra, succeeding Erich Leinsdorf. There he imposed famously exacting standards — rehearsing in minute detail and insisting on precision, clarity of texture and chamber-like balance — and raised the orchestra to international stature. He expanded its size, lengthened its season, oversaw improvements to Severance Hall, and made a long series of recordings for Epic and Columbia, including cycles of the Beethoven, Brahms and Schubert symphonies and the Beethoven piano concertos with Leon Fleisher, that remain benchmarks.

Szell also served as artistic advisor to Amsterdam's Concertgebouw Orchestra and was a regular guest at the Salzburg Festival. He remained in Cleveland until his death in 1970; his successor as music director was Lorin Maazel. His conducting style — lean, disciplined and architecturally lucid — influenced a generation of conductors, and the "Szell sound" became a byword for orchestral refinement.
