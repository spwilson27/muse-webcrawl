Fluorescent lamp

Fluorescent lamp
Working principleGas discharge; mercury vapour UV excites phosphor
Typical efficacy50–100 lm/W
Typical life7,500–30,000 hours
RequiresBallast

A fluorescent lamp, or fluorescent tube, is a low-pressure mercury-vapour gas-discharge lamp that uses fluorescence to produce visible light. An electric current excites mercury vapour, producing ultraviolet light that causes a phosphor coating inside the tube to glow.

Operation

The lamp requires a ballast to limit current and provide the starting voltage. Compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) integrate an electronic ballast into a screw or bayonet base, allowing them to replace incandescent bulbs. Fluorescent lamps are several times more efficient than incandescent lamps of equal light output, though LEDs have since surpassed them in efficacy and lifetime.

History

Commercial fluorescent lamps were introduced by General Electric in 1938 following work by Edmund Germer and others. They became the dominant lighting for offices and industry in the post-war decades. Because the tubes contain small amounts of mercury, disposal is regulated in many jurisdictions.
