Great Stand on the Ugra River

The Great Stand on the Ugra River (Russian: Великое стояние на Угре) was a military standoff in 1480 between the forces of Akhmat, Khan of the Great Horde, and Ivan III, Grand Prince of Moscow. The two armies faced each other across the Ugra River, a tributary of the Oka southwest of Moscow, from October into November 1480. Without a major battle, the Horde eventually withdrew, an event traditionally taken to mark the end of the "Tatar Yoke" — the period of Mongol-Tatar domination over the Russian principalities that had lasted since the 13th century.

Background

By the late 15th century, Moscow had gathered most of north-eastern Rus' under its rule and Ivan III had ceased paying the tribute ("vykhod") demanded by the Horde; he also quarrelled with his brothers, whose appanages he had trimmed. In 1480 Akhmat Khan, seeking to restore his authority and expecting help from Casimir IV of Poland-Lithuania — who never arrived, being distracted by the Teutonic Order and Crimean raids — marched on Moscow. Ivan sent forces under his son Ivan the Young and his brother Andrey the Lesser to block the fords of the Oka, and the armies finally confronted one another on the Ugra.

The stand

Early in October 1480 the Tatars attempted to force a crossing and were repelled by Russian arquebus and artillery fire — an early notable use of firearms by a Muscovite army. There followed weeks of stand-off, skirmishing and fruitless negotiation. As winter approached, the river began to freeze, removing the Russians' defensive advantage, and Ivan controversially ordered a withdrawal to stronger positions near Kremenets; but Akhmat, short of supplies, his expected allies absent and fearing an attack on his own exposed capital by Ivan's allies (including the Crimean Khanate, Moscow's partner against the Horde), decided to retreat. On 11 November 1480 the Horde broke camp and withdrew. Akhmat was killed a few months later in a clash with Siberian and Nogai forces.

Legacy

Russian chronicles celebrated the stand as a providential deliverance, sometimes called the "bloodless" liberation from the Yoke. Modern historians note that the Horde remained a factor in steppe politics and that Moscow's emancipation was a gradual process; nevertheless 1480 is conventionally taught in Russia as the year Mongol-Tatar overlordship ended. The event is commemorated by a monument near the Ugra and was widely marked at its 500th anniversary.
