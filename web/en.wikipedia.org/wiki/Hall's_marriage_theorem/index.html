In mathematics, Hall's marriage theorem, proved by Philip Hall in 1935, is a theorem with two equivalent formulations. In its set-theoretic formulation, it gives a necessary and sufficient condition for selecting a distinct element from each of a finite collection of sets. In its graph-theoretic formulation, it characterises when a bipartite graph contains a matching that covers one side of the bipartition.

The theorem's name comes from a popularised framing: given a group of men and women, where each woman knows some of the men, under what conditions can every woman marry a man she knows? The answer is that this is possible exactly when every subset of women collectively knows at least as many men as there are women in the subset.

Set-theoretic formulation

Let S1, …, Sn be finite sets. A system of distinct representatives (SDR) is a choice of distinct elements x1, …, xn with xi ∈ Si. Hall's theorem states that an SDR exists if and only if, for every subset I of {1, …, n}, the union of the sets Si for i ∈ I has at least |I| elements. This requirement is known as Hall's condition.

Graph-theoretic formulation

For a bipartite graph with parts X and Y, there is a matching that saturates X if and only if |N(S)| ≥ |S| for every subset S of X, where N(S) is the set of neighbours of S in Y. Hall's theorem is closely related to König's theorem on bipartite matching and to the max-flow min-cut theorem; it can be proved by induction on n or derived from network flow theory.

Applications

Applications include the existence of Latin rectangles and their completion to Latin squares, scheduling and assignment problems, the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem that doubly stochastic matrices are convex combinations of permutation matrices, and results on transversals in combinatorics, for which Hall's theorem became the founding result of transversal theory.
