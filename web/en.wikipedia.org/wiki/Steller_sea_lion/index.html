The Steller sea lion (Eumetopias jubatus), also called the northern sea lion, is the largest of the eared seals (otariids) and the only member of the genus Eumetopias. It is named for the German naturalist Georg Wilhelm Steller, who first described it in 1741 during Vitus Bering's expedition; the species name jubatus ("maned") refers to the thick mane of coarse hair on adult males. Its range traces the North Pacific rim from northern Japan and the Kuril Islands through the Aleutian chain and Alaska, and down the west coast of North America to central California.

Biology

Steller sea lions are strongly sexually dimorphic: adult males weigh up to about a tonne and develop a bulky neck and mane, while females rarely exceed 300 kg. Pups are born mostly in May–July on island rookeries, where territorial males fast for weeks while defending groups of females. The diet is dominated by fish and cephalopods — walleye pollock, Pacific cod, Atka mackerel, herring, salmon, and squid — and they are preyed on by killer whales and sharks. Unlike true seals, they can rotate their hind flippers forward to move on land and to climb rocky haul-outs.

Population decline

The western distinct population segment in Alaska declined by roughly 70–80% between the late 1970s and 2000, falling from some 200,000 animals, and was listed as endangered under the U.S. Endangered Species Act in 1997. Proposed causes include competition with large-scale fisheries for prey, nutritional stress from shifts in prey composition (the "junk food hypothesis"), predation, and disease. The eastern stock (southeast Alaska to California) recovered steadily and was removed from the U.S. threatened list in 2013.
