Roll film

Roll film or rollfilm is any type of spool-wound photographic film protected from white light exposure by a paper backing, as opposed to film wound on a standard reel or in a cassette, or sheet film cut to individual sizes. The term originated in contrast to sheet film, since before the advent of roll film most cameras used glass plates or individual sheets of film. Roll film was popularised by George Eastman's Kodak company from 1888 onward, beginning with the original Kodak camera loaded with a roll sufficient for 100 exposures.

A roll of 120 film, showing the protective paper backing.

The light-tight roll of film and backing paper winds across the camera's film gate and collects on a take-up spool. Frame numbers and other markings are printed on the backing paper and are visible through a small red or clear window in the camera back on many older designs. After exposure the whole roll is removed for processing in daylight.

History

The first commercially successful roll film system was introduced with the Kodak camera of 1888, marketed with the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest." Early rolls used paper-backed stripping film, in which the emulsion was separated from its paper support during processing; this was soon replaced by transparent celluloid film base. In 1891 Kodak introduced daylight-loading spools, and the spool dimensions and numbering systems developed during this period became the basis of the standard sizes still used today.

Formats

Most roll film in current production is 120 film, introduced by Kodak in 1901 for the Brownie No. 2 camera. It is 61 mm wide and nominally 84 cm long, and depending on frame size a roll yields between 8 and 16 exposures. Related historical formats include 220 (double length, without backing paper), 127, 620, and 828.

Frame sizes on 120 film

Because 120 film has no perforations and only a single row of frame numbers, cameras using it employ masks or interchangeable backs to define different image formats: 6×4.5 cm (645), 6×6 cm square, 6×7 cm, 6×8 cm, 6×9 cm, and panoramic formats such as 6×12 cm and 6×17 cm.
