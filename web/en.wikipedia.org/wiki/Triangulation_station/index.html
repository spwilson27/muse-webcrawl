Triangulation station

A triangulation station, also called a trig point or triangulation pillar, is a fixed surveying station used in geodetic surveying. The stations formed the visible skeleton of national triangulation networks: each was positioned on a hilltop or other vantage point within sight of its neighbours, and its coordinates were computed from chains of measured angles anchored to a precisely measured baseline.

Markers

Stations are marked in different ways around the world. In Britain, the retriangulation of the 1930s produced about 6,500 concrete "trig pillars" designed by Brigadier Martin Hotine — tapered square pillars about four feet high with a brass plate on top for mounting a theodolite. In the United States, benchmarks are typically brass disks set in bedrock or concrete; Australia and New Zealand use a mixture of cairns, beacons and plaques.

Modern use

Satellite geodesy has displaced classical triangulation, but the stations are still used as known reference points, are visited by hobbyist "trigpointers" and hillwalkers, and many are protected as historic monuments. Their recorded heights historically defined the summit elevations shown on maps.
