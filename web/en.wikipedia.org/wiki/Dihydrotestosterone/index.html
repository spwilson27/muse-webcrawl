Dihydrotestosterone

Dihydrotestosterone (DHT, or 5α-dihydrotestosterone), also known as androstanolone, is an endogenous androgen sex steroid and hormone. The enzyme 5α-reductase catalyzes the formation of DHT from testosterone in certain tissues including the prostate gland, seminal vesicles, epididymides, skin, hair follicles, liver, and brain. DHT is biologically more potent than testosterone, binding to the androgen receptor with greater affinity and dissociating more slowly.

Biological function

DHT is essential for the development of male external genitalia and the prostate during fetal life: in its absence (5α-reductase type 2 deficiency), genetic males are born with ambiguous genitalia. At puberty, DHT drives growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice contributing together with testosterone, and prostate growth. In adults, DHT is implicated in benign prostatic hyperplasia, prostate cancer growth, and androgenic alopecia (pattern hair loss), because hair follicles on the scalp are sensitive to it. It is not a major driver of muscle growth, since skeletal muscle expresses little 5α-reductase.

Synthesis and metabolism

About 5–10% of testosterone is converted to DHT daily, chiefly by the type 2 isoenzyme of 5α-reductase in genital tissues and the type 1 isoenzyme in skin and liver. DHT is inactivated in muscle and other tissues by 3α-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase to 3α-androstanediol. Circulating DHT levels are roughly one tenth of testosterone levels, but local concentrations in the prostate are much higher.

Medical significance

Drugs that inhibit 5α-reductase — finasteride and dutasteride — lower DHT and are used to treat benign prostatic hyperplasia and pattern hair loss. Conversely, DHT itself and synthetic derivatives (such as mesterolone and various designer steroids) have medical and non-medical uses. Elevated or deficient DHT states feature in the differential diagnosis of disorders of sex development and hypogonadism.
