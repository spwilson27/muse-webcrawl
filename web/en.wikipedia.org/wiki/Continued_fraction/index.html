Continued fraction

In mathematics, a continued fraction is an expression obtained through an iterative process of representing a number as the sum of its integer part and the reciprocal of another number, then writing this other number as the sum of its integer part and another reciprocal, and so on. A finite continued fraction terminates after a finite number of steps; an infinite continued fraction is an infinite expression.

A simple continued fraction has the form

x = a0 + 1/(a1 + 1/(a2 + 1/(a3 + ...)))

where a0 is an integer and all other an are positive integers. The notation [a0; a1, a2, ...] is commonly used.

Convergents

Truncating a continued fraction at successive steps gives a sequence of rational approximations, called convergents, which alternate above and below the target value and converge to it. Convergents are best rational approximations in a precise sense: any fraction closer to the value than a convergent must have a larger denominator. Every rational number has a finite simple continued fraction, and every irrational number has an infinite one; quadratic irrationals have eventually periodic continued fractions (Lagrange's theorem).

Famous examples

The golden ratio has the simplest infinite continued fraction, [1; 1, 1, 1, ...]. Euler's number e equals [2; 1, 2, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 6, ...], displaying a regular pattern, while π has no obvious pattern in its terms.

History and applications

Continued fractions were developed by mathematicians including Rafael Bombelli, John Wallis, Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange. They are used in number theory, in solving Diophantine equations and Pell's equation, in rational approximation, and in the analysis of algorithms such as the Euclidean algorithm, whose steps generate the continued fraction of a ratio of two integers.
