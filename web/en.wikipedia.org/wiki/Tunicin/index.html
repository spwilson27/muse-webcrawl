Tunicin

Tunicin is a form of cellulose produced by tunicates (sea squirts), a group of marine invertebrate chordates. It forms the main structural component of the tunic — the tough outer covering from which the animals take their name — making tunicates the only animals known to synthesise cellulose, a polymer otherwise characteristic of plants, algae and some bacteria.

Structure and properties

Tunicin is chemically identical in composition to plant cellulose but is noted for its exceptionally high crystallinity and purity. In species such as Halocynthia and Ciona, the cellulose microfibrils are almost perfectly aligned, producing a material of unusual stiffness and strength for a biological polymer. Because it can be extracted nearly pure, tunicate cellulose has attracted interest as a source of cellulose nanocrystals and nanofibres for biomedical and materials applications.

Origin

The ability of tunicates to make cellulose is believed to result from horizontal gene transfer: the cellulose synthase gene in tunicate genomes appears to have been acquired from a bacterium early in the group's evolution. The enzyme complex sits in the epidermal cell membrane and extrudes microfibrils directly into the tunic.
