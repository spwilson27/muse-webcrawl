Psychology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour. It spans perception, cognition, emotion, personality, development, social interaction and mental disorders, using experiments, observation, neuroimaging and computational models. Psychology sits between neuroscience and the social sciences: its subject — the human brain at work — is biological, but its phenomena (language, culture, selfhood) are not reducible to biology alone.

The discipline became an experimental science in the late 19th century (Wundt's Leipzig laboratory, 1879), passed through behaviourism (Watson, Skinner) and psychoanalysis (Freud), and from the 1950s–60s underwent a 'cognitive revolution' (Miller, Chomsky's critique of behaviourist language theory, later Kahneman) restoring mind as its object.

Branches

Clinical and counselling psychology treat mental disorders (with Psychiatry, the medical branch), using evidence-based therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy. Other branches: cognitive, developmental (Piaget, Vygotsky), social (Milgram, Asch), personality, educational, occupational, health and forensic psychology.

Methods and debates

Psychology has faced a replication crisis since the 2010s — many famous findings (social priming, ego depletion) have weakened under retest — prompting reforms: preregistration, larger samples, open data. The episode is now a standard case study in how sciences self-correct.
