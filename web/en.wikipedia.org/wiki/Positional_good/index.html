Positional good

A positional good is a good whose value depends largely on how it compares with goods owned by others — on its relative ranking or the status it confers — rather than on its absolute usefulness. The term was introduced by economist Fred Hirsch in his 1976 book Social Limits to Growth. Examples include elite university degrees, luxury goods, exclusive locations, and high-status jobs: if everyone obtains them, their advantage disappears, so they cannot be mass-produced without destroying their value.

Characteristics

Positional goods are subject to "positional externalities": one person's gain in rank implies another's loss. Competition for them can be a positional arms race — for instance, escalating spending on weddings, housing in "good school" districts, or credentials — in which everyone spends more yet relative positions, and often overall welfare, do not improve. Hirsch argued this creates a structural frustration in affluent societies: material growth can satisfy absolute wants but not positional ones.

Relation to Veblen goods

Positional goods overlap with Veblen goods, whose demand rises with price because price itself signals status (a concept from Thorstein Veblen's Theory of the Leisure Class, 1899). Not all positional goods are Veblen goods, however: a place at a selective university is positional even though its price is not its appeal.

Policy implications

Economists such as Robert Frank have argued that positional competition justifies progressive consumption taxes, since taxing positional spending can reduce wasteful status arms races without reducing welfare. The concept also informs debates about inequality, education, and the Easterlin paradox of happiness and income.
