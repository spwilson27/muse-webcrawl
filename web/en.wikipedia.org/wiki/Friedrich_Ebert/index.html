Friedrich Ebert (4 February 1871 – 28 February 1925) was a German politician of the Social Democratic Party (SPD) who served as the first president of Germany from 1919 until his death in 1925. He also briefly served as chancellor during the transition from the German Empire to the Weimar Republic in 1918–1919.

Early life and party career

Ebert was born in Heidelberg, the seventh of nine children of a tailor. He trained as a saddler and travelled as a journeyman before settling in Bremen, where he became a trade union organiser and editor of the SPD newspaper. He was elected to the Reichstag in 1912 and became chairman of the SPD in 1913, jointly with Hugo Haase, after the death of August Bebel. A pragmatic reformist, Ebert supported the war credits of 1914 and worked to hold the party together as it split over the First World War.

Revolution and republic

On 9 November 1918, at the height of the German Revolution, Chancellor Prince Max of Baden handed the chancellorship to Ebert. Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed the republic the same day, pre-empting the proclamation of a soviet republic by Karl Liebknecht. As head of the Council of the People's Deputies, Ebert concluded the controversial Ebert–Groener pact with the army leadership, securing military support against far-left uprisings in return for leaving the officer corps intact. His government organised elections to the Weimar National Assembly in January 1919, which in turn elected him Reich President on 11 February 1919.

Presidency

Ebert's presidency was dominated by crisis: the Spartacist uprising, the Kapp Putsch of 1920, the Ruhr occupation, the hyperinflation of 1923 and the Beer Hall Putsch. He made extensive use of his emergency powers under Article 48 of the Weimar Constitution, signing more than 130 emergency decrees, mostly to defend the republic against extremists of left and right. He sought to preserve democracy and national unity, embodying the so-called "republic of reason" of the moderate parties.

Ebert died in office on 28 February 1925 from appendicitis and peritonitis, aged 54. He was succeeded by Paul von Hindenburg. The Friedrich Ebert Foundation, affiliated with the SPD, is named in his honour.
