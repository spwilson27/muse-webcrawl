Bloch equations

The Bloch equations are a set of macroscopic equations used in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to calculate the nuclear magnetisation as a function of time in the presence of relaxation processes. They were introduced by Felix Bloch in his foundational 1946 paper on nuclear induction.

Form

In a frame rotating at the Larmor frequency, the equations describe how the components of the magnetisation vector M evolve: the transverse components (Mx, My) decay exponentially with the spin–spin relaxation time T2, while the longitudinal component Mz recovers toward its equilibrium value M0 with the spin–lattice relaxation time T1. Schematically:

dMx/dt = −Mx/T2,   dMy/dt = −My/T2,   dMz/dt = (M0 − Mz)/T1

In a static field B0, the magnetisation precesses about the field at the Larmor frequency ω = γB0, where γ is the gyromagnetic ratio. Radio-frequency pulses are used to tip the magnetisation away from equilibrium, after which the Bloch equations govern its return.

Applications

The Bloch equations underpin pulse-sequence design in MRI and spectroscopy: inversion recovery, spin echo and gradient echo sequences are all analysed through them. Bloch's phenomenological treatment was later connected to the quantum-mechanical density-matrix formalism, and extensions such as the Bloch–McConnell equations describe magnetisation exchange between sites, used in chemical exchange and magnetisation transfer studies.
