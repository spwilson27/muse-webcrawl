Sleeve gastrectomy is a surgical weight-loss procedure in which the stomach is reduced to about 15 percent of its original size, by the surgical removal of a large portion of the stomach along the greater curvature. The result is a sleeve or tube-like structure. The procedure permanently reduces the size of the stomach, although there could be some dilatation of the stomach later on in life. The procedure is generally performed laparoscopically and is irreversible.

Together with the gastric bypass, the sleeve gastrectomy is one of the two most commonly performed bariatric operations worldwide and has been performed since 1988, when it was first described as the first stage of the duodenal switch procedure in patients at high surgical risk.

Procedure

Originally, the sleeve gastrectomy was performed as the first stage of a two-stage procedure, the biliopancreatic diversion with duodenal switch, in patients who were at too high a risk for the full operation. Many patients lost enough weight after the sleeve alone that the second stage proved unnecessary, and the procedure was adopted as a standalone operation.

Most sleeve gastrectomies are performed with laparoscopic (keyhole) technique. The surgeon removes approximately 75–80 percent of the stomach, leaving a narrow gastric tube or "sleeve" of roughly 100–150 mL capacity. The pylorus and the function of the stomach are largely preserved, and unlike the gastric bypass, the intestines are not rerouted. Removal of the gastric fundus also removes most of the cells that produce ghrelin, the "hunger hormone", which contributes to reduced appetite after surgery.

Indications

Sleeve gastrectomy is indicated for patients with severe obesity, typically a body mass index (BMI) of 40 or greater, or 35 or greater with obesity-related comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, or obstructive sleep apnoea. Since 2022, guidelines from the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery recommend considering surgery at BMI 35 regardless of comorbidity, and at BMI 30–34.9 with metabolic disease.

Risks and complications

Complications include staple-line leak (the most serious early complication, occurring in roughly 1–3 percent of cases), bleeding, stricture or stenosis of the sleeve, and venous thromboembolism. Long-term issues include worsening or new-onset gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), nutritional deficiencies, and weight regain. Unlike malabsorptive procedures, the sleeve causes fewer vitamin and mineral deficiencies, but lifelong supplementation and follow-up are still recommended.

Outcomes

Patients typically lose 50–70 percent of their excess body weight within the first two years. Type 2 diabetes remission rates are substantial, though generally lower than after gastric bypass. Because of its relative technical simplicity, shorter operating time, and effectiveness, the sleeve gastrectomy became the most frequently performed bariatric procedure in the United States in the 2010s, accounting for well over half of all weight-loss operations.
