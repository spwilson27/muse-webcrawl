Magnetic tape

Magnetic tape is a medium for magnetic storage, made of a thin, magnetizable coating on a long, narrow strip of plastic film. It was developed in Germany in 1928, based on magnetic wire recording, and devices that record and play audio and video using magnetic tape are called tape recorders and video tape recorders.

Audio

Fritz Pfleumer's tape, commercialized by AEG as the Magnetophon, revolutionized radio broadcasting and music recording in the 1930s and 1940s. After World War II the technology spread to the United States, where Ampex built professional studio recorders; multitrack recording transformed popular music production. Consumer formats included open reels, the Compact Cassette (Philips, 1963), and 8-track cartridges.

Video

Ampex's Quadruplex system (1956) made videotape practical for television. Later formats included U-matic, Betamax, VHS, and professional digital formats such as D1 and Digital Betacam, before file-based recording displaced tape in production.

Data storage

Tape was the primary storage medium of early computing, from UNIVAC's metal tape to IBM's 7- and 9-track reels. Modern LTO (Linear Tape-Open) cartridges remain widely used for backup and archival storage because of their low cost per terabyte, longevity, and the "air gap" they provide against ransomware: data written to a removed cartridge cannot be attacked over a network.

Durability

Tape degrades through binder hydrolysis ("sticky-shed syndrome"), print-through, and demagnetization; archival practice requires cool, dry storage and periodic migration to new media.
