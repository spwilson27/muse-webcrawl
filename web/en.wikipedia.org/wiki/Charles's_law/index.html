Charles's law

Charles's law (also known as the law of volumes) is an experimental gas law that describes how gases tend to expand when heated. A modern statement of Charles's law is: when the pressure on a sample of a dry gas is held constant, the Kelvin temperature and the volume are in direct proportion.

Statement

V ∝ T   or   V / T = k

where V is the volume of the gas, T is its absolute temperature, and k is a constant. The law implies that extrapolating volume against temperature leads to zero volume at absolute zero (−273.15 °C).

History

The law was named after the scientist Jacques Charles, who formulated the original law in unpublished work from the 1780s. Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac published it in 1802 and credited Charles. Combined with Boyle's law and Gay-Lussac's law of pressure and temperature, it forms part of the ideal gas law.

Kinetic interpretation

Under kinetic theory, raising the temperature increases the average kinetic energy of gas molecules; at constant pressure the gas expands so that the increased rate of collisions with the container walls is balanced by the larger volume.
