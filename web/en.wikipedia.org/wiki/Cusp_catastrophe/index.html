Cusp catastrophe

In mathematics, a cusp catastrophe is a type of bifurcation in which a small, smooth change in control parameters can cause a sudden, discontinuous jump in the state of a system. It is one of the elementary catastrophes classified by René Thom and is the most widely applied model in catastrophe theory.

The model

The cusp arises from the potential function V(x) = x4 + a x2 + b x, where x is the state variable and a and b are control parameters — conventionally called the splitting factor (a) and the normal factor (b). Equilibrium states satisfy 4x3 + 2a x + b = 0. Plotting the equilibria over the (a, b) control plane gives a folded surface whose fold lines meet in a cusp on the plane, described by the bifurcation set 8a3 + 27b2 = 0.

Behaviour

Inside the cusp region the system has three equilibria, two stable and one unstable; outside it there is only one. As the parameters cross a fold, the state jumps abruptly to the other stable branch, producing hysteresis: the path back differs from the path in, because the jump occurs at the opposite fold. These features — sudden jumps, hysteresis, bimodality, and divergence — are the classic "flags" of a cusp catastrophe.

Applications

The cusp model has been applied to phenomena as varied as the buckling of beams, phase transitions and ferromagnetism in physics, Zeeman's model of the heartbeat and nerve impulse, and proposed models of aggression, market crashes, and attitude change. Some social-science applications, popularised in the 1970s, were criticised as more metaphorical than quantitative, but the underlying mathematics remains a standard part of bifurcation theory.
