Semi-vegetarianism

Semi-vegetarianism, also called flexitarianism, describes dietary practices that are primarily vegetarian but occasionally include meat or fish. A person following such a diet is a flexitarian. Unlike vegetarianism, which excludes flesh foods entirely, semi-vegetarian diets reduce meat consumption rather than eliminating it, typically for health, environmental, or animal-welfare reasons.

Varieties

  • Flexitarian — mostly vegetarian, with meat eaten occasionally.
  • Pescetarian — vegetarian foods plus fish and seafood, but no meat.
  • Pollotarian — vegetarian foods plus poultry, but no red meat or fish.
  • Macrobiotic diets in some forms permit occasional fish.

Terminology

The word "flexitarian" — a portmanteau of flexible and vegetarian — entered wide use in the 1990s and 2000s and was recognised by major dictionaries in the following decade. Dietitian Dawn Jackson Blatner popularised the term with The Flexitarian Diet (2009). Because semi-vegetarians do eat meat, vegetarian organisations generally do not classify the diet as a form of vegetarianism.

Prevalence and motives

Surveys in Europe and North America suggest a growing minority of consumers describe themselves as reducing meat consumption without going fully vegetarian. Reported motives include cardiovascular health, the environmental footprint of livestock, cost, and concern for animal welfare, as well as campaigns such as Meatless Monday and Veganuary encouraging part-time plant-based eating.
