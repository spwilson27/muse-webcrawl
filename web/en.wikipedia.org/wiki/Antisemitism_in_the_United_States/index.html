Antisemitism in the United States

Antisemitism in the United States has taken social, institutional, economic and violent forms since the colonial period, though scholars generally note it was less pervasive and less often state-sanctioned than in Europe. It has included discriminatory quotas, social exclusion, conspiracy theories, vandalism, assaults and mass shootings directed at Jewish Americans.

Early history

Jews faced sporadic restrictions in the Dutch and English colonies; in 1654 Peter Stuyvesant sought to expel Jews from New Amsterdam but was overruled. During the Civil War, General Ulysses S. Grant issued General Order No. 11 (1862) expelling Jews from his military district, an order President Lincoln quickly revoked.

Immigration era and quotas

Mass Eastern European Jewish immigration after 1880 coincided with rising nativism. In 1915 Leo Frank, a Jewish factory superintendent in Georgia, was lynched after a widely condemned trial. Ivy League universities and medical schools imposed Jewish quotas in the 1920s; restrictive covenants barred Jews from housing, and many hotels, clubs and employers excluded them.

1930s–1945

The Great Depression saw radio priest Charles Coughlin broadcast antisemitic conspiracy theories to millions, Henry Ford's newspaper had earlier spread the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, and the German American Bund held rallies, including a 1939 rally at Madison Square Garden. Restrictive immigration laws limited Jewish refugees fleeing the Holocaust.

Postwar decline and resurgence

After World War II institutional antisemitism declined sharply: quotas fell, suburbs and professions opened, and public-opinion surveys recorded steadily falling prejudice for decades. However, antisemitic incidents persisted, and the late 2010s saw major violent attacks, including the 2018 Pittsburgh synagogue shooting, the deadliest attack on Jews in U.S. history, and the 2019 Poway and Jersey City attacks. Incident-tracking organisations reported further sharp increases in the 2020s.
