Evaporative cooler
Other namesSwamp cooler, desert cooler
PrincipleEvaporative cooling (latent heat of vaporisation)
Best suited toHot, dry climates

An evaporative cooler (also known as a swamp cooler or desert cooler) is a device that cools air through the evaporation of water. It differs from conventional air conditioning, which uses vapour-compression refrigeration: evaporation consumes latent heat from the air, lowering its temperature while increasing its humidity.

Operation

In a typical unit, a fan draws warm outside air through pads saturated with water recirculated from a reservoir. As the air passes through the wet pads, water evaporates into it, reducing the air temperature by up to roughly 15 °C in very dry conditions, and the cooled air is blown into the building. Because the cooling effect depends on the air's capacity to absorb moisture, evaporative coolers are most effective in hot arid regions — such as the southwestern United States, inland Australia and the Middle East — and perform poorly in humid weather.

Advantages and limitations

Evaporative coolers use a fraction of the electricity of refrigerated systems — often around a quarter — and are simpler and cheaper to install and maintain. Disadvantages include water consumption, the need for constant air exchange (windows or vents must remain partly open), mineral build-up and maintenance of pads, and reduced effectiveness on humid days. The ancient Persian windcatcher combined with a qanat is an early architectural application of the same principle.
