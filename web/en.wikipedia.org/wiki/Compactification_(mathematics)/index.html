Compactification (mathematics)

[Image: One-point compactification of the plane]

In mathematics, particularly in general topology, compactification is the process or result of making a topological space into a compact space. A compactification of a space X is a compact space Y together with an embedding of X as a dense subset of Y, so that properties of compact spaces — such as the convergence of every sequence along a subnet, or the attainment of maxima by continuous real-valued functions — can be applied.

Examples

The one-point compactification (or Alexandroff compactification) adds a single "point at infinity" to a locally compact Hausdorff space. Under stereographic projection, the real line compactifies to a circle and the plane to the Riemann sphere, which is the basis of the extended complex plane used in complex analysis. The Stone–Čech compactification βX is the largest possible compactification of a Tychonoff space: every other compactification of X is a quotient of it, and every continuous function from X to a compact Hausdorff space extends uniquely to βX.

Other contexts

In algebraic geometry, one compactifies varieties by embedding them into projective space, as in the passage from affine to projective varieties; Deligne and Mumford compactified the moduli space of curves by allowing stable nodal curves. In theoretical physics, "compactification" also refers to the curling-up of extra spatial dimensions in Kaluza–Klein theory and string theory, a distinct usage treated in its own article.
