Market cross

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A market cross (also called a butter cross in some localities) is a monument erected in the centre of a market town or village in the British Isles, marking the place where markets were held under a royal or manorial charter. Market crosses are found throughout England, Scotland, Wales and Ireland; in Scotland they are known as mercat crosses.

History

Markets required legal authorisation, and the cross marked the location where trade could lawfully take place. The cross also had a religious and civic function: traders swore oaths and struck bargains "before the cross," prayers were said for fair dealing, and proclamations, public notices and punishments took place there. Steps at the base were often used for displaying goods; some crosses had sheltered structures built around them, ranging from covered pavilions to substantial buildings such as the market cross at Malmesbury or the poultry cross at Salisbury.

Many market crosses are medieval, though they were frequently rebuilt or restored in later centuries. Some were damaged or removed during the Reformation and the English Civil War, when crosses were targets of iconoclasm. In Scotland, the mercat cross served as the focus of royal proclamations — new monarchs are still proclaimed at the Mercat Cross in Edinburgh — and as a place of public punishment and civic ceremony.

Design

The typical market cross consists of a tall shaft, often set on a stepped circular or polygonal base, surmounted by a cross, lantern, sundial, heraldic carving or a figure such as a unicorn (a symbol of Scotland). Elaborate examples, such as the Eleanor crosses erected by Edward I, are ornately decorated Gothic monuments.

Notable examples

  • Malmesbury market cross, Wiltshire — an elaborate covered cross of about 1490.
  • Chichester Cross, West Sussex — a large, ornate cross built in 1501 by Bishop Edward Story.
  • Mercat Cross, Edinburgh — the historic focus of proclamations in Scotland's capital.
  • Banbury Cross, Oxfordshire — immortalised in the nursery rhyme "Ride a cock horse to Banbury Cross"; the present cross dates from 1859.
  • Castle Combe, Wiltshire — a well-preserved 14th-century market cross.
