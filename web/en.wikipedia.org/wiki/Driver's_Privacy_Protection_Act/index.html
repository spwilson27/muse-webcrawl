Driver's Privacy Protection Act


The Act restricts disclosure of personal information from motor vehicle records.

The Driver's Privacy Protection Act of 1994 (DPPA), codified at 18 U.S.C. §§ 2721–2725, is a United States federal statute governing the privacy and disclosure of personal information gathered by state departments of motor vehicles. It took effect in 1997.

Background

Before the Act, many states routinely sold drivers' personal information, raising revenue but exposing motorists to stalking, harassment and unsolicited marketing. The 1989 murder of actress Rebecca Schaeffer — whose home address was obtained from California DMV records by the obsessed fan who killed her — became a prominent catalyst for reform, alongside other cases in which abortion providers and motorists were targeted using licence records.

Provisions

The Act prohibits state DMVs and anyone who obtains records from them from disclosing "personal information" — including names, addresses, photographs, Social Security numbers, licence numbers and medical information — except for defined permissible purposes. These include use by government agencies, vehicle safety and emissions matters, insurance underwriting and claims, litigation, law enforcement, licensed private investigation, towing and impound notification, and uses with the individual's written consent. "Highly restricted" data such as photographs, Social Security numbers and medical information receive additional protection. Violations carry criminal fines and a private right of action with statutory damages. A 2000 amendment tightened the rules so that states must obtain express consent ("opt-in") before releasing records for marketing or surveys.

Legal challenges

In Reno v. Condon (2000), the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the DPPA as a valid exercise of Congress's Commerce Clause power, rejecting a Tenth Amendment challenge by South Carolina. The Act has also featured in litigation over journalists' and data brokers' access to motor vehicle databases.
