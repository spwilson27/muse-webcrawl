Ashlar

Ashlar
MaterialStone (limestone, sandstone, etc.)
UseMasonry walls; building facades
FinishFinely dressed, square-cut blocks
Contrasts withRubble masonry

Ashlar is finely dressed (cut and worked) stone masonry, in which individual blocks are squared, given smooth faces, and laid in regular courses with very thin joints. Ashlar work is contrasted with rubble masonry, which uses irregularly shaped stones. The word may derive from Old French aisseler or from Latin axillaris. It has been the prestige form of stone construction since antiquity.

History

Sophisticated ashlar masonry appears in ancient Egypt and in the Bronze Age palace cultures of the Aegean, such as Knossos on Crete. The Inca of Peru achieved remarkably precise dry-stone ashlar walls — for example at Sacsayhuamán and Machu Picchu — in which polygonal blocks fit together without mortar so tightly that a blade cannot be inserted between them. Classical Greek and Roman builders used squared ashlar extensively; Greek work often dispensed with mortar, relying on metal clamps and dowels. In medieval and Renaissance Europe, ashlar faced cathedrals, castles, and civic buildings, often as a veneer over a rubble core.

Types

Ashlar faces may be smooth, tooled, or deliberately roughened in "rustication," in which the face of each block projects and its edges are bevelled — a treatment characteristic of Italian Renaissance palazzi and later classical revival architecture. In modern construction, thin ashlar veneer stone or manufactured stone is applied over structural walls to imitate solid ashlar.

In Freemasonry

In Masonic symbolism, the "rough ashlar" is an undressed stone representing the uninitiated or unrefined person, while the "perfect ashlar" is a smoothly finished block symbolizing the state of moral improvement the Mason seeks; both appear as emblems in lodge rooms.
