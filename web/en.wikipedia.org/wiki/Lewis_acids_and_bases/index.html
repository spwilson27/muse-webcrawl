Lewis acids and bases

A Lewis acid is a chemical species containing an empty orbital capable of accepting an electron pair from a Lewis base, thereby forming a Lewis adduct. A Lewis base thus has an available electron pair. The definitions were proposed by the American physical chemist Gilbert N. Lewis in 1923 and generalise the Brønsted–Lowry theory: every Brønsted acid is a Lewis acid in its reaction with a base, but many species with no transferable proton — such as boron trifluoride (BF₃), aluminium chloride (AlCl₃), and metal cations — are Lewis acids.

Adducts and bonding

In a typical example, ammonia donates its lone pair into the empty orbital of BF₃, producing the adduct H₃N–BF₃ with a dative (coordinate) covalent bond. Lewis structures depict the accepting atom with a vacant orbital or positive formal charge and the base with a lone pair. Reaction between a Lewis acid and base underlies much of coordination chemistry: ligands are Lewis bases binding metal-ion Lewis acids, and the formation of complex ions can be written as a series of adduct-forming steps.

Applications

Lewis acids are widely used as catalysts. Aluminium chloride catalyses Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation by accepting a halide and generating a carbocationic electrophile. Boron, silicon, titanium, and lanthanide compounds serve as Lewis-acid catalysts in Diels–Alder reactions, carbonyl chemistry, and polymerisation. Frustrated Lewis pairs — sterically hindered acid/base combinations that cannot form an adduct — activate small molecules such as hydrogen and carbon dioxide.

Hard and soft acids and bases

The HSAB concept, introduced by Ralph Pearson, classifies Lewis acids and bases as "hard" (small, weakly polarisable) or "soft" (large, polarisable), with the empirical rule that hard acids prefer hard bases and soft acids prefer soft bases. Quantitative scales of Lewis acidity and basicity include the Gutmann donor and acceptor numbers and the Drago–Wayland parameters.

Comparison with other definitions

The Arrhenius definition is restricted to aqueous solutions producing H⁺ and OH⁻. The Brønsted–Lowry definition frames acids as proton donors and bases as proton acceptors. The Lewis definition, the most general of the three, includes proton transfer as a special case (the proton being the Lewis acid) and extends acid–base behaviour to aprotic and non-aqueous chemistry.
