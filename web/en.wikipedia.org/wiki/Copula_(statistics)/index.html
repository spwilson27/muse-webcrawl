Copula (statistics)

In probability theory and statistics, a copula is a multivariate cumulative distribution function for which the marginal probability distribution of each variable is uniform on the interval [0, 1]. Copulas are used to describe and model the dependence between random variables, separating the dependence structure of a joint distribution from its marginal distributions. The word copula is Latin for "a link" or "tie", and the term was introduced into statistics by Abe Sklar in 1959.

Sklar's theorem

Sklar's theorem states that any multivariate joint distribution can be written in terms of univariate marginal distribution functions and a copula describing the dependence structure. If F is a joint distribution function with margins F1, ..., Fd, then there exists a copula C such that F(x1, ..., xd) = C(F1(x1), ..., Fd(xd)). When the margins are continuous, the copula is unique.

Common families

Important copula families include the Gaussian copula and other elliptical copulas derived from elliptical distributions; Archimedean copulas such as the Clayton, Gumbel, and Frank copulas, which are generated by a single univariate function and can capture asymmetric dependence; and vine copulas, which build high-dimensional dependence from cascades of bivariate copulas. Dependence measures such as Kendall's tau and Spearman's rho can be expressed directly in terms of the copula, and tail dependence coefficients describe the probability of joint extremes.

Applications

Copulas are widely used in quantitative finance for modeling correlated risks, pricing collateralized debt obligations, and portfolio risk management. The use of the Gaussian copula in pricing CDOs — popularized through David X. Li's 2000 paper — was criticized after the 2008 financial crisis for understating tail dependence between mortgage defaults. Copulas are also applied in hydrology, actuarial science, reliability engineering, and survival analysis.
