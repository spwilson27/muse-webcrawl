The prostate is both an accessory gland of the male reproductive system and a muscle-driven switch between urination and ejaculation that is found in all male mammals. It is a walnut-sized exocrine gland in the male urinary system which surrounds the urethra just below the bladder. The prostate produces prostatic fluid, a component of semen that nourishes and transports sperm.

Structure and function

The prostate is divided into several zones—peripheral, central, and transition zones—surrounded by a fibromuscular capsule. Its secretion is a slightly alkaline, milky fluid containing enzymes (including prostate-specific antigen, PSA, which liquefies semen), citric acid, zinc, and acid phosphatase. During ejaculation, smooth muscle in the prostate contracts to expel the fluid into the urethra, mixing with sperm from the vas deferens and secretions of the seminal vesicles and bulbourethral glands. Prostatic growth and function are regulated principally by dihydrotestosterone (DHT).

Clinical significance

Three main disorders affect the prostate. Prostatitis is inflammation or infection of the gland, causing pelvic pain and urinary symptoms. Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), the non-cancerous enlargement of the transition zone common in older men, can compress the urethra and obstruct urination; it is treated with alpha-blockers, 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors, or surgery such as transurethral resection (TURP). Prostate cancer, arising mostly in the peripheral zone, is one of the most common cancers in men; it is investigated by PSA testing, digital rectal examination, MRI, and biopsy, and managed by active surveillance, surgery, radiotherapy, or hormone therapy according to risk.

Examination

Because the prostate lies directly in front of the rectum, its posterior surface can be palpated on digital rectal examination, allowing assessment of size, symmetry, and nodules.
