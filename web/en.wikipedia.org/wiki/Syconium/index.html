Syconium

A syconium (plural syconia) is a type of inflorescence borne by figs (genus Ficus), formed by an enlarged, fleshy, hollow receptacle with multiple tiny ovaries on its inner surface. In essence it is a fleshy stem with a number of flowers, and is often described as being like a vase or flask in shape, with a small opening — the ostiole — at the far end, guarded by overlapping scale-like bracts.

Structure and pollination

The tiny flowers line the inside of the syconium and are therefore invisible from outside. Most fig species are pollinated by highly specialised fig wasps (family Agaonidae), which enter through the ostiole in one of the most celebrated examples of obligate mutualism in nature: the wasp pollinates the flowers and lays eggs in some of them, while the fig provides a protected nursery for the wasp's offspring.

Development

After pollination, the individual flowers set small drupelets or achenes, and the receptacle swells into the familiar fig "fruit" — botanically an infructescence or multiple fruit, rather than a single true fruit. In the common fig (Ficus carica), some varieties require pollination by the wasp Blastophaga psenes, while parthenocarpic varieties set fruit without it.
