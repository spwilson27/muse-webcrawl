Intrinsic and extrinsic properties


Intrinsic properties belong to a thing itself; extrinsic properties depend on its relations.

An intrinsic property is a property that an object or substance has of itself, independently of other things, including its context. An extrinsic property (or relational property) is a property that depends on a thing's relationship with other things. The distinction is central to metaphysics, philosophy of mind and value theory.

Examples and criteria

Mass, charge and shape are usually offered as intrinsic properties of a physical object, whereas weight — which depends on a gravitational field — and being a key, a parent or far away are extrinsic. Proposed tests include whether a perfect duplicate of a thing must share the property: exact duplicates share all intrinsic properties, but may differ in extrinsic ones, such as being observed or being the tallest in a group.

Philosophical debate

Philosophers disagree about sharp criteria, and about candidates such as being lonely, being such that 2 + 2 = 4, and identity properties. David Lewis analysed intrinsicality in terms of duplication. The distinction matters in ethics, where intrinsic value is value a thing has in itself, as opposed to instrumental or extrinsic value as a means to something else; and in philosophy of mind, where supervenience claims are often stated over intrinsic properties.

In physics

In materials science a similar usage distinguishes intrinsic properties of a material (such as density) from those influenced by external conditions; intrinsic semiconductors, for example, are undoped, with behaviour determined by the material itself.
