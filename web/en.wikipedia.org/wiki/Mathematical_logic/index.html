Mathematical logic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A page of symbolic logic notation with quantifiers and connectives]

Mathematical logic is a branch of mathematics exploring the applications of formal logic to mathematics. It emerged in the mid-19th century as logicians and mathematicians sought rigorous foundations, and it is now divided into model theory, proof theory, set theory, and recursion (computability) theory.

The field gave rise to landmark limitative results: Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and Turing's work on computable functions defined the theoretical limits of computation.

History

Mathematical logic grew from Aristotle's syllogistic through Leibniz's dream of a universal formal language, to Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879), which introduced modern quantifier notation. Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica (1910–13) attempted to derive mathematics from logic; Hilbert's programme then sought a finitary consistency proof for mathematics, an ambition Gödel's theorems undermined.

In the 1930s and afterwards the field professionalised around journals and the Association for Symbolic Logic (founded 1936).

Subfields

Model theory studies the relation between formal theories and their interpretations (Tarski); proof theory analyses the structure of proofs (Gentzen); set theory examines the axiomatic basis of mathematics (ZFC); and recursion theory classifies problems by their computability and complexity.
