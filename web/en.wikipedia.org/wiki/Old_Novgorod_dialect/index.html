The Old Novgorod dialect (Russian: древненовгородский диалект) is the name given to the distinctive variety of East Slavic spoken in the medieval Novgorod Republic, known principally from the birch bark letters (berestyanye gramoty) unearthed in Novgorod since 1951 and from other northwestern Rus' texts.

Distinctive features

The dialect differs from the Old East Slavic of Kiev and the chronicles in striking ways. It lacked the second palatalisation of velars — so where standard Old Russian has c from earlier k, Novgorod texts keep k (e.g. kěle "whole" for standard cělъ). It merged the sounds /t͡s/ and /t͡ɕ/ ("tsokanye"), used nominative singular endings in -e for masculine o-stems where other dialects had -ъ (as in names like Žiznomire), and had its own vocabulary and forms of address. Andrey Zaliznyak argued that these features show the Novgorod dialect diverged from the rest of East Slavic exceptionally early — essentially descending from a separate Proto-Slavic tribal dialect rather than from a uniform Old Russian — a view that reshaped the historical linguistics of Russian.

Birch bark letters

More than a thousand letters, scratched with a stylus onto birch bark, have been excavated in Novgorod and dozens more in other towns. They record everyday life — debts, orders for goods, love letters, a child's alphabet exercises (the famous notes of the boy Onfim, 13th century) — and demonstrate literacy well beyond the clergy. Because the letters are dated by the soil layers in which they lie, they allow unusually precise tracking of language change year by year.
