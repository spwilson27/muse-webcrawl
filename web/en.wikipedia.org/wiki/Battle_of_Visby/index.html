The Battle of Visby was fought on 27 July 1361, during the invasion of the Swedish island of Gotland by King Valdemar IV of Denmark. A professional Danish army defeated a hastily assembled militia of Gotlandic farmers outside the walls of the Hanseatic town of Visby. The battle is famous in archaeology for the mass graves of the fallen, whose armour and wounds have provided unique evidence for medieval warfare.

Remains of the fallen of 1361, excavated from the mass graves outside Visby.

Background

Valdemar IV, rebuilding Danish power, campaigned in 1361 against Scania, Öland, and finally wealthy Gotland, whose trade through Visby was a rich prize. The islanders, mostly peasant freeholders, mustered a militia — chronicles suggest it included the old and the very young — and met the Danes in a series of clashes as the invaders advanced on Visby.

The battle

After fighting at Mästerby earlier in the week, the Gotlanders made their last stand on 27 July before the southern wall of Visby, whose burghers — largely German Hanseatic merchants — shut the gates and watched rather than risk the town. The Danish veterans overwhelmed the militia; around 1,800 Gotlanders are estimated to have died. The dead were buried hastily in mass graves at Korsbetningen, many still wearing their armour because of the summer heat and rapid decomposition.

Aftermath and archaeology

Visby ransomed itself and Gotland passed under Danish control. Excavations of the mass graves in 1905 and 1912–1928 recovered some 2,000 skeletons along with mail armour, coats of plates, weapons, and personal items — an assemblage now displayed at the Gotland Museum. Studies of the skeletal injuries show blows concentrated on the head and lower legs, and the preserved armour has been central to modern understanding of mid-14th-century equipment.
