Nixie tube

A Nixie tube is an electronic display device that shows numerals or other information using glow discharge. Inside a glass tube filled with a low-pressure gas (usually neon with a small amount of mercury and/or argon) are stacked wire-mesh cathodes shaped as the symbols to be displayed, surrounded by a mesh anode. Applying power to one cathode causes it to be covered by an orange glow discharge, making that symbol visible through the stacked shapes in front of it.

History

Early glow-discharge indicators existed from the 1930s, but the modern Nixie was developed by the Haydu Brothers Laboratories and commercialised by the Burroughs Corporation, which introduced it in 1955 and trademarked the name "Nixie", derived from an early device labelled "NIX I" (Numeric Indicator eXperimental No. 1). Equivalent tubes were manufactured in large numbers in the Soviet Union, where they were designated with the prefix IN (ИН), and by manufacturers in Europe and Japan.

Use

Nixies were widely used in 1950s–1970s digital instrumentation — voltmeters, frequency counters, military equipment, early computers and stock-exchange tickers — and in lifts to indicate floors. They were displaced in the 1970s by more practical, lower-voltage technologies: vacuum fluorescent displays and then light-emitting diode seven-segment displays. Soviet-made tubes remained in production into the 1980s, leaving large surplus stocks.

Characteristics

Nixies require a relatively high voltage (around 170 V or more) but draw only a few milliamps. Digits are full-formed characters rather than segmented approximations, giving them a distinctive appearance prized by hobbyists. Lifespans range from thousands to hundreds of thousands of hours; unused cathodes can suffer "cathode poisoning", a build-up of sputtered material that obscures the digit, which can sometimes be reversed by overdriving the affected cathode.

Revival

Since the 2000s, Nixie displays have enjoyed a nostalgic revival in clocks and wristwatches built by hobbyists and small manufacturers, using surviving Soviet-era stocks or newly hand-made tubes produced by a handful of workshops. The warm orange glow and layered numerals have made them a popular aesthetic in retro and steampunk design.
