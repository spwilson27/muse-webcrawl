The Great Railroad Strike of 1877, also known as the Great Strike, was the first nationwide labour strike in the United States. It began on 14 July 1877 in Martinsburg, West Virginia, after the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad cut wages for the second time in a year, and spread across the country in the midst of the economic depression following the Panic of 1873. At its height some 100,000 workers were on strike, and sympathetic stoppages shut down much of the national rail network.

Course of the strike

From Martinsburg the strike spread along the B&O and to other lines: Baltimore, Cumberland, Pittsburgh, Chicago, St. Louis and dozens of smaller cities. In Pittsburgh, after state militia from Philadelphia fired on a crowd on 21 July, killing about 20 people, enraged strikers and residents burned the Pennsylvania Railroad's yards, destroying over 100 locomotives and more than a thousand freight cars. In Reading, Pennsylvania, militia killed ten people on 23 July. In St. Louis a general strike briefly placed the city's transport under workers' control. President Rutherford B. Hayes, at the request of governors, sent federal troops — the first large-scale use of the US Army in a labour dispute — and by early September the strike had collapsed without achieving its demands.

Aftermath

At least 100 people died in strike-related violence. Railroads largely conceded nothing at the time, but the upheaval shook contemporaries, who compared it to the Paris Commune of 1871. It led states to build armouries and strengthen National Guard forces, spurred the growth of organised labour — notably the Knights of Labor — and is regarded as a foundational event in American labour history, preceding the Haymarket affair of 1886.
