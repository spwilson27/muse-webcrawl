Nielsen ratings

Nielsen ratings are audience measurement statistics for television (and historically radio) developed by Nielsen Media Research, intended to determine the size and composition of audiences for programming. The ratings are a key input to the television industry: networks use them to set advertising prices and decide whether to renew or cancel shows. A rating point represents one percent of the total number of households (or of a demographic group) tuned to a program.

A Nielsen "people meter" records which household members are watching

History

Arthur C. Nielsen founded his company in 1923 and began measuring radio audiences in the 1930s and 1940s with the Nielsen Radio Index, using devices attached to radios in sample households. Television measurement followed in 1950. For decades, data was collected through paper diaries in smaller markets and electronic meters in larger ones; the "people meter", introduced in 1987, allowed measurement of individual viewers within a household. The system expanded to account for time-shifted viewing (such as "Live+3" and "Live+7" ratings), digital video recorders, and streaming.

Methodology

Nielsen recruits a statistically selected panel of households intended to represent the viewing population. Meters record what is watched and when; panelists register their presence, allowing estimates by age and sex demographics — most importantly the adults 18–49 demographic prized by advertisers. Results are expressed as a rating (percentage of all households with televisions) and a share (percentage of households actually using television at the time).

Sweeps and criticism

Historically, detailed local measurement occurred during "sweeps" periods in February, May, July, and November, prompting networks to schedule special programming and stunts. The system has been criticized for sampling limitations, for undercounting younger, mobile, and minority audiences, and for struggling to capture out-of-home and streaming viewing; Nielsen has progressively added broadband-only homes, wearable meters, and big-data sources to its panels.
