46° halo

The faint 46° halo encircling the Sun, far outside the common 22° halo.

A 46° halo is a rare atmospheric optical phenomenon: a large, faint ring of light around the Sun or Moon with an angular radius of about 46 degrees. It is caused by refraction of sunlight through hexagonal ice crystals in cirrus or cirrostratus clouds high in the troposphere.

Formation

Whereas the common 22° halo forms when light passes through two side faces of a hexagonal prism crystal meeting at 60°, the 46° halo forms when light enters a crystal through a side face and leaves through a basal (end) face, or vice versa — faces that meet at 90°. The minimum deviation through such a 90° ice prism is about 46°, which sets the ring's radius. The crystals must be oriented with their long axes horizontal (column crystals) or in other restricted orientations, which is one reason the phenomenon is much rarer than the 22° halo.

Appearance

The 46° halo is broad and diffuse, and its colors are less pure than those of the 22° halo because the refracted light is spread over a wider band. When any color separation is visible, red appears on the inner edge of the ring and blue or violet on the outer edge. Because it is so faint, the 46° halo is often missed, and it can be confused with the much more frequently seen supralateral and infralateral arcs, which occupy similar positions in the sky but are produced by different crystal orientations and never form a complete uniform circle.

Like other halo phenomena, the 46° halo can appear around the Moon as well, though lunar examples are fainter still.
