Scheme (mathematics)

A scheme glues together affine pieces built from commutative rings

In mathematics, a scheme is a mathematical structure that enlarges the notion of algebraic variety in several ways, such as taking account of multiplicities (the equations x = 0 and x² = 0 define different schemes, but the same variety) and allowing "varieties" defined over any commutative ring. Schemes were introduced by Alexander Grothendieck in 1960 in his treatise Éléments de géométrie algébrique, revolutionising algebraic geometry.

Definition

A scheme is a locally ringed space that admits an open covering by affine schemes. An affine scheme is the spectrum Spec(R) of a commutative ring R: its points are the prime ideals of R, equipped with the Zariski topology and a sheaf of rings (the structure sheaf) whose sections are functions given locally by fractions of ring elements. General schemes are obtained by gluing affine schemes along open subsets, much as manifolds are glued from Euclidean patches.

Motivation

  • Nilpotents — schemes record infinitesimal information: the "double point" Spec(k[x]/(x²)) captures tangency and deformation data invisible to varieties.
  • Arithmetic geometry — Spec(ℤ) behaves like a curve whose points are the prime numbers, letting number theory and geometry share methods; this underlies the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.
  • Generic points — irreducible closed subsets have generic points, making dimension theory and specialisation arguments precise.
  • Families — morphisms of schemes model families of varieties varying over a base.

Examples

Affine and projective spaces over a field, elliptic curves given by Weierstrass equations over ℤ, and arithmetic surfaces are all schemes. Important classes include Noetherian schemes, reduced schemes, integral schemes, and schemes of finite type over a field, the last being closest to classical varieties (though possibly non-reduced).

Impact

The language of schemes, together with sheaves, categories, and cohomology, is the foundation of modern algebraic geometry and is essential to contemporary number theory, including the Langlands program, moduli theory, and Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem via modularity of elliptic curves.
