The First Jewish–Roman War (66–73/74 CE), also known as the Great Jewish Revolt, was the first of three major rebellions by the Jews of Judaea Province against the Roman Empire. Sparked by religious tensions, heavy taxation and the misrule of the procurator Gessius Florus — who seized funds from the Temple treasury — the revolt began in 66 CE with riots in Jerusalem and the defeat of a Roman retaliatory expedition under Cestius Gallus at Beth-Horon.

Course of the war

Nero appointed the general Vespasian to crush the revolt; with his son Titus he methodically conquered Galilee in 67, including the siege of Jotapata where the Jewish commander Josephus was captured and later became the war's historian. When Nero died and Vespasian was proclaimed emperor in 69, Titus took command and besieged Jerusalem in 70. After months of famine and fighting, the Romans stormed the city and burned the Second Temple on the ninth of Av; the city's population was massacred or enslaved, and its treasures were paraded in Rome and depicted on the Arch of Titus.

Masada and aftermath

Remaining fortresses fell one by one; the last, Masada, held by Sicarii under Eleazar ben Ya'ir, was taken in 73 or 74 CE after the Romans built an enormous siege ramp — Josephus reports the defenders died by suicide rather than be captured. The war shattered Jewish political life in Judaea: the Temple and its priesthood were never restored, the Sanhedrin's authority waned, and Judaism re-centred on rabbinic teaching and the synagogue. Further revolts followed in the Diaspora Revolt (115–117) and the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136).
