Lechatelierite

FormulaSiO2 (amorphous)
FormationMelting of quartz sand by lightning, impacts or heat

Lechatelierite is a mineraloid consisting of fused silica glass (SiO2) formed by the melting of quartz sand or rock at extremely high temperatures followed by rapid cooling. It was first described in 1915 and named in honour of the French chemist Henry Louis Le Chatelier. Because it is amorphous rather than crystalline, it is classified as a mineraloid rather than a true mineral.

The best-known occurrence of lechatelierite is in fulgurites — the glassy tubes formed when lightning strikes sandy soil, heating the sand above the melting point of silica (about 1,700 °C). It also occurs in meteorite impact structures, where shock melting of quartz-bearing rock produces silica glass, and in volcanic settings where lava has fused siliceous material. In impact craters, lechatelierite is a useful indicator of shock metamorphism.

Properties

Lechatelierite is nearly pure silica, typically containing only trace impurities swept in from the host sand or rock. It has a density near 2.20 g/cm³ — lower than crystalline quartz (2.65) — a refractive index around 1.46, and a conchoidal fracture. It is chemically the natural analogue of manufactured fused quartz (fused silica) glass.

Occurrences

Notable occurrences include fulgurites from sandy deserts worldwide; impact glass at craters such as Wabar (Saudi Arabia), where nearly pure lechatelierite has been reported, and the Ries crater in Germany; and the Libyan desert glass of western Egypt, which consists largely of lechatelierite and is thought to be of impact origin.
