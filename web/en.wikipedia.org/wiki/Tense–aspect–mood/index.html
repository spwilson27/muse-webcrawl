Tense–aspect–mood

Tense–aspect–mood (commonly abbreviated TAM) is a grammatical system covering the expression of tense (location in time), grammatical aspect (the temporal structure of an event — whether completed, ongoing, habitual, and so on), and grammatical mood (the speaker's attitude toward the proposition, including modality). These three semantic domains are grouped together because in many languages they are expressed by a single fused inflection on the verb, and because their meanings interact: a single verb form often carries a bundle of tense, aspect, and mood values that cannot be cleanly separated.

Interaction of the categories

Not all combinations of tense, aspect, and mood are possible in every language. In many Indo-European languages, for example, the subjunctive mood has a reduced set of tense distinctions, and aspectual distinctions are prominent mainly in the past tense (as in the Greek aorist versus imperfect, or the Slavic perfective–imperfective opposition, which runs through the whole verb system). In creole languages, TAM is frequently expressed by preverbal particles whose ordering and combinations have been extensively studied: typically markers of anterior tense, irrealis mood, and non-punctual aspect can combine in a fixed order before the verb, as in Haitian Creole te (anterior), ap (progressive), and future/irrealis a(va).

Typology

Languages differ in which category dominates their verb morphology. Mandarin Chinese has no grammatical tense but rich aspect marking (perfective le, durative zhe, experiential guo). Many Bantu languages fuse tense and aspect into numerous contrasting "tenses" distinguishing degrees of remoteness in past and future. Linguists therefore often analyse verb systems in terms of TAM as a whole rather than assuming that "tense" is a universal, independent category.
