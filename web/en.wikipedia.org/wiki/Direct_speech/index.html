Direct speech

FieldLinguistics / rhetoric
ContrastIndirect (reported) speech
Typical markersQuotation marks, reporting clause

Direct speech, also called quoted speech or direct quotation, is a sentence or passage that reports speech or thought in its original spoken form, usually as phrased by the original speaker. It is conventionally enclosed in quotation marks: "I am going home," she said.

Features

In direct speech the pronouns, verb tenses and deictic expressions (such as "here", "now" and "tomorrow") are those of the original utterance, anchored to the speaker's point of view. A reporting clause — "he said", "she asked" — may precede, follow or interrupt the quotation.

Contrast with indirect speech

Indirect or reported speech reproduces the content of an utterance in the reporter's own words: She said that she was going home. Pronouns shift, tenses often backshift, and quotation marks are not used. Between the two lies free indirect speech, a narrative technique that blends the narrator's voice with a character's words and thoughts without quotation marks, used extensively in the modern novel.

Uses

Direct speech is central to dialogue in fiction and drama, to quotation in journalism and scholarship, and to testimony in legal settings, where the exact words spoken may matter.
