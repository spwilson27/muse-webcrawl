Renaissance sculpture

Renaissance sculpture is understood as a process of recovery of the sculpture of classical antiquity, pursued by sculptors in Italy and northern Europe from roughly 1400 to the later 16th century. It developed alongside Renaissance painting and architecture as part of the broader humanist revival of classical forms, and it restored sculpture — especially the free-standing nude figure in the round — to a prestige it had not held since antiquity.

Early Renaissance

The conventional starting point is the 1401 competition for the bronze doors of the Florence Baptistery, won by Lorenzo Ghiberti over Filippo Brunelleschi. Donatello pioneered the revived classical nude with his bronze David, developed the rilievo schiacciato (flattened relief) technique, and produced monumental works such as the equestrian Gattamelata monument in Padua. Luca della Robbia and his family popularised glazed terracotta.

High Renaissance and Mannerism

Michelangelo dominated the High Renaissance with the Pietà (1498–99), the colossal marble David (1501–1504), the Medici Chapel figures and the Slaves. Later sculptors such as Benvenuto Cellini, Giambologna and Bartolomeo Ammannati pursued the serpentine figura serpentinata characteristic of Mannerism.

Northern Europe

North of the Alps, sculptors including Claus Sluter (earlier, at Dijon), Tilman Riemenschneider and Veit Stoss in Germany, and later the Fontainebleau school in France adapted Renaissance naturalism, often in wood altarpieces and tomb sculpture.
