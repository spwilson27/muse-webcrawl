The Act of Abjuration (Dutch: Plakkaat van Verlatinghe, "Placard of Abandonment"), signed on 26 July 1581 in The Hague, is the formal declaration of independence of the northern Low Countries from the Spanish king Philip II. It is often described as the Dutch declaration of independence.

Background

The provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands had revolted against Spanish rule in the Eighty Years' War, driven by religious persecution, taxation and centralisation. After earlier attempts at reconciliation and the offer of sovereignty to the French Duke of Anjou, the States General concluded that Philip had forfeited his throne.

Content

The Act argues that a ruler is appointed by God to govern and protect his subjects, and that a prince who oppresses them and violates their privileges acts as a tyrant whom his subjects are entitled to abandon and replace. It therefore declared Philip deposed as ruler of the Netherlands. Its reasoning about the contractual basis of kingship foreshadowed later declarations, and it has been compared to the American Declaration of Independence of 1776.

Consequences

The Act did not end the war: fighting continued until Spain recognised Dutch independence in the Peace of Münster in 1648, but it provided the legal foundation of the Dutch Republic.
