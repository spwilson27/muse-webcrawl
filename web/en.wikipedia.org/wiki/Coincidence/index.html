Coincidence

A coincidence is a remarkable concurrence of events or circumstances that have no apparent causal connection with one another. The perception of remarkable coincidences may lead to supernatural, occult or paranormal claims, or it may lead to belief in fatalism, a doctrine that events will happen in the exact manner of a predetermined plan. In general, the perception of coincidence, for merely being an observation, may incline the individual towards some theistic creation assumptions.

Psychology

Psychologists note that humans are predisposed to detect patterns and meaning in random data, a tendency related to apophenia. Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung developed the concept of synchronicity, proposing that coincidental events could reflect an underlying acausal connecting principle. Psychologists Ruma Falk and others have studied how people judge the probability of coincidences and find that surprisingness is often overestimated because the many ways an event could have occurred are ignored.

Probability and statistics

From a statistical perspective, coincidences are inevitable given large numbers of opportunities. The classic example is the birthday problem: in a group of only 23 people there is a greater than 50 percent chance that two share a birthday. Littlewood's law argues that individuals can expect a roughly one-in-a-million event — a "miracle" — to occur about once a month. Statisticians Persi Diaconis and Frederick Mosteller formalised methods for studying coincidences, noting that with a large enough sample space, any outrageous thing is likely to happen.

In science and philosophy

Philosophers and scientists debate what counts as an explanation for a coincidence. In causal terms, a genuine coincidence is the intersection of two independent causal chains. Apparent coincidences may, however, reveal hidden common causes, which is one reason unusual concurrences can drive scientific inquiry.
