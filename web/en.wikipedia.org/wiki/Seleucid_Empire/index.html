Seleucid Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Silver tetradrachm coin of Seleucus I wearing a lion-scalp headdress]

The Seleucid Empire (312–63 BC) was a Hellenistic state founded by Seleucus I Nicator, one of Alexander the Great's generals, and at its height the largest of the successor kingdoms, ruling from Anatolia and Syria through Mesopotamia and Persia to the borders of India. Its kings founded scores of Greek cities — Antioch its western capital, Seleucia on the Tigris its eastern — and governed a vast, diverse realm of Greeks, Babylonians, Persians, Jews and many others. Weakened by dynastic strife, Parthian expansion and Roman intervention, it shrank to a Syrian rump that Pompey annexed in 64 BC.

Rise

Seleucus, having taken Babylon in 312 BC (the epoch of the Seleucid era), fought the Diadochi wars, ceded the far-eastern provinces to Chandragupta Maurya in exchange for 500 elephants, and used those elephants to win at Ipsus (301 BC). Antiochus I and Antiochus II consolidated the realm; Antiochus III 'the Great' (r. 223–187 BC) campaigned as far as India and Greece before Rome crushed him at Magnesia (190/189 BC) and imposed the Peace of Apamea.

Decline

Antiochus IV Epiphanes' persecution of Judaism provoked the Maccabean revolt (167 BC) and the loss of Judaea. The Parthians took Mesopotamia and Persia by 141 BC; thereafter rival Seleucid princes fought endless civil wars while Tigranes of Armenia and then Rome closed in. Pompey's settlement of 64 BC turned Syria into a Roman province, ending the last major successor kingdom of Alexander in Asia.
