Epilepsy is a group of non-communicable neurological disorders characterised by recurrent, unprovoked seizures. Epileptic seizures can vary from brief and nearly undetectable periods to long periods of vigorous shaking due to abnormal electrical activity in the brain. These episodes can result in physical injuries, either directly such as broken bones or through causing accidents, and people with epilepsy can suffer social stigma and psychological consequences.

Causes and diagnosis

The cause of most cases of epilepsy is unknown (cryptogenic); some cases occur as the result of brain injury, stroke, brain tumours, infections of the brain, or birth defects through a process known as epileptogenesis. Known genetic mutations are directly linked to a small proportion of cases. Diagnosis involves an electroencephalogram (EEG) and brain imaging, together with ruling out other conditions that may cause similar symptoms, such as fainting.

Seizure types

Seizures are classified as focal (confined to one area of the brain, with or without loss of awareness) or generalised (involving both sides of the brain), the latter including tonic-clonic, absence, myoclonic and atonic types. A prolonged seizure, status epilepticus, is a medical emergency.

Treatment

Epilepsy is usually treated with daily anti-seizure medication, which renders about two-thirds of patients seizure-free. In drug-resistant cases, epilepsy surgery, vagus nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, or ketogenic diet may be considered. Epilepsy affects about 50 million people worldwide, and with treatment most people with epilepsy live full lives, though life expectancy is modestly reduced, partly owing to sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) and injuries.
