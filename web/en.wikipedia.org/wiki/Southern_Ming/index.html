Southern Ming

The Southern Ming was a series of loyalist regimes established by members of the Ming imperial family and their supporters in southern China after the fall of Beijing to rebel forces and the subsequent Manchu conquest in 1644. It lasted, in diminishing form, until 1662, when the Yongli Emperor was executed in Burma.

History

After the Chongzhen Emperor's death, Ming officials in the southern capital Nanjing enthroned the Prince of Fu as the Hongguang Emperor. His court collapsed within a year amid factional conflict as Qing armies advanced. Successive courts followed: the Longwu Emperor in Fujian, backed by the maritime warlord Zheng Zhilong and his son Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga), and the Yongli Emperor, whose court retreated through Guangdong, Guangxi and Yunnan before fleeing into Burma.

End of resistance

The Qing general Wu Sangui captured the Yongli Emperor and had him strangled in 1662. Ming loyalism survived in the Kingdom of Tungning founded by Koxinga on Taiwan, which held out until 1683, but it is not usually counted as part of the Southern Ming proper.

Assessment

Historians debate whether the Southern Ming should be treated as a legitimate continuation of the Ming dynasty. The regimes were plagued by rival claimants, court intrigue and dependence on regional military strongmen, which fatally weakened resistance to the Qing dynasty.
