Centriole

[Image: Electron micrograph of a centriole showing its nine triplet microtubules]

In cell biology, a centriole is a cylindrical organelle, about 500 nm long and 200 nm in diameter, composed mainly of a protein called tubulin. Centrioles are found in most eukaryotic cells that can divide, though they are absent from higher plants and most fungi. A pair of centrioles, arranged perpendicular to one another and surrounded by pericentriolar material, forms the centrosome, the main microtubule-organizing centre of the animal cell.

Structure

Each centriole has a barrel-shaped wall of nine triplet microtubules arranged in a pinwheel pattern, connected by protein linkers. The proximal end contains a cartwheel structure built around the protein SAS-6, which establishes the ninefold symmetry during formation. Centrioles also serve as basal bodies: the template from which cilia and flagella grow, in which case the microtubule arrangement continues into the axoneme.

Function and duplication

During cell division, centrioles help organize the mitotic spindle poles, although plant cells divide without them. Centriole duplication is tightly coupled to the cell cycle: each existing centriole templates one new "procentriole" once per cycle, during S phase, under control of the kinase PLK4. Defects in centriole number or structure are implicated in cancer, and inherited mutations in centrosomal proteins cause ciliopathies and primary microcephaly.
