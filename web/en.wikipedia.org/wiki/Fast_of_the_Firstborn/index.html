Fast of the Firstborn

The Fast of the Firstborn (Hebrew: תענית בכורות, Ta'anit B'khorot) is a minor fast day in Judaism observed by firstborn sons (bekhorim) on the day before Passover — the fourteenth of Nisan — or, in some circumstances, on the preceding Thursday. It commemorates the salvation of the Israelite firstborn during the Plague of the Firstborn in ancient Egypt, the tenth plague that preceded the Exodus.

Origin and observance

Unlike other Jewish fasts, this one applies only to firstborn males. Its origins are post-Talmudic: the practice is mentioned in the minor tractate Soferim and in the Jerusalem Talmud, and was widely adopted in medieval communities. A firstborn who is under the age of religious majority is not obliged, though in some communities his father fasts in his place. When the eve of Passover falls on a Saturday, the fast is moved earlier, because fasting is not permitted on the Sabbath (except for Yom Kippur).

The siyum exemption

In contemporary practice, the fast is frequently avoided by means of a siyum: a celebration marking the completion of a tractate of the Talmud or another unit of Torah study, held after morning prayers on the day of the fast. Participation in the festive meal that follows a siyum is itself a mitzvah that takes precedence over the fast, so firstborns who attend may eat for the rest of the day. Synagogues commonly schedule a siyum for this purpose, making a full day's fasting relatively rare in practice. Some firstborns attend a siyum but nonetheless abstain from meat and wine afterward in keeping with the spirit of the day.
