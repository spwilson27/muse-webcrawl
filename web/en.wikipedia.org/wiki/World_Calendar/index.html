The World Calendar divides the year into four identical quarters.

The World Calendar is a proposed reform of the Gregorian calendar consisting of twelve months arranged in four equal quarters of 91 days each. It was devised by Elisabeth Achelis of New York in 1930 and promoted for much of the twentieth century by The World Calendar Association, which Achelis founded. Because every quarter is identical and each year begins on a Sunday, the calendar is perennial: the same date always falls on the same weekday, year after year.

Structure

Each quarter contains three months of 31, 30 and 30 days respectively, in that order, so that every quarter begins on a Sunday and its first month has five Sundays. The twelve months keep their traditional names, with January, April, July and October having 31 days and beginning each quarter. The 364 regular days are supplemented by an extra day at the end of the year, following 30 December, called Worldsday (or Year Day), which belongs to no week and no month. In leap years a second intercalary day, Leapyear Day, follows 30 June.

History

Achelis presented the plan publicly in 1930, publishing it in her Journal of Calendar Reform and in the book The World Calendar (1937). The proposal grew out of the simpler fixed-calendar ideas of earlier reformers and was one of dozens of schemes considered during the calendar-reform debates at the League of Nations in the 1930s. The Association opened offices in several countries and lobbied governments, businesses and churches; supporters argued that a fixed calendar would simplify statistics, scheduling, accounting and the fixing of movable feasts such as Easter.

In the 1950s the proposal came before the United Nations Economic and Social Council, where India was a prominent supporter. Adoption stalled in 1955–56, in part because the United States delegation did not support it, and the Association's influence declined after Achelis's death in 1973, though successor organisations have continued to advocate the plan.

Objections

The chief objection, pressed by many Jewish, Seventh-day Adventist and other religious groups, is that the intercalary Worldsday breaks the unbroken seven-day cycle of the week: because Worldsday is not a weekday, the Sabbath would drift through the calendar week in successive years, so that observant believers would periodically be required to rest on days the state treated as workdays. Similar objections had earlier defeated the International Fixed Calendar in the League of Nations debates. Critics also questioned the cost of change and the disruption of historical and legal records.
