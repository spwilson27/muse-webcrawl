Buddhist modernism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: Lay meditators at a modern meditation retreat centre]
Lay meditation practice is a hallmark of Buddhist modernism

Buddhist modernism (also called modern Buddhism, Buddhist modernity, and in one influential strand "Protestant Buddhism") refers to forms of Buddhism that have emerged since the 19th century out of the encounter between Asian Buddhist traditions and Western modernity — including colonialism, Christian missions, Enlightenment rationalism, and modern science. Buddhist modernist movements tend to emphasise reason, meditation, lay practice, social engagement, and the compatibility of Buddhism with science, while downplaying ritual, cosmology, and devotional elements regarded as later accretions.

History

During the 19th century, Asian Buddhists responded to colonial domination and Christian missionary criticism by reformulating Buddhism in modern terms. In Sri Lanka, figures such as Anagarika Dharmapala promoted a reformed Buddhism addressed to the laity; the Theosophist Henry Steel Olcott's Buddhist Catechism (1881) presented Buddhism in a form modelled partly on Protestant catechisms. In Japan, Meiji-era thinkers defended Buddhism against state persecution and sought to reconcile it with science and philosophy. In Burma and Thailand, royal and monastic reformers promoted scriptural study and lay meditation.

Characteristics

Scholars such as Donald Lopez and David McMahan identify recurring features of Buddhist modernism: an insistence that Buddhism is rational and compatible with science; a return to early texts as the standard of authenticity; the universalisation of meditation, formerly largely a monastic practice, as something for laypeople; an emphasis on this-worldly social engagement; and presentations of Buddhism as a philosophy or psychology rather than a religion. The global mindfulness movement is often seen as a descendant of these developments.

Protestant Buddhism

Anthropologist Gananath Obeyesekere coined the term "Protestant Buddhism" for the reformed Buddhism of Sri Lanka, noting its parallels with Protestant Christianity: protest against colonial and clerical authority, an emphasis on individual effort and interiority, and the mobilisation of laypeople as religious agents. The concept has been widely used, and contested, in studies of modern Theravada.

Modern movements

Buddhist modernist tendencies appear in engaged Buddhism associated with Thich Nhat Hanh and the Dalai Lama's dialogues with scientists; in Taiwan's large humanitarian organisations; in the lay meditation movements of Burma (such as those stemming from Mahasi Sayadaw and S. N. Goenka); and in Western convert Buddhism, including secular Buddhism.

Criticism

Critics argue that Buddhist modernism distorts tradition by projecting modern Western values onto premodern Buddhism, marginalising ritual, merit-making, and cosmology that remain central for most Asian Buddhists. Scholars also note that "traditional" and "modern" forms coexist and interpenetrate rather than forming a clean succession.
