Takeno Jōō

Born: 1502

Died: 1555 (aged 52–53)

Occupation: Japanese tea master

Known for: Developing wabi-cha; teacher of Sen no Rikyū

Takeno Jōō (Japanese: 武野 紹鷗; 1502–1555) was a Japanese tea master of the Muromachi and Sengoku periods who played a central role in the development of the Japanese tea ceremony (chanoyu). He is remembered as the teacher of Sen no Rikyū and as a key figure in refining the aesthetic of wabi — rustic simplicity — in tea practice.

Life

Jōō was born into a wealthy merchant family connected with the Shimogyō district of Kyoto and the port city of Sakai, whose merchant class patronized tea culture. He studied Zen at Daitoku-ji temple and took lay vows; his Buddhist name Jōō reflects this background. He studied tea under masters in the lineage of Murata Jukō, the founder of the wabi-cha style, transmitting and refining Jukō's emphasis on understated, imperfect utensils and a more intimate tea setting.

Contributions to tea culture

Jōō is credited with important innovations: he promoted the use of Japanese-made tea utensils alongside prized Chinese pieces, advocated smaller, simpler tea rooms, and helped codify the use of the daime tatami layout. He composed a famous verse comparing the ideal of wabi to a horse tethered to a flowering cherry tree by its bridle — combining splendour with restraint. His aesthetic judgement and connoisseurship shaped the tastes of Sakai's merchant tea men.

Legacy

Jōō's most famous pupil, Sen no Rikyū, carried his master's ideas to their fullest expression under the patrons Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, becoming the most influential tea master in Japanese history. Jōō is thus regarded as a pivotal link between Jukō's founding vision and Rikyū's mature wabi-cha.
