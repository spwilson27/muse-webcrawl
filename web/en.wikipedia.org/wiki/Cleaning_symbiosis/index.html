Cleaning symbiosis

Cleaning symbiosis
Type: Mutualism
Cleaners: Cleaner fish, cleaner shrimp, oxpeckers, plovers
Clients: Fish, turtles, mammals, birds
Settings: Coral reefs, savannas, wetlands

Cleaning symbiosis is a mutually beneficial association between individuals of two species, in which one (the cleaner) removes and eats parasites, dead tissue and mucus from the surface of the other (the client). It is a classic example of mutualism, best known from coral reefs but also found among terrestrial animals.

Marine cleaning

On coral reefs, cleaner fish — especially wrasses of the genus Labroides — and cleaner shrimp set up "cleaning stations" to which client fish return repeatedly. Clients adopt distinctive postures and colour changes signalling that they will not eat the cleaner, which may even enter the mouth and gill chambers of predatory fish such as groupers. Research has shown that reefs from which cleaner wrasse are experimentally removed suffer declines in fish diversity and health.

Terrestrial cleaning

On land, oxpeckers pick ticks and other parasites from large mammals such as buffalo and giraffes, though their habit of also feeding on blood from wounds complicates the relationship. Egyptian plovers are traditionally said to clean the teeth of Nile crocodiles, and various birds glean insects from grazing cattle.

Cheating and honesty

Cleaners sometimes "cheat" by biting off healthy tissue or mucus, which clients punish by chasing the cleaner or leaving the station. Some species, such as the false cleanerfish (Aspidontus taeniatus), mimic cleaner wrasse in appearance and dance, then bite chunks from unwary clients — an aggressive mimicry that exploits the cleaning system.
