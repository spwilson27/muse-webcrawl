The siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE was the decisive event of the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73 CE). A Roman army under Titus, son of Emperor Vespasian, besieged and captured the city, which had been controlled by Jewish rebel factions, and destroyed the Second Temple.

Background

Revolt broke out in 66 CE. Vespasian subdued Galilee; proclaimed emperor in 69, he left his son Titus to finish the campaign. Inside Jerusalem, Zealots, Sicarii and followers of John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora fought each other even as the Romans approached, burning food stocks.

The siege

Titus arrived before Passover with four legions — V Macedonica, X Fretensis, XII Fulminata and XV Apollinaris — plus auxiliaries, some 70,000 men. Romans breached the third and second walls in May, built a circumvallation wall around the city, and starved the defenders. Josephus, a former Jewish commander serving as interpreter, gives harrowing accounts of famine and crucifixions of escapees.

In July the Antonia Fortress fell; daily sacrifices ceased. On the 9th/10th of Av (August) the Temple was burned — whether by Titus's order or accident remains debated. The upper city fell in September. Josephus claimed 1.1 million died, a figure modern historians regard as exaggerated, but tens of thousands were killed or enslaved. Spoils, including the Menorah, were paraded in Rome and depicted on the Arch of Titus; the Colosseum was partly funded by the loot.

Aftermath

The destruction ended the Temple cult and reshaped Judaism toward rabbinic, synagogue-centred practice. Masada held out until 73/74 CE. Compare the later Six-Day War fought over the same city, and the ancient Egyptian capital Thebes.
