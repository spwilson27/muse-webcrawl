Super VHS, commonly abbreviated S-VHS, is an improved version of the VHS standard for consumer-level video recording, introduced by JVC in Japan in April 1987. It raised horizontal luminance resolution from roughly 240 lines to about 400 lines and recorded luminance and chrominance as separate signals, derived from the S-Video (Y/C) interface.

Technical details

S-VHS achieved its sharper picture by increasing the luminance carrier frequency, using higher-quality magnetic tape formulations, and widening the recorded bandwidth. S-VHS cassettes are physically identical in shell dimensions to VHS tapes but use a different tape stock; an identification hole lets decks distinguish them. S-VHS recordings are not properly playable on ordinary VHS machines, though S-VHS decks can play VHS tapes, and a later feature called S-VHS ET (expansion technology) allowed near-S-VHS quality recording onto standard VHS tape in specially equipped decks.

Use and legacy

The format found its main markets in prosumer camcorders (including the compact S-VHS-C variant), industrial video, and education, where its improved image suited editing and duplication. It never displaced VHS in the mass market, partly because prerecorded S-VHS movies were rare, and it was eventually superseded by digital formats such as MiniDV, DVD, and tapeless recording. The related professional/industrial format W-VHS later supported early high-definition analogue recording in Japan.
