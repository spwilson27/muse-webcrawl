Sextus Empiricus

Sextus Empiricus (Greek: Σέξτος Ἐμπειρικός; fl. late 2nd century CE) was a Greek physician and philosopher of the Pyrrhonist school of scepticism who lived in the Roman Empire, probably in Alexandria or Athens. His surviving writings are the fullest extant account of ancient Greek and Roman scepticism and were a major influence on early modern philosophy, particularly on Montaigne and Descartes.

Life

Little is known of Sextus's life. His name "Empiricus" indicates adherence to the Empiric school of medicine, which, like Pyrrhonism, rejected hidden causes and relied on observed phenomena. Diogenes Laërtius places him in a succession of sceptic teachers; he probably flourished around 180–200 CE.

Works

Two works survive. The Outlines of Pyrrhonism (Pyrrhōneioi hypotypōseis), in three books, summarises sceptical philosophy: Book I introduces Pyrrhonism, its aim of tranquillity (ataraxia) through suspension of judgement (epochē), and sets out the Ten Modes attributed to Aenesidemus and the Five Modes of Agrippa. Books II and III attack dogmatic logic, physics, and ethics. Against the Mathematicians (Pros Mathēmatikous) attacks the professors of grammar, rhetoric, geometry, arithmetic, astrology, and music (books I–VI) and continues the critique of dogmatic philosophy (books VII–XI, sometimes treated as a separate work, Against the Dogmatists).

Philosophy

Sextus presents the sceptic as one who, confronted by equipollent opposing arguments, suspends judgement and thereby attains tranquillity. He famously argues that the sceptic lives by appearances without holding beliefs about how things are in their real nature, addresses the problem of the criterion of truth, and deploys arguments — such as the regress and circularity modes — that became staples of later epistemology.

Influence

Sextus's works were translated into Latin in the 16th century (1562–1569) and ignited the early modern "Pyrrhonian crisis," shaping Montaigne's essays, the querelle over certainty, and Descartes's methodical doubt, as documented in Richard Popkin's History of Scepticism.
