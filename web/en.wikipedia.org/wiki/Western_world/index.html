Western world

The Western world, also known as the West, primarily refers to the regions of Europe, the Americas and Australasia whose cultures derive substantially from European civilisation. The concept contrasts with the Eastern world and is rooted in the heritage of ancient Greece and Rome, the adoption of Christianity, and later developments such as the Renaissance, the Enlightenment, and industrialisation and colonisation, which spread Western institutions globally.

Definitions

Definitions vary. In a cultural sense, the West comprises countries shaped by Western European languages, Latin-derived or Germanic legal and political traditions, and predominantly Christian heritage. In Cold War usage, "the West" meant the capitalist "First World" aligned with the United States and NATO, as opposed to the communist Eastern Bloc. Geopolitical usage today usually includes the European Union, the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, though scholars debate the status of Latin America and Eastern Europe.

Criticism

Historians note that the concept is a modern construct, projected back onto periods — such as the ancient Mediterranean — in which no such divide existed, and that "Western civilisation" narratives can understate exchanges with Islamic, Asian and African societies.
