Tracking (hunting)

Tracking in hunting and ecology is the practice of following an animal by interpreting the signs it leaves — footprints, droppings (scat), feeding marks, disturbed vegetation, scent, and sounds — a body of evidence trackers call spoor. It is among the oldest human skills and remains central to subsistence hunting, wildlife research, anti-poaching work, and search and rescue.

Techniques

A tracker reads the age, direction, speed, and condition of an animal from its trail: the sharpness and moisture of prints, displaced dew or soil, broken twigs, and rub marks. Following a trail requires distinguishing the target's sign from that of other animals and predicting behaviour from terrain, wind, and time of day. Skilled trackers, such as the San hunters of the Kalahari or Aboriginal trackers in Australia, are renowned for reconstructing events from minimal sign; some scholars have argued that systematic tracking was an early form of scientific, hypothetico-deductive reasoning.

Modern use

Beyond hunting, tracking is used in conservation (census and monitoring of elusive species), in forensic and border-security contexts (sometimes called "sign cutting"), and in man-tracking for locating lost persons. Field guides to tracks and scat are standard tools for naturalists.
