Charles Mingus Jr. (April 22, 1922 – January 5, 1979) was an American jazz double bassist, composer, and bandleader. Sometimes known as "The Angry Man of Jazz", Mingus was a major proponent of collective improvisation and is considered one of the greatest jazz musicians and composers in history, with a career spanning three decades and collaborations with other jazz greats such as Duke Ellington, Charlie Parker, Miles Davis, and Louis Armstrong.

Early life

Mingus was born in Nogales, Arizona, and raised in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles. He studied cello as a child before taking up the double bass, studying with Herman Rheinshott and with New York Philharmonic principal bassist Red Callender. His early influences included Ellington, whose music he heard on the radio as a boy.

Career

Mingus played with Louis Armstrong in the early 1940s and toured with Lionel Hampton's band later in the decade. In the early 1950s he co-founded Debut Records with Max Roach, one of the first musician-owned record labels. His breakthrough as a composer came with the album Pithecanthropus Erectus (1956), followed by the landmark recordings Mingus Ah Um (1959), Blues & Roots (1960), and The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady (1963), the last widely regarded as one of the greatest orchestral jazz albums ever made.

His compositions include "Goodbye Pork Pie Hat" (an elegy for Lester Young), "Haitian Fight Song", "Fables of Faubus" (a protest against segregationist governor Orval Faubus), and "Better Git It in Your Soul". His large ensemble, the Jazz Workshop, served as a proving ground for musicians including Eric Dolphy, Booker Ervin, Rahsaan Roland Kirk, and Jaki Byard.

Later years and death

After a period of relative inactivity in the late 1960s, Mingus returned to prominence in the 1970s, signing with Atlantic Records and recording albums such as Changes One and Changes Two. He published his autobiography, Beneath the Underdog, in 1971. Diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, he died in Cuernavaca, Mexico, in 1979. His ashes were scattered in the Ganges River. His final project, Epitaph, a sprawling composition for a 31-piece orchestra, was premiered posthumously in 1989.
