Thyroxine

Thyroxine, also called T4, is a hormone produced by the follicular cells of the thyroid gland from the amino acid tyrosine and iodine. It is the principal hormone secreted by the thyroid and acts as a prohormone for the more active triiodothyronine (T3), into which it is converted in peripheral tissues. Thyroxine regulates metabolic rate, growth and development, and cardiovascular and nervous system function. Its synthetic form, levothyroxine, is among the most commonly prescribed medicines worldwide.

Production and metabolism

Thyroid follicular cells trap iodide and incorporate it into tyrosyl residues of thyroglobulin, coupling them to form T4 and T3 under the control of thyroid peroxidase. On stimulation by TSH, the hormones are released into the blood, where they circulate bound to thyroxine-binding globulin and other carrier proteins. In target tissues, deiodinase enzymes remove one iodine atom from T4 to yield active T3, or inactive reverse T3.

Function

Thyroid hormones increase basal metabolic rate, heat production, heart rate and responsiveness to catecholamines, and are essential for normal brain development in infants. Both deficiency (hypothyroidism) and excess (hyperthyroidism/thyrotoxicosis) cause widespread symptoms; severe untreated congenital deficiency impairs intellectual development, which is why newborn screening is routine in many countries.

History

Thyroxine was isolated in crystalline form by Edward Calvin Kendall in 1914–1915 at the Mayo Clinic, and its structure was determined and synthesis achieved by Charles Harington and George Barger in 1926–1927.
