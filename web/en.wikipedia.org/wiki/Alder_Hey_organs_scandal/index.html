Alder Hey organs scandal

Medical scandal
DateRevealed 1999; inquiry report 2001
LocationAlder Hey Children's Hospital, Liverpool, England
Key figureDick van Velzen
OutcomeHuman Tissue Act 2004

The Alder Hey organs scandal involved the widespread, unauthorised removal, retention and disposal of human tissue, including children's organs, at Alder Hey Children's Hospital in Liverpool, England, during the period 1988 to 1995. It became one of the most prominent medical scandals in modern British history and led to fundamental reform of the law governing human tissue in the United Kingdom.

Background and revelations

The practice came to light in 1999 during the public inquiry into paediatric heart surgery at the Bristol Royal Infirmary, when it emerged that hospitals held large collections of children's hearts and other organs. At Alder Hey, the pathologist Dick van Velzen, who headed the foetal and infant pathology service from 1988 to 1995, had systematically ordered the retention of every organ from every post-mortem examination he conducted, building a large collection that was neither properly catalogued nor used for research. Parents discovered that organs — in some cases entire organ systems, and in one case a child's head — had been kept without their knowledge or consent, after they believed their children had been buried or cremated intact.

The Redfern inquiry

The government commissioned an independent inquiry chaired by Michael Redfern QC. Its report, published in January 2001, found that more than 2,000 pots containing organs from about 850 infants were held at Alder Hey, and documented widespread failures of management and oversight. Van Velzen was suspended and later struck off the medical register by the General Medical Council; criminal proceedings relating to the retention were ultimately not pursued, though in 2004 he was convicted of offences connected to organs retained at another hospital.

Aftermath and legal reform

Families received compensation in a mediated settlement, and many held second funerals for recovered remains. Nationally, the scandal — together with similar revelations at other hospitals — prompted the Human Tissue Act 2004, which made informed consent the fundamental principle governing the removal, storage and use of human tissue, and established the Human Tissue Authority to regulate the field.
