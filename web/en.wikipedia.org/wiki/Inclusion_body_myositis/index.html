Inclusion body myositis

Inclusion body myositis
Specialty: Rheumatology, neurology
Symptoms: Slowly progressive muscle weakness, especially quadriceps and finger flexors; swallowing difficulty
Usual onset: After age 50
Causes: Unknown; inflammatory and degenerative mechanisms
Treatment: No effective drug therapy; exercise and supportive care

Inclusion body myositis (IBM) is the most common idiopathic inflammatory myopathy affecting people over the age of 50. It is characterised by slowly progressive weakness that typically involves the quadriceps muscles of the thighs and the long flexors of the fingers, often asymmetrically, and later may cause dysphagia (difficulty swallowing).

Pathology

Muscle biopsy in IBM shows a combination of inflammation — endomysial inflammatory infiltrates invading non-necrotic fibres — and degenerative features, including rimmed vacuoles and intracellular deposits of proteins such as amyloid and TDP-43. This dual character distinguishes IBM from polymyositis and dermatomyositis and helps explain its resistance to immunosuppressive treatment.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on the clinical pattern, creatine kinase levels (often only mildly elevated), electromyography and muscle biopsy. Unlike other inflammatory myopathies, IBM generally does not respond to corticosteroids or other immunotherapy; management focuses on supervised exercise, fall prevention, assistive devices and treatment of dysphagia. The condition progresses gradually over years.
