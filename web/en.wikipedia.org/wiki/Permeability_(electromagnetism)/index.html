In electromagnetism, permeability is the measure of the resistance of a material to the formation of a magnetic field within itself — equivalently, the degree to which a material concentrates magnetic flux. It is represented by the Greek letter μ and, for a linear isotropic material, relates the magnetic flux density B to the magnetic field strength H by B = μH. The term was coined in 1885 by Oliver Heaviside.

Relative permeability

Permeability is usually expressed relative to the vacuum permeability μ0 as the dimensionless relative permeability μr = μ/μ0. Materials are classified by their response: diamagnetic materials have μr slightly less than 1 (e.g. copper ≈ 0.99999); paramagnetic materials slightly more than 1 (aluminium ≈ 1.00002); and ferromagnetic materials — iron, nickel, cobalt and their alloys — have μr values from hundreds to hundreds of thousands, but their permeability is strongly non-linear, varying with field strength and exhibiting hysteresis and saturation.

Complex permeability

For alternating fields, permeability is treated as a complex quantity: the in-phase component μ′ represents stored energy and the out-of-phase component μ″ represents loss. Both vary with frequency, which matters in the design of transformer cores, inductors and ferrite components for radio-frequency use.

Applications

High-permeability materials such as soft iron, silicon steel, permalloy and mu-metal are used to guide magnetic flux in transformer and motor cores, and to provide magnetic shielding. Permeability also determines inductance and hence the behaviour of inductors and antennas, and it enters Maxwell's equations through the speed of electromagnetic waves in a medium, c = 1/√(με).
