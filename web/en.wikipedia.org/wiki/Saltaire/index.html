Saltaire is a Victorian model village in Shipley, West Yorkshire, England, built between 1851 and 1871 by the woollen manufacturer Sir Titus Salt for the workers of his great textile mill, Salts Mill. It takes its name from its founder and the River Aire on whose banks it stands. Saltaire is regarded as one of the finest and best-preserved examples of a 19th-century industrial model settlement and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001.

History

Titus Salt, a Bradford alpaca and worsted manufacturer, resolved to move his scattered mills out of polluted Bradford to a greenfield site beside the river, the Leeds and Liverpool Canal, and the Midland Railway, combining his entire operation in a single mill and housing his workforce in a healthy planned village. Salts Mill opened with great ceremony on Salt's fiftieth birthday, 20 September 1853; at the time it was the largest industrial building in the world, its main spinning shed a third of a mile long. The village, designed by Bradford architects Lockwood and Mawson in a harmonious Italianate style, grew over two decades to more than 800 houses.

The settlement was comprehensively equipped: stone-built houses with water and gas, schools, a hospital, almshouses, a public institute with library and concert hall (now Victoria Hall), a Congregational church (1859, now United Reformed, containing Salt's mausoleum), wash-houses, a park across the river, and allotment gardens. Notably, there were no public houses — Salt was a temperance supporter. Rents were modest but the housing was far superior to the Bradford slums it replaced, and Salt exercised a paternalist, sometimes strict, oversight of village life. The mill remained with the Salt family and successors until the 1980s; textile production ended in 1986.

Present day

In 1987 entrepreneur Jonathan Silver bought the derelict mill complex and regenerated it as a centre for galleries, shops, restaurants, and technology businesses. Salts Mill houses one of the world's largest collections of works by Bradford-born artist David Hockney. The village is a conservation area; its streets of workers' cottages are now highly desirable homes, and the Saltaire Festival and arts trail draw large numbers of visitors. The site's World Heritage inscription cites it as an outstanding example of mid-19th-century philanthropic paternalism that influenced the garden city movement.
