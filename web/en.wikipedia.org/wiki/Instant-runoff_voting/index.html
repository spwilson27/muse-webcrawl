Instant-runoff voting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Instant-runoff voting (IRV), called ranked-choice voting (RCV) in the United States, the alternative vote (AV) in Britain, and preferential voting in Australia, is a single-winner electoral system in which voters rank candidates in order of preference; if no candidate has a majority of first preferences, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and their ballots are transferred to the next-ranked candidate still in contention, repeating until one candidate holds a majority of continuing ballots.

Use and evaluation

IRV elects Australia's House of Representatives (since 1918–19), Ireland's president, and a growing number of US cities and two states (Maine, Alaska — Alaska pairing it with a top-four primary), and is used by many parties and organisations internally. Supporters claim it eliminates the spoiler effect, discourages negative campaigning (candidates court second preferences), and produces majority winners without a costly second election day; social-choice critics note that it is not in fact identical to a runoff (the eventual winner may differ from who a real second round would elect), can exhibit non-monotonicity (ranking a candidate higher can hurt them), and does not guarantee the Condorcet winner. British voters rejected AV for Westminster in the 2011 referendum by a wide margin — the system's largest popular test to date.
