Ruth Jones Graves Wakefield (June 17, 1903 – January 10, 1977) was an American chef, best known as the inventor of the Toll House Cookie, the first chocolate chip cookie, which she created in the 1930s at the Toll House Inn in Whitman, Massachusetts. She also wrote a best-selling cookbook and ran the inn with her husband Kenneth for three decades.

Early life and the Toll House Inn

Wakefield was born in Walpole, Massachusetts, and educated at the Framingham State Normal School (now Framingham State University), graduating in 1924 with training in household arts. She worked as a dietitian and lecturer on food before marrying Kenneth Wakefield. In 1930 the couple purchased a tourist lodge in Whitman, on the old Boston–New Bedford road where travellers had once paid tolls and left their horses — hence the name Toll House Inn. Ruth cooked and Kenneth managed the business; her skill, particularly with desserts, made the inn a celebrated New England dining destination.

The chocolate chip cookie

Around 1938, Wakefield added chopped pieces from a bar of Nestlé semi-sweet chocolate to a butter cookie dough. Contrary to the popular story that the invention was an accident (that she expected the chocolate to melt through the dough, or that she was substituting for baker's chocolate that had run out), Wakefield stated that she deliberately created the recipe: "We had been serving a thin butterscotch nut cookie with ice cream. Everybody seemed to love it, but I was trying to give them something different." The cookies — first called "Chocolate Crunch Cookies" — became famous, and the recipe was printed in her cookbook Toll House Tried and True Recipes.

As demand grew, Nestlé negotiated with Wakefield, acquiring the rights to the recipe and the Toll House name — reportedly for one dollar and a lifetime supply of chocolate, together with consulting work — and began printing the recipe on its packaging. In 1939 Nestlé introduced pre-scored chocolate and then the ready-made morsels known as Toll House Real Semi-Sweet Chocolate Morsels. The cookie became an American icon, was sent in care packages to soldiers during the Second World War, and in 1997 was designated the official state cookie of Massachusetts.

Later life and legacy

The Wakefields sold the Toll House Inn in 1966; the building burned down on New Year's Eve 1984. Ruth Wakefield died in 1977 in Plymouth, Massachusetts. Her cookie is one of the most widely baked recipes in the world, and she is frequently cited as an example of a professional chef whose innovation was long retold as a happy accident. In 2003 she was celebrated in Massachusetts on the centenary of her birth.
