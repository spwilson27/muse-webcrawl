Asylum seeker

Asylum seekers register their claims at a state reception facility.

An asylum seeker is a person who has fled their country of origin and formally applied for international protection in another country, but whose claim has not yet been decided. If the claim succeeds, the person is recognised as a refugee entitled to protection under the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol; if it fails, the applicant may be required to leave, subject to appeal and to the principle of non-refoulement, which forbids return to a country where the person faces persecution or serious harm.

Legal basis

The Convention defines a refugee as a person outside their country of nationality who is unable or unwilling to return owing to a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion. States operate their own determination procedures, and the UN refugee agency UNHCR may also conduct status determination. In the European Union, the Dublin Regulation generally assigns responsibility for a claim to the first member state of entry, and subsidiary protection may be granted to those who do not qualify as refugees but face serious harm.

Reception and rights

While claims are processed — often taking months or years — asylum seekers may be housed in reception centres, given subsistence support, and in many countries permitted to work only after a waiting period. Access to healthcare and education for children is usually provided. Long processing delays, restrictions on employment and the risk of detention are recurring concerns of refugee advocates.

Statistics and politics

Global forced displacement exceeded 110 million people in the mid-2020s, driven by conflicts in Ukraine, Syria, Afghanistan, Sudan and elsewhere. Asylum policy is politically contentious in receiving countries, with debates over border control, processing backlogs, safe-country concepts and resettlement quotas shaping elections and legislation.
