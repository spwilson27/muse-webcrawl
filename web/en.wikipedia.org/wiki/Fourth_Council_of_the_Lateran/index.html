Fourth Council of the Lateran

The Fourth Council of the Lateran (Lateran IV) was an ecumenical council of the Roman Catholic Church convoked by Pope Innocent III and held in Rome in November 1215. It was the largest council of the Middle Ages, attended by over 1,200 prelates and representatives of secular rulers, and is regarded as one of the most consequential councils in Church history.

Decrees

The council issued some seventy canons. The first canon, Firmiter, defined the doctrine of transubstantiation—the first conciliar use of the term—affirming that the bread and wine are transubstantiated into the body and blood of Christ. Canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, required every Christian to confess sins to their own priest at least once a year and to receive the Eucharist at Easter, establishing the framework of annual confession that persists today.

Other decrees reformed clerical discipline and education, regulated episcopal elections, prohibited the founding of new religious orders without approval (a measure soon circumvented by the Franciscans and Dominicans), condemned the heresies of the Cathars and Waldensians, and called for a new crusade to recover the Holy Land, which became the Fifth Crusade. Canons concerning Jews and Muslims required them to wear distinguishing dress, restricted their holding of public office, and addressed Jewish moneylending—measures that had lasting and damaging effects on Jewish life in Christian Europe.
