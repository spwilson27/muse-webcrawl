The canonical hours are the divisions of the day in terms of fixed times of prayer in Christianity, as prayed in the Liturgy of the Hours (Divine Office). The term is also used for the prayers themselves, which consist chiefly of psalms, hymns, scripture readings and intercessions, recited daily by clergy, religious orders and many laypeople.

The hours

The traditional eightfold office comprises Matins (during the night), Lauds (at dawn), Prime (first hour, c. 6 a.m.), Terce (third hour, c. 9 a.m.), Sext (sixth hour, noon), None (ninth hour, c. 3 p.m.), Vespers (evening) and Compline (before retiring). The names Terce, Sext and None preserve Roman divisions of daylight into twelve hours. Monastic rules, especially the Rule of St Benedict, organised communal life around this cycle — ora et labora, pray and work.

Development

Fixed-hour prayer has roots in Jewish practice and is attested in the early Church. The office took shape in Egyptian and Syrian monasticism, was codified for the West by Benedict of Nursia in the 6th century, and was spread by the Roman breviary. The Book of Common Prayer condensed the office into Morning and Evening Prayer for Anglican use, while the Second Vatican Council reformed the Roman Liturgy of the Hours.
