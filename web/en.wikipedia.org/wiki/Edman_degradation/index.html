Edman degradation

Edman degradation, developed by Pehr Edman in the late 1940s and published in 1950, is a method of sequencing amino acids in a peptide. In this method, the amino-terminal (N-terminal) residue is labelled and cleaved from the peptide without disrupting the peptide bonds between other amino acid residues, allowing the process to be repeated cycle by cycle to read off the sequence one residue at a time.

Mechanism

Phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC, Edman's reagent) reacts with the free N-terminal amino group under mildly alkaline conditions to form a phenylthiocarbamoyl peptide. Treatment with anhydrous acid (such as trifluoroacetic acid) then cleaves the first peptide bond, releasing the N-terminal residue as an unstable anilinothiazolinone (ATZ) derivative and leaving the shortened peptide — shortened by one residue, with a new free N-terminus — intact for the next cycle. The ATZ-amino acid is extracted and converted, in aqueous acid, to the more stable phenylthiohydantoin (PTH) derivative, which is identified by chromatography (historically thin-layer chromatography, later HPLC) by comparison with standards.

Automation and limitations

The chemistry was automated in the "sequenator" developed by Edman and Geoffrey Begg in 1967, which enabled routine determination of protein sequences and underpinned landmark work such as early antibody and enzyme sequences. Practical limitations include: each cycle is less than 100% efficient, so reads are realistically limited to about 30–60 residues; the method requires a free N-terminus, so N-terminally blocked proteins cannot be sequenced directly; and it needs relatively pure peptide in picomole-to-nanomole amounts. Since the 1990s, mass spectrometry (especially tandem MS) has largely superseded Edman degradation for de novo sequencing, though Edman sequencing is still used to confirm N-terminal sequences and in regulated industrial settings.
