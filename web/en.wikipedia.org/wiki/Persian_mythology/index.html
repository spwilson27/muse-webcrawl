Persian mythology

Persian mythology (or Iranian mythology) is the body of myths originally told by the ancient Persians and other Iranian peoples. Rooted in the Zoroastrian religion and in pre-Zoroastrian Iranian folk belief, it is preserved above all in the Avesta and in later Persian literature, most famously Ferdowsi's epic Shahnameh (Book of Kings).

Cosmology

Iranian myth is structured by a cosmic dualism between asha — truth, order, and light — and druj, the lie. Ahura Mazda, the wise lord, stands at the head of the forces of good, opposed by Angra Mainyu (Ahriman), the destructive spirit. Between them stand divine beings (yazatas) such as Mithra, god of covenant and light, and Anahita, goddess of waters, as well as demonic daevas, whom the faithful must resist through good thoughts, good words, and good deeds.

Heroes and creatures

The Shahnameh recounts the legendary kings of Iran — Jamshid, Fereydun, and the doomed prince Siyavash — and the hero Rostam, whose feats, including his seven labours and his unwitting killing of his son Sohrab, form the tragic heart of the epic. Mythical beings include the Simurgh, the great benevolent bird that raised the hero Zal; the Huma bird of fortune; peri (fairy-like beings); and divs, ogre-like demons.

Legacy

Persian mythology shaped festivals still celebrated today, such as Nowruz, the New Year at the spring equinox, and influenced the mythologies and literatures of neighbouring cultures across Central and South Asia.
