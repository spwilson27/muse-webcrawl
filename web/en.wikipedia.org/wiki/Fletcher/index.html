Fletcher

This article is about the occupational surname and the arrow-maker's craft. For other uses, see Fletcher (disambiguation).

OccupationMaking arrows and fitting fletchings
EtymologyOld French flechier, from flèche, arrow

A fletcher is a person who makes arrows — specifically, one who attaches the fletchings (the feathers or vanes that stabilise an arrow in flight) to the shaft. The word comes from the Old French flechier, derived from flèche ("arrow"), and has given rise to the common English-language surname Fletcher, borne historically by families whose ancestors practised the trade.

The craft

Traditionally the fletcher worked alongside the bowyer (bow-maker) and the arrowsmith (who forged heads). Fletchings were usually goose or swan feathers, split, trimmed, and glued and bound to the shaft with sinew or thread, either straight or with a slight helical twist to impart spin. English law once protected the trade: statutes under Henry VIII regulated fletchers' wages and required goose feathers to be reserved for arrow-making, reflecting the military importance of the longbow.

The surname

As an occupational surname, Fletcher became widespread in England and Scotland and travelled with emigration worldwide. Notable bearers include the playwright John Fletcher (1579–1625), collaborator of Francis Beaumont and successor to Shakespeare as house playwright of the King's Men; the poet Giles Fletcher; and the nineteenth-century American physicist's son Harvey Fletcher, a pioneer of audiology and stereophonic sound after whom the Fletcher–Munson equal-loudness curves are partly named.

Other uses

"Fletcher" names many places (including Fletcher, North Carolina and Fletcher, Vermont), the Fletcher-class destroyers of the U.S. Navy — the largest class of destroyers ever built, named for Admiral Frank Friday Fletcher — and the Fletcher School of Law and Diplomacy at Tufts University, founded with a bequest from Austin Barclay Fletcher.
