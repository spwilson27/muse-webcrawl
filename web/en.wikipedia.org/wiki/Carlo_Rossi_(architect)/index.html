Carlo di Giovanni Rossi (Russian: Карл Иванович Росси; 29 December 1775 – 18 April 1849) was an Italian architect who worked in Imperial Russia. He is regarded, alongside Andreyan Zakharov, as one of the chief masters of the late Neoclassical or Empire style in Saint Petersburg, where his monumental ensembles reshaped the face of the imperial capital.

Early life

Rossi was born in Naples and came to Russia as a child with his mother, a ballerina, and his stepfather, the ballet choreographer Charles Le Picq. He trained in the studio of the architect Vincenzo Brenna, assistant to Giacomo Quarenghi, and traveled to Italy in 1802–1803 to complete his studies. On his return he worked in Moscow, where he rebuilt parts of the Kremlin after the fire of 1812, and in Tver.

Works in Saint Petersburg

Summoned to Saint Petersburg by Alexander I, Rossi designed a series of grand urban ensembles that gave the city much of its present imperial character:

  • The Yelagin Palace (1818–1822) for the Dowager Empress Maria Feodorovna.
  • The Mikhailovsky Palace (1819–1825), now the main building of the Russian Museum, with its adjoining square and street layout.
  • The General Staff Building (1819–1829) on Palace Square, with its celebrated triumphal arch topped by a chariot of Victory, commemorating Russia's triumph over Napoleon.
  • The ensemble of Arts Square and the Alexandrinsky Theatre (1828–1832), fronted by Rossi Street, famous for its perfect classical proportions.
  • The buildings of the Senate and Synod (1829–1834), linked by an arch across Galernaya Street.

Later life

Rossi's relations with the bureaucracy of Nicholas I deteriorated, and after the cholera riots of 1831 he was dismissed in 1832. He died in poverty in 1849 and was buried in the Volkovo Lutheran cemetery; his remains were later transferred to the Alexander Nevsky Lavra. The scale and unity of his planning—designing whole streets and squares as single compositions—made him the last great architect of imperial Saint Petersburg.
