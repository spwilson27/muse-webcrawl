Medieval Latin

Medieval Latin was the form of Latin used in Roman Catholic Western and Central Europe from the ninth century until the Renaissance. It was used as a language of scholarship and as the liturgical language of the Latin Church, as well as the language of science, literature, law, and administration.

Development

Medieval Latin diverged from Classical Latin in vocabulary, syntax, and style: it absorbed words from vernacular languages, Greek, and Hebrew, coined terms for new institutions and technologies, and often simplified or regularized classical grammar while also developing elaborate rhetorical styles in scholastic prose. Because Latin was a learned second language for its writers, styles ranged from the polished verse of the goliards and hymns of the Church to the highly technical Latin of scholastic philosophy and theology. Major authors include Boethius (transitional), Bede, Alcuin, Hildegard of Bingen, and Thomas Aquinas.

Influence

Medieval Latin decisively shaped the vocabularies of the Romance languages and English (especially legal, medical, and scientific terminology). During the Renaissance, humanists such as Lorenzo Valla and Erasmus criticized medieval usage and sought to restore classical norms, producing the Neo-Latin of early modern scholarship.
