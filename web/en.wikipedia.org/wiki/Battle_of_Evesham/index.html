The Battle of Evesham was fought on 4 August 1265 in the Second Barons' War, in the rain outside Evesham in Worcestershire. The royalist army led by the Lord Edward (the future Edward I) destroyed the smaller force of Simon de Montfort, Earl of Leicester, who was killed in the fighting. The battle ended organized baronial resistance to Henry III.

Background

De Montfort, victor at Lewes in 1264, had lost support among the barons, and his Welsh allies were slow to join him. Edward, having escaped captivity, marched with Gilbert de Clare and captured de Montfort's son's army at Kenilworth, seizing its banners. Advancing on Evesham, Edward trapped de Montfort in the loop of the River Avon.

Battle

Seeing Edward's forces deploy before him — and at first mistaking the captured Kenilworth banners for his son's reinforcements — de Montfort recognized the trap and reportedly resolved to die fighting. A thunderstorm broke over the field as the royalists attacked. De Montfort's army, hemmed in by the river, was slaughtered; chroniclers described the killing as a "murder of Evesham, for battle it was none." Simon de Montfort, his son Henry, and many of his leading supporters were killed, and his body was mutilated.

Aftermath

The victory restored royal authority, though resistance lingered at Kenilworth and the Isle of Ely until 1267. De Montfort was nevertheless venerated as a popular saint, and the parliament he had summoned in 1265, including burgesses, left a lasting constitutional legacy.
