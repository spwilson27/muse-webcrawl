Lake Cahuilla was a large prehistoric lake that repeatedly filled the Salton Trough in Southern California when the Colorado River diverted northwestward into the basin instead of flowing south to the Gulf of California. At its maximum the lake covered an area many times larger than the modern Salton Sea, which occupies only the lowest part of the ancient lakebed.

Formation and cycles

The Salton Trough is a below-sea-level depression formed by tectonic extension along the San Andreas Fault system. Whenever the Colorado River's course shifted — typically by silting up its channels across its delta — the entire river flowed into the trough for centuries at a time, filling Lake Cahuilla to a shoreline roughly 12 metres above sea level, marked today by travertine deposits, beach ridges, and bathtub-ring-like shorelines visible on the surrounding hillsides. When the river cut back to the Gulf, the lake evaporated over decades. The lake filled and dried many times; its most recent high stand ended only a few centuries before European contact, within the era of Indigenous occupation.

Human history

The lakeshore supported rich fisheries and attracted the Cahuilla people and other Indigenous groups, whose oral traditions, fish traps, campsites, and trails along the ancient shoreline remain important archaeological and cultural resources. The modern Salton Sea, created accidentally in 1905–1907 when Colorado River floodwaters breached an irrigation canal, briefly recreated a small fraction of the ancient lake.
