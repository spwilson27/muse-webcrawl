Paraneoplastic syndrome

Paraneoplastic syndrome
SpecialtyOncology, neurology, endocrinology
DefinitionSigns and symptoms caused by a cancer elsewhere in the body, not by the tumour's local presence or metastases
MechanismsTumour-secreted hormones/peptides; autoimmune cross-reactivity (onconeural antibodies)
Commonly associated cancersSmall-cell lung cancer, breast and ovarian cancer, lymphomas, thymoma
[Image placeholder] Paraneoplastic effects are remote consequences of a malignancy.

A paraneoplastic syndrome is a group of uncommon disorders that develop in people with cancer, arising when the disease provokes effects in parts of the body remote from the tumour itself — through hormones or cytokines secreted by tumour cells, or through an immune response directed against the tumour that cross-reacts with normal tissues, especially the nervous system. Paraneoplastic syndromes may precede the diagnosis of the underlying cancer and can be its first manifestation.

Classification by system

  • Endocrine: syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH, classically with small-cell lung cancer); humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy (PTHrP secretion); Cushing syndrome from ectopic ACTH; hypoglycaemia from IGF-2 ("Doege–Potter syndrome").
  • Neurological (paraneoplastic neurological syndromes, often antibody-associated): Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome; paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration; limbic encephalitis; opsoclonus-myoclonus; sensory neuronopathy. Characteristic "onconeural" antibodies include anti-Hu, anti-Yo, anti-Ri, anti-Ma2 and anti-CV2/CRMP5.
  • Mucocutaneous: acanthosis nigricans, dermatomyositis, Leser-Trélat sign, paraneoplastic pemphigus.
  • Haematological and others: Trousseau syndrome (migratory thrombophlebitis), erythrocytosis from renal tumours, membranous nephropathy, hypertrophic osteoarthropathy and clubbing.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis combines the clinical picture with detection of specific autoantibodies in serum and CSF, imaging and biopsy to find the occult tumour, and exclusion of metastatic or treatment-related causes. Treatment is directed first at the tumour itself; neurological syndromes additionally may respond to immunosuppression (corticosteroids, IVIG, plasma exchange, rituximab), although antibody-mediated neuronal loss often leaves fixed deficits, making early tumour treatment critical.
