Abbasid Caliphate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The round city of Baghdad as planned by al-Mansur, in a later artist's reconstruction]

The Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258) was the third Islamic caliphate, founded by descendants of Muhammad's uncle al-Abbas after their revolution overthrew the Umayyads. Ruling from Baghdad — founded by al-Mansur in 762 and for centuries among the largest and most learned cities on earth — the Abbasids presided over the Islamic Golden Age of science, philosophy, medicine and literature. Real power drained to provincial dynasties, Turkish guards, Buyid and Seljuk protectors, yet the caliphs remained Islam's symbolic heads until Hulagu's Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258.

Golden Age

Harun al-Rashid (r. 786–809), of Arabian Nights fame, and his son al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833) patronised the translation movement that rendered Greek, Persian and Indian learning into Arabic, and the House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma) in Baghdad. In this milieu al-Khwarizmi founded algebra, Ibn al-Haytham pioneered optics, al-Razi and Ibn Sina transformed medicine, and Persian bureaucrats reshaped Islamic government. Trade linked Baghdad to China, India, Africa and Europe.

Decline and fall

From the mid-9th century the caliphs fell under the control of their Turkish slave-soldiers at Samarra, then of the Persian Buyids (945) and Turkish Seljuks (1055), while provinces — Egypt, Spain, Khurasan — seceded under local dynasties. A partial caliphal revival under al-Nasir (r. 1180–1225) could not restore the empire. In February 1258 Hulagu's Mongols stormed Baghdad, killed the last caliph al-Musta'sim and, in the traditional accounts, massacred much of the city; a shadow Abbasid caliphate continued in Mamluk Cairo until 1517.
