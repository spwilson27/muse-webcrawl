Scotch-Irish Americans are American descendants of Ulster Protestants who emigrated from Ulster in northern Ireland to America during the 18th and 19th centuries. Their ancestors had originally migrated to Ireland, mainly from the Scottish Lowlands and Northern England, during the Plantation of Ulster in the 17th century. In the United States, "Scotch-Irish" is used primarily to refer to these 18th-century emigrants and their descendants, while in the British Isles the group is usually called Ulster Scots.

Migration

Between roughly 1717 and 1775, an estimated 200,000 or more Ulster Protestants emigrated to British North America, driven by rising rents, religious disabilities under the penal laws (as Presbyterians they were dissenters from the established Church of Ireland), and economic hardship. They entered chiefly through Philadelphia and New Castle, Delaware, and spread into the Pennsylvania backcountry, the Shenandoah Valley, the Carolinas and eventually across the Appalachian frontier. Further waves followed in the 19th century, though the term is less often applied to later arrivals.

Culture and influence

Scotch-Irish settlers were prominent on the American frontier and in the Revolutionary War. Their cultural legacy is strongly associated with Appalachia: folk music traditions that fed into country and bluegrass, distinctive dialects, Presbyterian (and later evangelical) religious life, and a reputation — much debated by historians — for individualism and clan loyalty, explored in David Hackett Fischer's Albion's Seed under the "borderers" folkway. A number of U.S. presidents, including Andrew Jackson, James K. Polk, James Buchanan, Chester A. Arthur, William McKinley and Woodrow Wilson, had Scotch-Irish ancestry.

Terminology

The term "Scotch-Irish" gained currency in 19th-century America, partly to distinguish earlier Protestant immigrants from the largely Catholic Irish who arrived during and after the Great Famine. Some consider "Scots-Irish" the more correct form; "Scotch" as an adjective for people is now generally avoided in Scotland itself.
