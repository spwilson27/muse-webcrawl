Cell junction

Cell junctions are specialised structures in the plasma membranes of animal cells that connect cells to one another or to the extracellular matrix, enabling tissues to function as coordinated units. They are most abundant in epithelial tissues and combine mechanical attachment with intercellular communication.

Types

Tight junctions (zonula occludens) seal adjacent cells together, preventing leakage of molecules between them and maintaining cell polarity; their principal proteins are claudins and occludin. Adherens junctions link the actin cytoskeletons of neighbouring cells via cadherin proteins. Desmosomes provide strong spot adhesion resisting mechanical stress, anchoring intermediate filaments. Gap junctions are channels of connexin proteins that allow ions and small molecules to pass directly between cells, coordinating activity in tissues such as cardiac muscle. Hemidesmosomes anchor cells to the basement membrane through integrins.

Function and disease

Defects in junction proteins underlie diseases including blistering skin disorders (desmosomal mutations), deafness (connexin mutations) and some cancers, in which loss of cell adhesion contributes to invasion and metastasis.
