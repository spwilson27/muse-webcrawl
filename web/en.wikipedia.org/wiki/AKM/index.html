The AKM (Russian: Автомат Калашникова модернизированный, "Kalashnikov's Automatic Rifle Modernised") is a 7.62×39mm assault rifle designed by Mikhail Kalashnikov and adopted by the Soviet Union in 1959 as the standard service rifle, replacing the original AK-47. Thanks to simplified mass-production methods — above all a stamped sheet-metal receiver — the AKM is the most widely produced and most commonly encountered member of the Kalashnikov family, and what most people picture when they say "AK-47".

Design

The AKM retains the AK's long-stroke gas piston, rotating bolt and generous internal clearances, which give the family its famous tolerance of dirt and neglect. The chief innovation was manufacturing the receiver by stamping and riveting sheet steel rather than milling it from a forging, cutting weight to about 3.1 kg (6.8 lb) unloaded and greatly reducing cost. Other changes include a rate-reducing mechanism in the trigger group, a slightly raised stock to reduce muzzle climb, a slanted muzzle compensator, lighter magazines (first steel, then distinctive orange-brown Bakelite and later polymer), and redesigned bayonet and furniture. The AKMS variant substitutes an under-folding metal stock for airborne and armoured crews.

Production and use

The Soviet Union distributed AKM tooling and licences widely: the rifle was produced across the Warsaw Pact and in China (as the Type 56 family), Egypt, India and many other countries, with total production of the AKM pattern estimated in the tens of millions. It armed Soviet and allied forces through the Cold War — including the Vietnam War era, the Soviet–Afghan War and countless proxy conflicts — and remains in frontline and reserve service worldwide in the 21st century. In Soviet service the AKM was itself succeeded from the late 1970s by the small-calibre AK-74, but the AKM's combination of the harder-hitting 7.62×39mm cartridge and extreme durability keeps it ubiquitous wherever Kalashnikovs are found.
