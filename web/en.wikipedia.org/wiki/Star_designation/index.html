Star designation

Star charts traditionally label bright stars with Bayer and Flamsteed designations.

A star designation is a name or code used in astronomy to identify a particular star. Over the centuries many different systems have been used, from traditional proper names to entries in large modern catalogues containing hundreds of millions of objects.

Proper names

The brightest and most prominent stars carry traditional proper names, many derived from Arabic (such as Betelgeuse, Rigel, and Aldebaran), Latin, or Greek. Since 2016, the International Astronomical Union, through its Working Group on Star Names, has been formally approving standard spellings of such names.

Bayer designation

In 1603, Johann Bayer published a system assigning a lowercase Greek letter, combined with the genitive form of the constellation name, to the stars of each constellation — generally in order of brightness. Examples include Alpha Centauri and Beta Orionis (Rigel). Later stars were given lowercase and uppercase Latin letters.

Flamsteed designation

The Flamsteed designation, based on the work of John Flamsteed, numbers stars within a constellation roughly in order of right ascension, as in 61 Cygni. It is commonly used for stars lacking a Bayer designation.

Variable star designations

Variable stars receive designations in the Argelander system, using letters (R, S, T ... Z, then RR–RZ, AA–AZ and so on) followed by the constellation name, unless they already hold a Bayer designation.

Catalogue numbers

Modern catalogues identify stars by number or coordinates: the Henry Draper Catalogue (HD), the Bright Star Catalogue (HR), the Hipparcos catalogue (HIP), and the huge survey catalogues from missions such as Gaia and the 2MASS survey.
