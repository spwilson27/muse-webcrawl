Charles Edward Spearman, FRS (10 September 1863 – 17 September 1945) was an English psychologist known for work in statistics, as a pioneer of factor analysis, and for Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. He is best known for proposing that human intelligence consists of a general factor, g, together with specific abilities (s), a theory that remains influential and controversial in the study of intelligence.

Life

Spearman spent fifteen years as an officer in the British Army before resigning to study experimental psychology under Wilhelm Wundt at Leipzig, where he took his PhD in 1904. He held posts at University College London from 1907, succeeding to the Grote Professorship of Mind and Logic, and taught there until his retirement in 1931.

Two-factor theory

In his 1904 paper "'General Intelligence,' Objectively Determined and Measured", Spearman observed that schoolchildren's grades across subjects were positively correlated, and argued that performance on any mental test reflects one general factor plus one factor specific to the test. The statistical technique he developed to extract this structure — an early form of factor analysis, including correction for attenuation — is still taught today. His The Abilities of Man (1927) set out the mature theory. Later psychologists, including L. L. Thurstone, challenged the primacy of g with multi-factor models, and Raymond Cattell and others subdivided g into fluid and crystallised abilities.

Legacy

Spearman's rank correlation coefficient ρ and his "prophecy formula" (the Spearman–Brown formula for test reliability) remain standard tools in psychometrics and statistics.
