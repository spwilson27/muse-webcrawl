Rape in English law is a statutory offence under section 1 of the Sexual Offences Act 2003, which governs the law of England and Wales. A person commits rape if he intentionally penetrates the vagina, anus or mouth of another person with his penis, the other person does not consent to the penetration, and the defendant does not reasonably believe that the other person consents. The offence is triable only on indictment and carries a maximum sentence of life imprisonment.

Definition and elements

The actus reus is penile penetration without consent; the mens rea is intention as to the penetration together with the absence of a reasonable belief in consent. Reasonableness is judged having regard to all the circumstances, including any steps the defendant took to ascertain consent. Section 74 defines consent as agreement by choice, with the freedom and capacity to make that choice, and sections 75 and 76 set out evidential and conclusive presumptions — for example where violence was used, or where the complainant was deceived as to the nature or purpose of the act or by impersonation of a person known to them.

History

Rape was historically a common-law felony, punishable by death until the 19th century. The former marital rape exemption — the idea that a husband could not rape his wife — was abolished by the House of Lords in R v R (1991). The Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act 1976 introduced complainant anonymity, and the 2003 Act widened the offence to include oral penetration and replaced the old recklessness standard (from DPP v Morgan) with the reasonable-belief test. Because of the statutory definition, only a person with a penis can commit rape as a principal, though others may be liable as accomplices; related offences include assault by penetration and causing sexual activity without consent.

Sentencing and procedure

Sentencing follows the Sentencing Council's definitive guideline for sexual offences, with starting points and ranges determined by harm and culpability; the maximum is life imprisonment. Trials take place in the Crown Court before a jury, and complainants in sexual offence cases have lifelong anonymity under the Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act 1992. Persistent concerns in public debate include low charging and conviction rates relative to reported offences, addressed in part by the government's end-to-end Rape Review of 2021.
