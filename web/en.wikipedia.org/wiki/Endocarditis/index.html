Endocarditis

Endocarditis is an inflammation of the inner layer of the heart, the endocardium. It usually involves the heart valves. Other structures that may be involved include the interventricular septum, the chordae tendineae, the mural endocardium, or the surfaces of intracardiac devices. Endocarditis is characterised by lesions known as vegetations, which are masses of platelets, fibrin and microcolonies of microorganisms, together with a variable infiltrate of inflammatory cells.

Vegetations on a valve leaflet are the hallmark lesion of infective endocarditis.

Classification

Endocarditis is broadly divided into infective and non-infective forms. Infective endocarditis is most often bacterial: Staphylococcus aureus is the leading cause in the developed world, followed by viridans-group streptococci and enterococci. Non-infective forms include nonbacterial thrombotic (marantic) endocarditis, associated with malignancy and hypercoagulable states, and the sterile valvular lesions of systemic lupus erythematosus (Libman–Sacks endocarditis).

Signs and symptoms

Classical features include persistent fever, a new or changed heart murmur, and immunological and vascular phenomena: splinter haemorrhages under the nails, painless Janeway lesions on the palms and soles, painful Osler nodes on the fingers, and Roth spots in the retina. Complications include heart failure from valve destruction, stroke and systemic embolism, and metastatic abscesses.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the modified Duke criteria, which combine blood culture results, echocardiographic findings (vegetation, abscess, new valvular regurgitation), and clinical features. Transoesophageal echocardiography is more sensitive than transthoracic imaging, particularly for prosthetic valves.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment requires prolonged (typically 4–6 weeks) high-dose intravenous antibiotics directed at the cultured organism. Surgery — valve repair or replacement — is indicated for heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or prevention of embolism. Even with modern care, in-hospital mortality remains around 15–20%, and risk is increased by prosthetic valves, intravenous drug use, and pre-existing valvular disease such as rheumatic heart disease.
