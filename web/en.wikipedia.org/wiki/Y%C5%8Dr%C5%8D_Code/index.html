The Yōrō Code (養老律令, Yōrō-ritsuryō) is one of the major law compilations of the ritsuryō system of ancient Japan. It was compiled in 718 CE, during the Yōrō era of the Nara period, as a revision of the earlier Taihō Code of 701–702. Although compiled in 718, it was not put into force until 757, under Emperor Kōnin's predecessor's administration, largely superseding the Taihō Code as the operative legal framework of the Japanese state.

History

The Yōrō Code was drafted under the direction of senior court officials, including Fujiwara no Fuhito, one of the principal architects of the ritsuryō state, who had also worked on the Taihō Code. The compilation adjusted and clarified provisions of its predecessor rather than introducing a wholly new system. Political circumstances delayed its implementation for nearly four decades; it was finally promulgated in 757 during the reign of Empress Kōken.

Contents

Like the Taihō Code, the Yōrō Code followed the Chinese Tang dynasty model and consisted of two parts: the ritsu (律), penal laws, and the ryō (令), administrative laws. The administrative code contained roughly 955 articles organized into 30 volumes, governing the structure of central government ministries, provincial administration, land allotment under the handen-shūju system, taxation, corvée labour, ranks and court protocol.

Survival and scholarship

The original text of the Yōrō Code does not survive intact. However, much of its content can be reconstructed from later commentaries, most importantly the Ryō no Shūge (c. 920), a compilation of official and private commentaries on the codes. Modern scholars have produced reconstructed editions that are widely used in the study of early Japanese law and administration.
