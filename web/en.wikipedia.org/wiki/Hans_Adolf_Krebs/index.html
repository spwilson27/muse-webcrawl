Hans Adolf Krebs

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sir Hans Adolf Krebs FRS (25 August 1900 – 22 November 1981) was a German-born British biochemist and physician. He is best known for identifying the urea cycle (1932) and the citric acid cycle (1937), the latter widely known as the Krebs cycle, for which he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1953.

Life

Krebs was born in Hildesheim, Germany, the son of an ear, nose and throat surgeon. He studied medicine at Göttingen, Freiburg, Munich, Berlin and Hamburg, and worked in Berlin with the Nobel laureate Otto Warburg, learning the manometric techniques he would use throughout his career.

Being Jewish, Krebs was dismissed from his post at Freiburg in 1933 after the Nazi rise to power. He emigrated to England, working first at Cambridge and then at the University of Sheffield, where he discovered the citric acid cycle in 1937 using pigeon breast muscle. His paper announcing the cycle was famously rejected by the journal Nature and published instead in Enzymologia. In 1954 he moved to Oxford as Whitley Professor of Biochemistry, remaining there until his retirement.

Discoveries

With Kurt Henseleit, Krebs elucidated the urea cycle in 1932, the first metabolic cycle to be described. The citric acid cycle, worked out with William Johnson, explained how cells oxidise foodstuffs to produce energy, and is a cornerstone of biochemistry. He later described the glyoxylate cycle with Hans Kornberg. Krebs received the Nobel Prize in 1953, sharing it with Fritz Lipmann, and was knighted in 1958.
