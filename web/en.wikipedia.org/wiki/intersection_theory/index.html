Intersection theory

Intersection theory is a branch of algebraic geometry and algebraic topology that studies intersections of algebraic varieties and subvarieties, assigning to them algebraic objects — such as Chow groups and intersection products — that count intersections with multiplicities in a deformation-invariant way.

Overview

The guiding problem is enumerative: for example, how many lines meet four given lines in space, or the classical result that there are 27 lines on a smooth cubic surface. When subvarieties intersect improperly or with excess dimension, rational equivalence and moving lemmas are used to define a well-behaved intersection product.

Chow ring

For a smooth variety, the Chow ring A*(X) organizes algebraic cycles modulo rational equivalence, with the intersection product as multiplication. Bézout's theorem, computing the number of intersection points of plane curves from their degrees, is a foundational special case.

Modern developments

William Fulton's Intersection Theory (1984) systematized the subject. Gromov–Witten invariants, which count curves on varieties, have made intersection theory on moduli spaces central to modern enumerative geometry and string theory.
