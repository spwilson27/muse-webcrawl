English Gothic architecture

English Gothic is an architectural style that flourished in England from about 1180 to about 1520, succeeding Norman (Romanesque) architecture and preceding Tudor and Renaissance styles. Its defining features are the pointed arch, the ribbed vault, flying buttresses, large windows with elaborate tracery, and a characteristic emphasis — especially compared with French Gothic — on long, low proportions and intricate surface decoration. Traditionally the style is divided into three periods: Early English (c. 1180–1275), Decorated (c. 1275–1380) and Perpendicular (c. 1380–1520), the last being unique to England.

Salisbury Cathedral, a landmark of the Early English style, was built largely in one campaign (1220–1258).

Early English (c. 1180–1275)

The style appeared in the rebuilding of Canterbury Cathedral's choir after the fire of 1174, under the French master mason William of Sens, and in William the Englishman's continuation. Hallmarks are tall, narrow lancet windows grouped in twos and threes, minimal ornament, clustered shafts of dark Purbeck marble, and pointed arches throughout. Salisbury Cathedral (begun 1220) is the purest example, notable for being built almost entirely in one style; Wells Cathedral pioneered the "scissor arches" inserted in 1338–48 to support its crossing tower.

Decorated (c. 1275–1380)

The Decorated period is characterised by elaborate window tracery: first geometric patterns of circles and trefoils (as in the chapter house of York Minster), then flowing, curvilinear designs (the west window of York, the "Heart of Yorkshire"). Vaulting grew more complex with additional lierne ribs forming star patterns, and sculpture, ballflower ornament and ogee arches proliferated. Exeter Cathedral, rebuilt from 1275, possesses the longest continuous medieval stone vault in the world. Ely Cathedral's octagonal lantern tower (1322–40), built after the central tower collapsed, is a masterpiece of engineered timber and stone.

Perpendicular (c. 1380–1520)

Perpendicular Gothic, which began with the remodelling of Gloucester Cathedral's choir (c. 1335–77), emphasised vertical lines: windows became enormous grids of vertical mullions and transoms, walls turned into screens of glass (as in the choir of York and Gloucester's east window, the largest medieval window in Britain), and the fan vault was perfected — in Gloucester's cloisters and supremely in King's College Chapel, Cambridge (vault completed 1512–15) and Henry VII's Chapel at Westminster Abbey. The style coincided with the great age of parish church building funded by wool wealth, particularly in East Anglia and the Cotswolds.

Legacy

English Gothic was revived in the nineteenth-century Gothic Revival, which produced the Palace of Westminster (Charles Barry and A. W. N. Pugin, from 1840) and thousands of churches, universities and railway stations across Britain and its empire.
