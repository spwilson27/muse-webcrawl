Sea power

Sea power is a nation's ability to project power by sea, encompassing naval forces, merchant shipping, maritime trade, shipbuilding, ports and the geographic and economic factors that sustain them. The concept was given its classic formulation by the American naval officer and historian Alfred Thayer Mahan in The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783 (1890).

A fleet as an instrument of national sea power.

Mahan's theory

Mahan argued that great national prosperity and security depend on command of the sea: control of maritime trade routes in peace and the ability to deny them to an enemy in war, secured by concentrated battle fleets seeking decisive engagement. He identified conditions of sea power including geographical position, physical conformation of coasts, extent of territory, population, national character and government policy. His work influenced naval expansion in the United States, Britain, Germany and Japan.

Critiques and development

Julian Corbett qualified Mahan by stressing that command of the sea is normally relative and local, and serves the control of communications rather than being an end in itself. In the twentieth century sea power expanded to include submarines, naval aviation, amphibious forces and sea-based nuclear deterrents; in the twenty-first, it also involves undersea infrastructure, maritime law enforcement and the protection of global supply chains.
