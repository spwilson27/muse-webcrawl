Thai language

Thai (ภาษาไทย, phasa thai)
Native to: Thailand
Speakers: tens of millions (native and second-language)
Family: Kra–Dai → Tai → Southwestern Tai
Script: Thai script
Official status: Official language of Thailand

Thai, historically also known as Siamese, is a Tai language of the Kra–Dai language family, spoken natively by the Thai people and as a lingua franca throughout Thailand. It is the sole official language of Thailand and the main language of education and government. Central Thai, based on the speech of Bangkok and the central plains, forms the standard. Thai is closely related to Lao, and speakers of the two languages have a substantial degree of mutual intelligibility.

Classification and history

Thai belongs to the Southwestern branch of the Tai languages. The Thai script, an abugida derived via Old Khmer from the Brahmic scripts of India, is traditionally dated to 1283 and attributed to King Ramkhamhaeng the Great of Sukhothai, whose famous inscription is among the earliest Thai texts. Over the centuries the language absorbed large numbers of loanwords from Sanskrit and Pali (via Khmer and through Buddhism), as well as from Chinese, Portuguese, Persian, and, in the modern era, English.

Phonology

Standard Thai is a tonal language with five tones: mid, low, falling, high, and rising. Its syllable structure is relatively simple, with twenty consonant phonemes in initial position, nine pure vowels contrasting in length, and three diphthongs. Unstressed syllables tend toward a reduced set of forms.

Grammar

Thai is an analytic language: words do not inflect for tense, number, or case. Grammatical relations are conveyed by word order (subject–verb–object) and particles. Nouns are used with classifiers when counted, verbs are modified by aspect and modal particles, and an elaborate system of pronouns and sentence-final particles encodes politeness, social status, and the speaker's attitude. Special vocabularies, including royal language (rachasap), are used in formal and ceremonial contexts.

Writing system

The Thai script has 44 consonant letters, numerous vowel signs written around the consonant, and four tone marks. Words are written without spaces between them; spaces instead separate clauses or phrases. Because the script's spelling reflects older pronunciations and etymology, many letters represent the same modern sound, particularly among loanwords from Indic languages.
