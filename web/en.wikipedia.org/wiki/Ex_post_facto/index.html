Ex post facto law

An ex post facto law (Latin for "from a thing done afterward") is a law that retroactively changes the legal consequences of actions that were committed, or relationships that existed, before the enactment of the law. In criminal law, it may criminalise actions that were legal when committed, or it may increase the punishment for a crime after it was committed. Such laws are widely considered contrary to the rule of law.

United States

The United States Constitution forbids Congress and the states from passing ex post facto laws, in Article I, Sections 9 and 10. In Calder v. Bull (1798), Justice Samuel Chase identified four categories of law within the prohibition: laws criminalising previously innocent acts, laws aggravating a crime beyond its earlier classification, laws increasing the punishment, and laws altering the rules of evidence to convict on lesser proof. Later cases, such as Stogner v. California (2003), held that extending a statute of limitations retroactively for already-expired periods can also violate the clause.

The prohibition applies to criminal and penal measures, not to civil legislation generally, though laws that are punitive in effect may be treated as criminal for this purpose.

International law

The principle nulla poena sine lege ("no punishment without law") is recognised in the European Convention on Human Rights (Article 7), the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Article 15), and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 11). Many national constitutions contain equivalent bans.
