Non-imaging optics

Non-imaging optics is the branch of optics concerned with the optimal transfer of light radiation between a source and a target, without regard to forming an image of the source. Where imaging optics (as in cameras and telescopes) seeks to preserve spatial information, non-imaging optics seeks to maximize the amount of light delivered, or to shape its distribution, subject to the limits imposed by thermodynamics and the conservation of étendue.

Principles

The fundamental limit on concentration is set by the second law of thermodynamics: no passive optical system can make a target hotter, in radiative terms, than the source. For a concentrator accepting light within an angle θ, the maximum concentration ratio in three dimensions is proportional to 1/sin²θ. Design methods include the edge-ray principle, in which rays from the edge of the source are directed to the edge of the target, and flow-line (string) methods.

The classic element of the field is the compound parabolic concentrator (CPC), developed in the 1960s and 1970s by Roland Winston and others. Non-imaging designs use tailored freeform reflectors and refractors rather than conic sections.

Applications

Non-imaging optics is used in solar energy concentrators, LED illumination and headlamps, light guides and fibre couplers, infrared detectors, and architectural daylighting. Its design techniques are also central to modern luminaire design, where precise control of light distribution improves efficiency and visual comfort.
