The Shroud of Turin (Italian: Sindone di Torino), also known as the Holy Shroud, is a length of linen cloth that bears a faint negative image of a man. Some Christians believe it to be the burial shroud of Jesus of Nazareth, into which the image was miraculously imprinted at the Resurrection. The shroud has been kept in Turin, Italy, since 1578 and is now housed in the Cathedral of Saint John the Baptist.

History

The shroud's documented history begins in the 1350s at Lirey, France, where it was exhibited by the family of the knight Geoffroi de Charny. A local bishop, Pierre d'Arcis, later wrote that his predecessor had investigated and declared it a painted forgery whose artist had confessed. The cloth passed to the House of Savoy in 1453 and survived a fire in 1532 that left scorch marks and patches. In 1898, amateur photographer Secondo Pia took the first photographs of the shroud and discovered that the image on the cloth behaves like a photographic negative, dramatically increasing interest in it.

Scientific examination

In 1978 the Shroud of Turin Research Project (STURP) examined the cloth and concluded that the image was not the product of paint or pigment, though how it was formed remained unexplained. In 1988, radiocarbon dating by three laboratories (Oxford, Zurich, and the University of Arizona) dated samples of the cloth to between 1260 and 1390 AD, with 95% confidence — consistent with the medieval documentary record. Supporters of authenticity have challenged the dating, arguing that the sample came from a medieval repair area or was contaminated, while mainstream scientific consensus treats the shroud as a medieval artefact. The Catholic Church neither officially endorses nor rejects its authenticity, presenting it as an object of devotion.

Image characteristics

The image depicts the front and back of a naked man with his hands crossed, with markings consistent with scourging, puncture wounds around the head, a wound in the side, and bloodstains. Debates continue over the image-formation mechanism, the blood claims, the pollen and coin evidence, and proposed earlier histories identifying the shroud with the Image of Edessa.
