The siege of Acre of 1799 was an unsuccessful French siege of the Ottoman city of Acre (now in Israel), the turning point of Napoleon Bonaparte's invasion of Egypt and Syria during the French Revolutionary Wars.

Background

After victories in Egypt and at Jaffa, Napoleon advanced into Syria hoping to break Ottoman power and threaten British interests. Acre was defended by the Ottoman governor Jezzar Pasha, supported by a British naval squadron under Commodore Sir Sidney Smith, which had captured Napoleon's siege artillery at sea.

The siege

For two months the French repeatedly assaulted the city's medieval walls but were repelled by the garrison, reinforced by British sailors and marines and aided by the French émigré officer Phélippeaux. Plague weakened the French army, and on the night of 20–21 May 1799 Napoleon abandoned the siege and retreated to Egypt.

Legacy

The failure ended Napoleon's ambitions in the Levant. He is reputed to have said of Acre that had he taken it, he would have changed the face of the world.
