Magnetic confinement fusion

Magnetic confinement fusion (MCF) is an approach to generating thermonuclear fusion power in which a hot, ionised fuel — a plasma of hydrogen isotopes, usually deuterium and tritium — is confined by magnetic fields in a vacuum chamber, because no material container can withstand the required temperatures of over 100 million degrees Celsius. It is the leading approach to practical fusion energy, embodied in the tokamak and stellarator designs.

Principle

Charged particles spiral around magnetic field lines, so a suitably shaped field can hold plasma away from the vessel walls. In a tokamak, a doughnut-shaped (toroidal) field is combined with a poloidal field generated by a strong electric current driven through the plasma itself, twisting the field lines into a nested set of helical surfaces. A stellarator achieves the twist with complex external coils alone, avoiding the need for plasma current but at the price of intricate engineering. Plasma is heated by ohmic heating, neutral beam injection and radio-frequency waves.

Key challenges and milestones

Success requires simultaneously achieving high temperature, density and confinement time — the "triple product" of the Lawson criterion — while controlling instabilities, turbulence-driven transport and disruptions, and surviving intense neutron bombardment of the vessel walls. The Joint European Torus (JET) set records for fusion power and energy, reaching a fusion energy gain near Q ≈ 0.67 transiently in 1997 and producing a record 69 megajoules of fusion energy in a 2023 shot. The international ITER project in France aims to produce 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of heating (Q = 10) and demonstrate the feasibility of a power-plant-scale burning plasma.
