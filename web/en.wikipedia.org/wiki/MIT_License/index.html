The MIT License is a permissive free software license originating at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) in the late 1980s. As a permissive license, it puts only very limited restriction on reuse and has, therefore, high license compatibility. Unlike copyleft licenses, the MIT License permits reuse within proprietary software; the only conditions are that the copyright notice and license text be included in copies or substantial portions of the software.

Origin and naming

The license derives from the notice applied to software released by MIT, including parts of the X Window System (hence the variant known as the X11 License). The Free Software Foundation distinguishes between the MIT License and the X11 license, and also uses the clarifying term "Expat License" for the version catalogued in the Expat project's archive, to avoid ambiguity among similar short licenses MIT has used. The Open Source Initiative lists the text simply as the MIT License.

Use

The MIT License is among the most popular software licenses in the world. It ranked first in GitHub's analysis of licensed repositories and is used by prominent projects including the jQuery, Rails, Node.js ecosystem packages, Angular, and Visual Studio Code's open-source codebase. Its brevity and permissiveness make it a default choice for libraries and frameworks intended for the widest possible adoption.

Comparison

The MIT License is similar to the 2-clause BSD license, differing mainly in that BSD-derived texts contain an explicit non-endorsement clause in their 3-clause form and phrase the notice condition differently. Because it imposes no patent or copyleft obligations, MIT-licensed code can be combined with code under the GNU GPL, and incorporated into closed-source commercial products without releasing source.
