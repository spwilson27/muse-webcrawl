A heritage railway or heritage railroad (in the United States) is a railway operated as living history — a preserved line, run to maintain and demonstrate historic railway equipment, stations and operating practices, usually for tourists and enthusiasts. Most are former commercial lines closed to regular traffic and later re-opened by preservation societies, staffed largely by volunteers.

History

Railway preservation began almost as soon as railways themselves, but organised preservation of whole lines dates from 1951, when enthusiasts took over the narrow-gauge Talyllyn Railway in Wales — the first railway in the world to be preserved and operated by volunteers. The idea spread as mass closures of rural lines in the 1950s and 1960s (in Britain, the Beeching cuts) made lines and steam locomotives available. The Bluebell Railway in Sussex, reopened in 1960, was the first preserved standard-gauge line in Britain.

Operations

Heritage railways typically operate steam, and often vintage diesel, trains over relatively short routes, with period stations, signalling and uniforms. Income comes from fares, special events (such as "Santa special" trains and dining services), film and television work, and membership schemes. Many lines have been extended in stages and some have reconnected to the national network. Safety regulation applies as on any passenger railway, with oversight in the UK by the Office of Rail and Road.

Around the world

The movement is strongest in the United Kingdom, which has well over 100 heritage lines including the Severn Valley Railway, the North Yorkshire Moors Railway and the narrow-gauge Ffestiniog and Welsh Highland railways. Significant heritage railways also exist across Europe, North America (where they are usually called tourist railroads), Australia, India and elsewhere.
