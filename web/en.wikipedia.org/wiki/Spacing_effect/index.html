Spacing effect

The spacing effect demonstrates that learning is more effective when study sessions are spaced out over time, as opposed to studying the same amount of content in a single session ("cramming"). This phenomenon, also known as distributed practice, was first described quantitatively by Hermann Ebbinghaus in his 1885 work Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology, in which he also documented the forgetting curve. The effect applies to a wide range of material — vocabulary, facts, concepts and motor skills — and across many species and ages.

Research

The spacing effect is one of the most robust findings in learning research. A 2006 meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues reviewed 254 studies and confirmed that distributed practice improves retention across tasks and retention intervals, with the optimal gap between sessions depending on how long the material must be remembered: longer target intervals call for longer spacing gaps. The phenomenon underlies modern spaced repetition software such as SuperMemo (using Piotr Woźniak's algorithms) and Anki, which schedule reviews of flashcards at expanding intervals just before the items would be forgotten.

Proposed mechanisms

Several explanations have been advanced. Deficient processing theories propose that massed repetitions receive less attention and encoding effort. Encoding variability theories hold that spaced presentations occur in more varied contexts, creating multiple retrieval routes. Study-phase retrieval theories argue that a spaced second presentation prompts effortful retrieval of the first, strengthening the memory trace; this links the spacing effect to the testing effect and to the idea of desirable difficulties introduced by Robert Bjork.

Applications

Distributed practice informs curriculum design (spiralling topics over a course), language-learning applications, medical education, and corporate training. It consistently outperforms massed practice for long-term retention, though cramming can be competitive for tests administered immediately afterwards.
