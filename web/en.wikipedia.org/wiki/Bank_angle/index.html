Bank angle

The bank angle is the angle at which a vehicle is inclined about its longitudinal (roll) axis with respect to the horizontal. In aviation, when an aircraft turns, it banks by rolling so that its wings tilt; the lift vector then has both a vertical component, supporting the aircraft's weight, and a horizontal component, which provides the centripetal force that changes the direction of flight. Bank angle is measured in degrees and is shown to the pilot on the attitude indicator and turn coordinator.

Flight mechanics

In a coordinated, level turn the required bank angle depends on the true airspeed and the desired rate of turn: faster flight or a tighter turn demands a steeper bank. Increasing bank increases the load factor — the ratio of lift to weight — which rises as the reciprocal of the cosine of the bank angle; at 60° of bank the load factor is 2 g, and the stall speed rises accordingly. A "standard rate turn" (3° per second) at typical general-aviation speeds requires roughly 15–20° of bank. Airliners usually limit bank to about 25–30° for passenger comfort, and most autopilots and flight-envelope protections impose similar limits.

Ground vehicles

The term is also used for motorcycles and bicycles, where the rider leans the machine into a turn, and in road and railway engineering, where the banking of a curve — called cant or superelevation — tilts the surface to counteract lateral forces.
