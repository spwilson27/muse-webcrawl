John Steinbeck

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: John Steinbeck in 1939, the year of The Grapes of Wrath: the novelist in work shirt and jacket]

John Ernst Steinbeck (27 February 1902 – 20 December 1968) was an American writer and Nobel Prize laureate (1962), the great chronicler of California's working poor. In direct, compassionate prose he wrote of migrant farmworkers, cannery hands and the dispossessed of the Depression, and his books have sold tens of millions of copies and entered the permanent school canon — sometimes amid bans and burnings.

His best-known works are the novella Of Mice and Men (1937), the novel The Grapes of Wrath (1939, Pulitzer Prize), the short novel The Pearl (1947), and the sprawling East of Eden (1952), which he called his 'first book'. Later works include the travel memoir Travels with Charley (1962).

The Depression books

Born in Salinas, California, Steinbeck worked as a ranch hand and in a sugar factory before writing paid. Tortilla Flat (1935) made his name with its Monterey paisanos; In Dubious Battle (1936) took on a fruit-pickers' strike; and The Grapes of Wrath — researched in migrant camps with the photographer-era reporting of the FSA years — followed the Joad family from dust-bowl Oklahoma to California, becoming the best-selling and most denounced American novel of 1939–40.

Later life

Steinbeck served as a war correspondent in 1943, wrote screenplays (Viva Zapata!), and travelled the Soviet Union with Robert Capa (A Russian Journal, 1948). His Nobel was greeted by some critics as unmerited — a judgement posterity has softened — and his support for the Vietnam War estranged his liberal readership. He died in New York in 1968.
