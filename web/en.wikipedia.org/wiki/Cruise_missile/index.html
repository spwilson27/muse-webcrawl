Cruise missile

A cruise missile is an unmanned self-propelled guided missile that sustains flight through aerodynamic lift for most of its flight path and is used to deliver a large warhead over long distances with high precision. Modern cruise missiles are capable of travelling at high subsonic, supersonic, or hypersonic speeds, are self-navigating, and are able to fly on a non-ballistic, extremely low-altitude trajectory.

History

The concept dates to the First World War with devices such as the American Kettering Bug aerial torpedo. The most famous early operational cruise missile was the German V-1 flying bomb of the Second World War, powered by a pulsejet engine. During the Cold War, both the United States and the Soviet Union developed nuclear-armed cruise missiles for launch from aircraft, ships and submarines.

Guidance and navigation

Modern land-attack cruise missiles such as the American Tomahawk combine inertial navigation with GPS updates and terrain-contour matching (TERCOM) and digital scene-matching (DSMAC) for terminal guidance, achieving accuracies of a few metres over ranges exceeding 1,500 km.

Categories

  • Land-attack cruise missiles (LACM) — strike fixed targets on land.
  • Anti-ship cruise missiles (ASCM) — such as the Harpoon, Exocet and Neptune.
  • Air-launched cruise missiles (ALCM) — such as the AGM-86 and Storm Shadow/SCALP.
  • Hypersonic cruise missiles — powered by scramjets in development or early service.

Notable examples

Widely used systems include the US Tomahawk, the Russian Kalibr family, the Anglo-French Storm Shadow/SCALP, the Indian-Russian BrahMos (supersonic), and Ukraine's Neptune, which gained prominence in 2022.
