Post–World War II economic expansion

Suburbanisation and mass consumption characterised the post-war boom in the United States

The post–World War II economic expansion, also known as the postwar economic boom, the Golden Age of Capitalism, and in France as the Trente Glorieuses ("Thirty Glorious Years"), was a period of strong and broadly shared economic growth across much of the world from the end of the Second World War in 1945 until the early 1970s, conventionally ending with the 1973 oil crisis and the collapse of the Bretton Woods system. Advanced economies grew rapidly, unemployment remained low, and living standards and real wages rose markedly for much of the population.

Causes

Explanations emphasise several mutually reinforcing factors: reconstruction of war-damaged economies in Europe and Japan; American aid through the Marshall Plan; the stable monetary and trading framework of Bretton Woods, GATT, and fixed exchange rates; cheap energy, especially oil; pent-up consumer demand and accumulated savings; government investment in infrastructure, housing, and education; the baby boom; and the diffusion of wartime and pre-war technologies — electrification, the internal combustion engine, petrochemicals, and mass-production methods — into civilian industry. Keynesian demand management and strong trade unions in many countries supported high employment and rising wages, which in turn sustained mass consumption of cars, appliances, and suburban housing.

By region

The United States, already prosperous in 1945, saw the suburbanisation of its population, the GI Bill's expansion of education and home ownership, and the growth of a broad middle class, though prosperity was unevenly shared across racial lines. Western Europe experienced its fastest growth in history — West Germany's Wirtschaftswunder, Italy's "economic miracle", and France's thirty glorious years — while Japan achieved double-digit growth that made it the world's second-largest economy by 1968. Growth was slower in Britain, giving rise to debate about relative economic decline. The Soviet Union and Eastern bloc also grew quickly at first, albeit from wartime devastation and with persistent consumer shortages.

End of the boom

By the late 1960s the expansion was faltering under rising inflation, labour unrest, the fiscal strain of the Vietnam War, and growing international competition. The United States suspended dollar–gold convertibility in 1971, and the 1973 oil embargo triggered stagflation across the West, bringing the golden age to an end and ushering in a period of slower, more unequal growth.
