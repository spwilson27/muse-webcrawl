Wu Sangui (1612 – 2 October 1678), courtesy name Changbai, was a Chinese military leader who played a pivotal role in the fall of the Ming dynasty and the establishment of the Qing dynasty. As commander of the Ming garrison at Shanhai Pass in 1644, he opened the gates of the Great Wall to the Manchu forces of the regent Dorgon, enabling them to defeat the rebel army of Li Zicheng and march on Beijing. He is remembered in Chinese culture as a archetypal traitor, though historians view his choices as shaped by the collapse of the Ming state around him.

Early career

Wu Sangui came from a military family of Liaodong; his father Wu Xiang was a Ming general. Wu Sangui distinguished himself in the Ming–Manchu wars of the 1630s and early 1640s and by 1644 commanded the Guanning Army, one of the Ming's last effective field forces, stationed around Ningyuan and Shanhai Pass at the eastern end of the Great Wall.

The crisis of 1644

In April 1644 the rebel leader Li Zicheng captured Beijing and the Chongzhen Emperor hanged himself. Li's army then advanced on Shanhai Pass. Wu, caught between Li's forces to the west and the Manchu Qing to the northeast, negotiated with both sides before choosing to ally with the Qing regent Dorgon. On 27 May 1644, Wu's forces and the Manchu banner armies jointly crushed Li's army at the Battle of Shanhai Pass. The Qing entered Beijing days later and proclaimed the Shunzhi Emperor ruler of China. Popular legend — later dramatized in fiction — attributed Wu's decision to rage over Li's seizure of his concubine Chen Yuanyuan, giving rise to the famous phrase "for the sake of a woman, he opened the pass".

Service to the Qing

Wu campaigned for the Qing across China, pursuing Ming loyalist forces into the southwest. In 1659 he captured Yunnan and in 1662 oversaw the execution of the Yongli Emperor, the last Ming claimant, whom he had pursued into Burma. In reward, the Qing enfeoffed him as Prince Pingxi, ruling Yunnan and Guizhou as a semi-autonomous feudatory.

Revolt of the Three Feudatories

In 1673 the Kangxi Emperor moved to abolish the feudatories. Wu rose in revolt in December 1673, joined by the feudatories of Guangdong and Fujian. Wu's armies initially advanced deep into central China, but the revolt stalled; Wu failed to cross the Yangtze and his allies were progressively detached or defeated. In 1678, with his cause failing, Wu proclaimed himself emperor of a new "Zhou" dynasty at Hengzhou, but died of illness a few months later, on 2 October 1678. His grandson Wu Shifan held out until Kunming fell in 1681, ending the revolt.

Legacy

Wu Sangui's name became a byword for betrayal in Chinese political culture, cited whenever an official collaborates with an enemy power. Modern historians note that by 1644 the Ming dynasty had already collapsed, and that Wu's options were constrained; nevertheless his decision at Shanhai Pass decisively enabled the Qing conquest.
