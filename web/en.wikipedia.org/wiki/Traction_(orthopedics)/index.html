Traction (orthopedics)

Traction in orthopedics is the application of a sustained pulling force to a limb or body part to align fractured bones, relieve pressure on joints, reduce dislocations, or relieve muscle spasm and nerve compression. A counter-force, often the patient's own body weight, opposes the pull.

Types

Skin traction applies weights through adhesive straps or boots on the skin and is used for short-term, low-force treatment, such as temporary management of hip fractures before surgery. Skeletal traction applies force directly through pins or wires drilled into bone (for example a tibial or femoral pin), allowing greater force for longer periods, classically for femoral shaft fractures. Cervical traction, by head halter or halo device, is used for some neck injuries and radicular pain. Named historical systems include Buck's traction, Russell's traction, and the Thomas splint with traction used for femoral fractures in the First World War, which dramatically reduced mortality.

Modern role

Internal fixation surgery has replaced prolonged traction for most fractures in adults, but traction remains valuable as temporary stabilisation, in paediatric fractures, in settings without surgical facilities, and for spinal decompression in selected cases. Risks include pin-site infection, pressure sores, nerve injury, and the complications of prolonged bed rest.
