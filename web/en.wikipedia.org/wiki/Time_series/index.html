In statistics, signal processing, and many other fields, a time series is a series of data points indexed (or listed or graphed) in time order. Most commonly, a time series is a sequence taken at successive equally spaced points in time. Thus it is a sequence of discrete-time data. Examples of time series are heights of ocean tides, counts of sunspots, and the daily closing value of the Dow Jones Industrial Average.

Time series analysis comprises methods for analyzing time series data in order to extract meaningful statistics and other characteristics of the data. Time series forecasting is the use of a model to predict future values based on previously observed values. While regression analysis is often employed in such a way as to test relationships between one or more different time series, this type of analysis is not usually called "time series analysis", which refers in particular to relationships between different points in time within a single series.

Features

Time series models often incorporate the fact that data points taken over time may have an internal structure (such as autocorrelation, trend, or seasonal variation) that should be accounted for. Key descriptive features include:

  • Trend – a long-term increase or decrease in the data.
  • Seasonality – variations that occur at specific regular intervals less than a year, such as weekly, monthly, or quarterly.
  • Cycles – patterns that repeat over longer, irregular periods, often associated with economic conditions.
  • Stationarity – a series whose statistical properties, such as mean and variance, do not change over time.

Models

Models for time series data can have many forms and represent different stochastic processes. Two widely used classes are the autoregressive (AR) models and the moving average (MA) models, combined in the autoregressive moving average (ARMA) and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models. Box and Jenkins popularized the ARIMA approach in their 1970 book Time Series Analysis: Forecasting and Control. More recent developments include state-space models, the Kalman filter, and machine-learning approaches such as recurrent neural networks.

Analysis methods

Time series analysis may be divided into two main branches: frequency-domain methods, which include spectral analysis, and time-domain methods. Analysis techniques also include decomposition of a series into trend, seasonal and remainder components, smoothing methods such as exponential smoothing, and methods for handling non-stationarity such as differencing.

Applications

Time series data have a natural temporal ordering, making time series analysis distinct from cross-sectional studies. Applications include economic forecasting, stock market analysis, weather forecasting, earthquake prediction, electroencephalography, control engineering, astronomy, and communications engineering.
