The Beechcraft Bonanza is an American general aviation aircraft introduced in 1947 by the Beech Aircraft Corporation of Wichita, Kansas. With production continuing into the 2020s, the Bonanza has been in continuous production longer than any other aircraft in history. More than 18,000 have been built, in both the distinctive original V-tail configuration (Model 35, produced until 1982) and the conventional-tail Model 33 Debonair and Model 36 variants. The six-seat, single-engine Bonanza is renowned for its speed, build quality and handling, and it occupies the premium end of the piston single-engine market.

Development

Designed by a team led by Ralph Harmon, the Model 35 Bonanza was a radical departure from pre-war general aviation practice: an all-metal, low-wing monoplane with retractable tricycle landing gear, a laminar-flow wing and a novel V-tail combining rudder and elevator functions, derived from work by Polish engineer Jerzy Rudlicki. The prototype first flew on December 22, 1945, and deliveries began in 1947. At a time when most private aircraft were fabric-covered taildraggers cruising at 100 mph, the Bonanza cruised near 170 mph and was marketed as a businessman's aeroplane.

Variants

The line evolved through dozens of sub-variants. The Model 35 (V-tail) was joined in 1960 by the Model 33 Debonair, a cheaper conventional-tail version later absorbed into the Bonanza line, and in 1968 by the stretched six-seat Model 36, which remains the basis of today's G36. Engines grew from the original 165 hp Continental to 300 hp versions, and turbocharged and pressurised-adjacent variants were offered. Beechcraft also developed the twin-engine Travel Air and Baron from the Bonanza design.

Safety record and "doctor killer" reputation

The Bonanza acquired the macabre nickname "fork-tailed doctor killer" from a pattern of fatal accidents involving wealthy amateur pilots — often physicians — who bought the high-performance aircraft without the experience to manage it, particularly in instrument weather. The V-tail also suffered a series of in-flight breakups that led to extensive investigation and airworthiness directives strengthening the tail structure. Later analyses emphasised pilot factors and weather rather than design flaws, and the type's overall accident rate is broadly comparable to similar aircraft, but the nickname endures in aviation folklore.

Notable flights and accidents

In 1949, Bill Odom flew a Bonanza named Waikiki Beech nonstop from Honolulu to Teterboro, New Jersey — 4,957 miles — a celebrated long-distance record for a light aircraft. The type's most famous accident occurred on February 3, 1959, "The Day the Music Died", when a chartered 1947 V-tail Bonanza crashed shortly after takeoff from Mason City, Iowa, killing rock and roll musicians Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens and J. P. "The Big Bopper" Richardson, along with pilot Roger Peterson.
