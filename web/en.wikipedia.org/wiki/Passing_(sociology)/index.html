Passing (sociology)

In sociology, passing is the ability of a person to be regarded as a member of an identity group or category different from their own, which may include racial identity, ethnicity, caste, social class, sexual orientation, gender, religion, age, or disability status. Passing typically enables the person to gain privileges, rewards, or increased social acceptance that would otherwise be denied to them as a member of a stigmatised group, at the cost of concealing an aspect of their identity and risking exposure.

Racial passing

The best-studied form is racial passing in the United States, where light-skinned African Americans "passed" as white to escape slavery, Jim Crow segregation, and racial discrimination. The practice flourished under the "one-drop rule," which classified anyone with known African ancestry as Black regardless of appearance. Passing often required severing ties with family and community, and it is a central theme of Harlem Renaissance literature, including Nella Larsen's novels Quicksand (1928) and Passing (1929) and James Weldon Johnson's The Autobiography of an Ex-Colored Man (1912). Journalist John Howard Griffin's Black Like Me (1961) described a temporary, investigative inversion of the practice.

Sociologist Erving Goffman analysed passing in Stigma (1963) as a strategy of information control by people whose stigmatised attribute is not immediately visible, distinguishing it from "covering," in which the attribute is known but downplayed. Passing has also been used by Jews concealing their identity under persecution, by women who lived as men, by gay people remaining "closeted," and by working-class people adopting the speech and manners of a higher social class.

Criticism and significance

Passing is double-edged in scholarly and community debates: it can be a pragmatic route to safety and opportunity for individuals while leaving discriminatory structures unchallenged, and those who pass have historically been accused of disloyalty to their communities. At the same time, the very possibility of passing has been used to argue that identities such as race are socially constructed rather than natural facts.
