Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is an abnormal narrowing of the arteries other than those that supply the heart or brain — most commonly the arteries of the legs. When narrowing occurs in the heart it is called coronary artery disease, and in the brain, cerebrovascular disease. Peripheral artery disease most commonly affects the legs, but other arteries may also be involved, such as those of the arms, neck, or kidneys. The classic symptom is leg pain when walking, known as intermittent claudication, which resolves with rest; however, many people with PAD have no symptoms at all. Severe disease can progress to chronic limb-threatening ischaemia with rest pain, ulcers and gangrene, and may lead to amputation.

Causes and risk factors

PAD is overwhelmingly caused by atherosclerosis — the build-up of fatty plaque in the arteries. Risk factors mirror those of other atherosclerotic disease: smoking (the strongest risk factor), diabetes mellitus, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and increasing age. People with PAD have a markedly increased risk of heart attack and stroke, since the disease is a marker of widespread atherosclerosis.

Diagnosis

The standard test is the ankle–brachial index (ABI), the ratio of blood pressure at the ankle to that in the arm; a value of 0.90 or below is diagnostic. Duplex ultrasonography, CT angiography and MR angiography locate and grade stenoses, and catheter angiography is used when intervention is planned. In 2019 about 113 million people worldwide were affected — the large majority undiagnosed.

Management

Treatment focuses on risk-factor modification — smoking cessation, statin and antiplatelet therapy, blood-pressure and diabetes control — together with supervised exercise therapy, which improves walking distance. The drug cilostazol can help claudication. Revascularisation by angioplasty with or without stenting, or by bypass surgery, is reserved for lifestyle-limiting symptoms or limb-threatening ischaemia.
