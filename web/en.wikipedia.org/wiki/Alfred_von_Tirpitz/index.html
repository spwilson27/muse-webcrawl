Alfred Peter Friedrich von Tirpitz (19 March 1849 – 6 March 1930) was a German grand admiral and statesman who, as Secretary of State of the Imperial Naval Office from 1897 to 1916, built the Imperial German Navy into a battle fleet intended to rival Britain's Royal Navy. His name is inseparably linked with the Anglo-German naval arms race that preceded the First World War.

Early career

Born in Küstrin, the son of a senior judge, Tirpitz joined the Prussian Navy in 1865. He rose through torpedo development work in the 1870s and 1880s, gaining a reputation as an able organiser. In the 1890s he commanded the East Asian cruiser squadron and served as chief of staff of the Naval High Command, where he drafted the 1897 memorandum calling for a fleet of battleships concentrated in home waters.

The Tirpitz Plan

Appointed State Secretary in 1897 with the backing of Kaiser Wilhelm II, Tirpitz steered the First and Second Naval Laws of 1898 and 1900 through the Reichstag. His "risk theory" (Risikogedanke) held that a German fleet strong enough to inflict unacceptable losses on Britain — even if itself destroyed — would deter Britain from war and compel it to grant Germany colonial concessions. The resulting High Seas Fleet triggered a costly dreadnought race and pushed Britain towards the Entente with France and Russia.

First World War and resignation

When war came in 1914, Tirpitz favoured aggressive use of the fleet and unrestricted submarine warfare, but the Kaiser and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg restricted both. Frustrated and marginalised, Tirpitz resigned in March 1916 after the Chancellor blocked his U-boat demands. The battleship Tirpitz, launched in 1939, was later named after him.

Political career and death

In 1917 Tirpitz co-founded the nationalist German Fatherland Party to mobilise support for total war. After 1918 he sat in the Reichstag for the conservative DNVP from 1924 to 1928 and promoted the "stab-in-the-back" interpretation of Germany's defeat. He died at Ebenhausen near Munich in 1930.
