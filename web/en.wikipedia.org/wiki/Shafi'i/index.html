Shafi'i

The Shafi'i school (Arabic: ٱلْمَذْهَب ٱلشَّافِعِيّ) is one of the four major schools (madhhabs) of Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh) within Sunni Islam. It was founded by the Arab scholar Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (767–820 CE), whose systematic theory of jurisprudence, set out in his Al-Risala, is regarded as foundational for Sunni legal theory.

Principles

Al-Shafi'i's method ranked the sources of law as the Qur'an, then the Sunnah (prophetic tradition, authenticated through hadith), then consensus (ijma), and then analogical reasoning (qiyas). He restricted the use of juristic preference (istihsan) associated with the Hanafi school and of unrestricted reasoning, insisting instead on strict adherence to transmitted texts, while systematising qiyas as disciplined analogy from textual sources.

Distribution

The Shafi'i school predominates in:

  • East Africa (Somalia, Eritrea, coastal Kenya and Tanzania, Ethiopia)
  • Egypt (especially Lower Egypt), Yemen and the Hejaz historically
  • The Levant and Kurdish regions in part
  • Southeast Asia — Indonesia, Malaysia, Brunei and the southern Philippines, where it is the dominant school
  • Parts of South Asia (Malabar Coast, Sri Lanka) and Dagestan in the Caucasus

Notable scholars

Major later Shafi'i jurists and scholars include al-Muzani, al-Nawawi, Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani, al-Suyuti and, in kalam and Sufism, al-Ghazali, whose theological and spiritual works were produced within the school's milieu.
