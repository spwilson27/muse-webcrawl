Option (finance)

Payoff at expiration for long call and long put positions.

In finance, an option is a contract which conveys to its owner, the holder, the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a specific quantity of an underlying asset or instrument at a specified strike price on or before a specified date, depending on the style of the option. The party granting the right is the writer (or seller), who receives a payment called the premium from the buyer.

A call option gives the holder the right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price, while a put option gives the holder the right to sell it at the strike price. Options are a type of derivative security, deriving their value from the price movements of the underlying asset, which may be a stock, index, commodity, currency, or other instrument.

Valuation

The value of an option depends on the price of the underlying asset, the strike price, the time to expiration, volatility, and interest rates. The most famous model for valuing options is the Black–Scholes model, developed in 1973 by Fischer Black, Myron Scholes, and Robert Merton, which yields a theoretical estimate of the price of European-style options. Sensitivity measures known as "the Greeks" (delta, gamma, theta, vega, rho) describe how an option's price responds to changes in these inputs.

Styles and markets

American options can be exercised at any time before expiration, European options only at expiration, and Bermudan options on specified dates. Options trade on exchanges such as the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE, founded 1973) as well as over the counter. Common strategies combine options into spreads, straddles, and strangles to shape risk profiles.

Risks

Options involve leverage and can expire worthless; writers of uncovered ("naked") options face potentially large losses. For this reason options markets are heavily regulated and typically require margin accounts and investor suitability assessments.
