International arbitration is a method of resolving disputes between parties from different countries outside national courts, through a private tribunal whose award is binding and enforceable worldwide. It is the preferred mechanism for cross-border commercial disputes because it offers neutrality, confidentiality, procedural flexibility and expertise, and because awards travel across borders far more readily than court judgments.

Framework

Arbitration rests on the parties' agreement, usually an arbitration clause in a contract. The 1958 New York Convention, ratified by more than 170 states, obliges courts to recognize arbitration agreements and enforce awards subject to narrow exceptions. The UNCITRAL Model Law (1985, amended 2006) has harmonized national arbitration statutes in dozens of jurisdictions. Cases are administered by institutions such as the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) in Paris, the London Court of International Arbitration (LCIA), and centres in Singapore and Hong Kong, or conducted ad hoc under the UNCITRAL Rules. Popular seats include London, Paris, Singapore, Hong Kong, Geneva and New York.

Investor–state arbitration

A distinct branch, investor–state dispute settlement (ISDS), lets foreign investors sue host states under investment treaties, frequently administered by the World Bank's ICSID. ISDS has drawn criticism for its perceived lack of transparency and constraints on regulation — a debate about distributing decision-making power reminiscent of arguments over decentralisation — prompting reforms such as the EU's proposed investment court system.
