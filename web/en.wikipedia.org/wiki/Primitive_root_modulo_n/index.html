Primitive root modulo n

Powers of 3 modulo 7 cycle through all non-zero residues.

In modular arithmetic, a number g is a primitive root modulo n if every number coprime to n is congruent to a power of g modulo n; that is, the multiplicative order of g modulo n equals Euler's totient φ(n), the size of the multiplicative group of integers modulo n.

Example

The number 3 is a primitive root modulo 7: its powers are 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1 — all six non-zero residues. By contrast 2 is not, since 23 = 8 ≡ 1 (mod 7), so its order is only 3.

Existence

Gauss proved that primitive roots exist exactly for the moduli 1, 2, 4, pk and 2pk, where p is an odd prime and k ≥ 1. When primitive roots exist, there are exactly φ(φ(n)) of them. No simple formula for finding the smallest primitive root is known; Artin's conjecture (unproven in general) predicts that any integer that is neither −1 nor a perfect square is a primitive root modulo infinitely many primes.

Finding primitive roots

To test whether g is a primitive root modulo prime p, factorise p − 1. Then g is a primitive root iff g(p−1)/q ≢ 1 (mod p) for every prime factor q of p − 1.

Applications

Primitive roots underlie the Diffie–Hellman key exchange and the ElGamal cryptosystem, whose security rests on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem. They also appear in pseudorandom number generation and in the theory of cyclotomic fields.
