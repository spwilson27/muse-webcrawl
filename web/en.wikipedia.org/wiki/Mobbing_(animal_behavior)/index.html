Mobbing (animal behavior)

Mobbing in animals is an antipredator adaptation in which individuals of prey species cooperatively attack or harass a predator, usually to protect their offspring. A simple definition of mobbing is an assemblage of individuals around a potentially dangerous predator. Mobbing is most frequently seen in birds, but it has also been observed in mammals such as meerkats and prairie dogs, and in fish.

Function

Mobbing serves several functions. It can drive a predator away from a nesting area ("move-on" effect), advertise to the predator that it has been detected and lost the element of surprise, protect eggs and young, and teach inexperienced juveniles to recognise enemies — a cultural transmission function demonstrated in blackbirds by Eberhard Curio's experiments. Mobbing prey often use distinctive, loud, harsh calls that are easy to locate, recruiting other individuals, including members of other species, to join the mob.

Examples

Gulls, terns, blackbirds, crows and many other birds swoop at and defecate on predators near their nests or roosts. Small songbirds harass perched owls and hawks in daylight, when the raptor is at a disadvantage. Among mammals, California ground squirrels confront rattlesnakes, and meerkats take turns guarding and harassing predators. Hans Kruuk's classic study of black-headed gulls showed that mobbing measurably reduces the success of egg predators such as crows, though it carries a cost: mobbing individuals expose themselves to risk, and predators occasionally snatch a member of the mob.

Evolution

Mobbing is an example of cooperative antipredator behaviour whose evolution is explained by a combination of kin selection — parents defending their own offspring or relatives — direct benefits to participants, and the deterrent effect on predators. Konrad Lorenz discussed the behaviour in his book On Aggression (1966), and it remains a standard topic in behavioural ecology.
