Human genetic variation

Human genetic variation is the genetic difference both within and among human populations.


Human genetic diversity reflects the serial-founder expansion out of Africa.

Human genetic variation is the genetic difference among individuals and populations of Homo sapiens. No two humans are genetically identical: any two genomes differ at millions of positions, though humans are remarkably homogeneous as a species — about 99.9% of DNA sequence is shared between any two people, and total human diversity is less than that found within some single populations of chimpanzees.

Sources and types of variation

Variation arises from mutation, recombination and gene flow, and is shaped by genetic drift and natural selection. The most common variants are single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs); other forms include insertions and deletions (indels), copy-number variants, short tandem repeats and chromosomal rearrangements. Projects such as HapMap, the 1000 Genomes Project and gnomAD have catalogued hundreds of millions of human variants.

Distribution among populations

Richard Lewontin showed in 1972 that the great majority of human genetic variation (roughly 85%) occurs within populations, with only a small fraction distinguishing geographic groups — a result confirmed repeatedly with genome-wide data. Genetic diversity is greatest within Africa and declines with distance from Africa, consistent with a serial founder effect during the out-of-Africa expansion of modern humans roughly 60,000–100,000 years ago. Variation is mostly clinal (gradual with geography) rather than divided into discrete "races", and allele-frequency differences between regions (FST around 0.1) are modest. Ancestry-informative markers can nevertheless reconstruct broad geographic ancestry, and some variants are strongly differentiated due to local adaptation (lactase persistence, skin pigmentation, high-altitude adaptation, sickle-cell and other malaria-resistance alleles).

Archaic admixture

Ancient DNA has shown that non-African genomes carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry, and that some Oceanian and Asian populations also carry Denisovan ancestry, evidence of interbreeding as modern humans spread across Eurasia.

Medical significance

Genetic variation underlies susceptibility to diseases (from Mendelian disorders such as cystic fibrosis to polygenic risks for diabetes or schizophrenia), drug responses (pharmacogenomics), and traits generally. Genome-wide association studies map variants to traits, though most heritability is spread over very many small-effect loci, and prediction tools trained on one ancestry group transfer poorly to others — a driver for more diverse genomic sampling.
