B cells, also known as B lymphocytes, are a type of white blood cell of the lymphocyte subtype. They function in the humoral immunity component of the adaptive immune system by secreting antibodies. Additionally, B cells present antigens and secrete cytokines. In mammals, B cells mature in the bone marrow, which is at the core of most bones. In birds, B cells mature in the bursa of Fabricius, a lymphoid organ from which the "B" in B cell is derived.

B cells express B cell receptors (BCRs) on their cell membrane, which allow them to bind to a specific antigen. After an antigen binds to the BCR, the B cell is activated, with the help of T helper cells, and can differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells or long-lived memory B cells.

Development

B cells develop from haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. During maturation, gene rearrangement (VDJ recombination) of the immunoglobulin loci generates an enormous diversity of antigen specificities. Immature B cells that react strongly to self-antigens are eliminated or inactivated, a process central to immunological tolerance. Mature naive B cells circulate through the blood and secondary lymphoid organs such as the spleen and lymph nodes.

Activation

When a naive B cell encounters its cognate antigen, the antigen is internalised and presented on MHC class II molecules to helper T cells. In germinal centres of lymphoid follicles, activated B cells undergo clonal expansion, somatic hypermutation, and class-switch recombination, producing high-affinity antibodies of different isotypes (IgG, IgA, IgE) suited to different effector functions. Most activated cells become plasma cells, dedicated antibody factories, while others become memory B cells that respond faster and more strongly upon re-exposure — the basis of long-lasting vaccine protection.

Types

Major B cell populations include follicular B cells (the conventional majority), marginal zone B cells in the spleen, and B1 cells, which produce natural antibodies. Regulatory B cells secrete interleukin-10 and can suppress inflammation.

Clinical significance

B cell defects cause immunodeficiencies such as X-linked agammaglobulinaemia. B cell malignancies include Burkitt lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, and multiple myeloma (a cancer of plasma cells). Autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis involve pathogenic B cells; anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab deplete B cells and are used in their treatment.
