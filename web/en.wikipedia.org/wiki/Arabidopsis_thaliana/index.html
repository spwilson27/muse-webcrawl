Arabidopsis thaliana is the most widely used model organism in plant science.

Arabidopsis thaliana, the Arabidopsis, thale cress, mouse-ear cress or simply Arabidopsis, is a small plant in the mustard family (Brassicaceae) native to Eurasia and Africa. It is a popular model organism in plant biology and genetics: although of no agricultural significance itself, its small genome, short life cycle and ease of transformation make it ideal for laboratory study, and findings frequently transfer to crop species, its relatives.

Characteristics

A. thaliana is a winter annual with a basal rosette of hairy leaves and a flowering stem 20–25 cm tall bearing small white self-pollinating flowers. From germination to mature seed takes about six weeks. Its genome of roughly 135 megabase pairs on five chromosomes was the first plant genome sequenced, published in 2000 by the Arabidopsis Genome Initiative; it contains about 27,000 protein-coding genes. The species is readily transformed by the "floral dip" method using Agrobacterium tumefaciens, and large collections of T-DNA insertion mutants and natural accessions (such as the standard laboratory lines Columbia-0 and Landsberg erecta) are maintained by stock centres.

Research contributions

Classic work in Arabidopsis produced the ABC model of flower development, explaining how homeotic genes specify sepals, petals, stamens and carpels; the photoreceptors (phytochromes and cryptochromes) by which plants sense light; the vernalization pathway by which winter cold permits flowering, involving the gene FLC; and the molecular basis of plant immunity and hormone signalling, including auxin, gibberellins and abscisic acid. In 1986 Arabidopsis became the first plant grown and harvested for seed in space experiments, and it remains a staple of space biology.
