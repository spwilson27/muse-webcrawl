Art theft

Art theft, sometimes called artnapping, is the stealing of paintings, sculptures, or other forms of visual art from galleries, museums, or private and public collections. Stolen art is often resold or used as collateral by criminals; because famous works are difficult to sell openly, many are recovered, while others — including several of the most valuable paintings ever stolen — remain missing.

Empty frames at the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum mark works stolen in 1990 and never recovered.

Notable cases

In 1911 Vincenzo Peruggia, a former Louvre employee, stole the Mona Lisa, hiding it for two years before its recovery in Florence — the theft made the painting world-famous. The largest art theft in US history occurred on 18 March 1990 at the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in Boston, where thieves posing as police took thirteen works, including Vermeer's The Concert and Rembrandt's The Storm on the Sea of Galilee, worth an estimated $500 million; none has been recovered. Other famous thefts include the 1994 and 2004 takings of versions of Munch's The Scream (both recovered), the 2000 Stockholm Nationalmuseum robbery, and extensive wartime looting, most notoriously by Nazi Germany (see the work of the "Monuments Men" and ongoing restitution claims), and the looting of Iraqi and Syrian antiquities in recent wars.

Investigation and recovery

Specialised police units — such as the FBI's Art Crime Team, Scotland Yard's Art and Antiques Unit, and the Carabinieri's cultural property squad (TPC) in Italy — maintain databases of stolen works (Interpol's Stolen Works of Art database; the private Art Loss Register). Art crime is also linked to forgery, smuggling, and money laundering; museums have responded with improved security, though thefts from regional museums and churches remain common.
