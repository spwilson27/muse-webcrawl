Axiom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The title page of an early printed edition of Euclid's Elements]

An axiom (or postulate) is a statement taken to be true without proof, serving as a starting point from which other statements are logically derived. Axioms are the foundation stones of a mathematical theory: everything else in the theory must be proved from them.

The axiomatic method is exemplified by Euclid's five postulates for geometry. The fifth — the parallel postulate — resisted proof for two thousand years; attempts to derive it instead revealed consistent non-Euclidean geometries (Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Riemann), showing axioms are chosen, not self-evident.

Modern view

In modern mathematics an axiom system is judged by consistency, and ideally by independence of its axioms and fruitfulness. David Hilbert's 1899 Foundations of Geometry recast geometry rigorously, and his programme sought to secure all of mathematics axiomatically — an ambition limited by Gödel's incompleteness theorems.

Most of mathematics is founded on the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms of set theory, usually with the axiom of choice (ZFC). The axiom of choice is equivalent to Zorn's lemma and the well-ordering theorem, and implies some counter-intuitive results such as the Banach–Tarski paradox.

In logic

Other sciences use 'axiom' more loosely for basic principles (e.g. Newton's laws were styled axioms). In formal logic, axiom schemes generate infinitely many axioms in proof systems such as Hilbert-style calculi.
