Biocentrism (ethics)

Biocentrism (from Greek βίος bios, "life" and κέντρον kentron, "center"), in a political and ecological sense, as well as literally, is an ethical point of view that extends inherent value to all living things.

Overview

Biocentrism holds that all living organisms have moral standing, contrasting with anthropocentrism, which centres humans, and with ecocentrism, which extends value to whole ecosystems and species as well as individuals.

Thinkers

Albert Schweitzer's "reverence for life", Aldo Leopold's land ethic (often classed as ecocentric), Paul Taylor's Respect for Nature (1986) with its biocentric egalitarianism, and Arne Næss's deep ecology are key influences. Taylor argued every organism is a teleological centre of life with its own good.

Criticism

Critics question how to adjudicate conflicts between organisms' interests, whether equal value is practicable, and whether moral standing should depend on sentience instead.
