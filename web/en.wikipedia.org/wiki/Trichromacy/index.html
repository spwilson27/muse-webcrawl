Trichromacy

Trichromacy
Normalised spectral sensitivities of the three human cone types
Channels3 (S, M, L cones)
Found inHumans, most Old World monkeys and apes, some other species

Trichromacy or trichromaticism is the possession of three independent channels for conveying color information, derived from the three different types of cone cells in the eye. Organisms with trichromacy are called trichromats. Normal human colour vision is trichromatic.

Mechanism

The three cone types respond preferentially to short (S, blue), medium (M, green) and long (L, red) wavelengths of light. The visual system compares the relative signals from the three channels — an opponent process — allowing discrimination of millions of colours. The theory of trichromatic colour vision was proposed by Thomas Young and later developed by Hermann von Helmholtz in the 19th century.

Evolution

Most mammals are dichromats. Routine trichromacy evolved in catarrhine primates (Old World monkeys and apes, including humans), probably as an adaptation for detecting ripe fruit and young leaves against green foliage. Among New World monkeys, trichromacy is polymorphic, with heterozygous females being trichromatic. Trichromacy has also evolved independently in some marsupials and insects; many birds and reptiles are tetrachromats with four channels.

Variation

Faulty trichromacy, in which one cone type has a shifted sensitivity, causes forms of colour vision deficiency such as protanomaly and deuteranomaly. Some human females may be functional tetrachromats due to an extra opsin gene variant.
