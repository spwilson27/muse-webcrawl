Louis IV, Holy Roman Emperor

Louis IV (German: Ludwig IV.; 1 April 1282 – 11 October 1347), called the Bavarian, was Duke of Bavaria from 1294, King of the Romans from 1314, and Holy Roman Emperor from 1328 until his death in 1347. A member of the House of Wittelsbach, his reign was dominated by a long conflict with the papacy over the independence of the imperial office, during which he was excommunicated and installed an antipope in Rome.

Early life and disputed election

Louis was the son of Louis II, Duke of Upper Bavaria, and Matilda of Habsburg, daughter of King Rudolf I. He ruled Bavaria jointly, and then in rivalry, with his elder brother Rudolf I. After the death of Emperor Henry VII in 1313, the electors split: in October 1314 Louis was elected king by one group of electors while another chose his Habsburg cousin Frederick the Fair. The dispute was settled by war, culminating in Louis's victory at the Battle of Mühldorf in 1322, where Frederick was captured. Louis later released him and briefly shared power under a co-rulership agreement.

Conflict with the papacy

Pope John XXII, residing at Avignon, refused to recognise Louis's election, claiming the right to approve the emperor and to administer the empire during a vacancy. Louis was excommunicated in 1324. In response, Louis marched into Italy in 1327, was crowned king of Italy in Milan, and in January 1328 received the imperial crown in Rome — not from the pope but from the Roman senator Sciarra Colonna, acting in the name of the Roman people. Louis then deposed John XXII and installed the Franciscan Pietro Rainalducci as Antipope Nicholas V.

Supported by theorists such as Marsilius of Padua, whose Defensor pacis attacked papal claims to temporal power, and by the dissident Franciscans around William of Ockham at his Munich court, Louis became the focus of arguments for the separation of imperial and papal authority. In 1338 the prince-electors declared at Rhens that a king elected by them required no papal confirmation — a milestone in the constitutional development of the empire.

Dynastic expansion and death

Louis greatly enlarged Wittelsbach possessions. Through his second marriage to Margaret of Hainaut he acquired claims to Hainaut, Holland, Zeeland and Friesland. He granted Brandenburg to his son Louis, and secured Tyrol for the family by annulling the marriage of Margaret of Tyrol and marrying her to another of his sons. This aggressive dynastic policy alienated other princes, who in 1346 elected Charles of Luxembourg as anti-king. Before open war could decide the issue, Louis died suddenly of a stroke while bear hunting near Fürstenfeldbruck on 11 October 1347.
