Amnesia

Amnesia
SpecialtyPsychiatry, neurology
SymptomsLoss of memories, impaired ability to form new memories
CausesBrain injury, stroke, Alzheimer's disease, alcohol abuse, psychological trauma
TypesAnterograde, retrograde, transient global, dissociative

Amnesia is a deficit in memory caused by brain damage or disease, but it can also be caused temporarily by various sedatives, hypnotic drugs, or psychological trauma. The memory can be either wholly or partially lost due to the extent of damage. Amnesia differs from ordinary forgetfulness and from dementia in that other cognitive functions typically remain intact.

Types

In retrograde amnesia, a person cannot recall events that occurred before the onset of the condition, often with memories nearest the injury most affected (Ribot's law). In anterograde amnesia, the ability to form new long-term memories is impaired while older memories remain; the most famous case, patient H.M., followed bilateral removal of medial temporal lobe structures including the hippocampi, and demonstrated that short-term and long-term memory are dissociable. Transient global amnesia is a sudden, temporary episode of memory loss of uncertain cause. Dissociative amnesia is attributed to psychological stress rather than physical injury.

Causes and treatment

Common causes include traumatic brain injury, stroke, encephalitis, Korsakoff syndrome from thiamine deficiency in chronic alcohol misuse, and neurodegenerative disease. Treatment focuses on the underlying cause; rehabilitation uses occupational therapy and memory aids, and many cases improve spontaneously, though dense anterograde amnesia can be permanent.
