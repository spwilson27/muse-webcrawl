Compulsory sterilization

Compulsory sterilization, also known as forced or coerced sterilization, is the involuntary sterilization of a person, generally without their full consent, as part of state programmes, institutional practices, or discriminatory policies. It has been widely condemned as a human rights violation.

History and eugenics programmes

In the early 20th century, many countries adopted eugenic sterilization laws. In the United States, over 60,000 people were sterilized under state laws upheld in Buck v. Bell (1927). Nazi Germany's 1933 Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring led to about 400,000 forced sterilizations. Sweden, Canada (Alberta and British Columbia), Japan, and other states ran programmes targeting people with disabilities, Indigenous peoples, ethnic minorities, and the poor, many lasting into the 1970s.

Later and contemporary cases

Programmes and allegations have included Peru under Alberto Fujimori in the 1990s, China's one-child policy era practices, forced sterilization of Roma women in Central Europe, and of women living with HIV in several countries. Several governments have issued apologies and compensation schemes.

International law

The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court lists enforced sterilization as a crime against humanity when part of a widespread or systematic attack. UN agencies, the Council of Europe, and human rights bodies require free, prior, and informed consent for sterilization.
