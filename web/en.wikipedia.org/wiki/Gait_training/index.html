Gait training

Gait training is a form of physical therapy in which a person is taught or re-taught how to stand and walk, typically following injury, stroke, surgery, or neurological disease, or in the management of developmental conditions such as cerebral palsy. Its goals are safe, efficient, and as symmetrical as possible ambulation, with or without assistive devices.

Assessment

Training begins with gait analysis: observation of stride length, cadence, base of support, joint motion, muscle strength, balance, and deviations such as Trendelenburg gait, foot drop, or circumduction. Instrumented gait laboratories may supplement observation with motion capture, force plates, and electromyography.

Techniques

Patients commonly progress from parallel bars to a walker, crutches, or a cane, then to unaided walking. Body-weight-supported treadmill training partially unloads the patient in a harness while therapists guide stepping; robotic exoskeletons and electromechanical gait trainers automate repetitive stepping. Task-specific practice, strengthening, stretching to prevent contracture, orthoses such as ankle-foot orthoses, and functional electrical stimulation are combined according to diagnosis. Weight-bearing status after surgery (non-weight-bearing, partial, or weight-bearing as tolerated) governs progression.

Applications

Gait training is a core component of rehabilitation after stroke and spinal cord injury, in Parkinson's disease and multiple sclerosis, after lower-limb amputation (with prosthetic gait training), and following hip or knee replacement. In children, it is used to maximise independent mobility in cerebral palsy and spina bifida.
