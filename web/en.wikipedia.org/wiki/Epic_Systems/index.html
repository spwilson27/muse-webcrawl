Epic Systems Corporation is a privately held American healthcare software company based in Verona, Wisconsin. Founded in 1979 by Judith Faulkner, Epic develops electronic health record (EHR) software used by hospitals, academic medical centers, and health systems. Its software holds the medical records of well over 250 million patients worldwide, and Epic is the most widely used EHR vendor among large hospitals in the United States.

Epic's headquarters campus in Verona, Wisconsin, known for its themed buildings.

History

Faulkner founded the company in 1979 in Madison, Wisconsin, as Human Services Computing, with a small team and a database management system for patient scheduling and billing. The company grew steadily as its integrated clinical record system gained adoption; it was renamed Epic Systems in the 1980s. Unlike competitors, Epic has remained privately held, has never acquired another company of significance for its core product, and develops its software largely in-house on a single integrated platform. Its annual Users Group Meeting draws tens of thousands of healthcare IT professionals to Verona.

Products and market position

Epic's flagship product is an integrated suite covering clinical care, registration, scheduling, billing, and specialty modules. Its patient portal, MyChart, allows patients to view test results, message clinicians, schedule appointments, and pay bills, and became a mass-market product during the rollout of COVID-19 vaccination scheduling. Through its app developer program and support for interoperability standards such as FHIR, third parties can build applications that exchange data with Epic systems. Epic's largest competitors include Oracle Health (formerly Cerner) and MEDITECH.

Campus and culture

Epic's Verona campus is noted for its elaborate themed architecture — including buildings styled after a farm, a castle, an Emerald City inspired by The Wizard of Oz, and a "Deep Space" auditorium complex — and for an extensive art collection. The company has an internal culture sometimes described as distinctive and insular; it does not use external recruiters for most roles and hires heavily from top universities. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Epic's plan to require employees to return to the office in 2020 drew national attention before being reversed.

Criticism

Epic has been criticized over the years for the cost and length of its implementations, for usability burdens associated with clinician documentation and burnout, and for alleged resistance to interoperability with rival systems — a charge sharpened by debates over federal information-blocking rules, which Epic opposed in proposed form. The company responds that its customers achieve high interoperability through its own exchange network and standards support.
