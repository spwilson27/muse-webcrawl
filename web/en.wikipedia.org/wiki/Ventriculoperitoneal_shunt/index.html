Ventriculoperitoneal shunt

A ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt is a cerebral shunt used surgically to treat hydrocephalus, a condition in which excess cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) accumulates in the ventricles of the brain. The device drains CSF from a cerebral ventricle through a system of silicone catheters and a one-way valve, under the skin, to the peritoneal cavity in the abdomen, where the fluid is absorbed. It is the most commonly used type of shunt for hydrocephalus.

Components and placement

The system comprises a ventricular catheter inserted through a burr hole into a lateral ventricle, a valve (often programmable, allowing non-invasive adjustment of the opening pressure) usually placed behind the ear, and a long distal catheter tunnelled subcutaneously down the neck and chest into the peritoneum. The valve regulates flow to avoid over-drainage or under-drainage. The operation is typically performed by a neurosurgeon under general anaesthesia.

Complications

VP shunts are effective but have among the highest failure rates of any implanted device. Complications include obstruction (the most common), infection (often with skin organisms such as Staphylococcus epidermidis), over-drainage causing subdural collections or slit ventricle syndrome, catheter fracture or migration, abdominal complications such as pseudocysts, and bowel perforation. A substantial fraction of shunts fail within the first years after placement, especially in children, and many patients require one or more revisions over a lifetime.

Alternatives

Other drainage sites include the right atrium of the heart (ventriculoatrial shunt) and the pleural cavity; for obstructive hydrocephalus, endoscopic third ventriculostomy can create an internal bypass and avoid a shunt altogether in suitable patients.
