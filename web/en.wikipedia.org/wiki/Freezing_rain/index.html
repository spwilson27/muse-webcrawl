Freezing rain

Freezing rain is rain maintained at temperatures below freezing by the ambient air mass that causes freezing on contact with surfaces. Unlike ice pellets (sleet), which bounce on impact, freezing rain falls as supercooled liquid droplets and freezes instantly on contact, forming a coating of clear ice called glaze. A storm producing significant accretion of glaze is called an ice storm.

Formation

Freezing rain develops when falling snow encounters a layer of warm air in the middle altitudes, causing the snow to melt completely into rain, then passes through a thin layer of below-freezing air close to the surface. The shallow cold layer is not thick enough to refreeze the drops into ice pellets before they reach the ground, so they arrive as supercooled liquid and freeze on contact with roads, trees, power lines and any exposed surface at or below 0 °C.

Effects

Glaze accumulates on every surface, adding great weight: ice a few centimetres thick can bring down trees and power lines, and even a thin film makes roads effectively impassable. Freezing rain is a serious aviation hazard, since ice accreting on wings degrades lift rapidly. Notable disasters include the North American ice storm of 1998, which left millions without power in eastern Canada and the north-eastern United States, and the 2008 Chinese winter storms.

Observation and reporting

In METAR weather reports, freezing rain is coded FZRA. It is distinguished from freezing drizzle (FZDZ) by droplet size, and from rain that merely falls on frozen ground: the defining feature is the sub-freezing air layer and supercooling of the drops themselves.
