Plague (disease)

Cause: Yersinia pestis bacterium
Transmission: Flea bites; droplets (pneumonic form)
Main forms: Bubonic, septicaemic, pneumonic
Treatment: Antibiotics (e.g. gentamicin, doxycycline)

Plague is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. It circulates naturally among wild rodents and their fleas, and reaches humans mainly through flea bites. Plague has caused three great pandemics — the Plague of Justinian (from 541 AD), the Black Death (from 1346), which killed a third to a half of Europe's population, and a third pandemic that began in China in the nineteenth century — and remains endemic in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Americas.

Forms

Bubonic plague, the most common form, follows a flea bite: after an incubation of two to eight days it produces fever, chills, and buboes — extremely painful swollen lymph nodes, usually in the groin, armpit, or neck. Untreated, it kills 30–60% of victims. Septicaemic plague occurs when the bacteria multiply in the bloodstream, causing shock and bleeding into the skin, which can blacken the extremities. Pneumonic plague infects the lungs and is the only form that spreads directly between people, through respiratory droplets; it is almost invariably fatal if treatment is delayed beyond the first day of symptoms.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by detecting Y. pestis in blood, sputum, or bubo aspirate. Plague responds well to prompt antibiotics — gentamicin and fluoroquinolones are recommended first-line treatments in the United States, with doxycycline also widely used — and supportive care. Patients with pneumonic plague must be isolated, and close contacts are given prophylactic antibiotics.

Modern occurrence

A few thousand cases are reported worldwide in most years, chiefly in Madagascar, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Peru; the southwestern United States averages about seven cases a year, typically among people exposed to infected rodents or their fleas. Y. pestis is classified as a high-priority bioterrorism agent because of the contagiousness of its pneumonic form.
