The Latin Monetary Union (LMU) was a 19th- and early-20th-century monetary union established by treaty on 23 December 1865. France, Belgium, Italy and Switzerland agreed to mint their gold and silver coins to a common standard — the French franc system — so that each country's coins circulated freely at par in the others. Greece joined in 1868, and several other states adopted the standard without formal membership.

Operation and decline

The union rested on bimetallism at a gold-to-silver ratio of 15.5:1. When world silver prices fell in the 1870s, overvalued silver coins flooded member states, forcing restrictions on silver minting and a de facto move to the gold standard. Strains grew as members such as Italy and Greece issued paper money and debased subsidiary coinage. The union ceased to function effectively with the First World War, when members suspended gold convertibility, and it was formally wound up in 1927.

The LMU is often cited as a historical precursor of European monetary union and the euro.
