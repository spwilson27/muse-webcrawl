Autocorrelation, sometimes known as serial correlation in the discrete-time case, is the correlation of a signal with a delayed copy of itself as a function of the delay. Informally, it is the similarity between observations as a function of the time lag between them. The analysis of autocorrelation is a mathematical tool for finding repeating patterns, such as the presence of a periodic signal obscured by noise, or identifying the missing fundamental frequency in a signal implied by its harmonic frequencies.

Definition

For a stationary random process X, the autocorrelation at lag h is the correlation between values separated by h time units: ρ(h) = Corr(Xt, Xt+h). It ranges from −1 to +1, equalling 1 at lag zero. For a deterministic signal, the autocorrelation is defined via the (time-averaged) product of the signal with its shifted copy.

Applications

Autocorrelation is fundamental in time-series econometrics, where the autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) guide the identification of ARIMA models; residual autocorrelation invalidates ordinary regression inference and is checked with the Durbin–Watson statistic. In signal processing, autocorrelation underlies pitch detection, radar and sonar ranging, and — through the Wiener–Khinchin theorem — is the inverse Fourier transform of the power spectral density. In physics, autocorrelation functions describe diffusion and are used in dynamic light scattering and fluorescence correlation spectroscopy.
