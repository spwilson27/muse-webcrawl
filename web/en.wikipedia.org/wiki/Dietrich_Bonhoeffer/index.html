Dietrich Bonhoeffer (German: [ˈdiːtʁɪç ˈbɔn.høː.fɐ]; 4 February 1906 – 9 April 1945) was a German Lutheran pastor, theologian and anti-Nazi dissident who was a key founding member of the Confessing Church. His writings on Christianity's role in the secular world, including Discipleship (1937) and the posthumous Letters and Papers from Prison (1951), have become widely known.

Life and resistance

Born in Breslau into an accomplished family, Bonhoeffer studied theology at Tübingen and Berlin, completing his doctorate at 21, and spent a formative year at Union Theological Seminary in New York. After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933 he opposed the regime's control of the church, helped found the Confessing Church, and ran its illegal seminary at Finkenwalde. From 1940 he worked under cover of the Abwehr (military intelligence) with the resistance circle around Wilhelm Canaris, carrying messages to the Allies and becoming involved in plots against Hitler.

Arrest and death

Bonhoeffer was arrested in April 1943 and held at Tegel Prison, then transferred through Buchenwald to Flossenbürg concentration camp. After a summary court-martial, he was hanged on 9 April 1945, just weeks before the end of the war in Europe, together with Canaris and other conspirators. He is commemorated as a martyr by many churches, and his concepts of "cheap grace" and "religionless Christianity" remain influential — and contested — in modern theology.
