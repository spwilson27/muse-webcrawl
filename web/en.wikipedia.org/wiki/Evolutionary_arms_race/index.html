Evolutionary arms race

In evolutionary biology, an evolutionary arms race is an ongoing struggle between competing sets of co-evolving genes, traits, or species that develop adaptations and counter-adaptations against each other, resembling an arms race. Examples include predator–prey systems (faster prey select for faster predators), host–parasite systems, and conflict between the sexes.

Red Queen hypothesis

Arms races are a key mechanism behind the Red Queen hypothesis, proposed by Leigh Van Valen in 1973: organisms must constantly adapt merely to maintain their relative fitness against co-evolving antagonists. The analogy is drawn from Lewis Carroll's Through the Looking-Glass, in which the Red Queen tells Alice "it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place".

Examples

The rough-skinned newt produces tetrodotoxin, and its predator the common garter snake has evolved resistance, leading to escalating toxicity and resistance in different populations. Plants evolve chemical defenses such as alkaloids, while herbivorous insects evolve detoxification enzymes. Bats and moths show an acoustic arms race: bats echolocate, and some moths have evolved ears tuned to bat calls and even jamming clicks.

Escalation and limits

Arms races may be symmetric (two species adapting against each other) or asymmetric (one side under stronger selection). They are constrained by costs: extreme adaptations consume resources that could be used for reproduction, so escalation typically reaches an equilibrium rather than proceeding indefinitely.
