Popular science

Popular science (also called popsci) is an interpretation of science intended for a general audience. While scientific literature is a professional medium for scientists to communicate with one another, popular science is produced for the general public, usually by science journalists, scientists writing outside their own fields, and science communicators. It appears in books, magazines, television documentaries, radio programmes, films, websites, and museum exhibitions.

History

Popularisation of science has roots in the scientific revolution; Galileo Galilei wrote dialogues in Italian rather than Latin to reach general readers. In the 19th century, mass literacy and cheap print created a large market for popular science magazines and lecture series, and writers such as Mary Somerville and Michael Faraday's public lectures at the Royal Institution brought science to broad audiences. The genre's modern flowering came in the late 20th century with television presenters and authors such as Carl Sagan, whose book and series Cosmos (1980) reached hundreds of millions of viewers, Stephen Hawking's A Brief History of Time (1988), and Richard Dawkins' The Selfish Gene (1976).

Characteristics

Popular science commonly trades off precision for accessibility. It uses everyday analogies and vivid narrative, simplifies or omits mathematics, and may exaggerate the certainty, novelty or practical importance of results — habits sometimes criticised by scientists as hype. Good popular science nonetheless maintains fidelity to the underlying findings, acknowledges uncertainty, and distinguishes established knowledge from speculation.

Role

Popular science is credited with raising public scientific literacy, inspiring students to enter scientific careers, and informing public debate about topics such as climate change, vaccination, and evolution. It also serves institutional purposes — universities, space agencies and museums use popular formats to justify public funding and disseminate research. Critics of the "deficit model" argue that simply transmitting facts is an incomplete account of how publics engage with science.

Notable outlets

  • Magazines: Scientific American (founded 1845), New Scientist, Popular Science, Discover, National Geographic
  • Television: Cosmos, The Ascent of Man, Planet Earth, MythBusters, BBC Horizon
  • Radio and podcasts: BBC Radio 4 science programmes, Science Friday, Radiolab
