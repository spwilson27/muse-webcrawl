A design method is a procedure, technique, aid or tool for designing. It offers a number of different kinds of activities that a designer might use within an overall design process. Conventional procedures of design, such as drawing, can be regarded as design methods, but since the 1950s new procedures have been developed that are usually grouped together under the name "design methods"; what they have in common is that they are attempts to make public the hitherto private thinking of designers, and to externalise the design process.

History

The design methods movement emerged in the 1950s and 1960s, influenced by operations research and systems thinking, with a landmark conference on design methods held in London in 1962 and the founding of the Design Research Society in 1966. J. Christopher Jones's Design Methods (1970) catalogued dozens of techniques — from brainstorming to morphological charts — structured around divergence, transformation and convergence. Herbert A. Simon's The Sciences of the Artificial (1969) framed design as a science of problem-solving.

Reaction and development

In the 1970s, pioneers such as Jones and Horst Rittel reacted against rigid first-generation methods. Rittel, with Melvin Webber, introduced the notion of "wicked problems" — problems with no definitive formulation or stopping rule — arguing design is argumentative, not merely systematic. Later approaches emphasised reflection-in-action (Donald Schön), user participation (participatory design), and iterative prototyping, feeding into what is now popularised as design thinking and models such as the Design Council's Double Diamond (2005).
