Andrew Cecil Bradley (26 March 1851 – 2 September 1935) was an English literary scholar, best known for his book Shakespearean Tragedy (1904), one of the most influential works of Shakespeare criticism of the twentieth century. He was the younger brother of the idealist philosopher F. H. Bradley.

Career

Bradley studied at Balliol College, Oxford, and became a fellow there. He held the chair of English at University College, Liverpool, then the Regius Professorship of English Language and Literature at the University of Glasgow, and from 1901 to 1906 he was Professor of Poetry at Oxford, where his lectures formed the basis of Shakespearean Tragedy and of the later Oxford Lectures on Poetry (1909).

Shakespearean Tragedy

Bradley's study of Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth treated Shakespeare's characters almost as real people whose psychology could be analysed, and understood tragedy as arising from character expressed in action — "character is destiny". His approach dominated Shakespeare teaching for decades. Mid-century critics such as L. C. Knights, in the famous essay "How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth?" (1933), attacked Bradley's character-centred method, and later criticism emphasised language, staging and history instead; yet Shakespearean Tragedy remains continuously in print and widely read.
