Aleatoric music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aleatoric music (also aleatory music or chance music; from the Latin word alea, meaning "dice") is music in which some element of the composition is left to chance, and/or some primary element of a composed work's realization is left to the determination of its performer(s). The term is most often associated with procedures in which the chance element involves a relatively limited number of possibilities.

History

Precursors include the Musikalisches Würfelspiel (musical dice games) popular in the 18th century, in which phrases were assembled by dice throws, and Charles Ives's experiments with independent musical layers. The term "aleatoric" was promoted in the 1950s by the acoustician Werner Meyer-Eppler. In Europe, Pierre Boulez's essay "Alea" (1957) and works by Karlheinz Stockhausen (such as Klavierstück XI, in which the performer chooses the order of fragments) and Witold Lutosławski (whose "limited aleatorism" synchronised freely repeated passages) developed the idea of mobile form.

John Cage and indeterminacy

In the United States, John Cage used the ancient Chinese I Ching to make compositional decisions, as in Music of Changes (1951), and went on to write works such as 4′33″ (1952), in which the performer makes no deliberate sounds, and Imaginary Landscape No. 4 for twelve radios. Cage distinguished "chance operations" used in composition from "indeterminacy", in which the score itself allows the performer freedom in realisation. His circle — Morton Feldman, Christian Wolff and Earle Brown — developed graphic scores and open forms in related directions.

Techniques

Common aleatoric techniques include instructing performers to choose the ordering of sections, allowing improvised passages within a controlled framework, indeterminate notation, and the use of coin tosses, dice or random number generation during composition. Aleatoric procedures strongly influenced later experimental music, free improvisation and generative and algorithmic composition.
