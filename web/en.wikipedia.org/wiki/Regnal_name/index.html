Regnal name

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
A royal cypher bearing a monarch's regnal initials and number

A regnal name is the name taken by a monarch, pope or other sovereign at accession, under which they reign, as distinct from their birth name. Regnal names are frequently followed by a regnal number, an ordinal written in Roman numerals (e.g. Elizabeth II, Benedict XVI), used to distinguish rulers of the same name within a dynasty or state.

Usage

Many monarchs reign under their first given name, but others choose a different name for symbolic, religious or dynastic reasons. British monarchs may select any of their given names: Queen Victoria reigned under her second name (baptised Alexandrina Victoria), Edward VII under his second name (born Albert Edward), and George VI under his last name (born Albert Frederick Arthur George, known as "Bertie"). Charles III, by contrast, kept his first name on his accession in 2022.

The most systematic use of regnal names is by the papacy: since the early Middle Ages every pope has adopted a new name on election, a custom begun when John II (reigned 533–535), whose given name was Mercurius — the name of a pagan god — took a Christian name instead. Popes choose names to signal continuity with a predecessor, honour a saint, or declare a programme; John Paul I in 1978 was the first to adopt a double name. Papal regnal numbers can be irregular because of antipopes and counting errors, as in the numbering of the Johns and Stephens.

Other traditions

Chinese, Korean and Vietnamese monarchs are known posthumously by temple names and posthumous names, and during their reigns by era names that could change within a reign. Japanese emperors are referred to after death by the name of their era; the reigning emperor is styled simply "His Majesty the Emperor". Some rulers take throne names quite unlike their birth names, as in Thailand, where King Vajiralongkorn's regnal name is also a formal title-string, and in ancient Egypt, where pharaohs adopted a set of throne names (the fivefold titulary) at coronation.
