Wuji (philosophy)

Wuji (Chinese: 無極; pinyin: wújí; lit. 'without limit' or 'without pole') is a term in Chinese philosophy referring to the primordial state of the universe before the differentiation of yin and yang—an undifferentiated, limitless condition of potentiality. It appears in Taoist and Neo-Confucian cosmology, where it precedes and gives rise to Taiji, the "Supreme Ultimate".

Origins

The term appears in the Tao Te Ching (chapter 28), which speaks of returning "to the state of Wuji", and in the Zhuangzi. It also figures in the Yijing tradition. In the 11th century, the Neo-Confucian philosopher Zhou Dunyi placed Wuji at the head of his cosmogony in the Taijitu shuo ("Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate"): "Wuji and then Taiji" (無極而太極)—Taiji, moving, generates yang; at rest, it generates yin, from which arise the Five Phases and the myriad things. The interpretation of Zhou's phrase was famously debated by Zhu Xi and Lu Jiuyuan, as literal readings could imply a state prior to and separate from Taiji, which Zhu Xi resisted.

In martial arts and symbolism

Wuji is represented in Taoist symbolism by an empty circle, in contrast to the taijitu (yin-yang symbol) representing Taiji. In tai chi practice, "wuji" is also the name of the neutral standing posture with which forms begin—still, balanced, and empty of intention.
