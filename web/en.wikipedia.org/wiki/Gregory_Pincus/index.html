Gregory Goodwin Pincus (April 9, 1903 – August 22, 1967) was an American biologist and researcher who co-invented the combined oral contraceptive pill, one of the most significant pharmaceutical developments of the twentieth century.

Early life and education

Pincus was born in Woodbine, New Jersey, to Russian Jewish immigrant parents. He earned his bachelor's degree from Cornell University in 1924 and his doctorate from Harvard University in 1927, where he studied under William E. Castle. He later pursued postdoctoral work at Cambridge University and the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute in Berlin.

Career

After returning to the United States, Pincus taught at Harvard and at Clark University. In 1944 he co-founded the Worcester Foundation for Experimental Biology in Shrewsbury, Massachusetts, with Hudson Hoagland, where much of his later reproductive research was carried out.

His early work included experiments on in vitro fertilization in rabbits, which drew sensational and often hostile press coverage in the 1930s. Later, encouraged and funded by Margaret Sanger and Katharine McCormick, Pincus turned to the search for a hormonal contraceptive. Working with Min Chueh Chang, he demonstrated that progesterone could inhibit ovulation in mammals.

The contraceptive pill

Pincus collaborated with gynecologist John Rock to conduct clinical trials of an oral progestin formulation, first in Massachusetts and then in large-scale field trials in Puerto Rico beginning in 1956. The resulting product, Enovid, was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for menstrual disorders in 1957 and as a contraceptive in 1960. Pincus organized the international conferences on planned parenthood that helped spread family planning programs worldwide.

Death and legacy

Pincus died of myeloid metaplasia, a rare blood disease, in Boston in 1967. He is remembered as a central figure in reproductive biology and in the social transformation brought about by reliable oral contraception.
