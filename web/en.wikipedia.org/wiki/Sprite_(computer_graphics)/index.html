Sprite (computer graphics)

In computer graphics, a sprite is a two-dimensional bitmap that is integrated into a larger scene, most frequently in video games. Originally the term referred to hardware-supported graphical objects that could be moved around the screen without redrawing the background: graphics chips such as the TMS9918 (1979) and the MOS VIC-II of the Commodore 64 composited sprites over the playfield automatically, detecting collisions and priorities in hardware. The word derives from the idea of a small, free-moving "spirit" or fairy flitting over the image.

Hardware sprites

Classic systems imposed strict limits — the Nintendo Entertainment System could show 64 sprites of 8×8 or 8×16 pixels but only eight per scanline, producing the characteristic flicker when more overlapped. The Commodore 64's VIC-II offered eight 24×21-pixel sprites with hardware collision detection, a feature exploited by a generation of programmers. As frame buffers and fast CPUs became standard in the 1990s, dedicated sprite hardware disappeared, but the term survived for any independently moved 2D image.

Modern usage

Today sprites are stored in sprite sheets (texture atlases) combining all animation frames in one image; game engines such as Unity and Godot blit regions of the sheet each frame. In 3D games, "sprites" also denote billboards — flat images that always face the camera — used for particles, foliage and distant characters (impostors). CSS sprites apply the same one-image-many-regions trick to web design to reduce HTTP requests.
