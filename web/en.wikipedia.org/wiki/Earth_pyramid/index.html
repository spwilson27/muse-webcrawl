Earth pyramid

An earth pyramid (also known as an earth pillar or, particularly in North America, a hoodoo) is a tall, thin spire of rock that protrudes from the bottom of an arid basin or badland. Earth pyramids typically consist of relatively soft rock that has been protected from erosion by a harder, more resistant capstone, often a boulder resting on top of the column.

Formation

Earth pyramids form in deposits of glacial till, moraine debris, or weakly cemented sedimentary rock. Rain washes away the finer material on exposed slopes, but where a large hard stone sits on the surface, the material directly beneath it is shielded. Over time the surrounding ground is lowered while the protected column remains, producing a pillar crowned by its capstone. When the cap eventually falls, the pillar erodes rapidly.

Notable examples

Famous groups of earth pyramids occur in South Tyrol, Italy, notably the pyramids of Ritten (Renon) near Bolzano — among the tallest and most elegant in Europe — and the pyramids of Platten near Percha. Similar formations occur at Euseigne in the Swiss canton of Valais, in the French Alps, and in the badlands of the western United States and Canada, where hoodoos are a signature feature of Bryce Canyon National Park in Utah.

Unlike the mushroom rocks produced by wind abrasion in deserts, earth pyramids are chiefly the work of running water and frost weathering in formerly glaciated or sedimentary terrain.
