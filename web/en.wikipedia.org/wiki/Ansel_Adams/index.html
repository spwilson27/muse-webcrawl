Ansel Adams

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Moonrise, Hernandez, New Mexico (1941): a moonlit village and cemetery beneath towering clouds, Adams's most famous photograph]

Ansel Easton Adams (20 February 1902 – 22 April 1984) was an American landscape photographer and environmentalist, famed for his black-and-white images of the American West, especially Yosemite National Park. With Fred Archer he developed the Zone System of exposure and development, and with Edward Weston and others he co-founded Group f/64, championing sharply focused 'straight' photography.

His iconic photographs include Monolith, the Face of Half Dome (1927), Moonrise, Hernandez, New Mexico (1941), Clearing Winter Storm (c. 1937), The Tetons and the Snake River (1942) and Aspens, New Mexico (1958).

Yosemite and straight photography

A San Francisco childhood fall during the 1906 earthquake broke Adams's nose; at 14 a family trip to Yosemite with a Box Brownie fixed his life's subject. Trained as a pianist, he chose photography by 1930 after meeting Paul Strand, and his portfolios for the Sierra Club carried the national parks into American public life. In 1943 he photographed the Manzanar internment camp, publishing Born Free and Equal against the policy.

Legacy

Adams's technical manuals (The Camera, The Negative, The Print) taught generations of photographers; his prints' dramatic tonal range became the popular image of wilderness itself. A committed conservationist and Sierra Club director for 37 years, he lobbied for Kings Canyon National Park and, in 1980, received the Presidential Medal of Freedom. Wilderness areas in California bear his name.
