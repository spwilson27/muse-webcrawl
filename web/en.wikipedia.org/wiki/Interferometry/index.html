Interferometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Interferometry is a technique which uses the interference of superimposed waves to extract information about the object or phenomenon under study. Because the wavelength of light is so short, interferometric measurements can detect changes in distance of a small fraction of a wavelength, making the technique extraordinarily precise. Interferometry is used in astronomy, fibre optics, engineering metrology, seismology, spectroscopy, quantum mechanics, and gravitational-wave detection.

Principles

An interferometer typically splits a beam of electromagnetic radiation into two or more beams that travel different optical paths and are then recombined. The recombined beams produce an interference pattern of bright and dark fringes whose position and contrast depend on the difference in optical path length. By analysing the pattern, one can measure tiny displacements, changes in refractive index, or surface irregularities with sub-wavelength precision.

History

The interference of light was demonstrated by Thomas Young in his double-slit experiment of 1801. In the 1880s Albert A. Michelson developed the interferometer that bears his name; with Edward Morley he used it in the famous Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887, whose null result — no detectable "aether wind" — became a cornerstone of special relativity. In the 20th century, interferometry enabled the measurement of stellar diameters by Michelson and Francis Pease, and the development of Fourier-transform spectroscopy.

Applications

Astronomy

Long-baseline interferometry combines light or radio signals from separated telescopes to synthesise the resolution of a much larger instrument. Radio arrays such as the Very Large Array, and the Event Horizon Telescope — which produced the first image of a black hole shadow in 2019 — rely on interferometric aperture synthesis. Optical interferometers such as CHARA and the VLTI measure stellar diameters and image star surfaces.

Gravitational-wave detection

The LIGO and Virgo observatories are giant Michelson interferometers with arms several kilometres long. A passing gravitational wave changes the relative arm lengths by far less than the width of a proton, yet interferometry is sensitive enough to register the resulting fringe shift; the first direct detection was announced in 2016.

Metrology and industry

Interferometers test optical components, calibrate gauges, measure flatness and surface roughness, and inspect semiconductor wafers. Holographic and speckle interferometry reveal vibrations and stresses in structures, while fibre-optic interferometers serve as precision gyroscopes and sensors.
