Situational crime prevention

Situational crime prevention is a criminological approach that focuses on reducing opportunities for crime rather than on the disposition of offenders. It seeks to reduce the incidence of specific types of crime by making the environment tougher to exploit, using directed managerial and environmental changes. The approach was developed principally by the British criminologist Ronald V. Clarke in the 1980s.

Background

Situational crime prevention draws on rational choice theory and routine activity theory. It assumes that offenders make decisions based on perceived risks, effort, and rewards, and that crime can be reduced by manipulating the immediate environment in which offences occur. Unlike theories that emphasise social causes of crime, situational prevention concentrates on the settings and circumstances of particular crime problems.

Techniques

Clarke and his collaborators organised situational techniques into five categories: increasing the effort required to commit a crime (for example, target hardening and controlling access); increasing the risks (for example, extending guardianship and strengthening formal surveillance); reducing the rewards (for example, removing targets and identifying property); reducing provocations (for example, reducing frustration and avoiding disputes); and removing excuses (for example, setting rules and posting instructions). A widely cited taxonomy lists twenty-five techniques across these categories.

Applications and criticism

Situational measures have been applied to problems such as burglary, vehicle theft, vandalism, fare evasion, and suicide prevention at hot spots. Evaluations have generally found that displacement of crime to other places or methods is less common than critics feared, and that "diffusion of benefits" to nearby areas often occurs. Critics argue that situational approaches may neglect deeper social causes of crime and can produce more controlled, surveilled public spaces.
