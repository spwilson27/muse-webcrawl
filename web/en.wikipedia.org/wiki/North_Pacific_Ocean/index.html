North Pacific Ocean

The North Pacific Ocean is the northern division of the Pacific Ocean, separated from the South Pacific by the equator. It is bounded by Asia and Australia to the west and by the Americas to the east, extending north to the Bering Strait, where it meets the Arctic Ocean. It is the largest single body of water in the Northern Hemisphere.

Geography

Marginal seas of the North Pacific include the Bering Sea, the Sea of Okhotsk, the Sea of Japan, the Yellow Sea, the East China Sea, the South China Sea (often treated separately), and the Philippine Sea, as well as the Gulf of Alaska and the Gulf of California. Major island groups include the Aleutian Islands, the Kuril Islands, the Japanese archipelago, Taiwan, the Philippines, Hawaii, and the islands of Micronesia. The Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, the deepest point on Earth, lies in the western North Pacific.

Circulation

The basin is dominated by the North Pacific Gyre, a great clockwise circulation driven by the North Equatorial Current, the warm Kuroshio Current flowing north past Japan, the North Pacific Current, and the cold California Current returning south along the American coast. A separate counterclockwise subpolar gyre turns in the Gulf of Alaska. Within the subtropical gyre, floating debris accumulates in the area known as the Great Pacific garbage patch.

Climate and ecology

The North Pacific generates tropical cyclones (typhoons) in its western basin and powerful extratropical storms in the Gulf of Alaska. Climate patterns such as the Pacific Decadal Oscillation and El Niño strongly influence its surface temperatures and fisheries. The basin supports major fisheries for salmon, tuna, pollock, and squid, and its coasts — from Siberia and Japan to British Columbia and California — are lined with some of the world's busiest ports, including Shanghai, Singapore-adjacent routes, Busan, Los Angeles, and Long Beach.
