Otosclerosis

Otosclerosis is an abnormal remodelling of bone in the middle ear, in which the normally dense endochondral bone of the otic capsule is replaced by irregular spongy bone (otospongiosis) that may become sclerotic over time. Most characteristically it involves the footplate of the stapes, fixing it in the oval window and producing a progressive conductive hearing loss. It is one of the most common causes of acquired conductive hearing loss in adults.

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark is a slowly progressive conductive hearing loss, usually beginning between the ages of 15 and 45 and often affecting both ears. Tinnitus is common, and some patients experience mild vertigo. Paradoxically, hearing may seem better in noisy environments (paracusis of Willis), because speakers raise their voices. On otoscopy the eardrum usually appears normal, though a pink blush from hyperaemia of the promontory (Schwartze sign) is occasionally visible. If the cochlea is involved, a mixed or sensorineural loss occurs.

Causes

The exact cause is unknown. There is a strong genetic component — about half of patients have a family history, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance. Hormonal influences are suggested by a female-to-male ratio of about 2:1 and progression during pregnancy. Associations with persistent measles virus infection of the otic capsule and with autoimmune processes have also been proposed.

Diagnosis

Audiometry shows a conductive loss with a characteristic dip in bone-conduction thresholds at 2 kHz (Carhart's notch). Tympanometry may show a shallow (As-type) curve reflecting a stiffened ossicular chain, and the stapedial reflex is typically absent. High-resolution CT can demonstrate lucent foci in the otic capsule in more advanced disease.

Treatment

Hearing aids effectively treat the conductive loss. The main surgical treatment is stapedotomy (or stapedectomy), in which the fixed stapes is replaced by a prosthesis — typically a piston of Teflon or titanium — that transmits vibrations from the incus to the inner ear; success rates in restoring hearing exceed 90% in experienced hands, though there is a small risk of profound sensorineural loss. Sodium fluoride and bisphosphonates have been used in attempts to retard cochlear involvement, with uncertain benefit.

Epidemiology

Histological otosclerosis is found in about 10% of people of European ancestry, though only around 1% develop clinical hearing loss. It is less common in African and East Asian populations. The condition is famously speculated — without firm evidence — to have contributed to Ludwig van Beethoven's deafness.
