Gezira Scheme

The Gezira Scheme (Arabic: مشروع الجزيرة) is one of the largest irrigation projects in the world, situated in Sudan's Gezira State, in the fertile plain between the Blue Nile and the White Nile south of Khartoum. Established by the British colonial administration in 1925, it irrigates roughly 800,000 hectares (about two million acres), producing cotton, wheat, groundnuts, sorghum and vegetables, and for decades underpinned the Sudanese economy as its principal source of export earnings and employment.

History

The scheme began with pilot cotton plots at Tayiba in 1911. Its viability depended on the Sennar Dam on the Blue Nile, completed in 1925, which allowed gravity irrigation of the gently sloping plain through a vast network of canals — remarkable in that the water flows by gravity alone without pumping. Under the Anglo-Egyptian condominium, the scheme was run as a partnership between the government, the Sudan Plantations Syndicate (a British concession company) and tenant farmers, who shared the proceeds of the cotton crop. After Sudanese independence in 1956, management passed to the Sudan Gezira Board, and the Managil extension (opened 1962) added some 800,000 feddans to the project.

Organisation

Land was divided into standard tenancies (hawashas) rotated between cotton, food and fodder crops, and fallow. Tenants — numbering over 100,000 at the scheme's height — received water, seed and credit from the Board, while the state maintained canals, ginneries and the light Gezira railway. Cotton, especially long-staple varieties, supplied the bulk of Sudan's foreign exchange for much of the twentieth century.

Decline and reform

From the 1970s the scheme suffered from silted canals, deteriorating infrastructure, falling cotton prices, debt and mismanagement; yields and tenant incomes dropped sharply. A World Bank-supported rehabilitation began in the 1980s. The Gezira Scheme Act of 2005 reorganised the project, ending the Board's monopoly over inputs and marketing and giving farmers greater freedom to choose crops, though critics argued the reforms dismantled the scheme's cooperative structures. Reviving the scheme has remained a stated goal of successive Sudanese governments.
