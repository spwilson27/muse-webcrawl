USS Constitution

USS Constitution is a wooden-hulled, three-masted heavy frigate of the United States Navy. She is the world's oldest commissioned naval warship still afloat. She was launched in 1797, one of the six original frigates authorised by the Naval Act of 1794, and built in Boston to the design of Joshua Humphreys: ships longer and more heavily armed than European frigates, fast enough to outrun anything they could not outfight. She served against the Barbary corsairs in the Barbary Wars and won her immortal fame in the War of 1812, when her victories over the British frigates Guerriere and Java and the two sloops Cyane and Levant stunned the Royal Navy and earned her the nickname "Old Ironsides" — from the report that British shot were seen to bounce from her live-oak hull during the Guerriere action of 19 August 1812. Retired from frontline service in the 1850s and condemned to break-up in 1830 — a sentence reversed by public outcry and Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr.'s poem "Old Ironsides" — she survives as a museum ship in Boston, still crewed by US Navy sailors, and sails under her own power on ceremonial turnarounds.

Construction and early service

Humphreys' frigates were built of American live oak, an exceptionally dense timber, with diagonal riders stiffening a hull of unusual length and scantling for a frigate. Constitution was built at Edmund Hartt's shipyard in Boston's North End — Paul Revere supplied copper fastenings and fittings from his foundry — and launched on 21 October 1797 after two failed attempts that stuck her on the ways. She first put to sea in 1798 during the Quasi-War with France, then from 1803 served as flagship of Commodore Edward Preble's Mediterranean squadron in the First Barbary War, blockading Tripoli and supporting the bombardments of 1804.

The War of 1812

The frigate's legend was made in six months. In July 1812, days into the war, Captain Isaac Hull escaped a British squadron in a celebrated three-day chase of becalmed towing and kedging. On 19 August 1812 he met HMS Guerriere south of the Grand Banks and in a furious half-hour dismasted and shattered her; an American sailor, seeing British round shot rebound from Constitution's oak sides, was said to have cried "Huzzah! Her sides are made of iron!" — Guerriere, too damaged to keep, was burned. Under Captain William Bainbridge in December 1812 she destroyed HMS Java off Brazil (Bainbridge twice wounded, the ship's wheel shot away). In February 1815, under Charles Stewart, she captured the corvette Cyane and sloop Levant together off Madeira — after the peace treaty had been signed but before anyone in those waters knew. British frigate captains were thereafter forbidden to engage the big American frigates singly.

Later life and preservation

Constitution led squadrons in the Mediterranean and Pacific, suppressed the slave trade on the African station, and in 1844–46 made a 30-month circumnavigation of some 52,000 miles. In 1830, reported unseaworthy, she was ordered sold; Holmes's poem in the Boston Daily Advertiser — "Ay, tear her tattered ensign down!" — roused the public to demand her preservation, and she was rebuilt in 1833. She served as a training and receiving ship, was reclassified a museum in 1907, toured American ports in a national restoration cruise of 1931–34, and underwent full restorations in 1925–30, 1973–76 and 1992–97 — the last returning her to sailing condition, so that on 21 July 1997, for her bicentenary, "Old Ironsides" sailed unassisted for the first time in 116 years. She remains in commission, berthed at her Boston shipyard at the end of the Freedom Trail, making annual turnaround cruises in Boston Harbor each Fourth of July.
