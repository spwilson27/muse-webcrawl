Cervical cancer is a cancer arising from the cervix, the lower part of the uterus connecting to the vagina. It is the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide, with roughly 600,000 new cases and 340,000 deaths in 2020, the great majority in low- and middle-income countries. Virtually all cases are caused by persistent infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV), especially types 16 and 18.

Signs and prevention

Early disease is usually asymptomatic; later symptoms may include abnormal vaginal bleeding, pelvic pain and discharge. Cervical cancer is highly preventable: HPV vaccines (such as Gardasil and Cervarix) protect against the main oncogenic types, and screening with the Pap test or HPV DNA testing detects precancerous changes that can be treated before cancer develops. The World Health Organization has adopted a global strategy aiming to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem through vaccination, screening and treatment targets.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by biopsy, usually following colposcopy; staging uses imaging and the FIGO system. Treatment depends on stage and includes surgery (such as hysterectomy), radiation therapy and chemotherapy, often combined. Prognosis is good for early-stage disease but poorer once the cancer has spread.
