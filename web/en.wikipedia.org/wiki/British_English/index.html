British English

British English (BrE) is the set of varieties of the English language native to the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland and more broadly used across the British Isles. It encompasses regional accents and dialects from Received Pronunciation to Scots, Welsh English and Northern Irish English, as well as the written standard used in government, education and publishing.

History

English developed from the dialects of the Angles, Saxons and Jutes who settled Britain in the 5th and 6th centuries. The standard written form was shaped by the Chancery Standard of the 15th century, Caxton's printing press, and Samuel Johnson's Dictionary of 1755, which largely fixed British spelling conventions such as -our (colour) and -re (centre).

Spelling

Vocabulary

Distinctive British terms include lorry (truck), flat (apartment), holiday (vacation), rubber (eraser), boot (car trunk) and queue (line). Collective nouns may take plural verbs ("the team are winning"), and the present perfect is preferred where American English often uses the simple past ("I've just eaten" vs "I just ate").

Accents and dialects

Britain has remarkable accent diversity over short distances: Cockney, Geordie, Scouse, Brummie, West Country and Highland English among them. Received Pronunciation, once the prestige standard of the BBC and public schools, is now spoken natively by only a small minority, and regional accents are common in national broadcasting.
