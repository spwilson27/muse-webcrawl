Çatalhöyük

Çatalhöyük is a large Neolithic and Chalcolithic settlement in southern Anatolia, in present-day Turkey, occupied from about 7500 BC to 6400 BC. It was one of the world's earliest large settled communities, with a population estimated at up to 8,000 people. The site is famous for its densely packed mudbrick houses, accessed by ladders through openings in the roofs, and for its wall paintings, reliefs, and figurines. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2012.

Discovery and excavation

The site, two mounds near the village of Küçükköy on the Konya Plain, was identified in the 1950s and first excavated by James Mellaart between 1961 and 1965, revealing striking art including wall paintings and the famous seated female figurine flanked by felines. Large-scale excavations resumed under Ian Hodder from 1993 to 2017, employing reflexive and contextual archaeological methods.

The settlement

Houses at Çatalhöyük were built wall-to-wall with no streets; movement across the settlement took place over the rooftops, and rooms were entered by ladder. Interiors featured platforms, ovens, and plastered walls renewed many times. The dead were buried beneath the floors of houses, sometimes with grave goods, and skulls were occasionally retrieved and plastered.

Economy and society

Residents cultivated wheat, barley, peas, and lentils, herded sheep and goats, and hunted wild cattle and other game. Obsidian from Cappadocia was worked and traded widely. The relative uniformity of houses has suggested a broadly egalitarian society, though recent research emphasises variation between households.
