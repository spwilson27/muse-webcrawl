Rowland Hill

Sir Rowland Hill, KCB, FRS (3 December 1795 – 27 August 1879) was an English teacher, inventor and social reformer. He campaigned for a comprehensive reform of the British postal system based on the concept of Uniform Penny Post — a low, uniform rate of postage prepaid by the sender — and is credited with inventing the adhesive postage stamp. His reforms, implemented in 1840, transformed postal communication, made sending letters cheap for the general public, and provided the model for postal systems worldwide.

Early life

Hill was born in Kidderminster, the son of schoolmaster Thomas Wright Hill. He became a teacher at his father's school, Hazelwood School in Birmingham, which was run on progressive principles and described in the influential treatise Plans for the Government and Liberal Instruction of Boys (1822). He later helped found the Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge and campaigned on issues including emigration to South Australia, where he served as a colonisation commissioner.

Postal reform

In the 1830s the British post was expensive, complex and corruption-prone: charges depended on distance and the number of sheets, were usually paid by the recipient, and could amount to a day's wages for a single letter. In 1837 Hill published his pamphlet Post Office Reform: Its Importance and Practicability, arguing that postal costs were overwhelmingly in handling rather than transport, so that a single low charge — one penny per half-ounce, regardless of distance within the United Kingdom — would be both profitable and socially transformative. He proposed prepayment, evidenced by "a bit of paper just large enough to bear the stamp" that could be affixed to the letter: the postage stamp.

After a public campaign and parliamentary inquiry, the Uniform Penny Post was introduced on 10 January 1840. In May 1840 the world's first adhesive postage stamps went on sale: the Penny Black and the Two Pence Blue, bearing the profile of Queen Victoria. Letter volumes rose dramatically, and prepayment by stamps quickly became universal. Hill worked within the Post Office and the Treasury to implement his system, becoming Secretary to the Postmaster General, and later Secretary of the Post Office, before retiring in 1864 with many honours, including a knighthood.

Legacy

Hill's penny post and the postage stamp were adopted around the world within a few decades and laid the foundation for the Universal Postal Union (1874). He died in 1879 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. Statues of Hill stand in Kidderminster and outside the former General Post Office in London.
