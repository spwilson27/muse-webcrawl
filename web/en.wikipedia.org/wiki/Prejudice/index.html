Prejudice


Prejudice involves preconceived attitudes toward members of a group.
Prejudice
FieldSocial psychology, sociology
ComponentsAttitude (affect), stereotype (cognition), discrimination (behaviour)
Common targetsRace, ethnicity, religion, sex, age, disability, sexual orientation
Key theoristsGordon Allport, Henri Tajfel, Theodor Adorno

Prejudice is an affective feeling — usually negative — toward a person or group based solely on that person's group membership. The word, from the Latin praejudicium ("prior judgment"), often refers to preconceived evaluations or classifications made without adequate knowledge or reasoning. In social psychology, prejudice is classically analysed in an ABC model alongside its cognitive component, the stereotype, and its behavioural component, discrimination.

Theories of prejudice

  • Authoritarian personality: Adorno and colleagues (1950) proposed that ethnocentrism arises from a rigid, hierarchy-oriented personality style.
  • Realistic conflict theory: competition over scarce resources generates intergroup hostility, demonstrated in Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment.
  • Social identity theory: Henri Tajfel's minimal group experiments showed that mere categorisation into groups produces in-group favouritism; people derive self-esteem from group membership.
  • Cognitive accounts: categorisation, illusory correlation, and out-group homogeneity effects make stereotypes easy to form and hard to dislodge.
  • System justification and social dominance theory: prejudice may function to legitimise existing hierarchies.

Implicit prejudice

Contemporary research distinguishes explicit, self-reported prejudice from implicit bias — automatic associations measured by tools such as the Implicit Association Test (IAT), introduced by Greenwald, McGhee, and Schwartz in 1998. Implicit associations can predict discriminatory behaviour even among people who sincerely endorse egalitarian values, though the strength and practical implications of such links remain debated.

Reducing prejudice

Gordon Allport's contact hypothesis (1954) holds that intergroup contact reduces prejudice when groups meet with equal status, common goals, intergroup cooperation, and institutional support — conditions supported by later meta-analyses. Other interventions include perspective-taking, extended and imagined contact, media representation, and education.

Forms

Specific prejudices are often named for their targets: racism, antisemitism, Islamophobia, sexism, homophobia, ageism, ableism, and xenophobia, among others. Historically, prejudice has underpinned slavery, colonialism, segregation, and genocide, and it remains a subject of civil-rights law and public policy worldwide.
