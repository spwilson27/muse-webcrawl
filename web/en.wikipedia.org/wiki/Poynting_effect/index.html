Poynting effect

The Poynting effect is a phenomenon in solid mechanics, named after the English physicist John Henry Poynting, in which a material subjected to large shear or torsion develops stresses or strains in the direction normal to the shear — a second-order (nonlinear) elastic effect not predicted by classical infinitesimal elasticity.

Description

When a cylindrical rod is twisted, Poynting observed that it tends to elongate slightly; conversely, if its length is held fixed, a tensile force must be applied. The effect scales with the square of the twist angle, making it negligible for small strains but measurable in soft materials such as rubber under finite deformation.

Related effects

The Poynting effect is related to the normal-stress effects seen in non-Newtonian fluids, in which shearing a viscoelastic liquid generates normal stresses responsible for phenomena such as the Weissenberg (rod-climbing) effect. In modern continuum mechanics, these effects are described by nonlinear constitutive models such as the Mooney–Rivlin and Ogden models for hyperelastic materials.

Significance

The effect matters in the mechanics of rubber-like materials, biological soft tissues and polymers under large deformation, and it historically provided early evidence that elasticity at finite strain involves more than the linear Hooke's-law constants.
