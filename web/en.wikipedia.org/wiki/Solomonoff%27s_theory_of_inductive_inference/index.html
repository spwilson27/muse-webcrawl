Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference


A mathematical theory of prediction based on algorithmic probability, developed by Ray Solomonoff in the 1960s.

Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference is a mathematical framework for prediction developed by Ray Solomonoff in the 1960s. It combines Occam's razor with Bayesian probability: hypotheses (programs) that generate the observed data are weighted by their algorithmic probability, favouring shorter programs.

Algorithmic probability

The central quantity is the universal prior, defined by summing over all programs on a universal Turing machine that produce a given output, each weighted by 2 raised to the negative of its length in bits. Conditioning this prior on observed data yields predictions that are provably close to optimal for any computable environment.

Limitations and influence

Because the universal prior is incomputable, the theory serves as an ideal reference rather than a practical algorithm. It underlies later work on minimum description length, Kolmogorov complexity, and the theory of universal artificial intelligence (AIXI) developed by Marcus Hutter.
