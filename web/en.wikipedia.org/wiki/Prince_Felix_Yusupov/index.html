Prince Felix Felixovich Yusupov, Count Sumarokov-Elston (23 March [O.S. 11 March] 1887 – 27 September 1967) was a Russian aristocrat from the Yusupov family, one of the richest families in Imperial Russia. He is best known for participating in the assassination of Grigori Rasputin, the Siberian mystic and faith healer who had gained influence over Tsar Nicholas II and Tsarina Alexandra.

Early life

Yusupov was born in Saint Petersburg, the younger son of Princess Zinaida Yusupova, heiress of the immense Yusupov fortune, and Count Felix Sumarokov-Elston. After the death of his elder brother Nicholas in a duel in 1908, Felix became heir to the family estates, which included palaces in Saint Petersburg and Moscow and vast lands across Russia. From 1909 to 1913 he studied Fine Arts at the University of Oxford, where he was known for his lavish lifestyle and founded the Oxford University Russian Society.

Marriage

In 1914 Yusupov married Princess Irina Alexandrovna, the only daughter of Grand Duke Alexander Mikhailovich and Grand Duchess Xenia Alexandrovna and the only niece of Nicholas II. Because of the marriage he was permitted to use the Yusupov name and title. The couple had one daughter, Irina, born in 1915.

Assassination of Rasputin

Convinced that Rasputin's influence over the imperial couple was destroying the monarchy and Russia's war effort, Yusupov joined a conspiracy with Grand Duke Dmitri Pavlovich, the politician Vladimir Purishkevich and others. On the night of 29–30 December 1916 (16–17 December Old Style), Rasputin was lured to Yusupov's Moika Palace in Petrograd, where he was shot; his body was thrown into the Malaya Nevka River. Yusupov's later accounts of the evening — including the famous tale that Rasputin survived poison and multiple gunshots — are regarded by historians as partly embellished. The conspirators were punished lightly: Yusupov was exiled to one of his estates.

Exile and later life

After the Russian Revolution of 1917, the Yusupovs escaped Russia aboard a British warship in 1919 and settled in Paris, where Felix later ran the fashion house Irfé with his wife. In 1932 he won a libel action against MGM over the film Rasputin and the Empress, a case that led studios to adopt the standard "all persons fictitious" disclaimer. He published memoirs describing his role in the assassination and died in Paris in 1967.
