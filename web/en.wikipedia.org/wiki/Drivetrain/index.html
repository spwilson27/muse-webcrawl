Drivetrain

A drivetrain (also frequently spelled drive train) or transmission system is the group of components that deliver mechanical power from the prime mover to the driven components. In automotive engineering, the drivetrain includes all of the components used to transform stored (chemical, solar, nuclear, kinetic, potential, etc.) energy into kinetic energy for propulsion purposes. This includes the utilisation of power sources, such as the engine and transmission peripherals like couplings, gearbox, drive shafts, differential and drive wheels.

Components

  • Clutch / torque converter — connects and disconnects the engine from the rest of the system and smooths engagement.
  • Transmission (gearbox) — provides speed and torque conversions from a rotating power source using gear ratios.
  • Drive shaft — transmits power over distance between components.
  • Differential — splits power between wheels while allowing them to rotate at different speeds.
  • Axles and half-shafts — deliver power from the differential to the wheels.

Layouts

The most common drivetrain layouts in passenger cars are front-engine front-wheel drive (FF), front-engine rear-wheel drive (FR), and all-wheel drive (AWD) variants, along with mid-engine and rear-engine layouts used in sports cars. In electric vehicles the "drivetrain" may consist simply of one or more electric motors, a single-speed reduction gear, and a differential, greatly reducing the number of moving parts.

Powertrain

The term powertrain is often used interchangeably with drivetrain, although strictly it includes the engine (or motor) itself, whereas the drivetrain comprises only the components downstream of the power source. In industry usage the distinction is inconsistent.

Non-automotive applications

Drivetrains also exist in bicycles (chain and sprockets, belt or shaft drives), industrial machinery, wind turbines (rotor, low-speed shaft, gearbox, high-speed shaft, generator), and marine propulsion systems.
