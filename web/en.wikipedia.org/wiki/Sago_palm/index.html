The sago palm (Metroxylon sagu) is a species of palm in the genus Metroxylon, native to tropical southeastern Asia. It is the principal source of sago, a starch extracted from the pith of its stem. The name is also loosely applied to other palms whose stems yield sago, and to several unrelated cycads sometimes called "sago palms" (notably Cycas revoluta), which are toxic and not a source of food starch.

Description

The true sago palm is a suckering, once-flowering (hapaxanthic) palm. It forms a stout trunk 7–25 m tall topped by a crown of large pinnate leaves, each up to 7 m long. The palm grows for roughly 15 years, accumulating starch in its trunk, then produces a huge terminal inflorescence and dies after fruiting. Suckers at the base allow clumps to persist.

Distribution and habitat

Metroxylon sagu is native to Malaysia, Indonesia (including Maluku and New Guinea), Brunei, the Philippines, and parts of Melanesia. It thrives in freshwater swamps, floodplains, and lowland rainforest where few other food crops grow well, tolerating waterlogged, acidic, and nutrient-poor soils.

Cultivation and harvest

Sago palms are usually harvested at 10–15 years of age, just before flowering, when starch content peaks. The trunk is felled and split, the pith extracted and processed by washing to separate the starch, which settles out as a fine powder. A single trunk can yield 150–300 kg of dry sago. Because the plant regenerates from suckers, groves are semi-managed and can be harvested on a continuing basis.

Uses

The starch is a staple carbohydrate for millions of people in New Guinea and Maluku, eaten as a paste (papeda), pancakes, and baked goods. The leaves are used for thatch, the leaf sheaths and trunk fibre for building materials, and the grubs that live in decaying trunks (sago worms) are eaten as a delicacy.
