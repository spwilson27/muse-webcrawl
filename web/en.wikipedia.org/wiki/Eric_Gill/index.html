Eric Gill

Arthur Eric Rowton Gill (22 February 1882 – 17 November 1940) was an English sculptor, letter cutter, typeface designer, and printmaker, associated with the Arts and Crafts movement. He designed the widely used typefaces Gill Sans and Perpetua.

Work

Born in Brighton, Gill trained as an architect's draughtsman before turning to lettering and stone carving. His major sculptural commissions include the Stations of the Cross in Westminster Cathedral (1913–1918), the figures of Prospero and Ariel on Broadcasting House in London, and carvings for the League of Nations building in Geneva. Working with the Monotype Corporation he produced typefaces including Perpetua (1925), Gill Sans (1928), and Joanna. He founded workshops and communities at Ditchling in Sussex and later at Capel-y-ffin and Pigotts, and wrote influential essays on art, typography, and society.

Reputation

Gill's diaries, published in Fiona MacCarthy's 1989 biography, revealed sexual abuse within his family, prompting continuing debate about his works, including calls for the removal of some public sculptures, alongside recognition of his major contribution to twentieth-century British art and typography.
