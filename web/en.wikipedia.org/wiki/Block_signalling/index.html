Block signalling

[Hero image: a British lower-quadrant semaphore signal protecting the entrance to a block section]

Block signalling is a system of railway signalling in which the line is divided into fixed sections or "blocks", and trains may only enter a block when it is clear of other trains. It is one of the fundamental methods of preventing collisions on railways and underlies most signalling practice worldwide, including modern cab signalling systems such as ETCS, which retain the concept as "movement authorities".

Principles

A block section is a length of track between two designated points, usually signals. The basic rule is that only one train may occupy a block at a time. A signaller (dispatcher) or an automatic system grants permission for a train to enter the next block, traditionally by lowering a semaphore or showing a green light. Track circuits or axle counters detect the presence of trains and feed back to the signalling system whether each block is occupied.

Types

Absolute block

Under absolute block working, no train may enter an occupied block under any normal circumstances. It was developed in Britain in the 19th century and became mandatory there after the Regulation of Railways Act 1889, following accidents such as the Armagh rail disaster.

Permissive block

Permissive working allows a second train to enter an occupied block under strict conditions, commonly for freight trains to approach a preceding train at low speed, or for trains to couple together.

Token and staff systems

On single lines, physical tokens, staffs, or electronic token instruments ensure that only the driver holding the token for a section may enter it. Variants include the electric token block and the one-engine-in-steam system.

Automatic block signalling

In automatic block signalling (ABS), track circuits control the signals directly without signaller intervention, allowing shorter headways on busy lines. Multi-aspect colour-light signals divide the approach into several blocks, giving drivers progressive warnings.

Moving block

Modern communications-based train control (CBTC) and ETCS Level 3 abandon fixed blocks in favour of a "moving block", in which each train continuously calculates a safe braking distance to the train ahead, increasing capacity.
