Standoff missile

Standoff missiles allow launch aircraft to strike targets from outside defended airspace.

A standoff missile is a missile designed to be launched from a sufficient distance (standoff range) from its target that the launching platform — typically an aircraft or ship — remains outside the range of defensive weapons and interceptor aircraft. The concept applies to both air-to-surface missiles and some surface-launched weapons.

Rationale

As surface-to-air missile systems and integrated air defences became more capable during the Cold War, penetrating defended airspace to drop unguided or short-range weapons became increasingly hazardous. Standoff weapons allow bombers and strike aircraft to attack command posts, airfields, bridges, and air-defence sites from tens or hundreds of kilometres away, reducing losses of aircraft and crews.

Examples

  • AGM-86 ALCM — American air-launched cruise missile
  • AGM-158 JASSM — American low-observable cruise missile
  • Storm Shadow / SCALP — British-French air-launched cruise missile
  • Taurus KEPD 350 — German-Swedish cruise missile
  • Kalibr — Russian cruise missile family
  • AGM-154 JSOW — American glide bomb/standoff weapon

Characteristics

Most standoff missiles are cruise missiles powered by small turbofan or turbojet engines, flying at subsonic speed and low altitude, and guided by combinations of inertial navigation, GPS, terrain contour matching, and terminal seekers. Some are glide weapons without engines that rely on the altitude and speed of the launching aircraft. Stealth shaping and low radar cross-section are common design features.
