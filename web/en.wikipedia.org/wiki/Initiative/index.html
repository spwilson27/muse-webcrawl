Initiative

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The initiative is a process in direct democracy that enables citizens to propose a statute or constitutional amendment and, if sufficient support is gathered in the form of petition signatures, to force a popular vote on the proposal, bypassing the legislature. It exists at national level in Switzerland (100,000 signatures for a constitutional initiative) and in about half of US states (California's being the most used — signature thresholds are set as percentages of votes cast for governor), and in various forms elsewhere (Italy's abrogative referendums are a related citizen-triggered instrument; the EU's European Citizens' Initiative only invites the Commission to propose, without a vote).

Debates

Initiatives are praised for breaking legislative gridlock and enacting popular measures legislatures avoid (term limits, tax limits, animal welfare, drug liberalisation) — California's Proposition 13 (1978) reshaped US fiscal politics. Critics cite professionalised signature-gathering (money buying ballot access), cluttered and contradictory statute books, fiscal constraints imposed without budget responsibility, and vulnerability to well-funded campaigns and voter fatigue. Design variables — signature thresholds, single-subject rules, legislative counter-proposals, supermajorities for fiscal measures — mediate these trade-offs.
