Guide star

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In astronomy, a guide star is a reference star used to maintain the accurate pointing of a telescope or other instrument during an observation. Because of the apparent rotation of the sky and imperfections in telescope drives, long exposures would drift; a guide star, kept centred on a guiding camera or an autoguider, supplies corrections that keep the target fixed.

Guide stars are also central to adaptive optics: measuring how a bright star's image is distorted by the atmosphere lets deformable mirrors correct the blurring in real time. A natural guide star must be bright and lie close to the science target, which limits the sky that can be corrected. To overcome this, observatories create laser guide stars — artificial stars produced by shining a laser tuned to excite sodium atoms in the mesosphere, about 90 km up, or by Rayleigh scattering at lower altitudes. Facilities such as the Keck and Very Large Telescope observatories rely on laser guide stars for routine adaptive-optics observing.

In spacecraft navigation the same idea appears in star trackers and fine guidance sensors: the Hubble Space Telescope, for example, locks onto guide stars from a dedicated catalogue (the Guide Star Catalog, originally compiled for Hubble) to hold its pointing stable to milli-arcsecond precision.
