German Americans

Total populationOver 40 million (largest ancestry group in the United States)
RegionsMidwest (the "German Belt"), Pennsylvania, Texas, Ohio, Wisconsin, Indiana
LanguagesAmerican English; German and dialects (Pennsylvania Dutch, Texas German)
ReligionsProtestantism, Catholicism, Judaism, others

German Americans (German: Deutschamerikaner) are Americans of full or partial German ancestry. With a population of more than 40 million, they form the largest ancestry group in the United States, ahead of Irish and English Americans. German immigration began in the colonial era and peaked in the nineteenth century, profoundly shaping American culture, industry and civic life.

History

The first permanent German settlement, Germantown, Pennsylvania, was founded in 1683. Large waves followed in the eighteenth century (the "Pennsylvania Dutch") and especially after the failed revolutions of 1848, when political refugees known as Forty-Eighters arrived alongside millions of economic migrants. Germans settled heavily across the Midwest — Cincinnati, St. Louis and Milwaukee formed a "German triangle" — as well as in Texas and the Great Plains.

During the First and Second World Wars, German Americans faced suspicion, suppression of German-language institutions and pressure to assimilate, which sharply reduced public use of the German language. Many German cultural institutions nonetheless survived or were revived.

Culture and contributions

German immigrants introduced or popularised customs such as the Christmas tree and Easter bunny, foods including hamburgers, frankfurters and lager beer, and institutions such as kindergartens and Turnverein athletic societies. Prominent German Americans include Carl Schurz, Dwight D. Eisenhower (of Pennsylvania Dutch descent), and numerous figures in science, business and the arts. Annual celebrations such as Oktoberfest and Steuben parades remain popular in many American cities.
