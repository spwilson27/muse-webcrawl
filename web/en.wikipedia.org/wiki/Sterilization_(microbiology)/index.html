Sterilization (microbiology)

Sterilization (British spelling: sterilisation) refers to any process that removes, kills, or deactivates all forms of life (particularly microorganisms such as fungi, bacteria, spores, and unicellular eukaryotic organisms) and other biological agents such as prions present in or on a specific surface, object, or fluid. Sterilization is distinct from disinfection, sanitization, and pasteurization, in that those methods reduce rather than eliminate all forms of life and biological agents present. After sterilization, an object is referred to as being sterile or aseptic.

Quantification

Sterility is conventionally expressed in terms of a sterility assurance level (SAL): a SAL of 10−6 means there is at most a one-in-a-million probability that a single viable microorganism remains on an item after sterilization. The resistance of the most resistant organisms, bacterial endospores such as those of Geobacillus stearothermophilus, is characterised by D-values (the time needed to achieve a 90% reduction in population) and is monitored with biological indicators.

Heat sterilization

Moist heat

Saturated steam under pressure in an autoclave is the most widely used and dependable method. A typical cycle exposes items to steam at 121 °C (250 °F) for at least 15 minutes, or 134 °C for shorter periods. Moist heat kills by irreversibly denaturing proteins and is effective against spores.

Dry heat

Dry heat, applied in hot-air ovens at 160–170 °C for one to two hours, kills by oxidation. It is used for powders, oils, and glassware that cannot tolerate moisture, and at higher temperatures it can also depyrogenate.

Incineration and flaming

Direct flaming of inoculating loops and complete incineration of contaminated waste destroy microorganisms outright.

Chemical sterilization

Gases such as ethylene oxide and hydrogen peroxide plasma sterilise heat-sensitive medical devices, while liquid chemical sterilants such as glutaraldehyde and peracetic acid are used for instruments that cannot be heated. Ethylene oxide is highly effective but toxic and flammable, requiring long aeration times.

Radiation

Ionizing radiation — gamma rays from cobalt-60, electron beams, and X-rays — sterilises single-use medical supplies such as syringes and catheters on an industrial scale. Ultraviolet light is used for surface and air treatment but has poor penetrating power.

Filtration

Sterile filtration through membranes with 0.2 µm pores removes bacteria from heat-labile liquids such as pharmaceuticals and culture media, though it does not remove viruses or prions.

Applications

Sterilization is essential in surgery and medicine, pharmaceutical manufacturing, microbiology laboratories, food production (for example ultra-high-temperature processing of milk), and spacecraft assembly, where planetary protection protocols require stringent bioburden limits.
