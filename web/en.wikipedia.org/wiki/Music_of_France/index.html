Music of France

The music of France reflects a diverse array of styles. In the field of classical music, France has produced several prominent romantic composers, while folk and popular music have seen the rise of the chanson and cabaret style. The oldest preserved musical manuscript in France dates from the 9th century, and France was a major centre of the development of polyphony in the medieval period.

A chanson performer in a Paris café.

Classical music

France's classical tradition includes the Baroque composers Jean-Baptiste Lully, who dominated the court of Louis XIV and effectively founded French opera, and Jean-Philippe Rameau, whose operas and harpsichord works and treatise on harmony reshaped European music theory. François Couperin was a master of the clavecin.

The 19th and early 20th centuries produced Hector Berlioz, Camille Saint-Saëns, Gabriel Fauré, Claude Debussy, and Maurice Ravel — the last two associated with musical impressionism. Later figures include Olivier Messiaen, Pierre Boulez, and the spectralist composers of the 1970s. Paris has remained a centre for contemporary classical music through institutions such as IRCAM.

Chanson

The chanson française is a lyric-driven French song tradition, usually performed by singer-songwriters. Its golden age ran from the 1940s to the 1960s with artists such as Édith Piaf, Charles Aznavour, Jacques Brel (Belgian, but central to the Paris scene), Georges Brassens, Serge Gainsbourg, and Barbara. Lyrics, literary quality, and emotional delivery are paramount in the genre.

Popular music

France developed strong traditions in jazz manouche (Django Reinhardt), yé-yé pop of the 1960s (France Gall, Françoise Hardy, Johnny Hallyday — the "French Elvis"), and electronic music, from Jean-Michel Jarre to Daft Punk, Justice, and Air — the "French touch". French hip hop, with acts such as MC Solaar, IAM, and Suprême NTM, became the second-largest market for rap after the United States.

French law requires a significant proportion of radio airplay to be in the French language (the "Toubon Law" quotas), a policy intended to protect francophone music.

Folk and regional music

Regional traditions include Breton music with its bombarde and bagpipes (biniou), Occitan music in the south, Corsican polyphonic singing (paghjella), and the musette accordion style of Parisian dance halls. The musette waltz, played on accordion, became an emblem of Paris.
