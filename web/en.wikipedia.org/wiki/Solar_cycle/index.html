Solar cycle

The solar cycle, also known as the solar magnetic activity cycle, sunspot cycle, or Schwabe cycle, is a nearly periodic 11-year change in the Sun's activity measured in terms of variations in the number of observed sunspots on the solar surface.

History

Sunspots have been observed since ancient times. Systematic counts began in the early 17th century after the invention of the telescope. In 1843 Samuel Heinrich Schwabe announced an approximately 10-year periodicity. Rudolf Wolf compiled historical records and established the numbering scheme, with Cycle 1 beginning in 1755.

Sunspots and activity

Over a cycle, sunspot number rises from a solar minimum to a solar maximum and falls again. Accompanying phenomena include solar flares, coronal mass ejections, faculae, and changes in total solar irradiance of about 0.1%. The Sun's magnetic field reverses polarity near maximum, so the full magnetic cycle, the Hale cycle, is about 22 years.

Effects on Earth

Enhanced activity increases geomagnetic storms, aurorae, and disruptions to radio communications, satellites and power grids. See Space weather. Long minima such as the Maunder Minimum (c. 1645–1715) coincided with periods of reduced sunspot activity.

Recent cycles

Cycle 24 (2008–2019) was relatively weak. Cycle 25 began in December 2019 and proved stronger than many forecasts, with maximum expected around 2024–2025.
