Peking opera, or Beijing opera (Chinese: 京劇; pinyin: Jīngjù), is the most influential form of Chinese opera. It combines music, vocal performance, mime, dance, and acrobatics into a highly stylised performance art characterised by elaborate costumes, distinctive painted-face makeup (lianpu), and a largely symbolic, minimalist stage.

History

Peking opera arose in 1790 when the "Four Great Anhui Troupes" came to Beijing to perform for the Qianlong Emperor's eightieth birthday. Their Hui opera merged with Han opera and Kunqu elements, and by the reign of the Empress Dowager Cixi — an enthusiastic patron — it had become the dominant theatrical form at the Qing court and in the capital. In the 20th century, performers such as Mei Lanfang brought Peking opera to international audiences; Mei's 1930 tour of the United States was a landmark in East–West cultural exchange.

Role types

Performers specialise in one of four role types:

  • Sheng (生) — male roles, subdivided into old men (laosheng), young men (xiaosheng), and warriors (wusheng).
  • Dan (旦) — female roles, historically played by men; including the virtuous qingyi and the vivacious huadan.
  • Jing (淨) — painted-face male roles, forceful characters such as judges and bandits, identified by exaggerated facial designs.
  • Chou (丑) — male or female clowns, marked by a small patch of white chalk on the face.

Performance elements

The four skills of performance are song (chang), speech (nian), dance-acting (zuo), and combat (da). Speech and song use a stylised stage dialect mixing Beijing speech with archaic pronunciations. The orchestra divides into a melodic section (led by the high-pitched jinghu fiddle) and a percussion section (gongs, drums, and clappers led by the drummer-conductor). Actions are heavily codified: circling the stage signifies a long journey, and a horsewhip in hand implies riding.

Repertoire

The traditional repertoire numbers over a thousand plays, drawn from history, folklore, and classical novels such as Romance of the Three Kingdoms and Journey to the West. Famous plays include The Drunken Concubine, Farewell My Concubine, and The White Snake. During the Cultural Revolution the repertoire was restricted to the "eight model plays" (yangbanxi) such as The Red Lantern.
