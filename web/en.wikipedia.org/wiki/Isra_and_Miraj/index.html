Isra and Mi'raj

The Israʾ and Miʿraj (Arabic: الإسراء والمعراج) are the two parts of the Night Journey that, according to Islamic tradition, the Prophet Muhammad undertook during a single night around the year 621 CE. In the Israʾ, Muhammad travelled from the Sacred Mosque in Mecca to "the Farthest Mosque" — identified with al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem — mounted on the steed Buraq and accompanied by the angel Gabriel (Jibril). In the Miʿraj, he ascended through the seven heavens, meeting earlier prophets, and came into the divine presence.

Scriptural basis and narrative

The Qur'an alludes to the journey in Surah Al-Isra (17:1) and, on the majority reading, in Surah An-Najm; the detailed narrative — the prayer led by Muhammad with the prophets at Jerusalem, the ascent past Adam, Jesus and John, Abraham and Moses, the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary, and the negotiation that reduced fifty daily prayers to five — comes from the hadith literature. During the Mi'raj the five daily prayers (salah) were made obligatory, which is why the event is commemorated as the gift of prayer to the community.

Commemoration and interpretation

The night of 27 Rajab, Laylat al-Mi'raj, is observed in many Muslim countries with special prayers, sermons and, in some places, public holidays and illuminations of mosques. Scholars have debated whether the journey was bodily or a visionary experience; mainstream Sunni and Shia tradition generally affirms a bodily journey while awake. The story profoundly influenced Islamic art, Persian and Turkish miniature painting, and mystical literature, and some historians see echoes of the Mi'raj narrative in later European literature.
