Eleven-plus


Pupils sit the eleven-plus at about age eleven

The eleven-plus (11+) is a standardized examination administered to some students in England and Northern Ireland in their last year of primary education, which governs admission to grammar schools and other secondary schools which use academic selection. The name derives from the age group for secondary entry: 11 to 12 years.

History

The exam was introduced by the Education Act 1944 as part of the "tripartite system": children were allocated to grammar schools, secondary technical schools, or secondary modern schools largely on the basis of their eleven-plus results. Most local authorities later adopted comprehensive schooling, and the exam withered in most of England. It survives in areas that retain selective education, such as Kent, Medway, Buckinghamshire, and parts of Lincolnshire and Greater London, and in Northern Ireland, where an unregulated transfer test replaced the official exam after 2008.

Format

Modern eleven-plus tests typically assess verbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning, English, and mathematics, and are usually produced by providers such as GL Assessment or CEM. Scores are standardized by age so that younger children in a year group are not disadvantaged.

Debate

Critics argue that selection at eleven entrenches social inequality, that results can be heavily influenced by private tutoring, and that children who "fail" carry a lasting stigma; supporters contend that grammar schools offer high-quality academic education and social mobility for able pupils from poorer backgrounds.
