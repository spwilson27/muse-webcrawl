Factor Xa

Coagulation factor X, also known by the eponym Stuart–Prower factor, is an enzyme (EC 3.4.21.6) of the coagulation cascade. Its activated form, factor Xa, occupies the junction of the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways of blood clotting and is responsible for converting prothrombin into thrombin — the central step in the formation of a fibrin clot. Because of this pivotal role, factor Xa is a major target of modern anticoagulant drugs.

Biochemistry

Factor X is a vitamin K-dependent glycoprotein synthesised in the liver and circulating in plasma as a two-chain zymogen. It is activated to factor Xa by cleavage of a single peptide bond, either by the tissue factor–factor VIIa complex of the extrinsic pathway or by the intrinsic tenase complex (factor IXa with its cofactor VIIIa) on phospholipid surfaces in the presence of calcium ions.

Factor Xa, together with its cofactor factor Va, calcium, and a phospholipid surface, forms the prothrombinase complex, which cleaves prothrombin (factor II) to generate thrombin at a rate roughly 300,000 times faster than factor Xa alone. Thrombin then converts fibrinogen to fibrin and amplifies its own generation by activating factors V, VIII, and XI. Factor Xa is inhibited physiologically by antithrombin (greatly accelerated by heparin) and by tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI).

Clinical significance

Inherited factor X deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive bleeding disorder, first described in the 1950s in the patients Rufus Stuart and Audrey Prower, whose surnames the factor bears. Severe deficiency causes prolonged bleeding, while acquired deficiency can occur in amyloidosis, where factor X is adsorbed onto amyloid fibrils.

Factor Xa inhibitors

Drugs that inhibit factor Xa are widely used anticoagulants. The indirect inhibitor fondaparinux potentiates antithrombin. Direct oral factor Xa inhibitors — including rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban — are used for the prevention and treatment of venous thromboembolism and for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation; they largely displaced warfarin in these roles because they require no routine monitoring and have fewer interactions. A modified recombinant factor Xa decoy protein, andexanet alfa, is available as a reversal agent for life-threatening bleeding on rivaroxaban or apixaban.
