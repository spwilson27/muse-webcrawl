Swarm intelligence

Flocking is a classic example of swarm behaviour

Swarm intelligence (SI) is the collective behaviour of decentralised, self-organised systems, natural or artificial. The term was introduced by Gerardo Beni and Jing Wang in 1989 in the context of cellular robotics. In nature, ant colonies, bee swarms, bird flocks, fish schools, and bacterial colonies solve problems — finding food, choosing nest sites, evading predators — without any central controller, through many simple individuals following local rules and cues such as pheromone trails (stigmergy).

Computational models

Two landmark algorithms dominate the field. Ant colony optimisation (Marco Dorigo, 1992) mimics pheromone laying and evaporation to solve combinatorial problems such as the travelling salesman problem and network routing. Particle swarm optimisation (Kennedy and Eberhart, 1995) moves candidate solutions through a search space, each particle adjusting its velocity by its own best result and the swarm's best result, and is widely used for continuous optimisation and training models.

Principles

Swarm systems share positive feedback (successful behaviour is amplified), negative feedback (which stabilises the system, e.g. pheromone decay), amplification of random fluctuations, and multiple local interactions. The Boids model (Craig Reynolds, 1986) showed that three simple steering rules — separation, alignment, cohesion — reproduce realistic flocking. Applications now include swarm robotics, drone fleets, traffic management, and telecommunications.
