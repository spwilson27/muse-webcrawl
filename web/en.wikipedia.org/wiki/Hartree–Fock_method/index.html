Hartree–Fock method

In computational physics and chemistry, the Hartree–Fock (HF) method is a method of approximation for the determination of the wave function and the energy of a quantum many-body system in a stationary state. It is named after Douglas Hartree and Vladimir Fock.

Theory

The Hartree–Fock method assumes that the exact many-body wave function can be approximated by a single Slater determinant (for fermions) of one-particle orbitals. Each electron is treated as moving in the average (mean) field created by all other electrons. Applying the variational principle then yields a set of coupled integro-differential equations — the Hartree–Fock equations — for the orbitals, which contain Coulomb and exchange operators; the exchange term, absent from the earlier Hartree method, arises from the antisymmetry of the determinant.

Self-consistent field

Because the mean field depends on the orbitals being solved for, the equations are solved iteratively: an initial guess for the orbitals generates a field, from which new orbitals are computed, and the cycle repeats until the field is self-consistent. For molecules, the orbitals are usually expanded in a basis set, converting the problem into the matrix form known as the Roothaan–Hall equations.

Limitations

Hartree–Fock neglects electron correlation beyond the exchange interaction, so post-Hartree–Fock methods (such as Møller–Plesset perturbation theory, configuration interaction, and coupled cluster) build corrections on top of it. Nevertheless, HF energies typically recover about 99% of the total energy, and the method remains a foundational starting point in quantum chemistry, as well as a conceptual reference for density functional theory's Kohn–Sham scheme.
