Dahomey Amazons

The Dahomey Amazons (Fon: Mino, "our mothers") were a Fon all-female military regiment of the Kingdom of Dahomey, in present-day Benin, that existed from the 17th century until the late 19th century. They were so named by Western observers and historians due to their similarity to the semi-mythical Amazons of ancient Anatolia and the Black Sea. The regiment was unique in modern military history for its front-line combat role, and the women were formally recognised as soldiers, sworn to celibacy and to the king.

Origins

King Houegbadja (r. 1645–1685) is often credited with founding the group that would become the Amazons, initially as elephant hunters called the gbeto. His successor Queen Hangbè (r. 1708–1711) is said to have established them as palace bodyguards. Under King Agaja, the bodyguard was deployed in warfare, including the conquest of the neighbouring kingdom of Whydah in 1727. The regiment was greatly expanded under King Ghezo (r. 1818–1858), who turned Dahomey into a militaristic state and formally elevated the women to a regular corps within the army.

Recruitment and training

Recruitment drew on several sources: free Fon women who volunteered, wives of the king (ahosi) assigned to the guard, women offered by their families, and captives taken in war. Training was gruelling, designed to make the soldiers indifferent to pain and death; exercises included storming thorn barriers and public executions of prisoners. The women lived in the royal palace, were forbidden to marry or bear children while serving, and were armed first with muskets and later with Winchester rifles and clubs, machetes, and battle-axes.

Combat history

The Amazons fought in Dahomey's wars against the Oyo Empire, the Egba of Abeokuta — where they participated in a famous, unsuccessful assault in 1851 — and neighbouring peoples. Their final campaigns came in the Franco-Dahomean Wars (1890 and 1892–1894), in which French forces defeated King Béhanzin and dismantled the kingdom. French soldiers recorded grudging admiration for the women's ferocity and discipline, and the corps was disbanded as Dahomey became a French colony.

Legacy

The last surviving Amazon, Nawi, died in 1979 at a reported age of well over one hundred. The regiment has become a lasting symbol within Benin, and in popular culture the Amazons inspired the Dora Milaje of Marvel's Black Panther franchise and the 2022 film The Woman King, centred on the Agojie, another name for the corps.
