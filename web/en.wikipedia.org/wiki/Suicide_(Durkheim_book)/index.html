Suicide: A Study in Sociology (French: Le Suicide: Étude de sociologie) is an 1897 book by French sociologist Émile Durkheim. It was the first methodological study of a social fact in the context of society, and remains a foundational text of sociology. Durkheim used official statistics on suicide across European countries and regions to argue that suicide rates are social facts, explained by the social environment rather than by individual psychology alone.

Contents

Durkheim first dismissed extra-social explanations — psychopathology, race, heredity, climate, and imitation — as insufficient to explain the stable patterning of suicide rates. He then developed his famous typology based on two dimensions of social life: integration (the strength of social bonds) and regulation (the strength of social norms and constraints).

  • Egoistic suicide — from insufficient integration, when the individual is weakly attached to society (Durkheim found higher rates among Protestants than Catholics, and among the unmarried).
  • Altruistic suicide — from excessive integration, when the individual's life is subordinated to the group, as in some military or ritual contexts.
  • Anomic suicide — from insufficient regulation, when norms break down during economic crisis or rapid change (including sudden prosperity), leaving desires unlimited.
  • Fatalistic suicide — from excessive regulation, under oppressive discipline; Durkheim treated it only in a footnote as being of little contemporary importance.

Reception and criticism

Suicide established Durkheim's programme of explaining social facts by other social facts and demonstrated the use of multivariate statistical comparison. Later scholars have criticised the reliability of 19th-century official suicide statistics — recording practices differed between Catholic and Protestant regions — and have questioned the inference from aggregate rates to individual motives (the ecological fallacy), notably in Jack Douglas's The Social Meanings of Suicide (1967). The book's categories nonetheless continue to shape sociological research on suicide.
