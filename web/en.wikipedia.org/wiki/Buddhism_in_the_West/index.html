Buddhism in the West

Buddhism in the West
RegionsNorth America, Europe, Australia
Key influencesAsian immigration, convert movements, meditation and mindfulness
MilestonesWorld's Parliament of Religions (1893); post-1960s growth; mindfulness movement

Buddhism in the West (or more narrowly Western Buddhism) broadly encompasses the knowledge and practice of Buddhism outside of Asia, in Europe, the Americas and Oceania. Occasional Western interest dates to classical antiquity and to 19th-century scholarship and Theosophy, but Buddhism became an established presence through two channels: Asian immigrant communities ("ethnic Buddhism") and Western converts attracted mainly since the mid-20th century to meditation traditions such as Zen, Tibetan Buddhism and vipassanā insight meditation.

History

The 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago, where figures such as Anagarika Dharmapala and Soyen Shaku addressed Western audiences, is often taken as a founding moment. Philosophers from Schopenhauer onward had engaged Buddhist ideas earlier, and the first Western Buddhist organisations appeared in Germany, Britain and the United States in the early 1900s. Growth accelerated after the Second World War: Zen was popularised by D. T. Suzuki and Alan Watts, Tibetan teachers such as Chögyam Trungpa arrived after the 1959 diaspora, and the Theravada-derived mindfulness movement — through teachers such as Jon Kabat-Zinn (MBSR, from 1979) — carried Buddhist-derived meditation into secular medicine and psychology.

Characteristics

Scholars note distinctive features of much convert Western Buddhism: emphasis on meditation over ritual and merit-making, lay rather than monastic leadership, democratic and egalitarian structures, psychological interpretation of doctrine, and engagement with social and environmental issues ("engaged Buddhism"). Demographers distinguish immigrant and convert communities, which often worship separately, though the boundaries blur across generations.
