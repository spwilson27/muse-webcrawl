Railway signalling

A colour-light signal displaying a proceed aspect.

Railway signalling is the system used to direct railway traffic and to keep trains a safe distance apart. Its core principle is the block system: the line is divided into sections (blocks) that only one train may occupy at a time, with signals at block boundaries indicating whether a train may proceed.

Components

Signalling equipment includes lineside signals — historically semaphore arms, now usually colour lights — track circuits or axle counters that detect train occupancy, points (switches) and their interlocking, which prevents conflicting movements from being set, and signalling control centres. Train protection systems such as AWS, ETCS, and Positive Train Control warn drivers or automatically enforce signal compliance and speed limits.

History

Early railways relied on policemen with flags and time-interval working. The telegraph enabled absolute block working in the 19th century, and interlocking frames made routes mechanically safe. Colour-light signals and power operation spread in the 20th century; modern systems increasingly move authority into the cab (moving-block signalling), as on many metro lines and under ETCS Level 2.

Operations

Signals convey aspects such as stop, caution, and clear, and may also indicate route and permitted speed. Drivers must observe and obey them, and operating rules define how trains proceed when signals fail. See also signalling block system and interlocking.
