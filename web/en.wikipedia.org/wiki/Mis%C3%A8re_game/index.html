Misère game

A game of Nim, often played in misère form

In combinatorial game theory, a misère game is a game played under the misère play condition, in which the player who makes the last move loses, rather than wins as in normal play. The term comes from the French misère, used for a bid to lose every trick in certain card games.

Analysis

The Sprague–Grundy theory applies directly to impartial games under normal play; misère versions require modified analysis. For Nim, the misère strategy coincides with normal strategy except at the endgame: the player should leave an even number of heaps of size one (rather than an odd number) once all remaining heaps have size one. The general theory of misère impartial games was developed through the misère quotient construction of Aaron Siegel and others.

Examples

Examples include misère Nim, misère Treblecross, and avoidance games such as Treblecross variants and the card game "misère" contracts in whist-family games.
