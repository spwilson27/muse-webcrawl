Slavery in ancient Rome

Slavery in ancient Rome played an important role in society and the economy. Enslaved people (servi) undertook a wide range of work — domestic service, agriculture, mining, manufacturing, teaching, medicine, administration and even banking — and could come from any ethnic background. Roman slavery was not based on race: slaves were acquired mainly through warfare, piracy, trade, and birth to an enslaved mother, as well as by the exposure of unwanted infants and self-sale or debt in earlier periods.

Scale

Estimates of the slave population vary widely. In late Republican and early Imperial Italy, slaves may have constituted some 15–25% of the population — perhaps one to one and a half million people — one of the highest proportions of any pre-modern society. Large estates (latifundia) in Sicily and southern Italy were worked by chained gangs, while wealthy urban households might own dozens or hundreds of slaves.

Legal status and daily life

In Roman law a slave was property (a res), subject to the owner's power (dominica potestas), though legislation increasingly limited abuse: under the Empire, masters were forbidden to kill slaves arbitrarily, and slaves could appeal mistreatment. Slaves could hold a peculium — a fund of money or goods — with which they might eventually purchase their freedom. Conditions varied enormously: miners and agricultural labourers often endured brutal, short lives, while skilled Greek-speaking tutors, doctors and imperial administrators (the familia Caesaris) could enjoy considerable status and influence.

Revolts and resistance

Fear of slave revolt was a constant of Roman life. Sicily saw two great Servile Wars (135–132 and 104–100 BC), and the most famous uprising was led by the gladiator Spartacus in 73–71 BC, whose army defeated several Roman forces before being crushed by Crassus; some 6,000 captured rebels were crucified along the Appian Way. Everyday resistance — flight, theft, sabotage — was managed through fugitive slave-catchers, collars inscribed with owners' names, and harsh collective punishments.

Manumission and freedmen

Manumission was common, particularly for urban and domestic slaves, and uniquely in the Roman world freed slaves (liberti) became citizens, though with certain political disabilities. Their freeborn children were full citizens. Freedmen became prominent in trade and imperial administration, and elaborate tombs of wealthy freed families line the roads outside Rome. Augustus placed legal limits on manumission through laws such as the lex Fufia Caninia and lex Aelia Sentia.

Decline

As the Empire's expansion slowed, the supply of war captives contracted and slave prices rose. From late antiquity, slave labour on the land gradually gave way to the colonate — tenants tied to the soil — though slavery itself persisted in households and cities into the early medieval period rather than vanishing at a single moment.
