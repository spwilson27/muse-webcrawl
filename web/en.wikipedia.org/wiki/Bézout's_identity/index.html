Bézout's identity

In mathematics, Bézout's identity, also called Bézout's lemma, states that for two integers a and b with greatest common divisor d, there exist integers x and y such that ax + by = d. Moreover, the integers of the form az + bt are exactly the multiples of d. The integers x and y are called the Bézout coefficients for (a, b).

History

The identity is named after the French mathematician Étienne Bézout, who stated it for polynomials; the result for integers had earlier been given by Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac in 1624. The coefficients can be computed effectively using the extended Euclidean algorithm.

Example

For a = 12 and b = 42, the greatest common divisor is 6, and Bézout's identity gives 6 = 12 × (−3) + 42 × 1. The coefficients are not unique: adding any multiple of 42/6 = 7 to x while subtracting the corresponding multiple of 12/6 = 2 from y gives another pair.

Applications

Bézout's identity is fundamental in elementary number theory. It underlies the proof that a prime dividing a product must divide one of the factors, the existence of modular inverses (used in cryptography such as RSA), and the structure theory of principal ideal domains and Bézout domains, in which the identity generalises to sums of two elements generating any finitely generated ideal.
