Peruvian War of Independence

Date: 1811 – 1826

Location: Viceroyalty of Peru

Result: Patriot victory; independence of Peru

Key figures: José de San Martín, Simón Bolívar, Antonio José de Sucre

The Peruvian War of Independence (Spanish: Guerra de Independencia del Perú) was the struggle by which Peru freed itself from Spanish colonial rule, forming part of the wider Spanish American wars of independence. Unlike much of South America, Peru was a royalist stronghold: the viceregal government in Lima suppressed early patriot revolts, and independence was ultimately brought largely by liberating armies arriving from outside — from the south under José de San Martín and from the north under Simón Bolívar.

Background

Early uprisings, including the rebellion of Túpac Amaru II (1780–1783) and the Cuzco Rebellion of 1814 led by the Pumacahua brothers and Mateo Pumacahua's former allies, were crushed by royalist forces. Creole elites in Lima, fearful of indigenous revolution, largely remained loyal to Spain, making Peru the centre of royalist power on the continent.

San Martín and the proclamation of independence

After liberating Chile, San Martín organised the Liberating Expedition of Peru, a combined Argentine-Chilean fleet under Lord Cochrane that sailed from Valparaíso in August 1820 and landed at Paracas that September. Avoiding a direct assault on the royalist army, San Martín occupied Lima after the viceroy withdrew inland, and on 28 July 1821 he proclaimed the independence of Peru. He became head of the new Protectorate of Peru.

Bolívar, Sucre and Ayacucho

After the Guayaquil Conference of July 1822 between San Martín and Bolívar, San Martín resigned and left Peru. Bolívar arrived in 1823 with Colombian troops, and his lieutenant Antonio José de Sucre won the decisive Battle of Junín (6 August 1824) and the climactic Battle of Ayacucho (9 December 1824), where the royalist army of Viceroy José de la Serna was destroyed. The last royalist stronghold, the fortress of Real Felipe at Callao, held out under José Ramón Rodil until January 1826.

Legacy

Independence severed Peru from Spain but left deep social structures intact; the new republic endured decades of caudillo rule and instability. The 28 July proclamation is celebrated annually as Fiestas Patrias, Peru's national day.
