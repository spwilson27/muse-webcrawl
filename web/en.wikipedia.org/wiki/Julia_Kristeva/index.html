Julia Kristeva (born 24 June 1941) is a Bulgarian-French philosopher, literary critic, semiotician, psychoanalyst and novelist who has lived in France since the mid-1960s. She is one of the most influential figures in contemporary literary theory and feminist thought.

Life

Kristeva was born in Sliven, Bulgaria, and educated at the University of Sofia. In 1965 she moved to Paris on a research fellowship, where she studied under Roland Barthes and Lucien Goldmann. She joined the circle around the journal Tel Quel, edited by Philippe Sollers, whom she married. She became professor of linguistics at Paris Diderot University (Paris VII) and later trained as a psychoanalyst.

Work

Her doctoral work, published as Revolution in Poetic Language (1974), introduced the influential distinction between the semiotic — the pre-linguistic drives, rhythms and bodily energies — and the symbolic — the realm of grammar, law and social order. Kristeva argued that poetic language disrupts the symbolic order through the semiotic.

She coined the widespread use of the term intertextuality, developing Mikhail Bakhtin's ideas of dialogism: every text is a mosaic of quotations and transformations of other texts. Her essay "Word, Dialogue and Novel" (1966) brought Bakhtin to French attention.

In Powers of Horror: An Essay on Abjection (1980) she developed the concept of the abject — that which disturbs identity and borders, such as bodily wastes or the corpse, provoking horror. Tales of Love (1983) and Black Sun: Depression and Melancholia (1987) extended her psychoanalytic reflections on love and loss.

Criticism and honours

Her early Maoist sympathies, expressed after a 1974 trip to China in About Chinese Women, were later renounced. Some critics, notably in the Fashionable Nonsense debate, have charged her technical writing with obscurity. She received the Holberg International Memorial Prize in 2004 and the Hannah Arendt Prize in 2006, and was the first recipient of the Vaclav Havel Prize (2008).
