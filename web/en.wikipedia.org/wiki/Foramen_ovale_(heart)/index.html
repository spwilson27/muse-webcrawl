Foramen ovale (heart)

The foramen ovale (from Latin "oval hole") is a normal opening in the septum between the two upper chambers (atria) of the fetal heart. It allows blood to bypass the non-functioning fetal lungs: oxygenated blood from the placenta, returning via the umbilical vein and inferior vena cava, passes from the right atrium directly into the left atrium and from there into the systemic circulation.

Closure after birth

At birth, with the first breaths, pressure in the left atrium rises above that in the right atrium, pushing the flap-like septum primum against the septum secundum and functionally closing the opening. In most people the tissues fuse permanently over the following months, leaving a shallow depression in the adult heart called the fossa ovalis.

Patent foramen ovale

In roughly a quarter of adults, the foramen ovale does not seal completely, a condition known as a patent foramen ovale (PFO). Most people with a PFO have no symptoms and never know they have it. However, a PFO can allow small blood clots to pass from the venous to the arterial circulation (paradoxical embolism), and it has been associated with cryptogenic stroke, decompression sickness in divers, and possibly migraine with aura. In selected patients, particularly younger stroke survivors, the defect may be closed with a catheter-delivered device.
