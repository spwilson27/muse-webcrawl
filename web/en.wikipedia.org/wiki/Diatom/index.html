Diatoms are a major group of single-celled algae, and are among the most common types of phytoplankton in marine and freshwater environments. They are distinguished by their unique cell walls, called frustules, made of hydrated amorphous silica (opal-A), often elaborately patterned with pores, spines and ridges. Diatoms occur in oceans, lakes, rivers, soils and even damp terrestrial surfaces, and they are responsible for an estimated 20–25% of global primary production and a comparable share of the oxygen produced by photosynthesis each year.

Individual cells range from about 2 to 500 micrometres in size. Diatoms are broadly divided into two groups by symmetry: the radially symmetric centric diatoms and the bilaterally symmetric pennate diatoms, the latter including forms with a slit-like raphe used in gliding motility.

Frustule structure

The frustule consists of two overlapping halves, the larger epitheca and the smaller hypotheca, fitting together much like a Petri dish. Each half comprises a valve face and a series of girdle bands. Because the siliceous wall cannot expand, each round of asexual division produces slightly smaller daughter cells, so the mean size of a population declines over generations until sexual reproduction restores maximum size through formation of an auxospore.

Life cycle

Most reproduction is by binary fission. When cells shrink below a threshold size, sexual reproduction may be triggered: gametes are produced, fuse, and form an auxospore that expands and deposits a new, large frustule. Many diatoms also form resting spores or resting cells that sink and survive unfavourable conditions in sediments, germinating when conditions improve.

Ecology

Diatoms dominate spring blooms in temperate and polar seas wherever dissolved silica, nitrogen, phosphorus and iron are available. Because their dense silica frustules make them sink rapidly when blooms collapse, diatoms play a central role in the biological carbon pump, exporting organic carbon to the deep ocean. They form the base of many food webs, supporting krill, fish and whales. Blooms of some pennate genera such as Pseudo-nitzschia can produce domoic acid, a neurotoxin responsible for amnesic shellfish poisoning.

Fossil record and diatomaceous earth

The fossil record of diatoms extends to the Jurassic, with a major radiation in the Cretaceous and Cenozoic. Thick accumulations of fossil frustules form diatomaceous earth (diatomite), mined for use in filtration, abrasives, insulation and as a natural insecticide. Diatom assemblages in sediment cores are widely used to reconstruct past temperatures, salinity and nutrient conditions.

Classification

Diatoms are placed in the class Bacillariophyceae within the heterokont algae (Ochrophyta), relatives of brown algae and golden algae. Their plastids derive from a secondary endosymbiosis of a red alga and contain chlorophylls a and c and the accessory pigment fucoxanthin, which gives them a golden-brown colour.

Human uses

Beyond diatomite mining, diatoms are used in forensic science — the presence of diatoms in tissue can indicate drowning — and are cultured as feed in aquaculture. Their precisely nanostructured silica shells also inspire research in nanotechnology and drug delivery.
