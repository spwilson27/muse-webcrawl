Solid angle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geometry, a solid angle (symbol: Ω) is a measure of the amount of the field of view from some particular point that a given object covers. It is the three-dimensional analogue of the ordinary (plane) angle: just as an angle in radians is the length of a circular arc divided by its radius, a solid angle in steradians is the area of a portion of a sphere divided by the square of its radius.

Definition and properties

If a point is joined to every point of a closed surface by straight lines, forming a cone, the solid angle at the point is Ω = A / r², where A is the area of the portion of a sphere of radius r, centred at the point, that the cone intercepts. The SI unit is the steradian (sr), a dimensionless unit. Because the surface area of a complete sphere is 4πr², the total solid angle around a point is 4π steradians.

To find the solid angle subtended by a surface, the surface is projected onto a unit sphere centred at the point; the solid angle equals the area of that projection. Solid angles are additive: the solid angle of a composite region is the sum of the solid angles of its parts. For a cone with half-angle θ, the solid angle is 2π(1 − cos θ). A hemisphere subtends 2π sr, and each face of a cube seen from its centre subtends 2π/3 sr.

Applications

Solid angles appear throughout physics and engineering. Radiant intensity is measured in watts per steradian and luminous intensity in candela (lumens per steradian). Antenna gain relates radiated power to solid angle, detector acceptance in particle physics is expressed in steradians, and Gauss's law computes electric flux through the solid angle subtended by a charge distribution. In astronomy, the apparent size of an object in the sky is often given as a solid angle, for example in square degrees (one square degree ≈ 3.046 × 10⁻⁴ sr).
