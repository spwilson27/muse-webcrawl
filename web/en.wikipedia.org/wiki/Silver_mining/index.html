Silver mining is the extraction of silver from the Earth by mining. Silver has been mined since antiquity — the mines of Laurion financed classical Athens — and for centuries it was recovered primarily from its own ores. Today, most silver is produced as a by-product of mining lead, zinc, copper, and gold, with only a minority of output coming from primary silver mines.

Cerro Rico at Potosí, Bolivia, once the richest silver deposit in the world.

History

Silver was mined in Anatolia and the Aegean by the fourth millennium BC. The Spanish conquest of the Americas transformed global production: the great deposits of Potosí in present-day Bolivia (discovered 1545) and Zacatecas and Guanajuato in Mexico made Spanish America the dominant source of silver for three centuries, supplying the silver that flowed to Europe and, via the Manila galleons, to China. In the nineteenth century, the Comstock Lode in Nevada (1859) triggered a silver rush and pioneered new hard-rock mining methods. Mexico remains the world's largest producer, as it has been for most of the past five centuries.

Extraction and refining

Silver ores are typically crushed and concentrated by froth flotation. Historically, silver was recovered by amalgamation with mercury (the patio process) and later by cyanidation; modern operations use smelting and electrolytic refining, which yield silver of 99.9% purity or better. Because so much silver comes from lead-zinc ores, the Parkes process — using zinc to extract silver from molten lead — remains industrially important.

Economics

Mine production in recent years has been on the order of 800–830 million troy ounces annually, with additional supply from recycling. Mexico, China, and Peru are the leading producing countries. Demand comes from industry (electronics, photovoltaics, brazing alloys), jewellery and silverware, and investment in coins and bars.
