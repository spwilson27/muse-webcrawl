Hernán Cortés de Monroy y Pizarro Altamirano, 1st Marquess of the Valley of Oaxaca (1485 – December 2, 1547) was a Spanish conquistador who led the expedition that caused the fall of the Aztec Empire and brought much of what is now mainland Mexico under Spanish rule in the early 16th century. He is among the most consequential — and most controversial — figures of the Spanish colonisation of the Americas.

Conquest of Mexico

Sailing from Cuba in 1519 against the wishes of Governor Velázquez, Cortés founded Veracruz, famously scuttled his ships to prevent retreat, and marched inland with a few hundred men. Aided indispensably by the interpreter La Malinche (Malintzin) and by alliances with peoples subjugated by the Aztecs — above all the Tlaxcalans — he reached the capital Tenochtitlan and took the emperor Moctezuma II hostage. Expelled in the disastrous retreat of La Noche Triste (1520), Cortés regrouped, built brigantines to command Lake Texcoco, and after a brutal siege starved the city into surrender on 13 August 1521; smallpox, raging among the defenders, was decisive. On its ruins rose Mexico City, capital of New Spain.

Later life

Cortés governed New Spain, sent expeditions as far as Honduras and Baja California, and was made a marquess, but royal officials steadily stripped his power. He died near Seville in 1547, wealthy but embittered. His letters to Charles V (Cartas de relación) remain a primary — and self-justifying — source for the conquest. In Mexico his legacy is deeply contested: long officially vilified as a brutal invader, yet also a founder of the mestizo nation.
