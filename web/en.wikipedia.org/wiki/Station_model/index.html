Station model

A station model is a symbolic shorthand used by meteorologists to plot a compact set of weather observations for a particular location — a "station" — on a surface weather map (synoptic chart). Instead of writing out numbers and descriptions, the analyst draws a small circle for the station and arranges standardized numbers and symbols around it in fixed positions, allowing dozens of variables to be read at a glance across a map covered with hundreds of stations.

Elements

The central circle is filled according to total cloud cover (from empty for clear skies to fully black for overcast). A wind barb extends from the circle in the direction from which the wind blows; feathers on the barb encode wind speed — a short barb for 5 knots, a long barb for 10 knots, and a triangular pennant for 50 knots, summed to give the total. Numbers placed around the model follow a fixed scheme: air temperature (upper left), dew point (lower left), sea-level pressure (upper right, coded in tenths of millibars with the leading 9 or 10 dropped), the pressure change in the past three hours and its tendency symbol (middle right), and visibility (far left). Symbols denote present weather (rain, snow, fog, thunderstorms, etc.), past weather, and the types and amounts of low, middle, and high clouds, the latter drawn as pictographic symbols above and below the station circle.

Use

Station models let forecasters analyze fronts, pressure patterns, and air masses on hand-drawn and computer-plotted charts. The convention grew out of international synoptic codes standardized by the World Meteorological Organization (the SYNOP code) and is still taught to meteorology students, though automated plotting has largely replaced manual analysis. Upper-air data from radiosondes use a similar model on constant-pressure charts, with temperature, dew point, height, and wind plotted for each mandated level.
