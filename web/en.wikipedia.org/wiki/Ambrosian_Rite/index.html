The Ambrosian Rite (Italian: Rito ambrosiano) is a Latin liturgical rite of the Catholic Church used in the Archdiocese of Milan and surrounding areas of northern Italy and Swiss Ticino. It is named after Saint Ambrose, bishop of Milan in the 4th century, though its distinctive features developed over many centuries after him.

Distinctive features

The Ambrosian Mass differs from the Roman Rite in the order and wording of many prayers, in an extended offertory procession, in the placement of the Creed after the offertory, and in its own chant tradition, Ambrosian chant, which predates Gregorian chant. The Ambrosian calendar also keeps a distinct rhythm: Lent begins on the Monday after Ash Wednesday (there is no Ash Wednesday; the Lenten fast starts later), and Advent has six Sundays instead of four.

History

The rite survived repeated attempts at suppression, notably under Charlemagne and again after the Council of Trent, when Pope Pius V permitted local rites more than 200 years old to continue. After the Second Vatican Council the Ambrosian Rite was revised in its own right, with a new missal promulgated under Cardinal Giovanni Colombo in 1976.
