Stellar pulsation

Stellar pulsations are caused by expansions and contractions in the outer layers of a star. The pulsations emerge from a star's attempt to maintain thermodynamic equilibrium while possessing a temperature gradient whose length changes due to the surface emissions.

Mechanisms

The best-known driving mechanism is the kappa mechanism (κ-mechanism), in which the opacity of a partially ionized layer of the stellar envelope — commonly the second ionization zone of helium — increases on compression, trapping heat and driving expansion, then releases the heat on expansion, sustaining oscillation. Related mechanisms include the epsilon mechanism, driven by variations in nuclear energy generation in the core, and stochastic excitation by surface convection, which produces the tiny solar-like oscillations observed by asteroseismology.

Classes of pulsating stars

Pulsating variable stars occupy the instability strip of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram and include classical Cepheids (used as standard candles through the period–luminosity relation), RR Lyrae variables, Delta Scuti and Gamma Doradus stars, long-period Mira variables on the asymptotic giant branch, and pulsating white dwarfs (ZZ Ceti stars). Non-radial pulsations, in which different parts of the surface move in opposite senses, underpin the field of asteroseismology, which probes stellar interiors analogously to seismology on Earth.
