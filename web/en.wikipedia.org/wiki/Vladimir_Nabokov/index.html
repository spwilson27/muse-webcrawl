Vladimir Nabokov

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Vladimir Nabokov in Montreux in later life, with a butterfly net: the novelist was also a distinguished lepidopterist]

Vladimir Vladimirovich Nabokov (22 April 1899 – 2 July 1977) was a Russian-American novelist, poet, translator and lepidopterist, famed for prose of dazzling pattern and play in two languages. He wrote nine novels in Russian in Berlin exile — as 'V. Sirin' — then, emigrating to America in 1940, produced in English the works on which his world reputation rests: Lolita (1955), Pnin (1957) and Pale Fire (1962).

His memoir Speak, Memory (1951) and his monumental, deliberately literal translation of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin (1964) complete the central shelf. Nabokov despised generalisations, symbols (he claimed) and Freud, and demanded of readers attention to detail: 'a good reader is a rereader'.

From Berlin to Cornell

Born to a liberal aristocratic family in St Petersburg, Nabokov fled the Bolsheviks, studied at Cambridge, and settled in Berlin, where his father was assassinated in 1922. In America he taught literature at Wellesley and Cornell — his Cornell lectures, posthumously published, include the celebrated course on European fiction — while his wife Véra managed his career and famously rescued the Lolita manuscript from the incinerator.

Lolita and after

Rejected by American publishers and issued by Paris's Olympia Press in 1955, Lolita — the confession of the murderer and child-abuser Humbert Humbert, told in prose of seductive brilliance — became a succès de scandale that made Nabokov rich and let him retire to the Palace Hotel, Montreux, to write, translate and chase butterflies. Pale Fire (1962), a mad scholar's commentary devouring the poem it annotates, is the purest fruit of his late manner.
