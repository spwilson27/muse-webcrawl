Satisficing

Satisficing is a decision-making strategy or cognitive heuristic that entails searching through the available alternatives until an acceptability threshold is met. The term, a portmanteau of satisfy and suffice, was introduced by Herbert A. Simon in 1956. Simon used satisficing to model bounded rationality: because decision-makers cannot evaluate every possible option or consequence, they settle for an outcome that is "good enough" relative to their aspiration level, rather than the mathematically optimal one.

Bounded rationality

Simon contrasted the satisficing agent with the perfectly rational homo economicus of classical economics, who optimises a known utility function. Real decision-makers, he argued, face limits on information, computation, and time, and therefore form an aspiration level and stop searching once an alternative exceeds it. Aspiration levels adjust with experience: easy success raises them, repeated failure lowers them.

In psychology and survey research

In survey methodology, "satisficing" describes the behaviour of respondents who expend minimal effort — for example by choosing the first plausible answer, selecting "don't know", or straight-lining responses — rather than fully working through each question. In consumer behaviour and management, satisficing explains choices such as accepting a merely adequate product or candidate instead of searching exhaustively for the best.
