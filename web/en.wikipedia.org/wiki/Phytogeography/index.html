Phytogeography


The floristic kingdoms of the world

Phytogeography (from Greek phytón, "plant", and geographía, "description") or botanical geography is the branch of biogeography that is concerned with the geographic distribution of plant species and their influence on the earth's surface. Phytogeography is concerned with all aspects of plant distribution, from the controls on the distribution of individual species ranges (at both large and small scales) to the factors that govern the composition of entire communities and floras.

Overview

The field combines knowledge from ecology, botany, and geography. Plant geographers classify the world's land surface into floristic regions and kingdoms based on shared plant taxa. The most widely used scheme recognizes six floristic kingdoms: Holarctic, Palaeotropical, Neotropical, Australian, South African (Capensis), and Antarctic.

Key figures in the development of phytogeography include Alexander von Humboldt, often regarded as the father of phytogeography, Augustin Pyramus de Candolle, Adolf Engler, and Ronald Good, whose The Geography of the Flowering Plants (1947) was a landmark synthesis. The discipline also overlaps with zoogeography, the corresponding study of animal distributions; together they form biogeography.

Floristic regions

Floristic regions are areas with a relatively uniform composition of plant species, distinguished by endemic families and genera. Distribution patterns reflect evolutionary history, continental drift, climate, and barriers such as oceans and mountain ranges. Modern phytogeography incorporates phylogenetics and molecular data to reconstruct the historical processes behind present-day plant distributions.
