Energy audit

An energy audit is an inspection, survey and analysis of energy flows in a building, process or system, carried out with the aim of reducing the amount of energy input without negatively affecting output. It is the first step in identifying opportunities to reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions, and in commercial and industrial contexts it may be a formal requirement of an energy management scheme or of legislation such as Article 8 of the EU Energy Efficiency Directive.

Types

The American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) recognises three levels of effort. A Level 1 walk-through audit gives a preliminary assessment from utility bills and a brief site visit. A Level 2 energy survey and analysis adds detailed end-use breakdowns, measurements and financial analysis of recommended measures. A Level 3 detailed audit provides investment-grade ("capital grade") analysis with detailed engineering and cost estimates, often using calibrated simulation models. For homes, residential audits — such as those using the US Home Energy Score or the UK Energy Performance Certificate framework — assess insulation, air leakage, heating systems and appliances, often with a blower-door test.

Process

An audit typically begins with analysis of historical utility data to benchmark the building against comparable properties, followed by a site inspection of the building envelope, HVAC, lighting, water heating and process equipment. The auditor then identifies energy conservation measures — for example insulation, air sealing, LED lighting, boiler or controls upgrades, and heat recovery — and ranks them by payback period and life-cycle cost in a written report.

Outcomes

Audits are the gateway to retrofit programmes and financing mechanisms such as energy performance contracting, in which savings guarantee the cost of improvements. Studies by national laboratories typically find identified savings of 5–30% of a building's energy use, though only a fraction of recommended measures are typically implemented without incentives or mandates — a pattern known as the energy efficiency gap.
