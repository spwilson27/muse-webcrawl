Greco-Turkish population exchange

The 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey was a compulsory exchange of populations agreed in the Convention Concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations, signed at Lausanne on 30 January 1923 as part of the settlement ending the Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922). It is regarded as the first compulsory population exchange sanctioned by international treaty.

Terms

The convention required the transfer of Orthodox Christians from Turkey to Greece and of Muslims from Greece to Turkey, with the criterion being religion rather than language or ethnicity — so Turkish-speaking Orthodox Christians (Karamanlides) were sent to Greece, while Greek-speaking Muslims went to Turkey. Exempted were the Greeks of Constantinople and the Muslims of Western Thrace. A Mixed Commission supervised property liquidation and resettlement.

Numbers and consequences

In total roughly 1.2 million Orthodox Christians and some 400,000 Muslims were covered by the exchange, though most Greek refugees had already fled or been expelled during and after the war, including following the Great Fire of Smyrna in September 1922. The influx increased Greece's population by more than a fifth and strained its economy for decades, but the refugees also transformed Greek agriculture, industry, and urban life. In Turkey the departing Greeks left major commercial cities, notably Smyrna (İzmir) and the Anatolian interior, with lasting demographic and economic effects.

Legacy

The exchange was praised by some contemporaries as a means of preventing future conflict, and it was later cited — approvingly and critically — in debates over other proposed population transfers. Modern scholarship generally emphasises its character as forced migration and its heavy human cost.
