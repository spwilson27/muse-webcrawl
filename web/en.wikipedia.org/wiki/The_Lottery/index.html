"The Lottery" is a short story by the American writer Shirley Jackson, first published in the 26 June 1948 issue of The New Yorker. It describes a small American village that observes an annual rite known as "the lottery", in which one member of the community is chosen by lot and stoned to death by the others — a ritual the villagers continue out of unexamined tradition, believing it ensures a good harvest.

Plot

On the morning of 27 June the villagers assemble in the square. Mr Summers, who runs the lottery, brings the battered black box containing slips of paper, one marked with a black spot. Heads of households draw first; the Hutchinson family is selected, and in a second drawing among its members the marked slip falls to Tessie Hutchinson. As she protests that the draw was unfair, her neighbours — including her own family — pick up the stones they had gathered and advance on her.

Reception

The story provoked an unprecedented response: hundreds of readers wrote to The New Yorker, many cancelling their subscriptions, and Jackson received a flood of mail, much of it hostile or bewildered, asking where such lotteries took place. It has since become one of the most famous and most anthologised American short stories, widely taught in schools and frequently adapted for radio, television, film and the stage.

Themes

Critics read "The Lottery" as an allegory of the dangers of blind conformity and ritual violence, of scapegoating, and of the darkness beneath the surface of small-town life; Jackson herself resisted single interpretations, remarking that she hoped the story would unsettle readers into examining their own societies.
