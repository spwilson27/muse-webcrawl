Hydrocephalus

Hydrocephalus is a condition in which cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) accumulates within the brain's ventricles, increasing intracranial pressure. The name derives from the Greek words hydro (water) and kephalē (head). In infants, whose skull bones have not yet fused, the accumulation can cause rapid enlargement of the head; in older children and adults it causes symptoms from raised pressure such as headache, vomiting, visual disturbance, and impaired consciousness. Treatment is typically surgical, most commonly with a shunt that drains the excess fluid to another body cavity.

Signs and symptoms

Signs and symptoms vary by age. In infants, an unusually large head or rapidly increasing head circumference, a bulging fontanelle, irritability, poor feeding, and the "sunsetting" appearance of downward-deviated eyes may occur. In older children and adults, symptoms include headache (often worse in the morning), nausea and vomiting, blurred or double vision from papilloedema, difficulty walking, and cognitive decline. Acute hydrocephalus with rapidly rising pressure is a medical emergency that can lead to herniation and death.

Causes and types

Hydrocephalus is classified as communicating, in which CSF can still flow between the ventricles but its absorption is impaired, and non-communicating (obstructive), in which flow is blocked, commonly at the cerebral aqueduct. Causes include congenital aqueductal stenosis, neural tube defects such as spina bifida with Chiari II malformation, Dandy–Walker malformation, intraventricular haemorrhage of prematurity, meningitis, tumours, and head injury. Hydrocephalus ex vacuo refers to ventricular enlargement compensating for loss of brain tissue rather than a true disorder of CSF dynamics.

Pathophysiology

CSF is produced mainly by the choroid plexus at a rate of about 500 mL per day in adults, circulates through the ventricles and subarachnoid space, and is absorbed into the venous system, chiefly through arachnoid granulations. Hydrocephalus results when production exceeds absorption, usually because of obstruction of flow or failure of absorption; overproduction (as with a choroid plexus papilloma) is rare.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical examination and imaging. Cranial ultrasound can be used through an open fontanelle in infants; CT and MRI show ventriculomegaly and can reveal the underlying cause. Serial head circumference measurements are important in monitoring infants.

Treatment

The standard treatment is surgical insertion of a shunt — typically a ventriculoperitoneal shunt — consisting of a catheter in the ventricle, a one-way valve, and tubing that drains CSF to the peritoneal cavity, where it is absorbed. Shunts can malfunction or become infected and often require revision, particularly in children. An alternative for obstructive hydrocephalus is endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV), in which a small opening is made in the floor of the third ventricle to bypass the obstruction, avoiding an implanted device. Temporary measures include external ventricular drainage and lumbar puncture in selected communicating cases.

Normal pressure hydrocephalus

Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) is a communicating hydrocephalus of older adults characterised by the triad of gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, and cognitive impairment ("wobbly, wet, and wacky"), with enlarged ventricles but intermittently normal CSF pressure. It is one of the few potentially treatable causes of dementia-like symptoms, and some patients improve markedly after shunt placement.

History

Skeletal remains suggest hydrocephalus was recognised in antiquity, and Hippocrates and Galen described fluid collections around the brain. The modern understanding developed with the description of CSF circulation in the 19th century, and effective surgical treatment became possible with the introduction of valve-regulated shunt systems in the 1950s, notably the Spitz–Holter valve.
