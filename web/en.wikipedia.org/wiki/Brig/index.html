A brig is a type of sailing vessel defined by its rig: two masts, both of them square-rigged. Brigs were fast and manoeuvrable and were used both as naval warships and as merchant vessels through the 18th and 19th centuries. They were especially popular in the 18th and early 19th centuries before the arrival of steam, and declined in use as their large crews became uneconomical compared with fore-and-aft rigged vessels.

Rig and description

A brig carries square sails on both the foremast and mainmast, together with a fore-and-aft gaff sail (the "spanker" or "brig sail") behind the mainmast, plus headsails between the foremast and bowsprit. The rig should not be confused with the brigantine, on which the mainmast is fore-and-aft rigged, or the snow, a closely related type with a separate trysail mast. Typical brigs ranged from about 75 to 165 feet in length and up to roughly 480 tons burthen.

Naval and commercial use

Navies used brigs as sloops-of-war, dispatch vessels, and training ships; the Royal Navy's Cruizer-class brig-sloops were the most numerous warship class of the Napoleonic Wars. Merchant brigs carried cargoes in coastal and ocean trades, including coal, timber and emigrants. A number of historic brigs survive or have been recreated as sail-training vessels, such as the Lady Nelson, Niagara and Roald Amundsen.
