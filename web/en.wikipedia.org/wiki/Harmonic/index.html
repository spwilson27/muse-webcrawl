A harmonic is a sinusoidal wave with a frequency that is a positive integer multiple of the fundamental frequency of a periodic signal. The fundamental frequency is also called the 1st harmonic; the other harmonics are called higher harmonics. As all harmonics are periodic at the fundamental frequency, the sum of harmonics is also periodic at that frequency.

For example, if the fundamental frequency is 50 Hz, the frequencies of the first three higher harmonics are 100 Hz (2nd harmonic), 150 Hz (3rd harmonic), and 200 Hz (4th harmonic). Harmonics arise naturally in vibrating strings and air columns: by lightly touching a string at a node, a player can isolate a harmonic, producing the flute-like tones used in string playing.

Terminology

The terms harmonic and overtone are related but not identical: the first overtone is the second harmonic, because overtones are counted above the fundamental. A component that is not an integer multiple of the fundamental is an inharmonic partial, as in the spectra of bells and some percussion instruments. The collection of harmonics of a note forms the harmonic series, and their relative strengths determine the timbre of the sound.

Applications

In music, harmonics underlie the overtone structure of nearly all pitched instruments and the physics of consonance. In electrical engineering, harmonics are unwanted multiples of the mains frequency generated by non-linear loads, which can overheat equipment and are limited by power-quality standards. In signal processing, Fourier analysis decomposes periodic signals into their harmonic components.
