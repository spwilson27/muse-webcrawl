Ancient Corinth

Corinth (Greek: Κόρινθος, Kórinthos) was a wealthy and powerful city-state of ancient Greece, located on the Isthmus of Corinth, the narrow land bridge connecting the Peloponnese with the rest of mainland Greece. Commanding trade routes between the Aegean and Ionian seas through its ports of Lechaeum and Cenchreae, Corinth rivalled Athens and Sparta, founded colonies including Syracuse, and gave its name to a distinctive architectural order and famously fine pottery and bronze.

The Temple of Apollo (c. 540 BC) with the Acrocorinth in the background

History

Corinth was ruled in the archaic period by the Bacchiad clan and then by the tyrants Cypselus and Periander (c. 657–585 BC), under whom the city flourished and the diolkos — a paved trackway for hauling ships across the isthmus — was built. A member of the Peloponnesian League, Corinth helped push Sparta toward war with Athens in 431 BC. The city was destroyed by the Roman general Lucius Mummius in 146 BC and refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BC as a Roman colony; it became a prosperous provincial capital. The apostle Paul lived in Corinth for some eighteen months around AD 50–52, and two of his letters to its church survive in the New Testament.

The site

Excavations, conducted since 1896 by the American School of Classical Studies at Athens, have uncovered the agora, the Temple of Apollo with its monolithic Doric columns, the fountain of Peirene, the theatre, and the bema where Paul was traditionally said to have been tried before Gallio. Above the city rises the Acrocorinth, a massive fortified rock crowned in legend by a temple of Aphrodite.
