Birth control

Birth control, also known as contraception, anticonception, and fertility control, is the use of methods or devices to prevent pregnancy. Planning, making available, and using birth control is called family planning. Birth control has been used since ancient times, but effective and safe methods only became widely available in the 20th century. Some cultures limit or discourage access for moral, religious or political reasons.

Methods

The most effective methods are long-acting and independent of user action: the contraceptive implant, intrauterine devices (IUDs, hormonal and copper), and sterilization (vasectomy and tubal ligation), each with first-year failure rates below 1%. Hormonal methods — combined oral contraceptive pills, patches, vaginal rings, and injectables such as Depo-Provera — are highly effective with perfect use but have typical-use failure rates of roughly 4–7% per year. Barrier methods include male and female condoms (which also protect against sexually transmitted infections), diaphragms and cervical caps. Behavioural methods include fertility-awareness-based methods and withdrawal, which are less effective. Emergency contraception (levonorgestrel or ulipristal pills, or a copper IUD) can prevent pregnancy after unprotected intercourse.

Effectiveness

Effectiveness is expressed as the percentage of women becoming pregnant during the first year of use — the Pearl Index. Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) is recommended by bodies such as the WHO, ACOG and the CDC as first-line for most people, including adolescents, because effectiveness does not depend on adherence.

History

Ancient Egyptian and Greek texts describe pessaries and barrier methods; condoms of linen and animal gut date to early modern Europe. The modern birth control movement is associated with Margaret Sanger in the United States and Marie Stopes in Britain, who opened pioneering clinics in the 1910s–20s. Gregory Pincus and colleagues developed the first oral contraceptive, approved in the United States in 1960 ("the Pill"), a landmark in reproductive autonomy. Legal milestones include Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) in the U.S. Constitutional protection expanded with Eisenstadt v. Baird (1972).

Health, society and ethics

Access to contraception reduces maternal deaths and unsafe abortion, allows spacing of pregnancies, and is associated with women's education and earnings. About half of pregnancies worldwide are unintended. Religious positions vary: the Roman Catholic Church permits only natural family planning, while most other faiths accept contraception. Public funding debates continue in many countries; global family-planning programmes are coordinated in part by UNFPA.
