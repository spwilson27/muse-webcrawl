The Germans of Hungary (German: Ungarndeutsche, Hungarian: magyarországi németek) are the largest national minority in Hungary. Most Hungarian Germans are descendants of Danube Swabian settlers brought to the kingdom in the eighteenth century to repopulate lands reconquered from the Ottoman Empire, although German settlement in Hungary goes back to the Middle Ages, in the mining towns of the north and in the cities of Transdanubia and the Burgenland.

History

After the Ottoman wars, the Habsburg crown and Hungarian landowners recruited Catholic German peasants from Swabia, Hesse, the Palatinate and elsewhere in three great waves known as the Schwabenzüge, settling them in the Banat, Bačka and the so-called Swabian Turkey of Tolna, Baranya and Somogy counties. They travelled down the Danube in flat-bottomed boats called Ulmer Schachteln. By the early twentieth century, ethnic Germans numbered over half a million in post-Trianon Hungary.

The community suffered severely after the Second World War. The Volksbund movement had drawn many Hungarian Germans toward Nazi Germany; in retaliation, between 1946 and 1948 roughly 200,000–240,000 Germans — about half the community — were expelled to Germany, and many others were deported to forced labour in the Soviet Union (the so-called malenki robot). Property was confiscated and public use of German suppressed for years.

Present day

Since 1993, Hungary's minorities law has recognised the Germans as a national minority with rights to cultural autonomy; the National Self-Government of Germans in Hungary (LdU/MNOÖ) coordinates hundreds of local minority self-governments, schools and cultural associations. Nevertheless, assimilation has advanced greatly: only a minority of ethnic Germans still speak a Swabian dialect or standard German at home. Festivals, brass-band music, traditional costume and village museums, especially in Baranya county around Pécs, keep the culture visible.
