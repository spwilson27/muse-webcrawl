Legal anthropology

Community dispute resolution, a central subject of legal anthropology

Legal anthropology, also known as the anthropology of law, is a subfield of anthropology that studies how social order is created, maintained, and repaired in human societies — especially how disputes are processed, rules are invoked, and legal institutions interact with culture. It examines law not only in state courts but in kin groups, villages, religious communities, markets, and international organisations.

History

The field traces its origins to 19th-century comparative jurisprudence, including Henry Maine's Ancient Law (1861), which contrasted societies organised by status with those organised by contract. Bronisław Malinowski's Crime and Custom in Savage Society (1926), based on fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands, argued that binding obligations in non-state societies are upheld by reciprocity and public reputation rather than centralised coercion. Later landmarks include Karl Llewellyn and E. Adamson Hoebel's The Cheyenne Way (1941), which developed the "trouble case" method; Max Gluckman's study of Barotse courts, The Judicial Process Among the Barotse (1955); and P. H. Gulliver's work on mediation among the Arusha.

Themes

Central themes include legal pluralism — the coexistence of state law with customary, religious, and other normative orders in the same social field — and the anthropological study of human rights, indigenous land claims, transitional justice, and international courts. Scholars such as Sally Falk Moore and Sally Engle Merry have shown how law is both imposed from above and reshaped from below as people strategically invoke, evade, or reinterpret rules. Contemporary legal anthropology studies police, prisons, bureaucracies, corporations, and digital platforms as ethnographic sites.

Method

Legal anthropologists rely on long-term participant observation, following individual disputes over time and comparing what people say the rules are with what actually happens. The extended case method allows researchers to see law as a process rather than a static code, revealing negotiation, forum shopping, and the gap between formal doctrine and lived practice.
