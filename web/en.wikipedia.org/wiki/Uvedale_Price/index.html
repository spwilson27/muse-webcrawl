Uvedale Price


Sir Uvedale Price, 1st Baronet

Sir Uvedale Price, 1st Baronet (1747–1829) was a British landowner and writer, one of the chief theorists of the picturesque in landscape aesthetics. From his estate at Foxley in Herefordshire he reshaped his own grounds and argued, against the smooth, shaven lawns of Capability Brown and Humphry Repton, for roughness, irregularity and intricate detail in landscape design.

The picturesque controversy

Price's Essay on the Picturesque, as compared with the Sublime and the Beautiful (1794, expanded in later editions) proposed the picturesque as a third aesthetic category alongside Edmund Burke's sublime and beautiful: qualities such as roughness, sudden variation and decay — an old gnarled tree, a ruined cottage, a gypsy encampment — that strike the eye as a picture does. His close friend Richard Payne Knight developed similar ideas in the poem The Landscape (1794), and their attack on Repton sparked a pamphlet war that shaped Regency taste and, later, the naturalistic garden movement.

Life

Price inherited Foxley as a minor, travelled in Italy, served as MP-adjacent county figure and High Sheriff of Herefordshire, and corresponded with Burke and Sir William Gilpin, whose tours had popularised picturesque scenery. He was created a baronet in 1828, a year before his death at Foxley.
