Cuneiform law

[Image: The stele of the Code of Hammurabi, carved with cuneiform legal provisions]

Cuneiform law refers to the legal systems and written law codes of ancient Mesopotamia and neighbouring regions, recorded in cuneiform script on clay tablets and stone monuments between roughly 2100 BC and the late first millennium BC. These codes are among the earliest written laws in the world.

Major codes

The earliest surviving code is that of Ur-Namma, king of Ur (c. 2100 BC), which prescribes monetary compensation for many offences. The Laws of Eshnunna (c. 1930 BC) regulate prices and wages. The Laws of Lipit-Ishtar (c. 1870 BC) survive in fragments. The most famous and best preserved is the Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BC), a stele of some 282 case-law provisions covering commerce, property, family, and personal injury, famous for the principle of lex talionis — "an eye for an eye". Later collections include the Middle Assyrian Laws and the Neo-Babylonian Laws.

Form and character

Mesopotamian provisions are typically casuistic — written in the form "if a man does X, then Y shall happen to him" — rather than as general abstract principles. Punishments vary by social class: offences against a free man (awīlum) drew harsher penalties than those against a commoner (muškēnum) or slave. Whether the codes functioned as binding statutes or as royal displays of justice remains debated; court records rarely cite them directly.

Influence

Cuneiform law influenced surrounding legal traditions. Similarities in form and content between Hammurabi's code and biblical law (for example the Covenant Code of Exodus) have long been studied, though the degree of direct borrowing is disputed.
