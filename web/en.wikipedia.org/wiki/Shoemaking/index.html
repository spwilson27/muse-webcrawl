Shoemaking

Shoemaking is the process of making footwear. Originally, shoes were made one at a time by hand, often by groups of shoemakers, or cordwainers (sometimes misidentified as cobblers, who repair shoes rather than make them). In the 18th century, dozens or even hundreds of masters, journeymen, and apprentices were engaged in the trade, which was divided into distinct branches. Traditional shoemaking, now largely replaced by industrial mass production, survives as a craft in bespoke and orthopedic footwear.

History

Shoemaking was among the earliest trades to be partly mechanized. The rolling machine (1755), the Blake stitcher patented by Lyman Reed Blake in 1858, and Jan Ernst Matzeliger's lasting machine of 1883 transformed production; Matzeliger's invention, which mechanized the most difficult hand operation — lasting the upper over the last — dramatically cut prices and reshaped the industry, notably in Lynn, Massachusetts and Leicester, England. Charles Goodyear Jr.'s welt-stitching machine (1869) standardized the Goodyear welt construction still prized in high-end shoes.

Process

Hand shoemaking proceeds through several stages: measuring the foot; cutting the upper leather and lining; closing (stitching) the upper; lasting, in which the upper is shaped over a wooden or plastic last; welting or stitching the upper to the insole; attaching the sole and heel; and finishing — trimming, burnishing, and polishing. A bespoke pair may take 30–50 hours of labor and multiple fittings.

Tools and materials

Traditional tools include the last, awls, a curved needle, waxed linen thread, a fudge wheel for marking stitches, lasting pincers, hammers, and knives. Uppers are commonly of calf leather, cordovan, or suede; soles of oak-bark-tanned leather or rubber. Construction methods include Goodyear welted, Blake stitched, cemented, and Norwegian (storm-welted) construction.
