Urinary incontinence

Urinary incontinence (UI), also known as involuntary urination, is any uncontrolled leakage of urine. It is a common and often under-reported condition that increases in prevalence with age, affecting women roughly twice as often as men, although it occurs in both sexes and in children (in whom it is usually called enuresis). It ranges from occasional minor leaks to complete loss of bladder control, and can significantly affect quality of life, dignity, and mental health.

Types

  • Stress incontinence: leakage when abdominal pressure rises — coughing, sneezing, laughing, or lifting — typically due to weakness of the pelvic floor and urethral sphincter support, often following childbirth or prostate surgery.
  • Urge incontinence (overactive bladder): leakage preceded by a sudden, compelling urge to void, caused by involuntary detrusor contractions; it may be idiopathic or follow neurological disease.
  • Overflow incontinence: dribbling from an over-full bladder that fails to empty, as with bladder outlet obstruction from an enlarged prostate or an underactive detrusor.
  • Mixed incontinence: a combination of stress and urge symptoms, common in older women.
  • Functional incontinence: leakage because physical or cognitive impairments prevent reaching the toilet in time, despite a normal urinary tract.

Causes and diagnosis

Transient causes are summarized by the mnemonic DIAPPERS (delirium, infection, atrophic urethritis, pharmaceuticals, psychological factors, excess urine output, restricted mobility, stool impaction). Evaluation typically includes a history and bladder diary, physical examination, urinalysis, and post-void residual measurement; urodynamic studies are reserved for complex cases or before surgery.

Management

First-line management is usually conservative: lifestyle measures and weight loss, treatment of constipation, pelvic floor muscle training (Kegel exercises), bladder training, and scheduled voiding. Medications include antimuscarinics and the beta-3 agonist mirabegron for urge incontinence, and topical estrogen in selected postmenopausal women. Refractory cases may be treated with botulinum toxin injections into the bladder, sacral neuromodulation, or posterior tibial nerve stimulation; surgical options for stress incontinence include mid-urethral slings, colposuspension, and, in men, artificial urinary sphincters. Absorbent products and catheters are used when other measures fail or are unsuitable.
