Transformational leadership

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transformational leadership is a style of leadership in which leaders inspire and motivate followers to achieve extraordinary outcomes and, in the process, to develop their own leadership capacity. Rather than relying mainly on exchanges of rewards for performance (the mark of transactional leadership), transformational leaders raise followers' awareness of the importance of collective goals, appeal to higher ideals and values, and encourage followers to transcend their own self-interest for the sake of the team or organisation.

Origins

The concept was introduced by James V. Downton (1973) and developed by political historian James MacGregor Burns in his book Leadership (1978), which distinguished "transforming" from "transactional" leadership and emphasised the moral dimension of leaders and followers raising one another to higher levels of motivation and purpose. Bernard M. Bass extended the idea into organisational psychology, making it measurable and applicable to business and military settings, and later collaborated with Bruce Avolio on the "Full Range Leadership Model".

The four components

Bass's model describes four behaviours — the "Four I's":

  • Idealised influence: the leader acts as a charismatic role model, earning followers' trust and admiration.
  • Inspirational motivation: the leader articulates an appealing vision and communicates optimism and high expectations.
  • Intellectual stimulation: the leader challenges assumptions and encourages creativity and independent problem-solving.
  • Individualised consideration: the leader attends to each follower's needs, acting as coach or mentor.

Measurement and evidence

The style is standardly measured with the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ). Meta-analyses find transformational leadership positively associated with follower satisfaction, motivation, extra effort and unit performance across many settings, and it is widely taught in management and military education. Critics note conceptual overlap between its components and charisma, risks of manipulation or dependency on the leader, and measurement problems — including that many studies rely on followers rating both the leader's behaviour and the outcomes.
