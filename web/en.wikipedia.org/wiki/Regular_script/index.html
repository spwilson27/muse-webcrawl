Regular script

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Regular script (kaishu) is the standard style for Chinese characters

The regular script (traditional Chinese: 楷書; simplified Chinese: 楷书; pinyin: kǎishū; Japanese: 楷書, kaisho), also called kaishu or the standard script, is the newest of the Chinese script styles to develop and the standard style of handwriting and most printed Chinese characters today. It emerged during the Han dynasty from a formalised form of clerical script and matured in the Wei and Jin dynasties; by the Tang dynasty it had become the canonical style, elaborated by master calligraphers such as Ouyang Xun, Yu Shinan, Chu Suiliang and Yan Zhenqing.

Characteristics

In regular script every stroke of a character is written separately and discretely, in a fixed stroke order, with strokes clearly distinguished — unlike the running (semi-cursive) and cursive scripts, where strokes merge and forms are abbreviated. Characters are upright, balanced and roughly uniform in size, which makes kaishu the style taught first to learners and the model for typefaces (often called Ming or Song in print, derived historically from regular-script carving). Its name means "model script": it is the standard against which other styles are measured.

History and use

Kaishu developed between the late Han and the Three Kingdoms period — Zhong Yao is traditionally credited as its first master — and reached perfection in the Tang (618–907), when it was prescribed for official documents and examinations. Stelae such as Ouyang Xun's Stele of the Sweet Spring and Yan Zhenqing's massive, robust characters became canonical models studied for over a millennium. Today regular script dominates printing, signage, education and formal handwriting across China, Japan (where it is one of the fundamental styles of shodō) and Korea.
