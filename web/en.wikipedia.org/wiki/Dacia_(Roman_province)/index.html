Roman Dacia was a province of the Roman Empire from AD 106 to about 271, occupying much of modern Transylvania, the Banat and Oltenia in present-day Romania. It was created by the emperor Trajan after his conquest of the Dacian kingdom of Decebalus in the Dacian Wars (101–102 and 105–106).

Conquest and organisation

Trajan's campaigns, commemorated on Trajan's Column in Rome, ended with the fall of the Dacian capital Sarmizegetusa Regia and the death of Decebalus. The new province was organised around the colony of Ulpia Traiana Sarmizegetusa and later subdivided (Dacia Superior, Dacia Inferior and Dacia Porolissensis, and under Marcus Aurelius the "Three Dacias"). Two legions, XIII Gemina and V Macedonica, were stationed there, supported by an extensive frontier of forts and watchtowers (limes).

Resources and Romanisation

Dacia was prized for its gold and silver mines, notably at Alburnus Maior (Roșia Montană), worked with labour drawn from across the empire, as well as salt and iron. Colonists from many provinces settled there, Latin spread widely, and towns, roads, amphitheatres and temples were built on the Roman model. The province's conquest and colonisation are traditionally regarded as central to the ethnogenesis of the Romanian people and the Romanian language, though the continuity of the Romanised population after Rome's withdrawal is debated by historians.

Withdrawal

Under pressure from the Goths and Carpi, the emperor Aurelian withdrew the army and administration south of the Danube around AD 271–275, resettling provincials in a new "Dacia Aureliana" in Moesia. Dacia was the last major province added to the empire and one of the first abandoned.
