Jeeves

Jeeves, the impeccable valet from the stories of P. G. Wodehouse

Reginald Jeeves is a fictional valet created by the English humorist P. G. Wodehouse, appearing in short stories and novels from 1915 until 1974. Jeeves is employed by the idle young gentleman Bertie Wooster, who narrates the stories, and is famed for extricating his master and his friends from social catastrophes with effortless ingenuity.

Character

Jeeves is the archetype of the perfect English gentleman's gentleman: discreet, omniscient, immaculately dressed, and devoted to propriety. He attributes his intelligence to eating fish. Where Bertie is impulsive and slangy, Jeeves is formal, quoting Shakespeare, Spinoza, and the classics, and gliding silently — "shimmering" — in and out of rooms. His schemes resolve the plots, frequently involving breaking unwanted engagements, retrieving embarrassing documents, or outwitting formidable aunts, though his plans may involve temporary embarrassment for Bertie, especially when they begin as retaliation for some garment of Bertie's Jeeves disapproves of.

The stories

The canon comprises eleven novels and numerous short stories, including The Inimitable Jeeves (1923), Right Ho, Jeeves (1934), The Code of the Woosters (1938), and Jeeves and the Feudal Spirit (1954). The stories are set in a timeless interwar world of country houses and London clubs.

Legacy

"Jeeves" became a generic term for a model valet and entered dictionaries as such. The character has been played by Stephen Fry in the ITV series Jeeves and Wooster (1990–93, opposite Hugh Laurie), and inspired the name of the search engine Ask Jeeves. Scholars debate whether Wodehouse took the name from Percy Jeeves, a Warwickshire cricketer killed in the First World War.
