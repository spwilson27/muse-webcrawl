Uterine fibroid

Uterine fibroids, also known as uterine leiomyomas, are benign (non-cancerous) smooth muscle tumours of the uterus. They are the most common type of tumour found in the female pelvis, occurring in a large proportion of women by the age of 50. While most fibroids cause no symptoms, they can produce heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding, pelvic pressure or pain, frequent urination, and complications during pregnancy.

Classification

Fibroids are classified by their location in the uterus:

  • Intramural fibroids grow within the muscular wall of the uterus (the myometrium) and are the most common type.
  • Subserosal fibroids grow on the outer surface of the uterus and may become pedunculated, attached by a stalk.
  • Submucosal fibroids grow just beneath the lining of the uterine cavity and protrude into it; they are most likely to cause heavy bleeding and fertility problems.

Causes and risk factors

The exact cause is unknown, but fibroids arise from a single smooth muscle cell that proliferates clonally, and their growth is stimulated by the hormones estrogen and progesterone. Risk factors include a family history of fibroids, early onset of menstruation, obesity, and African ancestry — women of African descent develop fibroids more often, at younger ages, and with more severe symptoms. Fibroids typically shrink after menopause.

Diagnosis and treatment

Fibroids are often discovered during a pelvic examination and confirmed by ultrasound; MRI can map them precisely before surgery. Treatment depends on symptoms and the patient's desire for fertility. Options range from watchful waiting and medications (such as NSAIDs, hormonal contraceptives, and GnRH analogues) to procedures including uterine artery embolization, focused ultrasound, myomectomy (surgical removal of the fibroids), and hysterectomy, which remains the definitive treatment.
