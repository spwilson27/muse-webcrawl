Value-added tax in the United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, the value-added tax (VAT) is a consumption tax levied on most goods and services supplied by VAT-registered businesses, administered by HM Revenue and Customs. It was introduced on 1 April 1973, replacing purchase tax and selective employment tax, as a condition of the UK's entry into the European Economic Community.

Rates

The standard rate is 20%, applying to most goods and services. A reduced rate of 5% applies to items such as domestic fuel and power, energy-saving materials and children's car seats. Many essentials are zero-rated, including most food, books and newspapers, children's clothes, passenger transport and new dwellings; other supplies, such as financial services, health and education, are exempt — a distinction that matters because exempt businesses cannot reclaim input VAT.

History

VAT began in 1973 at a standard rate of 10%. Rates changed frequently in the 1970s, including a controversial "luxury" rate of 25%. In 1979 the Conservative government raised the standard rate to 15%; it rose to 17.5% in 1991 in place of the community charge ("poll tax"). After a temporary cut to 15% during the 2008 financial crisis, the rate rose to 20% on 4 January 2011, where it has remained. The registration threshold was raised to £90,000 in April 2024, its first increase in several years.

Administration

Businesses above the registration threshold must charge VAT on taxable supplies, file returns (normally quarterly) and may reclaim VAT paid on purchases. Since 2019 most businesses must keep records and file through compatible software under the Making Tax Digital programme. After Brexit, UK VAT law became fully independent of the EU VAT Directive, though Northern Ireland remains aligned with EU rules for goods under the Windsor Framework.

Controversies

Zero-rating anomalies have produced famous disputes, including the 1991 tribunal decision that Jaffa Cakes are cakes (zero-rated) rather than biscuits, and the proposed 2012 "pasty tax" on hot takeaway food, which was largely abandoned after public protest.
