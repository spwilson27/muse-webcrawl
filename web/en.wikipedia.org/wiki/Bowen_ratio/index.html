Bowen ratio

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Surface energy balance over contrasting surfaces.

The Bowen ratio is a dimensionless number used in meteorology and hydrology to describe the partitioning of available energy at a surface between sensible heat flux and latent heat flux. It is named after the astrophysicist Ira Sprague Bowen, who first used the ratio in his work on evaporation from water surfaces in 1926.[1]

The Bowen ratio, usually denoted by the Greek letter β (beta), is defined as the ratio of sensible heat flux H to latent heat flux LE:

β = H / LE

where both fluxes are measured in the same units, typically watts per square metre (W m−2). Because it is a ratio of two energy fluxes, the Bowen ratio is dimensionless.

Significance

The Bowen ratio provides a compact measure of how a surface "spends" the energy it receives from solar radiation. When water is freely available, as over oceans or well-watered vegetation, most of the available energy goes into evaporation and transpiration, so the latent heat flux dominates and the Bowen ratio is small. Over dry surfaces such as deserts, little energy can be used for evaporation, so the surface heats the air directly and the Bowen ratio is large.

Typical values of the Bowen ratio for different surfaces are approximately:

SurfaceTypical Bowen ratio
Ocean0.1
Wet grassland0.2 – 0.5
Forest0.4 – 1.0
Semi-arid terrain2 – 6
Desert10 or more

A Bowen ratio near unity means that sensible and latent heating are roughly equal, a condition often found over temperate land surfaces in the growing season. Negative values can occur when one flux is directed toward the surface while the other is directed away, for example during nocturnal condensation or over irrigated fields surrounded by hot dry land (the "oasis effect"), where the ratio may fall between −1 and 0.

Measurement

The Bowen ratio method is a widely used micrometeorological technique for estimating evaporation. It assumes that the turbulent transfer coefficients for heat and water vapour are equal. Using measurements of temperature and humidity at two heights above the surface, the ratio can be estimated from the vertical gradients:

β = γ (ΔT / Δe)

where γ is the psychrometric constant, ΔT is the difference in air temperature between the two measurement levels, and Δe is the corresponding difference in vapour pressure. Combined with a measurement of the net radiation and soil heat flux, the individual sensible and latent heat fluxes can then be solved from the surface energy balance. The method is simple and inexpensive but becomes unreliable when the measured gradients are small, such as near dawn and dusk, or when the Bowen ratio approaches −1, at which point the equations become ill-conditioned.

Applications

Bowen ratio measurements are used in agricultural meteorology to estimate crop water use, in climate studies to characterise land–atmosphere interactions, and in the validation of land-surface schemes in weather and climate models. Long-term changes in the Bowen ratio can signal shifts in regional climate, such as drying of soils or changes in vegetation cover.
