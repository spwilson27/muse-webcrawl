Crop diversity is the variety of plants used in agriculture — the differing species of crops grown and, within each species, the genetic diversity of varieties and landraces. It underpins food security by providing resilience against pests, diseases, and changing climates, and is the raw material for plant breeding.

Importance

Diverse cropping systems buffer production: if one variety fails, others may survive. Genetic diversity within a crop allows breeders to find traits such as drought tolerance or disease resistance. Humanity depends heavily on a narrow base — rice, wheat, and maize supply roughly half of global plant-derived calories — making the diversity within and beyond these staples strategically vital.

Loss of diversity

The FAO has estimated that about 75% of crop genetic diversity was lost during the 20th century as farmers abandoned local landraces for uniform high-yielding varieties. Varietal uniformity has caused famous failures: the Irish Great Famine was worsened by dependence on a single potato variety susceptible to late blight, and the 1970 southern corn leaf blight epidemic in the United States exploited uniform cytoplasm in maize.

Conservation

  • Ex situ: seed banks and gene banks store accessions; the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway, opened in 2008, backs up collections from around the world.
  • In situ / on-farm: farmers continue growing landraces in centres of origin, preserving ongoing adaptation.
  • Crop wild relatives: wild species related to crops are conserved as sources of useful genes.

The International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture (2001) established a multilateral system for access and benefit-sharing for major crops.
