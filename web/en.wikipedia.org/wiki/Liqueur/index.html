Liqueur

A liqueur (US: /lɪˈkɜːr/) is an alcoholic drink composed of spirits (such as brandy, vodka or rum) and additional flavorings such as sugar, fruits, herbs and spices. Often served with or after dessert, they are typically heavily sweetened and un-aged beyond a resting period during production, when necessary, for their flavors to mingle.

History

Liqueurs are historical descendants of herbal medicines prepared by monks in Italy as early as the 13th century for medicinal purposes. Early cordials combined distilled spirits with honey, herbs and spices believed to have curative properties. By the Renaissance, monastic orders such as the Carthusians (Chartreuse) and Benedictines (Bénédictine) had developed celebrated recipes that survive today.

Production

Liqueurs are made by flavouring a base spirit through infusion, percolation, maceration or distillation of the flavouring materials, then sweetening. In the United States, a liqueur must contain at least 2.5% sugar by weight; European rules for many categories are stricter. Alcohol content typically ranges from 15% to 30% ABV, though some, such as Chartreuse (55%), are much stronger.

Categories

Common categories include fruit liqueurs (orange liqueurs such as Cointreau and Grand Marnier, cherry liqueur), cream liqueurs (Baileys Irish Cream), coffee liqueurs (Kahlúa, Tia Maria), herbal liqueurs (Chartreuse, Jägermeister), nut-flavoured liqueurs (amaretto, Frangelico) and anise liqueurs (sambuca, pastis). Amaro, the Italian family of bitter herbal liqueurs, is traditionally drunk as a digestif.
