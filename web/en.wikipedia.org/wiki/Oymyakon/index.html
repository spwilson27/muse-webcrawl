Oymyakon

Oymyakon (Russian: Оймяко́н; Yakut: Өймөкөөн) is a rural locality in Oymyakonsky District of the Sakha Republic (Yakutia), Russia, in the valley of the Indigirka River. With a population of about 500, it is famous as one of the coldest permanently inhabited places on Earth and, together with nearby Verkhoyansk, contests the title of the Northern Hemisphere's "Pole of Cold". A temperature of −67.7 °C (−89.9 °F) was recorded there on 6 February 1933, the lowest temperature officially recorded in the Northern Hemisphere; an even earlier reading of −71.2 °C from 1924 is often cited but was not officially accepted as it was estimated rather than measured with standard instruments.

Geography and climate

The village lies in a valley between mountain ranges at roughly 63°N, about a two-day drive from the republic's capital, Yakutsk, along the Kolyma Highway — the notorious "Road of Bones" built by Gulag labour. Its extreme winter cold results from a combination of high latitude, great distance from moderating oceans, high elevation and persistent temperature inversions that trap dense cold air in the valley beneath the Siberian High. Winter temperatures routinely fall below −50 °C, and the ground is permanently frozen (permafrost). Summers, by contrast, are surprisingly warm and brief, with temperatures occasionally exceeding 30 °C, giving Oymyakon one of the largest seasonal temperature ranges on Earth — a swing of over 100 °C between record extremes.

Life in the village

Residents traditionally include Yakuts engaged in cattle and horse breeding, hunting, fishing and reindeer herding; the hardy Yakutian horse and cattle breeds are adapted to survive outdoors through the winter. Daily life poses distinctive problems: cars must be kept in heated garages or left running, batteries die quickly, ink freezes in pens, and funerals can require days of work with bonfires to thaw the ground for digging graves. Indoor plumbing is impractical because pipes freeze, so many homes use outdoor toilets, and a central coal-fired plant provides heating. The village school closes only when temperatures fall below about −55 °C. Despite — or because of — the extremes, "cold tourism" has grown, with visitors coming to experience the record cold, take photographs at the Pole of Cold monument and experience local traditions such as eating frozen raw fish (stroganina).

Name

The name is said to derive from a Yakut word meaning "unfrozen water" or "place where fish spend the winter", referring to a nearby thermal spring that keeps a stretch of water ice-free — a resource that originally attracted reindeer herders to stop at the site.
