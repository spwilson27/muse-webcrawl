The foreign exchange market (often abbreviated to forex, FX, or the currency market) is a global, decentralised, over-the-counter market for the trading of currencies. It determines the relative exchange rates between different currencies and facilitates international trade, investment and tourism by allowing businesses and other participants to convert one currency into another.

By most measures the foreign exchange market is the largest and most liquid financial market in the world. According to the Bank for International Settlements' triennial survey, average daily trading volume reached approximately US$7.5 trillion in April 2022. Trading takes place continuously around the clock on weekdays, moving between the major financial centres of Asia, Europe and North America as each business day begins.

History

Currency exchange is ancient in origin, with money changers operating in biblical and classical times. The modern market took shape after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates in the early 1970s, when major currencies began to float freely against one another. The growth of electronic trading from the 1990s onwards opened the market to institutional and eventually retail participants.

Market participants

Participants range from central banks, which intervene to manage their currencies, to commercial and investment banks — the so-called interbank market — multinational corporations hedging foreign revenues, investment managers, hedge funds, currency speculators and retail forex brokers. Unlike a stock exchange, the market has no central clearing location; trades are executed over electronic networks between counterparties.

Trading characteristics

Currencies are traded in pairs, such as the euro against the US dollar (EUR/USD), and prices are quoted with a bid–ask spread. Major instruments include spot transactions, outright forwards, foreign exchange swaps, currency swaps and options. The US dollar appears on one side of the great majority of trades, and the most traded currency pairs are known as "the majors".

Determinants of exchange rates

Exchange rates are influenced by interest-rate differentials, inflation, trade and current-account balances, government debt, political stability and economic performance, as well as market sentiment and speculation. Because of its depth and liquidity, the foreign exchange market is highly sensitive to macroeconomic news releases.
