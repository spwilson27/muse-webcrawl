Totipotency

Cell potency is a cell's ability to differentiate into other cell types. The more cell types a cell can differentiate into, the greater its potency. Totipotency (from Latin totus, "wholly") is the ability of a single cell to divide and produce all of the differentiated cells in an organism, including extraembryonic tissues. Totipotent cells can form a complete, viable organism on their own.

Totipotent cells

In mammals, the zygote and the blastomeres of the very early embryo (up to roughly the 8-cell stage in humans) are totipotent: each can give rise to an entire individual plus the placenta and other extraembryonic membranes, which is why identical twins can result when an early embryo splits. Plant cells are famously more plastic: many differentiated plant cells retain totipotency, allowing a whole plant to be regenerated from a cutting or even a single somatic cell in tissue culture — a fact demonstrated by F. C. Steward's regeneration of carrot plants from root cells in the 1950s.

Hierarchy of potency

  • Totipotent — can form all cell types, including extraembryonic tissues (e.g. the zygote).
  • Pluripotent — can form all cell types of the embryo proper (the three germ layers) but not extraembryonic tissues (e.g. embryonic stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells).
  • Multipotent — can form multiple cell types within a lineage (e.g. haematopoietic stem cells).
  • Oligopotent — can form a few cell types (e.g. lymphoid progenitors).
  • Unipotent — can form only one cell type, while retaining self-renewal.

Significance

Understanding and manipulating potency underpins regenerative medicine, cloning by somatic-cell nuclear transfer, and stem-cell therapy. Strictly, in mammals only the zygote and early blastomeres meet the full definition of totipotency, making experimental verification demanding: a totipotent cell must generate a complete organism when placed in a suitable environment.
