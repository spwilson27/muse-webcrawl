Isthmus of Corinth

The Corinth Canal cuts through the narrow Isthmus of Corinth.

The Isthmus of Corinth (Greek: Ισθμός της Κορίνθου) is the narrow land bridge which connects the Peloponnese peninsula in southern Greece with the rest of the country's mainland, near the city of Corinth. The isthmus was known in the ancient world as the landmark separating the Peloponnese from the rest of Greece. It is about 6.3 km (3.9 miles) wide at its narrowest point, bounded on one side by the Gulf of Corinth and the Ionian Sea and on the other by the Saronic Gulf and the Aegean Sea.

Since 1893 the Corinth Canal has cut through the isthmus, effectively making the Peloponnese an island. Before the canal, ships and cargoes were transported overland across the isthmus along the Diolkos, a paved trackway built in the 6th century BC.

History

Ancient period

The isthmus was strategically vital from antiquity: whoever controlled it controlled both land access to the Peloponnese and the portage route between the Aegean and Ionian seas. The city of Corinth grew wealthy from this trade. Near the isthmus stood the sanctuary of Poseidon at Isthmia, where the Isthmian Games — one of the four Panhellenic festivals of ancient Greece — were held every two years.

Several ancient rulers attempted to dig a canal through the isthmus, including the tyrant Periander in the 6th century BC, Demetrius Poliorcetes, Julius Caesar, Caligula, and Nero, who ceremonially began digging with a golden spade in AD 67 but abandoned the project. Instead, the Diolkos trackway allowed ships to be hauled across the isthmus on wheeled cradles.

The Hexamilion wall

In the early 5th century AD, the Byzantine emperor Theodosius II built the Hexamilion ("six-mile") wall across the isthmus to defend the Peloponnese against invasion from the north. The wall was repeatedly repaired — notably by Justinian I and later by the Despot Manuel II Palaiologos in 1415 — but was breached on several occasions and finally abandoned after the Ottoman conquest in the 15th century.

Corinth Canal

Construction of the modern Corinth Canal began in 1881 and was completed in 1893. The canal is 6.4 km long, 21.4 metres wide at its base, and cuts through limestone cliffs up to 90 metres high. Although too narrow for most modern ocean-going ships, it remains in use by smaller vessels and is a notable tourist attraction, famous for its sheer rock walls and the submersible bridges at either end.

Geology

The isthmus lies on an active fault zone between the subsiding Gulf of Corinth rift to the west and the Saronic Gulf. The region experiences frequent earthquakes, and uplifted marine terraces around the isthmus record its tectonic history. The ancient port of Lechaeum and other coastal sites show evidence of repeated uplift and subsidence.
