CoderDojo

A CoderDojo session in progress

Founded: 2011, Cork, Ireland

Founders: James Whelton, Bill Liao

Type: Non-profit volunteer movement

Focus: Free programming clubs for young people aged 7–17

CoderDojo is a global, volunteer-led movement of free community programming clubs ("dojos") for children and teenagers, typically aged 7 to 17. Founded in Cork, Ireland, in 2011 by James Whelton and entrepreneur Bill Liao, the movement grew out of a school coding club Whelton started after teaching himself to code. Participants, called "ninjas", learn to build websites, apps, games and hardware projects in languages and tools such as Scratch, HTML/CSS, Python and JavaScript, guided by volunteer "mentors" in an informal, peer-learning environment.

History and growth

The first CoderDojo was held in Cork in July 2011; the idea spread virally through the tech community, and within a few years hundreds of dojos were running in dozens of countries. The CoderDojo Foundation was established in Dublin in 2013 to support the community, coordinate resources and uphold the movement's open charter. In 2017 the Foundation joined forces with the Raspberry Pi Foundation, while dojos themselves remained independent volunteer-run clubs. At its peak, the network numbered well over a thousand active dojos in more than 100 countries, alongside spin-off and sibling initiatives such as CoderDojo's "Coolest Projects" showcase exhibition, where young makers present their creations.

Model

Any individual or organisation may start a dojo provided it follows the CoderDojo charter: sessions must be free, volunteer-led, safe for children, and inclusive. There is no set curriculum; learning is project-based and self-directed, with mentors assisting rather than lecturing. Sessions commonly take place in libraries, schools, universities, community centres and company offices on weekends.

Impact

CoderDojo is frequently cited as one of the largest grassroots movements addressing the digital-skills gap and diversifying access to computing outside formal education, alongside initiatives such as Code Club (with which the Raspberry Pi Foundation later aligned its programmes). Both organisations encourage girls' participation and outreach to disadvantaged communities.
