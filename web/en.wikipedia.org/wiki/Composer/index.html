Composer

A composer is a person who writes music, especially as a professional occupation. Composers create original musical works — in genres ranging from symphonies, operas, and chamber music to film scores, songs, and electronic music — which are typically recorded in musical notation for performance by musicians, or produced directly in recorded form. The term derives from Latin componere, "to put together."

History

Although music has been composed for as long as it has existed, the identity of the composer as a distinct creative author emerged with the development of musical notation in medieval Europe; early named composers of the Western tradition include Hildegard of Bingen, Guillaume de Machaut, and the masters of Renaissance polyphony such as Josquin des Prez. In the Baroque and Classical eras, composers such as Bach, Handel, Haydn, and Mozart typically worked under patronage from courts, churches, or aristocrats. The nineteenth century, beginning with Beethoven, saw the rise of the independent composer supported by publishing, public concerts, and copyright — and the beginnings of the modern "canon" of great composers. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, composition has diversified across concert music, jazz, popular song, film and television scoring, video game music, and electronic and experimental genres.

The composer's craft

Composition involves generating and developing musical ideas — melody, harmony, rhythm, form, timbre, and texture — and fixing them in a score or recording. Composers may work on commission, to deadlines set by performers, film directors, or publishers, and their works are protected by copyright, which typically lasts for decades after the composer's death. The roles of composer, arranger, orchestrator, and songwriter overlap but are distinguished by the degree of original material created.
