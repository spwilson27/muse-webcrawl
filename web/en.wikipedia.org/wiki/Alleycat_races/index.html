Alleycat races

An alleycat race is an informal bicycle race that originated among bicycle messengers. Alleycats are typically organised in cities, emphasise local knowledge and traffic skills over pure speed, and are named by analogy with alley cats — scrappy, streetwise and unconcerned with pedigree. The format blends racing with the checkpoint structure of a messenger's working day.

Format

Riders receive a manifest listing a series of checkpoints — addresses, intersections or businesses — shortly before the start, and must plan their own route between them, receiving a stamp, signature or token at each. The first rider to return with a complete manifest wins; some races require tasks at checkpoints, such as changing a tube or answering a question, and prizes are often deliberately modest: a bag, a trophy of junk, or cash from entry fees ("winner takes the pot"). Because alleycats run on open streets, organisation is informal and legal status varies; riders are responsible for their own conduct in traffic.

History

The first documented alleycat was held in Toronto in 1989, organised by messenger John Englar. The format spread through the courier communities of North America and Europe during the 1990s, alongside messenger culture and fixed-gear cycling. Major annual events include Monstertrack in New York City (first held in 2000, restricted to fixed-gear track bikes without brakes), Stupor Bowl in Minneapolis, and events attached to the Cycle Messenger World Championships, held since 1993.

Culture

Alleycats helped define urban cycling subculture: spoke cards from races are collected as trophies in wheel spokes. As messenger work declined with electronic documents, alleycats persisted as social and sporting events open to non-messengers, and the format has been adapted for charity rides and city-cycling celebrations worldwide.
