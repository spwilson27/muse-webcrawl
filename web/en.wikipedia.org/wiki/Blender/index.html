Blender

A countertop blender

A blender (sometimes called a mixer or liquidiser in British English) is a kitchen and laboratory appliance used to mix, crush, purée, or emulsify food and other substances. A stationary blender consists of a container with a rotating metal or plastic blade at the bottom, powered by an electric motor in the base. Hand-held immersion blenders have the motor at the top connected by a shaft to blades at the bottom and can be used directly in a cooking pot.

History

The electric blender was developed in the 1920s. Polish-American Stephen J. Poplawski patented a drink mixer in 1922, and Fred Waring popularized the "Waring Blendor" from 1937, initially for mixing cocktails in bars and later for home kitchens; Waring's company funded early experiments using blenders to homogenize tissue samples in laboratories. John Oster's company made the popular Osterizer. In the 1950s the Brazilian Walita and other makers spread blenders through Latin America, where they are kitchen essentials used for juices, soups, and sauces.

Uses

Blenders make smoothies, milkshakes, puréed soups, sauces such as pesto and mayonnaise, cocktails such as margaritas and piña coladas, and baby food; powerful models crush ice and grind nuts and grains. Countertop blenders range from inexpensive home units to high-performance machines (such as Vitamix and Blendtec models) costing several hundred dollars. In laboratories, blenders homogenize samples for microbiology and chemistry.

Design

The container (jar) is typically glass or plastic with measurement markings and a tight lid; vortex action created by the jar's shape draws ingredients down onto the blades. Motors range from about 200 to over 1,500 watts. Immersion blenders trade power for convenience and easy cleaning.

Similar appliances

A blender differs from a food processor, which uses interchangeable blades and discs for chopping, slicing, and kneading and works better with dry or thick mixtures; and from a juicer, which separates pulp from juice.
