Targa top


A sports car with its targa panel removed

A targa top, or targa for short, is a semi-convertible car body style with a removable roof section and a full-width roll bar behind the seats. The term was first used on the 1966 Porsche 911 Targa, and it remains a registered trademark of Porsche AG.

Design

Unlike a conventional convertible, a targa retains a fixed rollover hoop and usually a fixed rear window, giving the cabin greater structural rigidity and occupant protection while still allowing open-air motoring. The detachable roof panel typically stows in the luggage compartment. In Porsche's original design, the hoop was clad in brushed stainless steel and the model took its name from the Targa Florio road race in Sicily, where Porsche had scored many victories.

History

The targa concept answered growing concern in the 1960s United States that convertibles might be regulated or banned over rollover safety. Porsche introduced the 911 Targa in 1965 at the Frankfurt Motor Show, with production from 1966. Other manufacturers offered similar arrangements under generic names such as "T-top" (with two panels and a central bar), "targa band," or "surrey top" — the Triumph TR4's removable-panel Surrey top of 1961 anticipated the idea.

Targa-style models include the Ferrari Dino 246 GTS, Fiat X1/9, Toyota Supra Sport Roof, Honda NSX-T, Chevrolet Corvette coupe (whose removable panel follows the targa pattern), and later Porsche 911 Targa generations, which since the 991 series have used an automated retracting panel mechanism that preserves the classic silver hoop.

Related styles

The targa differs from the T-top, which has paired removable panels separated by a fixed centre spine, and from the targa band designs of cars like the Honda del Sol. Modern usage often applies "targa" loosely to any car with a single removable roof panel over the front seats.
