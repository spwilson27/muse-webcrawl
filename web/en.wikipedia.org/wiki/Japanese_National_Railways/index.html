Japanese National Railways (日本国有鉄道, Nihon Kokuyū Tetsudō), abbreviated JNR, was the state-owned railway operator of Japan from 1949 to 1987. Created as a public corporation to run the government railway network, it built the Shinkansen high-speed lines before mounting debts led to its breakup and privatisation into the Japan Railways Group (JR Group) in 1987.

History

Government railways in Japan dated to 1872, administered first by the Railway Agency and later by the Ministry of Transport. On June 1, 1949, under the Allied occupation, the system was reorganised as the Japanese National Railways public corporation, intended to operate at arm's length from government while remaining publicly owned.

JNR presided over postwar reconstruction, dieselisation and electrification, and the opening of the Tokaido Shinkansen in 1964 — the world's first high-speed railway — followed by extensions to Okayama, Fukuoka, Niigata, and Sendai/Morioka. However, competition from cars and airlines, politically imposed construction of unprofitable rural lines, and large payrolls drove JNR deep into debt; by the 1980s its accumulated liabilities exceeded ¥37 trillion.

Privatisation

On April 1, 1987, JNR was divided into seven regional and specialised companies — JR Hokkaido, JR East, JR Central, JR West, JR Shikoku, JR Kyushu, and JR Freight — along with research and settlement bodies. Long-term debt was largely transferred to the JNR Settlement Corporation, much of it ultimately borne by taxpayers. The successor companies, especially JR East, JR Central, and JR West, went on to successful stock-market listings and are generally regarded as a landmark case of railway privatisation.

Legacy

The JR companies inherited JNR's network, staff, and much of its operating culture. The Japan Rail Pass and other nationwide products continue under the JR Group, and JNR-era trains remain affectionately remembered in Japanese popular culture.
