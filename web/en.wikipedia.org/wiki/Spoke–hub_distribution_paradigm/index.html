Spoke–hub distribution paradigm

The spoke–hub distribution paradigm is a form of transport network topology in which traffic moves through a central hub (or hubs) rather than directly between pairs of destinations. Named by analogy to a bicycle wheel, in which spokes radiate from a central hub, the model contrasts with point-to-point transit. It is most famously applied in commercial aviation, where airlines route flights through designated hub airports, but also in freight logistics, warehousing, telecommunications, and parcel delivery.

How it works

In a hub-and-spoke system, "spoke" routes carry traffic between outlying points and a central hub, where passengers or cargo transfer to connecting services. This allows a carrier to serve many origin–destination pairs with relatively few routes: with n destinations, a fully connected point-to-point network needs roughly n(n−1)/2 routes, while a hub system needs only n−1. Traffic from many spokes is consolidated, enabling higher load factors and larger, more efficient vehicles on trunk segments.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include economies of scale at hubs, greater network reach, more frequent service on busy routes, and efficient sorting of freight—exemplified by overnight parcel networks in which all packages pass through a central "superhub". Disadvantages include longer travel times and circuitous routing for passengers, vulnerability to disruption when weather or congestion strikes a hub, high infrastructure costs, and congestion at peak "bank" times when many flights arrive and depart in waves.

Aviation

Delta Air Lines pioneered hub operations at Atlanta from the 1950s, and the model became dominant in the United States after airline deregulation in 1978. Fred Smith founded FedEx in 1973 around a Memphis hub, an idea he had outlined in a Yale economics paper. Large hubs such as Atlanta Hartsfield–Jackson, Chicago O'Hare, Frankfurt, and Dubai became among the world's busiest airports. Low-cost carriers such as Southwest Airlines and Ryanair instead emphasised point-to-point flying, though many maintain de facto bases. The growth of long-range twin-engine aircraft such as the Boeing 787 has enabled more long, thin point-to-point routes, while the Airbus A380 was designed for hub-to-hub trunk routes.

Other applications

The paradigm applies to trucking (less-than-truckload carriers), rail freight classification yards, hospital referral networks, and computer networks, where hub-and-spoke topologies simplify routing at the cost of a central single point of failure.
