Biot–Savart law

The Biot–Savart law gives the magnetic field generated by a steady electric current.

The Biot–Savart law is an equation describing the magnetic field generated by a constant electric current. It relates the magnetic field to the magnitude, direction, length and proximity of the electric current, and is a fundamental law of magnetostatics, analogous to Coulomb's law in electrostatics.

Formulation

For a current I flowing along a wire, the magnetic field B at a point in space is obtained by integrating the contributions of infinitesimal current elements along the path: each element I dl contributes a field proportional to I dl × r̂ / r², where r is the vector from the element to the point. The constant of proportionality involves the permeability of free space, μ0/4π. The direction of the contribution follows from the cross product, giving the familiar right-hand rule: field lines circle the wire in the direction of the curled fingers when the thumb points along the current.

History

The law is named after Jean-Baptiste Biot and Félix Savart, who in 1820 — shortly after Hans Christian Ørsted's discovery that a current deflects a compass needle — experimentally investigated the force exerted by a current-carrying wire on a magnet. Pierre-Simon Laplace developed the mathematical formulation, so the equation is sometimes called the Biot–Savart–Laplace law.

Applications

The law is used to compute the fields of finite wires, circular loops, solenoids and Helmholtz coils, and underlies Ampère's law in its integral form. A related form, replacing current density with the velocity of a moving point charge, gives an approximation of the magnetic field of a single moving charge.
