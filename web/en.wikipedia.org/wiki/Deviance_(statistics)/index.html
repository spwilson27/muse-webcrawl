Deviance (statistics)

Deviance compares a fitted model against a saturated model that fits the data perfectly.

In statistics, deviance is a goodness-of-fit statistic for a statistical model; it is most often used for statistical hypothesis testing. It is a generalisation of the idea of using the sum of squares of residuals (ordinary least squares) to cases where model-fitting is achieved by maximum likelihood. The concept was introduced by Nelder and Wedderburn in their work on generalized linear models.

Definition

The deviance of a fitted model is defined as twice the difference between the log-likelihood of the saturated model — a model with as many parameters as observations, which fits the data exactly — and the log-likelihood of the fitted model: D = 2[ln Lsaturated − ln Lfitted]. Smaller deviance indicates a better-fitting model.

Use in generalized linear models

For generalized linear models, deviance generalises residual sum of squares. In normal (Gaussian) models the deviance equals the residual sum of squares; for binomial and Poisson models it takes forms related to likelihood-ratio statistics. Differences in deviance between nested models are used to test hypotheses, approximately following a chi-squared distribution, in analogy with the analysis of variance — leading to the "analysis of deviance" table.

Deviance residuals

Signed square roots of the individual contributions to the deviance define deviance residuals, which are commonly used for model diagnostics and tend to be closer to normality than Pearson residuals for many models.
