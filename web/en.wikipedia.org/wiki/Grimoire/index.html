A grimoire (/ɡrɪmˈwɑːr/ grim-WAHR), also known as a book of spells, is a textbook of magic, typically including instructions on how to create magical objects like talismans and amulets, how to perform magical spells, charms and divination, and how to summon or invoke supernatural entities such as angels, spirits, deities, and demons. In many cases, the books themselves are believed to be imbued with magical powers. The only contents found in a grimoire are information on spells, rituals, the preparation of magical tools, and lists of ingredients and their magical correspondences.

The word grimoire is believed to derive from the Old French word grammaire, which had referred to any book written in Latin and initially to all books, and developed in the Middle Ages into a term for books of magic, which were understood only by the literate clergy and therefore associated with occult learning.

History

The earliest known written magical instructions come from ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt. Magical papyri from Greco-Roman Egypt, written between the 2nd century BCE and the 5th century CE and collectively known as the Greek Magical Papyri, contain spells, rituals and hymns. The Jewish and early Christian traditions contributed works attributed to figures such as Moses and Solomon, including the Key of Solomon tradition.

In medieval Europe, grimoires circulated among the clergy and educated elites. Many texts were attributed, often falsely, to biblical or classical authorities to lend them legitimacy. The invention of printing in the 15th century allowed grimoires to circulate more widely, though manuscripts continued to be produced. In the 19th century, occult revival movements in France and England republished and reinterpreted many older grimoires.

Notable grimoires

  • The Lesser Key of Solomon (the Lemegeton), a 17th-century compilation including the Ars Goetia, a catalogue of 72 demons.
  • The Key of Solomon (Clavicula Salomonis), one of the most influential Solomonic texts.
  • The Sworn Book of Honorius (Liber Juratus), a medieval text framed as a compilation of the knowledge of magicians.
  • The Grand Grimoire, a French grimoire of the 18th or 19th century concerned with pact-making with the demon Lucifugé Rofocale.
  • The Book of Abramelin, attributed to Abraham of Worms, describing an elaborate ritual for attaining the knowledge and conversation of one's guardian angel.
  • Picatrix, an Arabic work of astrological magic known in Latin translation.
  • Three Books of Occult Philosophy by Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa (1531–1533), a synthesis of Renaissance magic.

Contents

Grimoires typically contain astrological correspondences, lists of angels and demons, directions for casting circles and conjuring spirits, instructions for making talismans, and catalogues of herbs, stones and other materials with their occult properties. Many include protective prayers and exorcisms intended to safeguard the practitioner.

In popular culture

Grimoires feature prominently in fantasy literature, film, television and games, where they are often depicted as ancient, powerful and dangerous books, sometimes with a will of their own. The fictional Necronomicon of H. P. Lovecraft is a modern invented grimoire.
