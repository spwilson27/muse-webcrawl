Paramedic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A paramedic is a healthcare professional who responds to emergency calls for medical help outside of a hospital. Paramedics mainly work as part of emergency medical services (EMS), most often in ambulances, but also in rapid-response vehicles, helicopters, and hospital and community settings. They provide advanced life support, assess and treat patients at the scene, and transport them to hospital where necessary.

Scope of practice

Paramedics are trained to manage a wide range of emergencies, including cardiac arrest, heart attacks, strokes, major trauma, and breathing difficulties. Depending on jurisdiction, their skills may include advanced airway management (such as endotracheal intubation), intravenous and intraosseous access, defibrillation and cardiac pacing, 12-lead ECG interpretation, and administration of a broad range of medications. In many countries the profession is regulated, requiring registration and degree-level education; in the United States, paramedics are certified through the NREMT and licensed by the state, above the level of emergency medical technician (EMT).

History

The modern paramedic role emerged from battlefield medicine and from pioneering physician-led schemes in the 1960s, such as Frank Pantridge's mobile coronary care unit in Belfast (1966) and early paramedic programmes in Miami, Seattle, and Los Angeles. In the United States, the EMS Systems Act of 1973 and popular media such as the television series Emergency! helped establish the profession.

Roles and settings

Beyond ambulance work, paramedics now serve as critical care and air ambulance clinicians, community paramedics making home visits, paramedic practitioners in primary care and emergency departments, and in roles in the military, offshore industry, and event medicine. Related grades of ambulance clinician include emergency medical technicians and, in some systems, physician-staffed teams.
