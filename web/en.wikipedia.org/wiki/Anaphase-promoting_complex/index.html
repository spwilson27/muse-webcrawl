Anaphase-promoting complex

The anaphase-promoting complex (APC), also called the cyclosome (APC/C), is a large multi-subunit E3 ubiquitin ligase that controls progression through mitosis and the G1 phase of the cell cycle. By marking specific proteins for destruction by the proteasome, the APC triggers the separation of sister chromatids in anaphase and drives the cell's exit from mitosis.

The APC/C ubiquitinates securin and cyclin B, permitting sister chromatid separation

Function

The APC is activated by association with one of two co-activator proteins, Cdc20 or Cdh1, which help recruit substrates recognizable by short sequence motifs such as the destruction box (D-box) and KEN box. In metaphase, APCCdc20 ubiquitinates securin, freeing the protease separase to cleave the cohesin rings holding sister chromatids together, and cyclin B, inactivating the mitotic kinase CDK1. Later, APCCdh1 maintains the G1 state by continuously degrading mitotic regulators.

Regulation

The spindle assembly checkpoint restrains APCCdc20 until every chromosome is correctly attached to the mitotic spindle; the mitotic checkpoint complex sequesters Cdc20 to prevent premature anaphase. Defects in APC regulation can cause chromosome missegregation and aneuploidy, a hallmark of many cancers.

Structure

The APC is a roughly 1.2-megadalton assembly of about 14 subunits in vertebrates, including a cullin–RING catalytic core (APC2 and APC11) and a large scaffolding platform that positions substrates. Cryo-electron microscopy studies have revealed how co-activator binding reshapes the complex for substrate recognition.
