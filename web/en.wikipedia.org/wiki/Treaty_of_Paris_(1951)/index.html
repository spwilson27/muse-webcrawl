Treaty of Paris (1951)

The Treaty of Paris, signed on 18 April 1951 in Paris, established the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) between France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. It entered into force on 23 July 1952 and is regarded as the founding treaty of what became the European Union.

Background

The treaty implemented the Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950, proposed by French foreign minister Robert Schuman and designed by Jean Monnet. Its aim was to pool French and German coal and steel production — the industries of war — under a common High Authority, making war between the members "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible".

Provisions

The treaty created the ECSC's institutions: the High Authority, a Common Assembly, a Council of Ministers, and a Court of Justice — prototypes of the modern European Commission, Parliament, Council, and Court. It removed tariffs and quotas on coal and steel among the six members and set a 50-year term, expiring on 23 July 2002, when the ECSC's assets passed to the European Community.
