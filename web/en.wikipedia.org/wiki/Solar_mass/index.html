Solar mass

SymbolM☉
Value1.9885 × 1030 kg
≈333,000 Earth masses; 1,047 Jupiter masses

The solar mass (M☉) is a standard unit of mass in astronomy, equal to the mass of the Sun, approximately 1.9885 × 1030 kilograms. It is used to express the masses of other stars, star clusters, nebulae, galaxies, and black holes.

Value and determination

The solar mass is determined from the solar gravitational parameter GM☉, which is measured very precisely from planetary orbits, divided by the gravitational constant G, which is known far less precisely. The Sun comprises about 99.86% of the total mass of the Solar System; Jupiter accounts for most of the remainder. The Sun slowly loses mass through the solar wind and by converting mass to energy in nuclear fusion — in total roughly 4–6 billion kilograms per second.

Use

Stellar masses range from about 0.08 M☉ (the hydrogen-burning limit) to well over 100 M☉ for the most massive stars. The Chandrasekhar limit for white dwarfs is about 1.44 M☉. Supermassive black holes reach millions to billions of solar masses — Sagittarius A* at the centre of the Milky Way is about 4.3 million M☉ — and galaxies contain 1010–1012 M☉ in stars. In binary-star astronomy, masses are often derived directly in solar units from Kepler's third law.
