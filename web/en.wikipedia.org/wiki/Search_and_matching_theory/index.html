Search and matching theory

Search and matching theory is a mathematical framework in economics, principally in labour economics, which models the process by which economic agents form mutually beneficial relationships over time. Unlike the frictionless Walrasian model, in which trade is instantaneous and costless, search and matching models assume it takes time for unemployed workers and firms with job vacancies to find one another. The framework is central to the modern theory of unemployment and was recognised by the 2010 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, awarded to Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen and Christopher Pissarides.

The matching function

The heart of the model is the matching function, usually written M = m(U, V), which gives the number of new matches (hires) formed per period as a function of the stock of unemployed workers U and the stock of vacancies V. The function is typically assumed to be increasing in both arguments and to exhibit constant returns to scale. Labour market tightness, θ = V/U, then determines the rate at which vacancies are filled and the rate at which unemployed workers find jobs.

The Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides model

In the canonical DMP model, firms post vacancies at a cost and workers search while unemployed, receiving unemployment benefits. When a worker and firm meet, they bargain over the wage, commonly using the Nash bargaining solution, splitting the surplus of the match. Because firms can enter freely until the expected value of a vacancy is zero, the model determines equilibrium unemployment, vacancies and wages simultaneously. Unemployment in this framework is not a market failure per se but the outcome of frictions: jobs are destroyed exogenously at a separation rate, and new matches take time to form.

The Beveridge curve

A key empirical relationship associated with the framework is the Beveridge curve, the downward-sloping relationship between the unemployment rate and the vacancy rate. Movements along the curve reflect the business cycle, while shifts of the curve indicate changes in matching efficiency or labour market mismatch.

Applications and extensions

Search and matching models have been applied far beyond labour markets: to monetary economics (search models of money), housing markets, marriage markets, and the market for corporate control. Extensions include on-the-job search, wage posting (the Burdett–Mortensen model), directed search, and endogenous job destruction (Mortensen–Pissarides). The framework has also been used to evaluate unemployment insurance, minimum wages, firing taxes and active labour market policies.
