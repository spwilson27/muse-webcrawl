Reactive attachment disorder

Reactive attachment disorder
SpecialtyChild psychiatry, clinical psychology
SymptomsWithdrawn behaviour toward caregivers, limited emotional responsiveness, irritability or sadness without cause
CausesSevere early neglect or deprivation of adequate caregiving
Diagnostic methodClinical assessment per DSM-5 / ICD-11 criteria

Reactive attachment disorder (RAD) is a trauma- and stressor-related disorder of childhood in which a child exhibits a persistent pattern of inhibited, emotionally withdrawn behaviour toward adult caregivers, rarely seeking or responding to comfort when distressed. It arises from extremes of early insufficient care — severe social neglect, repeated changes of primary caregivers, or rearing in institutions with high child-to-caregiver ratios. RAD is considered uncommon and is distinct from the broader and loosely defined set of "attachment disorders" promoted in some alternative therapies.

Children with RAD characteristically fail to seek comfort from caregivers when distressed.

Signs and symptoms

Under the DSM-5, diagnosis requires a consistent pattern of withdrawn behaviour toward caregivers (the child rarely seeks or responds to comfort), a persistent social and emotional disturbance (minimal responsiveness to others, limited positive affect, and episodes of unexplained irritability, sadness, or fearfulness), and a history of extremes of insufficient care. Symptoms must be evident before the age of five, the child must have a developmental age of at least nine months, and the behaviour must not be better explained by autism spectrum disorder. In the DSM-IV era, a second, "disinhibited" subtype was included; in DSM-5 that presentation was separated out as disinhibited social engagement disorder (DSED).

Causes and research

The disorder is understood to result from the absence of a stable attachment figure in early life. Studies of children raised in Romanian orphanages under the Ceaușescu regime — notably the Bucharest Early Intervention Project — showed markedly elevated rates of RAD among institutionally reared children, and that placement into quality foster care, especially before age two, substantially reduced symptoms.

Treatment

Evidence-based treatment centres on providing the child with a stable, nurturing caregiving environment and on caregiver-focused interventions that build sensitive, responsive care; dyadic therapies such as Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up have empirical support. So-called "attachment therapies" involving coercive holding or rebirthing are not evidence-based, are regarded as pseudoscientific, and have been associated with child deaths; professional bodies such as the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry warn against them.
