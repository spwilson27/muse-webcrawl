Graduate school

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A graduate school (sometimes shortened to grad school) is a school that awards advanced academic degrees, such as master's and doctoral degrees, to students who have already completed an undergraduate (bachelor's) degree. Graduate schools are usually constituent parts of universities, though some are independent institutions.

Degrees

Master's degrees (e.g., MA, MS, MBA, LLM) typically require one to three years of coursework, sometimes with a thesis. Doctoral degrees — most commonly the PhD, and in the United States also professional doctorates such as the MD and JD — require advanced coursework, original research, and a dissertation. In the United States, the term "graduate school" typically excludes professional schools of medicine, law, and business in colloquial usage, though those are formally graduate-level programs.

Admission and funding

Admission generally requires a bachelor's degree, academic transcripts, letters of recommendation, and sometimes standardized tests such as the GRE. Doctoral students are often funded through fellowships, research assistantships, and teaching assistantships that waive tuition and provide a stipend in exchange for research or teaching work.

International terminology

In the United Kingdom and Commonwealth countries, equivalent study is usually called "postgraduate" education and is organized by departments and faculties rather than a single graduate school, though many universities have adopted graduate schools on the American model. In continental Europe, the Bologna Process standardized master's degrees as second-cycle qualifications.
