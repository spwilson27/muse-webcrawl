Gramm–Rudman–Hollings Balanced Budget Act

The Gramm–Rudman–Hollings Balanced Budget and Emergency Deficit Control Act of 1985 and the Balanced Budget and Emergency Deficit Control Reaffirmation Act of 1987 (both often known as the Gramm–Rudman–Hollings Act) were United States federal laws intended to reduce the federal budget deficit through automatic spending cuts known as sequestration.

Provisions

The Act was sponsored by Senators Phil Gramm, Warren Rudman, and Ernest Hollings. It set declining maximum deficit targets over a series of years, aiming at a balanced budget. If the projected deficit exceeded the target and Congress and the President could not agree on reductions, automatic, across-the-board cuts ("sequesters") would be imposed, split between defence and domestic programmes, with many programmes such as Social Security exempted.

Constitutional challenge and aftermath

In Bowsher v. Synar (1986), the Supreme Court held unconstitutional the role assigned to the Comptroller General in triggering the cuts, on separation-of-powers grounds. A revised version followed in 1987. Deficits continued to exceed the targets, and the Act's framework was largely superseded by the Budget Enforcement Act of 1990, which introduced discretionary spending caps and pay-as-you-go rules.
