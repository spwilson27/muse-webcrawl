Deadweight loss

[Image: Supply and demand diagram showing the deadweight loss triangle created by a tax]

Deadweight loss, also known as excess burden or allocative inefficiency, is a loss of economic efficiency that occurs when the equilibrium outcome for a good or service is not achieved or is not achievable. In other words, it is the cost borne by society when a market is not in a state of Pareto optimality, reducing the total of consumer and producer surplus below its maximum.

Causes

Deadweight loss can be caused by:

  • Taxes and subsidies — a tax drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive, discouraging mutually beneficial trades; the lost surplus on those trades is the deadweight loss (the "Harberger triangle").
  • Price controls — price ceilings (such as rent control) and price floors (such as minimum wages) that prevent markets from clearing.
  • Monopoly pricing — a monopolist restricts output to raise price above marginal cost, forgoing trades that would have benefited both parties.
  • Externalities — unpriced costs or benefits that lead to over- or under-production relative to the social optimum.

Example

If a tax of $1 per unit is imposed on a good, some transactions that would have created surplus for both buyer and seller no longer occur. The tax revenue collected is a transfer, not a loss, but the surplus that would have been generated by the trades that no longer happen vanishes entirely—that vanished surplus is the deadweight loss. It grows roughly with the square of the tax rate and with the elasticities of supply and demand.
