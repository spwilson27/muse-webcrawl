Error theory

Moral error theory (image placeholder)

In moral philosophy, an error theory is a position holding that a system of thought is committed to a set of claims that are systematically false, because the world simply lacks the features those claims presuppose. The most influential error theory is in ethics: the moral error theorist holds that moral judgements purport to state objective, categorically reason-giving facts, but that there are no such facts, so all first-order moral claims are false (or, on some versions, fail to be true).

Mackie's argument

The view is most closely associated with J. L. Mackie, who opened his Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977) with the claim "There are no objective values." Mackie offered the argument from queerness — objective values would be entities "of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe" — and the argument from relativity, which holds that the variation in moral codes is better explained by ways of life than by differing perceptions of objective truth.

Error theories in other domains

Analogous error theories have been defended about colour (colours as we experience them do not exist in the external world), about free will, about religious discourse, and about ordinary object talk in mereological nihilism. Error theories are usually accompanied by a proposal about what to do with the defective discourse: abolish it, conserve it, fictionalise it (treating it as a useful fiction), or reform it — options known respectively as abolitionism, conservationism, fictionalism and reformism.
