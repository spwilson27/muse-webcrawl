Syndactyly

Syndactyly is a condition wherein two or more digits are fused together. It occurs normally in some mammals, such as the siamang and diprotodonts, but is an unusual condition in humans. In humans it occurs in approximately 1 in every 2,000 to 3,000 live births, making it one of the most common congenital hand deformities. It may occur as an isolated finding or as part of a syndrome, such as Apert syndrome or Poland syndrome.

Classification

Syndactyly may be described as simple, involving only fusion of soft tissues, or complex, involving fusion of bones as well. It is complete when the fusion extends to the fingertips and incomplete when it extends only partway. The failure of separation is thought to result from a failure of programmed cell death (apoptosis) between the digits during embryonic development, around the sixth to eighth week of gestation.

Treatment

Treatment is generally surgical, separating the digits using zigzag incisions and skin grafts to avoid linear scar contracture. Timing depends on which digits are involved: borders digits such as the thumb and index, or ring and little finger, are usually separated early (around 6 to 12 months) because unequal growth can cause deformity, while central digits may be addressed later in early childhood.
