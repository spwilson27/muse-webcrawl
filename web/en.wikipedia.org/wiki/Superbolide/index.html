Superbolide

A superbolide is an exceptionally bright meteor, or fireball, that reaches an apparent magnitude of −17 or brighter — roughly comparable to the brightness of the full Moon or greater. The term is used by astronomers to describe the most energetic class of bolides, meteors that typically explode in the atmosphere and may produce audible sonic booms, shock waves, and occasionally meteorite falls.

Definition and energy

While an ordinary bolide is a very bright meteor (brighter than about magnitude −4), a superbolide is defined by its extreme luminosity. Such events are produced by meteoroids typically a metre or more in diameter, and their total radiated energy can reach the equivalent of kilotons of TNT. The largest superbolides may rival small nuclear explosions in total energy release, though the energy is deposited gradually along a long atmospheric path rather than at a single point.

Notable events

  • Chelyabinsk meteor (2013) — A superbolide of roughly 18–20 metres in diameter exploded over Chelyabinsk, Russia, on 15 February 2013, with an energy estimated at about 400–500 kilotons. Its shock wave damaged thousands of buildings and injured around 1,500 people, mostly from broken glass.
  • Tunguska event (1908) — The immense airburst over Siberia, which flattened some 2,000 square kilometres of forest, is commonly considered an extreme superbolide event, though no fireball was widely recorded.
  • 1972 Great Daylight Fireball — A bright meteor that passed through Earth's atmosphere over the western United States and Canada in daylight, skipping back into space.

Detection

Superbolides are detected by ground-based camera networks, eyewitness reports, infrasound arrays operated for nuclear-test monitoring, and United States government sensors on satellites, whose declassified flash data are published in databases such as NASA's Center for Near Earth Object Studies fireball catalogue. Their frequency is estimated at a few events per year worldwide for the largest examples.
