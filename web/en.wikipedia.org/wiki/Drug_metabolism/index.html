Drug metabolism


Most drug metabolism occurs in the liver.

Drug metabolism is the metabolic breakdown of drugs by living organisms, usually through specialised enzymatic systems. It is a major branch of pharmacokinetics and determines how long a drug acts, how potent it is, and how it interacts with other substances. Although the liver is the principal site, metabolism also occurs in the gut wall, lungs, kidneys, skin and blood plasma.

Phases

Metabolism is conventionally divided into phases. In phase I, enzymes — above all the cytochrome P450 family — introduce or expose reactive groups by oxidation, reduction or hydrolysis, often inactivating the drug but sometimes converting an inactive prodrug into its active form, or producing toxic metabolites. In phase II, the modified compound is conjugated with a bulky, water-soluble molecule such as glucuronic acid, sulfate, glutathione or an amino acid, increasing its solubility so it can be excreted in urine or bile. Phase III refers to further modification and transport out of cells by efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein.

Factors affecting metabolism

Rates of metabolism vary greatly between individuals. Genetic polymorphisms in enzymes such as CYP2D6 and CYP2C9 divide people into poor, normal, rapid and ultrarapid metabolisers, with consequences for dosing: codeine, for example, is dangerous in ultrarapid metabolisers who convert it quickly to morphine, and ineffective in many poor metabolisers. Age, liver disease, diet (for example grapefruit juice inhibiting intestinal CYP3A4), smoking and other drugs can induce or inhibit metabolic enzymes, causing clinically important drug interactions.

Significance

Understanding a drug's metabolic pathway is essential in drug development: candidate compounds are screened for metabolic stability, active or toxic metabolites, and interaction potential long before clinical trials. Metabolites themselves sometimes become drugs — terfenadine's metabolite fexofenadine replaced its cardiotoxic parent.
