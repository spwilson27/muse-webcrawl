Spectroscopy

Spectroscopy is the study of how matter absorbs, emits and scatters electromagnetic radiation, and of the spectra so produced. Because each atom and molecule has a unique pattern of energy levels, spectra act as fingerprints that reveal composition, structure and physical conditions.

Fraunhofer's dark solar lines (1814) and Kirchhoff and Bunsen's flame emission lines (1859) founded the science, leading to the discovery of helium in the Sun before Earth, and to modern techniques: UV–visible, infrared and NMR spectroscopy in chemistry, and the spectral analysis of stars and galaxies in astronomy.

[Image: A rainbow spectrum through a prism and flame tests of metal salts]

Types

Emission spectra are bright lines from excited atoms; absorption spectra show dark lines where a continuum source passed through cooler gas. Molecular spectra add bands from vibrations and rotations — infrared spectroscopy reads functional groups; NMR reads atomic environments in molecules.

Everyday reach

Spectroscopy calibrates everything from steel composition to blood-oxygen monitors (pulse oximetry) and breathalysers, and it identified the elements of distant nebulae without ever touching them.
