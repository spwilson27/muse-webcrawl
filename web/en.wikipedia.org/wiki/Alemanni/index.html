Alemanni

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

The Alemanni (also Alamanni) were a confederation of Germanic peoples on the upper Rhine valley. First mentioned by Roman authors in the third century AD, they settled the region of modern southwestern Germany, Alsace and northern Switzerland after the fall of the Roman frontier, and gave their name to the Alemannic German dialects still spoken there — and, in many languages, to Germany itself (French Allemagne, Spanish Alemania).

Origins

The Alemanni first appear in Roman sources around AD 213, when the emperor Caracalla campaigned against them near the river Main. Their name is generally interpreted as "all men", suggesting a confederation assembled from several groups, including elements of the Suebi. In the later third century they broke through the Upper Germanic-Rhaetian Limes and occupied the Agri Decumates, the Roman territory east of the Rhine and north of the Danube.

History

Alemannic kings raided Roman Gaul repeatedly during the fourth century; in 357 the Caesar Julian defeated a large Alemannic army at the Battle of Strasbourg, but the Alemanni remained a permanent presence on the Rhine frontier. After the collapse of Roman power in the fifth century they expanded into Alsace and northern Switzerland. In 496/497 the Frankish king Clovis I defeated them at the Battle of Tolbiac, and Alemannia was incorporated into the Frankish realm, though it retained its own dukes and law code, the Lex Alamannorum.

Society and language

Under Frankish rule the Alemanni were gradually Christianised between the sixth and eighth centuries. Their territory became the stem duchy of Swabia in the medieval Holy Roman Empire. The Alemannic dialects of German — including Swabian, Low and High Alemannic, and Swiss German — preserve the linguistic heritage of the confederation, and Alemannic Fastnacht carnival traditions remain prominent in the region.
