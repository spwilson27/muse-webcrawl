St Vitus Cathedral

The Metropolitan Cathedral of Saints Vitus, Wenceslaus and Adalbert (St Vitus Cathedral) is a Roman Catholic cathedral in Prague, Czech Republic, situated within Prague Castle. It is the seat of the Archbishop of Prague and the largest and most important church in the country, containing the tombs of many Bohemian kings and Holy Roman Emperors.

History

A Romanesque rotunda dedicated to Saint Vitus was founded on the site by Duke Wenceslaus around 930, followed by a basilica in the eleventh century. The present Gothic cathedral was begun in 1344 under Charles IV, with the French master builder Matthias of Arras as first architect; after his death, Peter Parler took over, building the choir, the St Wenceslaus Chapel and much of the south tower. Work halted during the Hussite Wars, and the cathedral remained unfinished for centuries — a temporary wall closed the choir — until a completion campaign in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries added the nave and west front. It was finally completed and consecrated in 1929, a thousand years after the first church on the site.

Features

The cathedral is noted for the St Wenceslaus Chapel, decorated with semi-precious stones and Gothic paintings, which houses relics of the saint and leads to the chamber containing the Bohemian Crown Jewels. The south tower, topped by a Renaissance gallery and Baroque dome, holds the great bell Zikmund. The west front's rose window and the Art Nouveau stained-glass window by Alphonse Mucha (1931) are among its best-known later features. Beneath the choir lies the royal crypt with the tombs of Charles IV and other rulers.
