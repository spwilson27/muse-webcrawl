Tecumseh

Tecumseh (c. 1768 – October 5, 1813) was a Shawnee chief and warrior who promoted resistance to the expansion of the United States onto Native American lands. A persuasive orator, he travelled widely in the years before the War of 1812 building a confederacy of Native nations in the Northwest Territory and the South, arguing that Indian land was held in common by all tribes and that no single tribe could cede it. Together with his brother Tenskwatawa, the religious leader known as the Prophet, he founded the settlement of Prophetstown in present-day Indiana. When war came, Tecumseh allied with the British and became their most effective Native commander; he was killed at the Battle of the Thames in October 1813, and his confederacy disintegrated with him.

Early life

Tecumseh — his name is usually rendered "Shooting Star" or "Panther Across the Sky" — was born about 1768 in the Shawnee towns on the Scioto River in the Ohio Country. His father, Puckshinwa, was killed fighting Virginians at Point Pleasant in 1774, and his mother, of the Creek nation, migrated west with part of the tribe. Raised amid the frontier wars of the Revolution and its aftermath, in which Shawnee villages were repeatedly burnt by American expeditions, Tecumseh fought as a young warrior against American expansion, including in the campaigns that ended at Fallen Timbers (1794) and the Treaty of Greenville (1795), which ceded most of Ohio. He refused to accept the treaty's terms.

The confederacy

From about 1805 Tecumseh built his movement alongside his brother Tenskwatawa, whose religious revival — prophecies, rejection of alcohol and of European goods and customs — gave the confederacy a spiritual centre at Prophetstown, founded in 1808 at the confluence of the Wabash and Tippecanoe rivers. Tecumseh's political doctrine was revolutionary in Indian diplomacy: the land belonged to all Native peoples in common, and the cessions by which the United States acquired millions of acres from individual tribes — above all the Treaty of Fort Wayne (1809), by which Governor William Henry Harrison bought some three million acres — were invalid without the consent of all. In two famous confrontations at Vincennes (1810 and 1811) Tecumseh faced Harrison down in person, telling him the sell-out chiefs would be punished and that he would seek a British alliance. In 1811–12 he travelled as far as the Creek, Choctaw and Chickasaw country of the South, making the speeches — remembered for their eloquence in translation — that recruited much of the southern nations' "Red Stick" faction. While he was absent, Harrison marched on Prophetstown and fought Tenskwatawa's warriors at Tippecanoe (November 1811); the town was burnt and the confederacy badly, though not fatally, damaged.

War of 1812 and death

On the outbreak of war in 1812 Tecumseh brought hundreds of warriors to the British at Amherstburg and became the key to the defence of the western frontier. His ruses before Detroit — parading his men in a loop through a clearing to multiply their apparent numbers, and intercepting American mail — helped persuade General Hull to surrender the place to Brock in August 1812. He raided into Ohio, was wounded at the sieges of Fort Meigs, and protested in vain against British retreat in 1813. When Procter's army fell back after Perry's victory on Lake Erie, Tecumseh famously shamed him — Procter was, he said, a fat animal that drops its tail between its legs and runs — but followed the retreat, and fought his last battle on the Thames near Moraviantown on 5 October 1813, where Harrison's army broke the British line and Tecumseh was killed commanding the Native right wing. His body was never certainly identified; legends of his bones' resting place persisted for decades.

Legacy

Tecumseh has been admired almost universally, by Americans and Canadians as much as by Native peoples: Harrison himself called him one of those "uncommon geniuses" who spring up occasionally to overturn empires. Four American towns and numerous ships (four US Navy vessels) bear his name; the Union general William Tecumseh Sherman was named for him. In Canada he is honoured as a hero of the defence of 1812. His political project died with him — after the Thames and the Creek War, no pan-Indian confederacy east of the Mississippi again resisted removal — but his doctrine of common land ownership remains a landmark in Native American political thought.
