Air–fuel ratio

The air–fuel ratio (AFR) is the mass ratio of air to a solid, liquid, or gaseous fuel present in a combustion process. The combustion may take place in a controlled manner, such as in an internal combustion engine or industrial furnace, or may result in an explosion.

Stoichiometric ratio

The chemically correct (stoichiometric) ratio for complete combustion of gasoline is about 14.7:1 — 14.7 parts of air to one part of fuel by mass. Mixtures with more fuel than this are termed "rich" and produce more power but also more unburned hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide; mixtures with more air are "lean", improving economy but risking higher nitrogen-oxide emissions, roughness, and engine damage. The Greek letter lambda (λ) expresses the actual ratio relative to stoichiometric, with λ = 1 at the stoichiometric point.

Measurement

Modern engine management systems continuously adjust the ratio using oxygen (lambda) sensors in the exhaust, oscillating around stoichiometric so that three-way catalytic converters can simultaneously control hydrocarbons, carbon monoxide, and nitrogen oxides. Stoichiometric values differ by fuel: roughly 9:1 for ethanol, 6.4:1 for methanol, and 17.2:1 for hydrogen.
