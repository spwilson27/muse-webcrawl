The Quebec Bridge (French: Pont de Québec) is a steel truss cantilever bridge crossing the Saint Lawrence River between Quebec City and Lévis in Quebec, Canada. Its main span of 549 metres (1,800 feet) remains the longest cantilever bridge span in the world. The bridge is notorious for having collapsed twice during construction, killing 88 workers, before its completion in 1917.

The 1907 collapse

The bridge was promoted by the Quebec Bridge and Railway Company to give Quebec City an all-rail link to the south shore. The design, supervised by the eminent American engineer Theodore Cooper, pushed cantilever spans beyond all precedent. On 29 August 1907, as the south anchor arm neared completion, its lower chords — which had been deflecting visibly for days — buckled, and some 19,000 tons of steel crashed into the river, killing 75 workers, many of them Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake. A royal commission blamed errors in design and judgement: the dead load had been underestimated and warnings from the site were not acted upon.

The 1916 collapse and completion

A redesigned, heavier bridge was begun by the federal government. On 11 September 1916, while the 5,100-ton centre span was being hoisted into place, a casting on a lifting jack failed and the span fell into the river, killing 13 more men. The span was rebuilt and successfully lifted in September 1917; the bridge opened to rail traffic that year and was formally inaugurated by the Prince of Wales in August 1919.

Legacy

The disasters became a defining cautionary tale in Canadian engineering education, and they are popularly — though inaccurately — linked to the iron ring worn by Canadian engineers. The bridge was designated a National Historic Site of Canada in 1995. Ownership and repainting of the rusting structure were contested for decades before the federal government repurchased it in 2024.
