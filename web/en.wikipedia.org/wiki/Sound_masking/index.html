Sound masking

Sound masking is the addition of generated sound (commonly, though inaccurately, referred to as "white noise" or "pink noise") into an environment to mask unwanted sounds. It relies on the psychoacoustic phenomenon of masking, in which the perception of one sound is affected by the presence of another. Sound masking is not a form of active noise control and does not cancel noise; instead it raises the ambient background level so that intruding sounds, especially speech, become less intelligible and less distracting.

Principle

Speech privacy in buildings depends on the difference between the level of a talker's voice and the background sound level at the listener's position. When background sound is low, speech can be understood at great distances. By introducing a continuous, unobtrusive masking sound shaped to resemble the spectrum of the human voice, the radius at which speech remains intelligible is reduced. The goal is not to make the space louder in a noticeable way, but to make it acoustically uniform so conversations blend into the background.

Applications

Sound masking systems are widely installed in open-plan offices, call centres, hospital wards, law firms, and other spaces where confidential conversations occur or where speech distraction reduces productivity. Loudspeakers are usually mounted in the ceiling plenum, facing upward so that the sound is reflected and diffused before reaching occupants. Systems are zoned and tuned with equalisation so the masking spectrum is consistent across the floor plate.

The effectiveness of sound masking is related to measures such as the Articulation Index and the Speech Intelligibility Index, and to privacy metrics including the Privacy Index used in standards for enclosed offices.

Masking sound spectrum

The ideal masking sound is typically filtered to emphasise frequencies between roughly 250 Hz and 5,000 Hz, rolling off at higher and lower frequencies, so it is perceived as a soft, neutral hiss similar to airflow noise. Because air-conditioning systems produce inconsistent noise, engineered systems provide a stable level, commonly around 40–48 dBA in open offices.
