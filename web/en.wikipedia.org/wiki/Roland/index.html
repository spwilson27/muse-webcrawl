Roland (died 15 August 778) was a Frankish military leader under Charlemagne who became one of the principal figures in the literary cycle known as the Matter of France. Historically, Roland was military governor of the Breton March, responsible for defending the frontier of Francia against the Bretons. His only historical mention, in Einhard's Vita Karoli Magni, describes the ambush at the Battle of Roncevaux Pass, where Roland was among the rearguard killed by Basque forces as Charlemagne's army returned from campaigning in Spain.

Historical record

Einhard names him "Hruodlandus Brittannici limitis praefectus" — Roland, prefect of the Breton frontier. On 15 August 778, Basque raiders ambushed the Frankish rearguard in the Pyrenees, killing Roland along with Eggihard, royal seneschal, and Anselm, count palatine. Later legend transformed the Basque attackers into Saracens and magnified the skirmish into an epic last stand.

Legend

Roland became the central hero of the Chanson de Roland (The Song of Roland), an Old French epic poem composed around the late 11th century and the oldest surviving major work of French literature. In the poem, Roland is Charlemagne's nephew and leader of the Twelve Peers. Betrayed by his stepfather Ganelon, he refuses to blow his horn, the olifant, to summon help until it is too late, and dies facing the enemy with his unbreakable sword Durendal in hand. He also appears prominently in Italian Renaissance epics, including Boiardo's Orlando Innamorato and Ariosto's Orlando Furioso, which depict him driven mad by love for Angelica.

In other traditions

Statues of Roland, symbols of civic freedom, stood in many northern German cities, most famously the Bremen Roland (1404). Spanish, Catalan, and Scandinavian traditions all absorbed versions of his story.
