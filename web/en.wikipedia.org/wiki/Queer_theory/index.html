Queer theory

Queer theory is a field of critical theory that emerged in the early 1990s, dealing with culture, society, sexuality and gender. It examines and challenges the normalisation of heterosexuality and binary gender categories — heteronormativity — arguing that sexual and gender identities are socially constructed rather than natural or fixed.

Origins

The field coalesced around the 1990s work of scholars including Judith Butler (Gender Trouble, 1990), which theorised gender as performative; Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick (Epistemology of the Closet, 1990); and Michel Foucault, whose History of Sexuality traced how sexuality became an object of discourse and power. The term "queer theory" was coined by Teresa de Lauretis in 1991, reclaiming "queer" as a term of resistance.

Key ideas

  • Performativity — gender is constituted through repeated acts, not expressed from an inner essence;
  • Anti-essentialism — identities are historically contingent and fluid;
  • The closet — the structural binary of concealment and disclosure organises modern sexuality;
  • Intersectionality — sexuality is inseparable from race, class and colonialism (a link developed by scholars such as Jasbir Puar).

Queer theory has since spread from literary studies into law, geography, history, medicine and design, and has in turn been challenged by critics who argue it has become institutionalised, overly abstract or disconnected from LGBTQ social movements.
