Indictable offence

An indictable offence is a serious criminal offence that, in jurisdictions retaining the term, can or must be tried on indictment — a formal accusation presented to a higher court, usually with trial by jury. The concept comes from English common law and survives, in modified forms, in countries including Canada, Australia, Ireland, and New Zealand. Indictable offences are contrasted with summary offences, which are minor matters tried without indictment in lower courts.

England and Wales

Historically, English law divided offences into indictable and summary categories, with indictable offences tried at assizes or quarter sessions. Today offences fall into three classes: indictable-only offences (such as murder, manslaughter, and robbery), which must be sent to the Crown Court; summary offences; and "either-way" offences, which may be tried in the magistrates' court or the Crown Court depending on seriousness and the defendant's election. In the United States, the roughly equivalent category is the felony.

Canada

Under the Criminal Code of Canada, offences are divided into indictable offences, summary conviction offences, and hybrid offences, for which the Crown elects how to proceed. Indictable offences carry maximum penalties ranging from two years' imprisonment to life, and the most serious (listed in section 469 of the Code, such as treason and murder) are tried in superior court.

Australia and elsewhere

Australian states similarly distinguish indictable offences, tried in district or supreme courts, from summary offences, with many offences classified as indictable but triable summarily. The classification determines court, mode of trial, limitation periods, and rights of appeal.
