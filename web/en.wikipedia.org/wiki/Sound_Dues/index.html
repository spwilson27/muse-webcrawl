Sound Dues

The Sound Dues (Danish: Øresundstolden) were a toll on the use of the Øresund (the Sound), the strait between Denmark and Sweden, levied by the Kingdom of Denmark from 1429 until 1857. All foreign ships passing through the strait were required to stop at Helsingør (Elsinore) and pay a duty based on the value of their cargo. For more than four centuries the dues were one of the Danish crown's most important sources of revenue.

Kronborg Castle at Helsingør, the collection point for the Sound Dues.

History

The toll was instituted in 1429 by King Eric of Pomerania, who built the fortress of Krogen (later Kronborg) at Helsingør to enforce it. The dues were originally a levy on ships, but from the mid-sixteenth century they became chiefly an ad valorem duty on cargo. The sound toll registers, which record the passage of some 1.8 million ships between 1497 and 1857, are among the most complete sources for the study of early modern European trade and are inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register.

Conflicts and abolition

The dues were a recurring source of conflict with the maritime powers. The Netherlands, the strait's heaviest user, repeatedly pressed for reductions; Sweden obtained exemption in 1658, though it later attempted to collect dues of its own at other straits. Denmark's enforcement of the toll also figured in the Sound being a strategic objective during the Dano-Swedish wars. In the nineteenth century, mounting pressure, especially from the United States, led to abolition: by the Copenhagen Convention of 14 March 1857, the maritime nations paid Denmark a capitalised sum of about 30 million rigsdaler in exchange for free passage, and the dues ended on 1 April 1857. The separate American settlement followed in 1857.

The registers

The Sound Toll Registers Online project, based at the University of Groningen and the Danish National Archives, has digitised the records of ship passages, cargoes, and home ports, providing an unparalleled database for maritime and economic historians.
