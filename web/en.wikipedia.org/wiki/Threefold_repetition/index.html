In chess, threefold repetition is a rule by which a player may claim a draw when the same position has occurred at least three times during the game, with the same player to move each time and the same possible moves (including castling and en passant rights). It is one of the common ways a game ends peacefully, especially in perpetual check.

Rules

Under the FIDE Laws of Chess, the claim is made by the player about to move (or who has just moved, if the third occurrence arises from that move). If the same position occurs five times, the game is automatically drawn. Positions are considered identical only if the same moves are available; repetition need not be consecutive.

Examples

Claims of threefold repetition frequently arise when a player who is worse off can force a perpetual check, or in endgames where both sides repeat moves to avoid losing. Famous games decided with the help of the rule include the 1972 Fischer–Petrosian candidates game in which Bobby Fischer claimed the repetition correctly while Tigran Petrosian had overlooked it.
