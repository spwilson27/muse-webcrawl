Pope Gregory VII (Latin: Gregorius VII; c. 1015 – 25 May 1085), born Hildebrand of Sovana, was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 22 April 1073 to his death in 1085. He is venerated as a saint in the Catholic Church. One of the great reforming popes, he is best known for initiating the Gregorian Reform and for his role in the Investiture Controversy with Emperor Henry IV, whom he excommunicated and who famously submitted to him at Canossa in 1077.

Early life

Hildebrand was born in Sovana in Tuscany, probably to a family of modest means, and was educated in Rome, possibly under his uncle at the monastery of St. Mary on the Aventine. He served as chaplain to Pope Gregory VI and accompanied him into exile in Germany in 1046. Returning with Pope Leo IX, he became a central figure in the reform party at the papal court, serving successive popes as adviser, legate, and archdeacon of the Roman Church. His influence on the election decree of 1059, which placed papal elections in the hands of the cardinals, was substantial.

Papacy and reform

On the death of Alexander II in 1073, Hildebrand was acclaimed pope by the Roman populace and clergy, taking the name Gregory VII. His programme, the Gregorian Reform, aimed to free the Church from lay control, root out simony (the buying of church offices), and enforce clerical celibacy. The Dictatus papae (1075), a set of 27 propositions preserved in his register, asserted sweeping claims for papal authority: the pope could depose emperors, no council could be called general without his command, and the Roman Church had never erred and never would.

Investiture Controversy

Gregory's prohibition of lay investiture — the appointment of bishops by lay rulers — brought him into collision with Henry IV of Germany, who depended on his bishops to govern. When Henry defied the ban and moved against the pope, Gregory in 1076 excommunicated him and released his subjects from their oaths of obedience. German princes rebelled, and in January 1077 Henry crossed the Alps and stood for three days as a penitent in the snow outside the castle of Canossa, where Gregory, staying with Matilda of Tuscany, absolved him. The reconciliation was temporary: civil war resumed, the princes elected an anti-king, and in 1080 Gregory excommunicated Henry again. Henry responded by installing the antipope Clement III and marching on Rome.

Death and legacy

Rome fell to Henry in 1084, and Gregory was rescued by his Norman ally Robert Guiscard, whose troops then sacked the city, turning Roman opinion against the pope. Gregory withdrew to Salerno, where he died on 25 May 1085; his reported last words were "I have loved righteousness and hated iniquity, therefore I die in exile." He was canonized in 1606. The controversy he ignited was only settled by the Concordat of Worms in 1122, but his reforms permanently transformed the medieval Church and the relationship between spiritual and temporal power.
