The Ebla tablets are a collection of as many as 1,800 complete clay tablets and thousands of fragments, preserving some 20,000 cuneiform texts, found in the palace archives of the ancient city of Ebla at Tell Mardikh in modern Syria. They were discovered in 1974 and 1975 by an Italian archaeological mission led by Paolo Matthiae, and date principally to the period around 2500–2250 BC, when Ebla was a powerful commercial kingdom.

Discovery

The tablets were found in two rooms of Palace G, where they had originally been stored on wooden shelves. When the palace was destroyed by fire — most likely during the Akkadian campaigns of Sargon or Naram-Sin — the shelves collapsed and the baked clay tablets were preserved in rough order on the floor, allowing archaeologists to reconstruct the archive's filing system.

Contents

Most tablets are administrative records detailing the palace economy: textiles, metals, agricultural deliveries, rations and trade. Others include treaties (among them a treaty with the city of Abarsal), royal edicts, hymns, myths and incantations, and lexical lists pairing Sumerian logograms with words in the local language, making the archive in effect the earliest known dictionaries. The texts revealed Eblaite, an East Semitic language, and demonstrated that a sophisticated, literate Semitic-speaking state flourished in Syria in the third millennium BC — a major revision to earlier views of the region's early history.
