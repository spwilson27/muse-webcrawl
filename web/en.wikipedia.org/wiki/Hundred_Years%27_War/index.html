Hundred Years' War

Hundred Years' War
Date24 May 1337 – 19 October 1453 (116 years)
LocationFrance, Low Countries, Britain, Iberia
ResultFrench victory; England retains only Calais

The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) was a series of conflicts between the kingdoms of England and France over the English monarch's claim to the French throne and control of lands in France. Lasting 116 years with long truces, it is usually divided into Edwardian, Caroline and Lancastrian phases.

Causes

English kings held Gascony as vassals of France, and Edward III claimed the French crown through his mother Isabella after the Capetian line ended in 1328; France chose Philip VI of Valois instead.

Course

England won famous victories at Crécy (1346), Poitiers (1356) and Agincourt (1415), captured Calais after the Siege of Calais, and by the Treaty of Troyes (1420) Henry V became heir to Charles VI. The tide turned with Joan of Arc's relief of Orléans (1429); by 1453 France had recovered everything except Calais.

Significance

The war fostered English and French national identities, saw the longbow and later gunpowder artillery transform warfare, and devastated the French population and economy.
