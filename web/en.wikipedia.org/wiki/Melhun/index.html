Melhun

Melhun (Arabic: الملحون, al-malḥūn), meaning "the melodic poem," is a genre of sung poetry originating in Morocco and traditional across the Maghreb. It emerged from the literary tradition of the region, developing from the zajal form and becoming closely associated with the working classes, artisans, and city guilds rather than the courtly elite, although it was performed at courts as well.

History

Melhun took shape in Morocco from around the 15th–16th centuries, with centres in cities such as Fez, Meknes, Marrakesh, and Taroudant. Poets composed in a Middle Arabic register close to the Moroccan dialect (Darija), making the poems accessible to ordinary audiences. Among the most celebrated melhun poets are Abdelaziz al-Maghrawi, often considered one of its founders, and Thami Mdaghri, whose compositions remain central to the repertoire.

Form and themes

Melhun poems, called qasidas, are typically long, strophic compositions with strict rhyme schemes, refrains known as harba, and internal patterns that challenge the poet's virtuosity. Themes range widely: religious devotion and praise of the Prophet Muhammad, Sufi mysticism, love, panegyrics of patrons, satire, social criticism, and laments over the passing of time. Melhun is usually performed with musical accompaniment, traditionally by ensembles including oud, violin, and percussion, and is closely related to the Moroccan chaabi and Andalusi music traditions.

Modern status

Melhun remains an important part of Moroccan cultural heritage, performed at weddings, festivals, and religious celebrations, and it has been the subject of preservation efforts and academic study as an oral literature tradition.
