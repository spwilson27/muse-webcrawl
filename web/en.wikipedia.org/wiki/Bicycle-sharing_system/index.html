A bicycle-sharing system, bike share program, or public bicycle scheme is a shared transport service where bicycles are available for shared use by individuals for a fee, usually for short trips in urban areas. Bike share systems allow people to borrow a bicycle from a point (a docking station or a designated area) and return it at another point, making them well suited to one-way journeys and "last mile" connections with public transport.

History

The first widely publicised scheme was Amsterdam's White Bike Plan of 1965, in which free white bicycles were left unlocked around the city; most were quickly stolen or vandalised. Coin-deposit systems followed in the 1990s, notably in Copenhagen. The breakthrough came with smart-card and electronic docking technology in the 2000s: Lyon's Vélo'v (2005) and especially Paris's Vélib' (2007), launched with some 20,000 bicycles, demonstrated large-scale viability and inspired schemes worldwide, including London's "Boris bikes" (2010) and New York's Citi Bike (2013).

Dockless systems

From about 2015, Chinese companies such as Mobike and Ofo popularised dockless bike share: GPS-equipped bicycles with app-unlocked wheel locks that can be left almost anywhere. The model spread rapidly across Asia and to cities worldwide, but oversupply led to notorious "bicycle graveyards", vandalism, and the collapse of several operators; many cities responded with licensing and fleet caps. Electrically assisted bicycles (e-bikes) have become an increasing share of shared fleets.

Benefits and criticism

Studies associate bike share with modest reductions in car trips, improved public health, and better transport accessibility at low cost. Criticisms include pavement clutter from dockless parking, safety concerns, uneven geographic coverage, and the fragility of operator business models. Operators continually rebalance fleets — moving bicycles from full to empty stations — a major operating expense.
