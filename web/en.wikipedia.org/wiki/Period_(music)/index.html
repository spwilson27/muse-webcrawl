Period (music)

In music theory, a period is a complete musical thought made up of two phrases, typically an antecedent phrase and a consequent phrase, that together form a balanced unit. The antecedent ends with a weaker, inconclusive cadence (commonly a half cadence), which the consequent answers with a stronger, conclusive cadence (commonly a perfect authentic cadence) on the same or similar melodic material.

Structure

A typical Classical period is eight bars long: four bars of antecedent and four of consequent. The two phrases share opening material, so the consequent sounds like a completion or rhyme of the antecedent. Longer periods of twelve or sixteen bars, and three-phrase variants, are also common.

Types

  • Parallel period — the consequent begins with the same melodic material as the antecedent.
  • Contrasting period — the consequent begins differently from the antecedent.
  • Interrupted (or deceptive) period — the expected final cadence is evaded and the music continues.
  • Modulating period — the consequent cadences in a different key from the tonic.

Periods may combine into larger forms. Two periods in which the second repeats the first with stronger closing material constitute a double period, a common formal unit in Classical themes and slow movements.

Relation to other units

The period sits between the phrase and the full section in the hierarchy of musical form. It resembles the smaller sentence in providing a self-contained presentation of an idea, but differs in its two-phrase question-and-answer structure. The concepts were developed in the analysis of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century music, particularly the works of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven.
