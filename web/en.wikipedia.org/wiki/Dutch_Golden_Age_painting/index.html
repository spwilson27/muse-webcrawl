Dutch Golden Age painting
Period: c. 1588–1672
Country: Dutch Republic
Major figures: Rembrandt, Vermeer, Frans Hals

Dutch Golden Age painting is the painting of the Dutch Golden Age, a period in Dutch history roughly spanning the 17th century, during and after the later part of the Eighty Years' War (1568–1648) for independence.

Overview

The Dutch Republic was among the most prosperous nations in Europe, leading in trade, science and art. Unlike earlier court-centred art, Dutch painting served a broad middle-class market. Religious painting declined after the Reformation, while portraiture, landscape, still life, genre scenes and marine painting flourished as distinct specialisms.

Genres

Artists often specialised: Jacob van Ruisdael in landscape, Pieter Claesz in still life, and Johannes Vermeer in quiet domestic interiors. Rembrandt van Rijn excelled in portraiture, history painting and etching, notably The Night Watch (1642). Frans Hals pioneered lively portraiture in Haarlem.

Market and technique

Paintings were sold at markets, fairs and by dealers, often produced speculatively rather than on commission. Small scale, oil on panel or canvas, and careful observation of light characterised the school. The Delft, Utrecht, Leiden and Amsterdam schools had distinct characters; the Utrecht Caravaggisti imported chiaroscuro from Italy.

Legacy

Collections in the Rijksmuseum, Mauritshuis and museums worldwide preserve thousands of works. Though production fell after the Rampjaar of 1672, Dutch Golden Age painting remains celebrated for naturalism, virtuosity and its depiction of everyday life.
