Yōga

Yōga (Japanese: 洋画, "Western painting") is the style of artistic painting in Japan produced in the Western tradition — using Western techniques such as oil on canvas and watercolour, Western materials, and typically Western subjects and perspective — in contrast to Nihonga, painting in the traditional Japanese manner.

History

Western-style painting entered Japan through contacts with the Dutch at Nagasaki and through examples of European art studied by Rangaku scholars in the Edo period; artists such as Shiba Kōkan experimented with oil paint and copperplate perspective. After the Meiji Restoration, the state promoted yōga as part of modernisation: the Technical Fine Arts School (Kōbu Bijutsu Gakkō) opened in 1876 with Italian instructors including Antonio Fontanesi. The painter Kuroda Seiki, trained in Paris, returned in 1893 and championed plein-air painting, founding the Hakubakai ("White Horse Society") in 1896 and teaching at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts. Asai Chū was another leading figure of the early Meiji yōga world.

Development

Twentieth-century yōga absorbed successive European movements — post-impressionism, fauvism, and surrealism — through circles such as the Nika Society (Nikakai, founded 1914), which broke from the official Bunten salon. The division between yōga and Nihonga still structures Japanese art institutions and exhibitions today, though the distinction of materials and the subjects depicted has blurred considerably in contemporary art.
