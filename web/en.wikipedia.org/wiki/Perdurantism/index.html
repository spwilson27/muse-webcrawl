Perdurantism

Perdurantism, or the theory of temporal parts, is a philosophical theory of persistence and identity in the metaphysics of time. According to perdurantism, a persisting object is not wholly present at each moment of its existence; instead, it is an aggregate of temporal parts — different stages at different times — extended through time much as an object is extended through space. The view treats time as a fourth dimension analogous to the three spatial dimensions.

Overview

Perdurantism stands opposed to endurantism, the view that objects are wholly present at each time at which they exist. A third position, exdurantism or stage theory, identifies persisting objects with instantaneous stages and explains persistence in terms of counterpart relations between stages. Prominent defenders of perdurantism include David Lewis, who argued that it alone solves the problem of temporary intrinsics — how an object can be bent at one time and straight at another without contradiction.

Arguments and objections

Supporters point to the theory's fit with relativistic physics, in which simultaneity is frame-relative, and to its elegant treatment of change, coincidence (for example, a statue and the lump of clay composing it), and vagueness. Critics object that ordinary objects do not seem to have temporal parts, that the view multiplies entities, and that it struggles to explain why we care specially about our own future stages. The debate remains central to contemporary metaphysics.
