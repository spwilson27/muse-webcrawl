Taxus is a genus of coniferous trees or shrubs known as yews in the family Taxaceae. Yews are small to medium-sized evergreens native to temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, with species in Europe, North Africa, Asia, and North and Central America. Taxonomy of the group is unsettled — authorities recognise between about eight and twenty-five species, and many named forms are now treated as subspecies or varieties of a broadly defined Taxus baccata.

Description

Yews grow slowly and can live for millennia; several churchyard yews in Britain are claimed to be 2,000 years old or more. The bark is reddish-brown and scaly, the leaves flat, dark-green needles arranged spirally but twisted to lie in two ranks. Yews are mostly dioecious. The seed is not borne in a woody cone but surrounded by a fleshy, cup-shaped red aril, open at the end — the only part of the plant not notably toxic.

Toxicity and medicine

Nearly all parts of yew contain taxine alkaloids, which are cardiotoxic and have caused fatal poisonings of humans and livestock; a handful of seeds can be lethal. Paradoxically, yews are also a source of major medicines: the chemotherapy drug paclitaxel (Taxol) was first isolated from the bark of the Pacific yew (T. brevifolia), and the related drug docetaxel is synthesised from a precursor in the needles of T. baccata.

Uses and culture

Yew wood is hard, elastic, and beautifully grained, historically prized for longbows — the English war bow was typically yew — and for cabinet work and turning. Yews are classic hedging and topiary plants in European gardens and are planted in churchyards across Britain, Ireland, and Normandy, where their association with death and immortality runs deep in folklore. Species include the European yew (T. baccata), Pacific yew (T. brevifolia), Canada yew (T. canadensis), Japanese yew (T. cuspidata), and Himalayan yew (T. wallichiana).
