Hui people

The Great Mosque of Xi'an, in the historic Hui quarter.

The Hui people (Chinese: 回族; pinyin: Huízú) are an East Asian ethnic group officially recognised in the People's Republic of China, numbering about 11 million in the 2020 census — the largest of China's ten traditionally Muslim ethnic groups and one of the country's most widely distributed minorities. Unlike most other Muslim groups of China, the Hui speak Chinese (Mandarin and other Sinitic languages) as their native tongue and are culturally close to the Han majority, being distinguished primarily by their Islamic faith and associated customs.

Origins and distribution

The Hui descend from a mixture of foreign Muslim merchants, soldiers and officials — Arabs, Persians and Central Asians who settled in China from the Tang dynasty onward, especially under the Mongol Yuan dynasty — and the Chinese among whom they lived and intermarried. Communities are found throughout China, with the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region as their designated autonomous area and large populations in Gansu, Qinghai, Yunnan, Henan, Shaanxi and Xinjiang. Famous Hui figures include the Ming-dynasty admiral Zheng He and the Qing-dynasty military and religious leaders of northwestern China.

Culture

Hui religious life centres on mosques, traditionally built in Chinese architectural style. Dietary law is a strong identity marker: Hui abstain from pork and alcohol, and qingzhen (halal) restaurants and food businesses — notably hand-pulled noodle shops — are a Hui hallmark across China. Historic tensions with the state and Han neighbours erupted in the great 19th-century Muslim rebellions (the Panthay Rebellion in Yunnan and the Dungan revolts in the northwest), which devastated Hui populations. Smaller Hui-speaking diasporas, known as Dungans, live in Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan.
