Reed–Solomon error correction

Reed–Solomon codes are a class of error-correcting codes invented in 1960 by Irving S. Reed and Gustave Solomon at MIT Lincoln Laboratory. They are non-binary cyclic codes that operate on blocks of symbols drawn from a finite field, and are especially effective against burst errors. A Reed–Solomon code that adds 2t parity symbols can correct up to t symbol errors anywhere in the block.

How it works

The message is interpreted as the coefficients of a polynomial over a finite field GF(2m). The polynomial is evaluated at more points than are needed to specify it; the redundancy lets the decoder detect and locate errors, using algorithms such as Berlekamp–Massey decoding. Because a degree-k polynomial is uniquely determined by k points, lost or corrupted symbols can be reconstructed — the same idea underlies treating the code as an erasure code.

Applications

Reed–Solomon correction is ubiquitous: it protects CDs and DVDs (in cross-interleaved form), Blu-ray discs, QR codes, DSL and broadband data transmission, satellite and deep-space communication (including the Voyager probes), and RAID 6 storage arrays.

History

Originally presented as a theoretical construction with an impractical decoder, the codes became practical after efficient decoding algorithms appeared in the late 1960s; the development of compact disc technology in the early 1980s made them one of the most widely deployed error-correction schemes in history.
