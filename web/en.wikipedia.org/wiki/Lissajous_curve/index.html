Lissajous curve

A Lissajous curve, also known as a Lissajous figure or Bowditch curve, is the graph of a system of parametric equations

x = A sin(at + δ),   y = B sin(bt),

which describe complex harmonic motion. This family of curves was investigated by Nathaniel Bowditch in 1815, and later in more detail by Jules Antoine Lissajous in 1857.

Appearance

The shape of the curve depends strongly on the ratio a/b. When the ratio is rational, the curve is closed and stationary; simple ratios give simple figures: 1:1 produces an ellipse (a circle or line in special cases), 2:1 and 3:1 give increasingly intricate braided patterns, and 3:2 resembles a pretzel. For irrational ratios the curve never closes and eventually fills the rectangle [−A, A] × [−B, B].

Applications

Lissajous figures appear on an oscilloscope when sinusoidal signals of different frequencies are applied to its horizontal and vertical inputs; the figure allows the frequency ratio and phase difference of the signals to be measured. Similar patterns are drawn by harmonographs, and variants are used as logos and in light shows and laser displays.
