Korean Buddhism is distinguished from other forms of Buddhism by its characteristic synthesis of schools, an approach known as tongbulgyo ("interpenetrated Buddhism"), which sought to resolve doctrinal disputes between meditative and scholastic traditions. Buddhism was introduced to the Korean peninsula from China in the 4th century CE during the Three Kingdoms period.

History

Buddhism was adopted as a state religion by Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla, and Korean monks such as Wonhyo (617–686) and Uisang became influential across East Asia. Silla's unification of the peninsula in 668 was accompanied by a flowering of temple building, exemplified by Bulguksa and Seokguram. Under Goryeo (918–1392), Buddhism flourished as the state faith; the massive woodblock carving of the Tripitaka Koreana, preserved at Haeinsa, dates from this era. The Confucian Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) suppressed Buddhism, driving monks into mountain temples, though royal patronage occasionally revived it.

During the Japanese colonial period (1910–1945), Korean Buddhism was reorganised under Japanese influence; after liberation, married-monk and celibate factions struggled over temple control, resulting in the modern division between the celibate Jogye Order—the largest order in South Korea today—and the Taego Order, which permits married clergy.

Practice

Seon (Zen) meditation predominates, often combined with Pure Land chanting and sutra study in a unified practice. Mountain temples remain centres of pilgrimage, and the Templestay programme invites visitors to experience monastic life. Roughly a fifth to a quarter of South Koreans identify as Buddhist in surveys.
