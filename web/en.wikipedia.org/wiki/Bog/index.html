Bog

A bog or bogland is a wetland that accumulates peat, a deposit of dead plant material — often mosses, principally sphagnum moss. It is one of the four main types of wetlands. Bogs occur where the water at the ground surface is acidic and low in nutrients, and are generally found in cool northern climates. Because decomposition is very slow in their waterlogged, oxygen-poor soils, bogs preserve organic matter remarkably well and store vast quantities of carbon.

Formation and types

Bogs are ombrotrophic — fed almost entirely by rain — so their vegetation receives minerals only from the atmosphere. Over thousands of years, sphagnum growth builds domes of peat above the surrounding water table (raised bogs); in cold regions with thin soils, peat can carpet whole landscapes as blanket bogs, as in Ireland, Scotland and Newfoundland. String bogs and valley bogs occur in patterned terrain of the boreal zone. Bogs differ from fens, which receive mineral-rich groundwater and are less acidic, and from marshes and swamps, which lack deep peat accumulation.

Ecology

Acidic, nutrient-poor conditions favour specialised life: sphagnum mosses, heather and bilberry, stunted pines and birches, and carnivorous plants such as sundews and pitcher plants that supplement scarce nitrogen by trapping insects. The anaerobic peat has preserved human remains — the famous "bog bodies" of northern Europe, including Tollund Man — as well as pollen records used to reconstruct past climates.

Human use and conservation

Peat was traditionally cut for fuel and is still harvested for horticulture, while drained bogs are used for agriculture and forestry. Because bogs hold more carbon than any other terrestrial ecosystem type per unit area, draining them releases large amounts of CO₂, and restoration by rewetting is now a major conservation goal. Bottled "bog butter" and Iron Age sacrifices testify to their long cultural role in northern Europe.
