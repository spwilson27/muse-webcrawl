ITER (initially the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor; iter means "the way" in Latin) is an international nuclear fusion research and engineering megaproject aimed at demonstrating the scientific and technological feasibility of fusion power. Under construction at Cadarache in southern France, it will be the world's largest tokamak, designed to produce 500 megawatts of fusion power from 50 megawatts of injected heating power — a tenfold gain (Q = 10) — though it will not generate electricity.

History

The project grew out of a 1985 summit initiative between Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev. After years of design work, the seven members — the European Union, China, India, Japan, Russia, South Korea, and the United States — signed the ITER Agreement in 2006 and chose the Cadarache site. Construction has suffered repeated delays and cost growth: original estimates near €5 billion have risen above €20 billion, and the schedule was re-baselined in 2024, pushing first plasma toward the mid-2030s with deuterium–tritium operation later in that decade.

Design

The ITER tokamak has a major radius of 6.2 metres and a plasma volume of about 830 cubic metres, confined by superconducting magnets (niobium–tin and niobium–titanium) cooled to 4 K, generating fields up to about 13 tesla. The deuterium–tritium plasma will reach 150 million degrees Celsius — ten times hotter than the core of the Sun. Components, including nine enormous vacuum-vessel sectors, are manufactured by member states around the world and shipped to France for assembly by a workforce of thousands. ITER is intended as the step between today's experiments, such as JET in England (which set a record 69 MJ fusion energy release in 2023), and a demonstration power plant, DEMO.
