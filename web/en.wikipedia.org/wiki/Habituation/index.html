Habituation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Habituation is a form of non-associative learning in which an innate response to a stimulus decreases after repeated or prolonged presentations of that stimulus. Responses that habituate include those that involve the whole organism (such as a startle response) or only components of it (such as the withdrawal of a limb from a repeated touch). Along with sensitisation — the opposite process, in which repeated stimulation strengthens a response — habituation is regarded as one of the simplest forms of learning, found in animals from sea slugs to humans.

Characteristics

In a widely cited formulation, Thompson and Spencer (1966) listed the properties that distinguish true habituation from other causes of waning responses, such as muscle fatigue or sensory adaptation: the response decrement is stimulus-specific; it recovers spontaneously with time; a novel stimulus can restore the response to the original stimulus (dishabituation); and repeated cycles of habituation and recovery make later habituation faster. Rankin and colleagues (2009) later updated this list on the basis of modern research.

Mechanisms

Eric Kandel's studies of the gill-withdrawal reflex of the marine snail Aplysia showed that short-term habituation results from decreased neurotransmitter release at the synapse between the sensory and motor neurons — homosynaptic depression — while long-term habituation involves structural changes at those synapses. In mammals, habituation is distributed across sensory pathways, the reticular formation and higher structures such as the hippocampus, which match incoming stimuli against an internal model (the comparator theory).

Significance

Habituation allows organisms to ignore irrelevant, unchanging stimuli and conserve attention for novel or significant ones; it underlies everyday phenomena such as ceasing to notice background noise or the feel of clothing. In developmental psychology, the habituation–dishabituation procedure is a standard tool for studying infant perception and cognition: looking time decreases as a display becomes familiar, and recovers when something changes. Deficient habituation is associated with several clinical conditions, and controlled exposure therapies exploit related extinction and habituation processes in treating phobias.
