Codex

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A codex (plural codices) is a book constructed of a number of sheets of paper, vellum, papyrus, or similar materials, folded and bound at one edge, with a cover. It is the form of the modern book. The codex replaced the scroll as the principal carrier of texts in the Mediterranean world during late antiquity, between the 1st and 5th centuries AD.

The codex format allowed random access, writing on both sides, and easy reference.

History

The codex developed from Roman wooden writing tablets (codices) bound together. Early Christians adopted the format with striking consistency for their scriptures — perhaps for economy (both sides of the sheet could be used), portability, ease of reference, and to distinguish their books from Jewish scrolls. By about 400 AD the codex had overtaken the roll even for pagan literature. Famous surviving codices include the Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus (4th-century Greek Bibles), the Codex Alexandrinus, and medieval illuminated manuscripts such as the Book of Kells and the Codex Gigas.

Mesoamerican codices

Independently, pre-Columbian Mesoamerican cultures produced screen-fold books of bark paper (amate) or deerskin, painted with glyphs and images. Surviving Maya codices (Dresden, Madrid, Paris, and the debated Grolier/Codex Maya of Mexico) and Mixtec and Aztec manuscripts recorded calendars, astronomy, ritual, and dynastic history; most were destroyed during the Spanish conquest.

Significance

Scholars of the book credit the codex with enabling new habits of reading: random access, cross-referencing, pagination, indexes, and silent private study. The discipline of codicology studies the physical construction of codices to date and localise them.
