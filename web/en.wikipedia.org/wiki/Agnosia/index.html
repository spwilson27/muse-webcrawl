Agnosia
SpecialtyNeurology, neuropsychology
DefinitionLoss of the ability to recognise objects, sounds, smells or people despite intact primary senses
CausesStroke, brain injury, tumour, neurodegenerative disease
TypesVisual (incl. prosopagnosia), auditory, tactile (astereognosis)
TermCoined by Sigmund Freud (1891)

Agnosia is a neurological disorder characterised by the inability to recognise and identify objects, sounds, shapes or people, even though the primary sensory systems (vision, hearing, touch) are intact and there is no general loss of memory or intellect. The deficit is specific to interpreting sensory information: a person with visual agnosia can see and may copy a drawing of a key, yet cannot say what a key is until they hold it.

Types

Visual agnosia includes apperceptive forms (failure to build a coherent percept) and associative forms (a percept is formed but not linked to meaning). Prosopagnosia is the selective inability to recognise faces, made famous by Oliver Sacks's case study "The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat". Auditory agnosia affects recognition of sounds or words (pure word deafness), and tactile agnosia (astereognosis) impairs recognition of objects by touch. Other forms affect smell, spatial awareness (simultanagnosia) or one's own condition (anosognosia).

Causes and treatment

Agnosia typically results from damage to the occipitotemporal ("what") pathway or parietal association cortex, most often from stroke, head injury or dementia. There is no direct cure; management focuses on treating the cause and on rehabilitative strategies that teach patients to use intact senses and contextual cues.
