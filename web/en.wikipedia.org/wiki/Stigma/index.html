Stigma

This article is about the social concept. For other uses, see Stigma (disambiguation).

Stigma is a set of negative and unfair beliefs that a society or group holds about a characteristic, condition or behaviour, leading the person who bears it to be discredited, devalued or excluded. The word derives from the Greek stigma, a mark cut or burned into the skin of criminals, slaves or traitors to identify them as blemished and to be avoided.

Goffman's theory

The modern sociological concept was defined by Erving Goffman in Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity (1963). Goffman described stigma as "an attribute that is deeply discrediting" and distinguished three forms: physical deformations; blemishes of individual character (such as addiction, mental illness or a criminal record); and "tribal" stigmas of race, nation or religion transmitted through lineages. He analysed how stigmatised people manage information about their failing — whether it is visible (the "discredited") or concealable (the "discreditable") — and the negotiations this imposes on everyday interaction.

Dimensions and effects

Later researchers, notably Bruce Link and Jo Phelan, formalised stigma as the convergence of labelling, stereotyping, separation of "us" from "them", status loss and discrimination, occurring in a situation where power is exercised. Self-stigma describes the internalisation of these attitudes by the stigmatised person; courtesy stigma affects those associated with them. Stigma is a recognised barrier to treatment and welfare in fields such as mental health, HIV/AIDS, obesity and substance use, and combating it is an explicit goal of many public-health campaigns.

Other uses of the term

In biology, the stigma is the receptive tip of a flower's carpel on which pollen lands, and in zoology a stigma (or spiracle-like eyespot) can refer to a dark marking on insects. In Christian tradition the stigmata are bodily wounds corresponding to those of Christ.
