Squaring the circle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A square and circle of the same area: the classical problem asks for an exact construction

Squaring the circle is a problem in geometry first proposed in Greek mathematics. It is the challenge of constructing a square with the area of a given circle by using only a finite number of steps with a compass and straightedge. The difficulty of the problem raised the question of whether specified axioms of Euclidean geometry concerning the existence of lines and circles implied the existence of such a square.

Impossibility

In 1882 the task was proved impossible as a consequence of the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem, by which Ferdinand von Lindemann showed that π (pi) is a transcendental number rather than an algebraic irrational number. Since compass-and-straightedge constructions can produce only algebraic lengths (indeed, lengths obtained by iterated quadratic extensions), constructing a segment of length √π from a unit segment — which squaring the circle would require — is impossible.

History

The problem was studied by ancient geometers including Anaxagoras, Hippocrates of Chios (who squared certain lunes), Antiphon, and Bryson of Heraclea, whose approximate methods anticipated exhaustion arguments. It remained a touchstone of mathematical culture for two millennia, alongside doubling the cube and trisecting the angle. James Gregory attempted a proof of impossibility in 1667. Approximate constructions, such as those by Srinivasa Ramanujan, can achieve great accuracy but are not exact.

Modern analogues

The phrase "squaring the circle" is used metaphorically for attempting the impossible. A related modern result, Tarski's circle-squaring problem, asks whether a disc can be dissected into finitely many pieces and reassembled into a square of equal area; Laczkovich proved in 1990 that this is possible using the axiom of choice, though the pieces are not measurable sets in a classical sense.
