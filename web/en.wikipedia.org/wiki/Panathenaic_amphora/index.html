Panathenaic amphorae were large ceramic vessels given as prizes in the Panathenaic Games, held in Athens from 566 BC. Filled with olive oil from the sacred olive trees (moriai) of Athena on the Acropolis plain, they were awarded to victors in the athletic and equestrian contests of the festival.

Form and decoration

The amphorae have a distinctive shape: a narrow neck and foot, a wide ovoid body and a capacity of roughly one metretes (about 39 litres). They were decorated in the black-figure technique long after red-figure had become fashionable elsewhere — a deliberate archaism. One side invariably shows Athena Promachos, armed, striding between two columns topped by cocks, with the inscription "from the games at Athens" (ton Athenethen athlon). The reverse depicts the event for which the vase was a prize: runners, wrestlers, chariot racing and so on. From the 4th century BC the name of the eponymous archon was added, allowing many examples to be dated precisely.

Distribution and survival

Prize amphorae have been found across the Greek world and beyond — in Italy, Libya (notably the Benghazi and Tocra finds), Egypt and the Black Sea region — since victors often dedicated or sold them. Hundreds survive in museums; the earliest known example, the Burgon vase (c. 565 BC), is in the British Museum. Production continued into the 2nd century BC, when the shape became smaller and more stylised.
