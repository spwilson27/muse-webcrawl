Harvard Mark I

Harvard Mark I Type: Electromechanical computer
Manufacturer: IBM
Introduced: 1944
Successor: Harvard Mark II

The Harvard Mark I, officially the IBM Automatic Sequence Controlled Calculator (ASCC), was a general-purpose electromechanical computer built by IBM and presented to Harvard University in 1944. It was one of the earliest large-scale digital computers, designed by a team under Howard H. Aiken and built by IBM engineers including Clair Lake, Frank Hamilton, Benjamin Durfee, and James Bryce.

The Harvard Mark I at Harvard University, a 51-foot-long electromechanical computer.

Design and operation

The machine was 51 feet (16 m) long, 8 feet high, and weighed about five tons. Its 765,000 components included 72 storage counters of 23 decimal digits each, driven by a rotating shaft synchronised by a 500-horsepower motor. Instructions were read from a punched paper tape, and the computer processed numbers in fixed-point decimal arithmetic. It could perform three additions or subtractions per second; a multiplication took about six seconds and a division about fifteen seconds.

History

Aiken proposed the machine in 1937, and IBM agreed to fund its construction, which took place at its Endicott, New York laboratories. The completed calculator was shipped to Harvard in February 1944 and formally dedicated on 7 August 1944. It was used by the United States Navy Bureau of Ships for gunnery and ballistic calculations. Mathematician Grace Hopper was one of its first programmers. The Mark I remained in service at Harvard until 1959 and directly inspired the Harvard Mark II and the Harvard architecture of separate instruction and data storage.
