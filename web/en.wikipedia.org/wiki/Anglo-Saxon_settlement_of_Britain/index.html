The Anglo-Saxon settlement of Britain is the process by which Germanic-speaking peoples from the North Sea coast of continental Europe migrated to, and came to dominate, lowland Britain during the 5th and 6th centuries AD, after the end of Roman rule. It laid the demographic, linguistic and political foundations of England.

Sources

Written evidence is sparse and late: Gildas's De Excidio, Bede's Ecclesiastical History and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle describe the arrival of Angles, Saxons and Jutes, traditionally dated to the "Adventus Saxonum" around 449 under Hengist and Horsa and the British king Vortigern. Archaeology — cemeteries, sunken-featured buildings, brooches and pottery — supplements these accounts.

Nature of the settlement

Scholars have debated the scale of the migration for generations. Earlier interpretations imagined wholesale replacement of the Romano-British population; later models emphasised small warrior elites. Recent ancient-DNA studies indicate substantial migration from the continental North Sea zone into eastern and southern England, with considerable mixing between migrants and locals, whose proportions varied by region.

Consequences

British (Celtic) language and culture retreated west and north, while Old English spread across the lowlands. By the 7th century a set of Anglo-Saxon kingdoms — among them Northumbria, Mercia, Wessex, Kent and East Anglia — had emerged, and the newcomers began converting to Christianity after the Gregorian mission of 597.
