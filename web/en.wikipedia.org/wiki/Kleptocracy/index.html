Kleptocracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Kleptocracy (from Greek kleptēs, thief, and kratos, rule) is a form of government in which corrupt leaders use political power to steal their country's resources for themselves and their networks, treating the state as a personal revenue-extraction machine. Where ordinary corruption skims a functioning system, kleptocracy makes theft the system: laws, courts, banks and security forces are organised around protecting the rulers' accumulation.

Examples and responses

Textbook cases include Mobutu Sese Seko's Zaire (personal fortune estimated in the billions while the state decayed), Ferdinand and Imelda Marcos's Philippines (ill-gotten wealth recovered only in part through decades of litigation), and post-Soviet and other "state capture" systems where oligarchic and official wealth fuse. International responses have grown from rhetoric to machinery: the UN Convention against Corruption (2003) with asset-recovery chapters, stolen-asset initiatives (StAR), targeted (Magnitsky-style) sanctions on kleptocrats and their enablers, beneficial-ownership registers aimed at the anonymous shell companies through which loot is laundered, and investigative journalism exposing the professional enabler class (lawyers, bankers, real-estate markets in London, New York and Dubai). Recovery rates remain dismal relative to estimated flows, and kleptocracy's analysts warn that the system depends as much on Western financial plumbing as on the thieves.
