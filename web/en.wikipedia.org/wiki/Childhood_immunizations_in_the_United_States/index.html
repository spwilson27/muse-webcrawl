Childhood immunizations in the United States

Childhood immunizations in the United States are recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) through its Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP). The recommended schedule covers children from birth through adolescence and is designed to protect against vaccine-preventable diseases during the periods of life when children are most vulnerable to them.

Recommended schedule

The CDC schedule begins with a first dose of hepatitis B vaccine within 24 hours of birth. At two, four, and six months, infants typically receive DTaP (diphtheria, tetanus, and acellular pertussis), IPV (inactivated poliovirus), Hib (Haemophilus influenzae type b), pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, and rotavirus vaccine. The MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) and varicella (chickenpox) vaccines are first given at 12–15 months, with boosters before kindergarten entry at 4–6 years. Adolescents receive Tdap, meningococcal conjugate vaccine, and HPV vaccine around ages 11–12.

School entry requirements

All 50 states require certain vaccinations for children attending public school, though the specific requirements and exemptions vary. Medical exemptions are universally available; most states also permit religious exemptions, and a smaller number allow philosophical or personal-belief exemptions. Requirements are generally enforced at kindergarten entry and again in middle school.

Vaccines for Children program

The Vaccines for Children (VFC) program, created in 1994 in response to a measles resurgence linked to cost barriers, provides vaccines at no cost to children who are Medicaid-eligible, uninsured, underinsured, or American Indian or Alaska Native. The program supplies roughly half of all childhood vaccines administered in the United States.

Controversy and public health

Childhood immunization has periodically faced public controversy, most notably following a retracted 1998 study falsely linking the MMR vaccine to autism. Extensive subsequent research found no such association. Public health officials credit high childhood coverage with the elimination of endemic measles transmission in the United States (declared in 2000) and the near-elimination of diseases such as Hib meningitis and congenital rubella syndrome.
