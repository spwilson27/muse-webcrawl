Acoustics

Acoustics is a branch of physics that deals with the study of mechanical waves in gases, liquids and solids, including topics such as vibration, sound, ultrasound and infrasound. A scientist who works in the field is called an acoustician, while someone working in acoustic technology may be called an acoustical engineer.

Fundamentals

Sound in air consists of longitudinal pressure waves. Their speed in dry air at 20 °C is about 343 m/s. Key quantities include frequency (perceived as pitch), amplitude (perceived as loudness, measured in decibels), wavelength and wave speed, related by v = f λ. The wave equation models how pressure disturbances propagate, reflect, refract and diffract.

Branches

Subfields include architectural acoustics (the sound quality of buildings and concert halls), musical acoustics, underwater acoustics (sonar), aeroacoustics, electroacoustics (microphones and loudspeakers), psychoacoustics (perception of sound), and noise control. Historical pioneers include Pythagoras, who related pitch to string length; Marin Mersenne; Lord Rayleigh, whose Theory of Sound (1877) founded modern acoustics; and Wallace Clement Sabine, founder of architectural acoustics. The behaviour of sound in rooms is closely tied to materials such as the stone of buildings like Blenheim Palace.
