Stalemate

Stalemate is a situation in chess where the player whose turn it is to move is not in check but has no legal move. Under the standard rules of chess, the game is immediately drawn. Stalemate is a key defensive resource for a player with an inferior position, and avoiding it is an essential consideration for the stronger side when converting an advantage, especially in king and pawn endgames.

Black to move is stalemated: the king is not in check but has no legal move

Rules and history

The treatment of stalemate varied historically: in some earlier forms of chess it counted as a win for the player who caused it, in others as a loss or a half-win. The rule that stalemate is a draw was standardised in the 19th century. Under FIDE rules the game ends immediately when a stalemate position arises.

Examples and strategy

The simplest stalemates occur in endings of king and queen versus king, where careless play can leave the lone king with no move, and in king and pawn endings, where a pawn blocked by the enemy king near the corner can produce stalemate. A celebrated composed example is the Saavedra position's underlying themes, while studies by composers such as Alexei Troitsky and Richard Réti exploit stalemate defences in depth. In practical play, the threat of stalemate also underlies some famous swindles, in which a seemingly lost player sacrifices remaining mobile pieces to force the draw.

Figurative use

By extension, "stalemate" is used in politics, negotiation and warfare for any deadlock in which neither side can progress or prevail.
