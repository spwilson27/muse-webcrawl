Endangered language

An endangered language is a language at risk of falling out of use as its speakers die out or shift to speaking another language. Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, a large proportion — estimates commonly run from 40% to over half — are endangered, and a language is often said to die every few weeks with its last fluent speaker. Language loss occurs when a community stops transmitting its language to children, usually under pressure from a dominant language of education, government, and media.

Degrees of endangerment

UNESCO's scale classifies languages as vulnerable (not spoken by many children outside the home), definitely endangered (no longer learned by children at home), severely endangered (spoken mainly by the grandparent generation), critically endangered (only a few elderly speakers, using it partially), and extinct. The single best predictor of survival is intergenerational transmission.

Causes

Drivers include colonisation and forced assimilation (including boarding schools that punished indigenous languages), urbanisation, economic marginalisation, mass media, and the prestige of global languages such as English, Spanish, Mandarin, and Russian. Hotspots of both diversity and loss include northern Australia, the Amazon basin, the Caucasus, Siberia, and the indigenous Americas.

Documentation and revitalisation

Linguists work with communities to document endangered languages — recording grammars, dictionaries, and texts — before they fall silent. Revitalisation efforts range from immersion preschools ("language nests"), pioneered by Māori kōhanga reo and Hawaiian Pūnana Leo, to master–apprentice programmes and digital resources. Some languages have been revived from documentation alone: Hebrew's revival is the classic large-scale case, and "sleeping" languages such as Kaurna (South Australia) and Wampanoag have been reawakened from written records. UNESCO's Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger tracks the status of languages globally.
