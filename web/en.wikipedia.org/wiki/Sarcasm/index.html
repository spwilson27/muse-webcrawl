Sarcasm

Sarcasm is the use of words that mean the opposite of what the speaker truly intends, typically in a mocking, contemptuous or humorous tone. It is closely related to irony, but whereas irony may be gentle or situational, sarcasm is generally understood to carry an edge of scorn directed at a target. The word derives from the Greek sarkazein, "to tear flesh" — hence, to speak bitterly.

Characteristics

Sarcastic utterances rely on context, tone of voice, facial expression and shared knowledge between speaker and listener. Because the literal meaning of a sarcastic statement is the reverse of the intended one, listeners must detect the incongruity between what is said and the situation. In writing, where vocal cues are absent, sarcasm is frequently missed; conventions such as scare quotes, italics or the "/s" tag in internet forums are sometimes used to signal it.

Psychology and comprehension

Understanding sarcasm is a developmental skill that typically emerges in middle childhood and depends on theory of mind — the ability to model what another person believes. Research links sarcasm comprehension with regions of the brain involved in social cognition; difficulties interpreting sarcasm are observed in some people with autism spectrum conditions, traumatic brain injury or certain forms of dementia. Studies also suggest sarcasm can enhance creativity in both speaker and listener by activating abstract thinking, though it can increase perceived conflict when trust between parties is low.

Sarcasm and irony

Usage authorities differ on the boundary: some treat sarcasm as a subset of verbal irony distinguished by intent to ridicule; others use the terms interchangeably. Henry Watson Fowler called sarcasm "the lowest form of wit" in a phrase often quoted — itself perhaps ironically.
