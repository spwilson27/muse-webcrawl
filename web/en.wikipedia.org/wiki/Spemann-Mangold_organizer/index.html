Spemann–Mangold organizer

The Spemann–Mangold organizer is a group of cells in the amphibian embryo that, during gastrulation, can induce the formation of the body axis and organize surrounding tissue into a secondary embryo. It corresponds to the dorsal lip of the blastopore and is considered one of the most important discoveries in developmental biology.

The 1924 experiment

Hans Spemann and his doctoral student Hilde Mangold, working at Freiburg and publishing in 1924, transplanted the dorsal blastopore lip from an early gastrula of a lightly pigmented newt species into the ventral side of a differently pigmented host embryo. The graft induced host tissue to form a second embryonic axis — a conjoined twin larva containing a complete, well-organized nervous system and other axial structures largely derived from the host, proving that the grafted region "organized" its surroundings rather than merely self-differentiating.

Significance

The experiment established the concept of embryonic induction and made the organizer a central object of developmental research. Spemann received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1935; Mangold, who had died in 1924, could not share it. Decades of work to identify the organizer's molecular signals eventually showed that it secretes antagonists of BMP and Wnt signalling — proteins including noggin, chordin, follistatin, and Cerberus — thereby permitting neural induction and dorsal patterning. Homologous signaling centers include Hensen's node in birds and the node in mammals.
