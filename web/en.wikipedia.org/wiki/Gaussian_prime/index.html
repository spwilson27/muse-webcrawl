Gaussian prime

A Gaussian prime is a prime element of the ring of Gaussian integers Z[i]: a Gaussian integer that cannot be written as a product of two non-unit Gaussian integers. Because Z[i] has unique factorisation, Gaussian primes play the same role there as prime numbers do in the ordinary integers.

Classification

The Gaussian primes are characterised completely in terms of ordinary prime numbers:

  • 2 ramifies: 2 = −i(1 + i)², so 1 + i (and its associates) is a Gaussian prime of norm 2.
  • Primes p ≡ 1 (mod 4) split: p = π·π̄, where π = a + bi with a² + b² = p; both π and its conjugate π̄ are Gaussian primes of norm p. For example 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i) and 13 = (3 + 2i)(3 − 2i).
  • Primes p ≡ 3 (mod 4) remain prime ("inert"): p itself is a Gaussian prime of norm p²: 3, 7, 11, 19, … stay prime in Z[i].

Equivalently, a Gaussian integer a + bi with both parts non-zero is prime exactly when a² + b² is an ordinary prime; and a ± bi with one part zero is prime exactly when the absolute value of the other part is an ordinary prime congruent to 3 modulo 4.

Geometry and open problems

Plotted in the complex plane, Gaussian primes form a pattern with the symmetry of the square, thinning out with distance from the origin. A famous unsolved problem, sometimes called the Gaussian moat problem, asks whether one can walk from the origin to infinity stepping only on Gaussian primes with steps of bounded length — whether arbitrarily wide "moats" of composite Gaussian integers surround the origin. Computations show moats of width greater than √6 exist, but the general question remains open.
