Cancer syndrome

A cancer syndrome, or family cancer syndrome, is a genetic disorder in which inherited mutations in one or more genes greatly increase the risk of developing certain types of cancer, often at an unusually early age. These syndromes are caused by mutations in genes involved in DNA repair, cell-cycle control or tumour suppression, and are typically inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern.

Examples

  • Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome, caused by mutations in BRCA1 or BRCA2, predisposing to breast, ovarian, prostate and pancreatic cancers.
  • Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer), caused by defects in DNA mismatch-repair genes such as MLH1 and MSH2.
  • Familial adenomatous polyposis, caused by mutations in APC, producing hundreds of colorectal polyps and near-certain colon cancer if untreated.
  • Li–Fraumeni syndrome, caused by mutations in TP53, associated with sarcomas, breast cancer, brain tumours and leukaemia.
  • Multiple endocrine neoplasia, von Hippel–Lindau disease, Cowden syndrome and the inherited forms of retinoblastoma.

Recognition and management

Clues to a hereditary cancer syndrome include several close relatives with the same or related cancers, cancer diagnosed at an unusually young age, bilateral disease, and rare tumours. Genetic counselling and genetic testing can identify mutation carriers, for whom management may include enhanced surveillance, risk-reducing medication, prophylactic surgery and cascade testing of relatives. Only a minority of all cancers — roughly five to ten per cent — arise from these high-penetrance inherited syndromes.
