San Juan de Ulúa

San Juan de Ulúa
Location: Veracruz, Mexico
Built: from 1565
Type: Sea fortress and former prison

San Juan de Ulúa, also known as the Castle of San Juan de Ulúa, is a large complex of fortresses, prisons, and one-time palace on an island of the same name in the Gulf of Mexico overlooking the port of Veracruz, Mexico. Juan de Grijalva's expedition landed on the island on Saint John's day, 24 June 1518, and named it for the saint and the local word heard as "culúa". Hernán Cortés landed here in 1519 at the start of the conquest of Mexico.

History

Construction of the fortress began in 1565, initially as a fortified dock and warehouse for the treasure fleets, and grew over two centuries into one of the strongest fortifications in the Americas, built largely of coral stone (múcara). The fort guarded Spain's principal Caribbean port and the terminus of the transcontinental road to Mexico City and Acapulco.

The fortress was the last Spanish stronghold in Mexico, holding out until 1825, four years after independence. It later served as a notorious prison, especially under Porfirio Díaz, whose inmates were held in damp cells sometimes flooded by the sea; the reformer Francisco I. Madero was imprisoned there. It was occupied by United States forces during the 1914 occupation of Veracruz, and in 1915 Venustiano Carranza made it a seat of government. It is now a museum administered by Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH).
