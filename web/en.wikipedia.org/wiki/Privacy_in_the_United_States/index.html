Privacy in the United States

Privacy in the United States is governed not by a single comprehensive law but by a patchwork of constitutional provisions, federal and state statutes, and common-law torts. The U.S. Constitution does not mention privacy expressly, but the Supreme Court has recognised a right to privacy in decisions concerning contraception (Griswold v. Connecticut, 1965), and the Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures.

Legal framework

Federal statutes regulate particular sectors: the Privacy Act of 1974 (federal agency records), the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (health information), the Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act (financial data), the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, and the Electronic Communications Privacy Act. At the state level, California's Consumer Privacy Act of 2018 and its successor, the California Privacy Rights Act, pioneered comprehensive consumer data rights, followed by laws in states including Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut and Utah.

Common law and scholarship

Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis's 1890 article "The Right to Privacy" argued for a legal right to be let alone, and William Prosser later organised privacy torts into intrusion upon seclusion, public disclosure of private facts, false light, and appropriation of name or likeness. Modern debates concern government surveillance (revealed in part by the 2013 disclosures about NSA programmes), data brokers, biometric identification, and the third-party doctrine after Carpenter v. United States (2018), which limited warrantless access to historical cell-site location records.
