Sovkhoz

A sovkhoz (Russian: совхо́з, IPA: [sɐfˈxos]; plural sovkhozy) was a form of state-owned farm in the Soviet Union. The word is an abbreviation of sovetskoye khozyaystvo (советское хозяйство), "Soviet farm/enterprise". Sovkhozes are contrasted with kolkhozes, which were collective farms nominally owned by their members.

Organisation

In a sovkhoz the state owned the land, machinery and output, and the workers were salaried employees paid regular wages rather than members sharing in the proceeds. The director of a sovkhoz was appointed by state authorities, and production was governed by targets set in the state plan. From the 1950s onward, many kolkhozes were converted into sovkhozes, in part to regularise peasant incomes and bring farms under closer state control.

History

The first sovkhozes were created on expropriated estates in the years after the 1917 Revolution. During the collectivisation drive of the 1930s they were promoted as the "highest" form of socialist agriculture, although the collective farm remained numerically dominant. Under Nikita Khrushchev's Virgin Lands campaign of the 1950s, huge new sovkhozes were established on the steppes of Kazakhstan and Siberia to expand grain production.

By the 1980s there were roughly 23,000 sovkhozes in the USSR, employing about half the agricultural workforce and often farming enormous areas — the largest ran to tens of thousands of hectares. Despite their scale and privileged access to machinery and investment, productivity lagged behind Western agriculture, and food imports remained necessary.

After 1991

Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, sovkhozes were nominally privatised, usually reorganised as joint-stock companies or agricultural cooperatives, though in many regions — notably in Russia, Belarus and Central Asia — the large-scale farm structure they embodied survived substantially intact.
