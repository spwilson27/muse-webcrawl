Bard

Bard
OccupationPoet, singer, storyteller, praise-maker
CulturesCeltic (Gaelic and Brythonic), later Welsh and Irish tradition
Related rolesDruid, fili, minstrel, skald

In Celtic cultures, a bard was a professional poet, singer and oral historian retained by a patron — such as a monarch or chieftain — to compose eulogies, elegies, genealogies and satires and to preserve the lore of the community. Classical authors described bards alongside druids and vates among the learned classes of Gaul and Britain.

In Wales and Ireland

In medieval Wales, bards formed an organized order with lengthy training in strict metres such as cynghanedd, and were attached to princely courts until the loss of Welsh independence in 1282–83. In Ireland the related filid held high social rank. The bardic tradition was revived and reshaped in the early modern period, and in the 18th century Iolo Morganwg invented ceremonies of the Gorsedd of the Bards that remain part of the Welsh National Eisteddfod.

Modern usage

"The Bard" is a famous epithet of William Shakespeare. The word also survives in the Welsh National Eisteddfod's Gorsedd and, by extension, in fantasy literature and role-playing games, where bards appear as wandering musician-magicians.
