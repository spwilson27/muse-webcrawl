Organizational behavior

Organizational behavior (OB) is a field of study that investigates the impact that individuals, groups, and structure have on behavior within organizations, for the purpose of applying such knowledge toward improving an organization's effectiveness. It is an interdisciplinary field that draws on psychology, sociology, anthropology, political science, and economics.

Group dynamics are a central object of organizational behavior research.

Origins

The field grew out of the human relations movement that followed the Hawthorne studies conducted at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works in Chicago between 1924 and 1932. Researchers including Elton Mayo concluded that social factors and workers' feelings of being observed and valued influenced productivity at least as much as physical conditions — the so-called Hawthorne effect. This marked a turn away from purely mechanistic scientific management toward the systematic study of people at work.

Levels and topics

At the individual level, OB examines personality, perception, learning, motivation (through theories such as Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Herzberg's two-factor theory, and expectancy theory), and job satisfaction. At the group level it studies team dynamics, communication, conflict, negotiation, power, and politics. At the organization level it addresses structure, culture, change management, and leadership. Contemporary concerns include workplace diversity, remote and hybrid work, employee well-being, and evidence-based management.

Methods

OB research uses experiments, field surveys, case studies, and meta-analysis. The field is noted for a tension between academic rigour and practical relevance, with debates over how directly findings generalize to managerial practice.
