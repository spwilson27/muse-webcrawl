World War I reparations

After the First World War, the victorious Allies imposed reparations on the defeated Central Powers, principally Germany. Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles — the so-called "War Guilt Clause" — laid the legal basis for Germany's obligation to compensate the Allies for damage caused to their civilian populations. The total sum was set in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks.

The settlement

The 1921 London Schedule of Payments fixed Germany's liability at 132 billion gold marks, though the politically charged "C bonds" made up much of that total and were widely understood to be unlikely ever to be paid. Germany made payments partly in cash and partly in kind — coal, timber, ships and industrial equipment. Default on coal deliveries in the Ruhr led to the Franco-Belgian occupation of the Ruhr in 1923, passive resistance, and contributed to the German hyperinflation of that year.

Dawes and Young Plans

The Dawes Plan (1924) restructured payments, stabilised the German currency with the Rentenmark and brought in large American loans. The Young Plan (1929) reduced the total to about 112 billion gold marks, payable until 1988, and created the Bank for International Settlements to handle transfers. The Wall Street Crash and the Great Depression undermined the scheme; President Hoover's moratorium of 1931 suspended payments, and the Lausanne Conference of 1932 effectively ended reparations. Germany made its final payment on bonds issued to fund the reparations-era debt in October 2010.

Historiography

John Maynard Keynes, in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919), condemned the reparations as a "Carthaginian peace" that would impoverish Europe. Later historians have debated how economically burdensome the payments actually were, how far Germany evaded or sabotaged them, and how far resentment over reparations fuelled the political instability of the Weimar Republic.
