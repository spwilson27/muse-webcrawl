Tocharian languages

Tocharian
Region: Tarim Basin
Extinct: 9th century AD
Family: Indo-European

The Tocharian languages are an extinct branch of the Indo-European language family known from manuscripts dating from about the 6th to the 8th century AD, found in the northern Tarim Basin in present-day Xinjiang, China.

Branches

Two languages are attested: Tocharian A (Agnean), used mainly in religious texts around Turfan, and Tocharian B (Kuchean), used more widely around Kucha for both religious and secular purposes. A third variety, Tocharian C, has been proposed on the basis of loanwords in Prakrit texts. Remarkably, Tocharian is a centum language despite being the easternmost Indo-European branch, a position more typical of satem languages.

Discovery

The languages were identified in the early twentieth century from expeditions by Aurel Stein, Paul Pelliot, and others. Emil Sieg and Wilhelm Siegling established the reading of the script, a derivative of the Indian Brahmi syllabary. The name "Tocharian" derives from a proposed identification of the speakers with the Tokharoi of Greek sources, an identification now widely questioned, but the name has stuck.

Features

Tocharian greatly restructured the Indo-European vowel system and developed an agglutinative secondary case system unknown in other early Indo-European languages. Most surviving texts are translations of Buddhist scriptures from Sanskrit.
