Articulated locomotive

Articulated locomotive
TypeSteam locomotive arrangement
Main systemsGarratt; Mallet; Meyer; Fairlie; Shay and other geared types
PurposeMore power and flexibility on sharp curves; spreading weight over more axles
Notable examplesUnion Pacific Big Boy (simple articulated, 4-8-8-4); Beyer-Garratt types worldwide

An articulated locomotive is a steam locomotive (occasionally, an early internal-combustion locomotive) with one or more engine units that can move independently of the main frame. Articulation allows a long, powerful locomotive to negotiate sharp curves that a rigid-frame engine of similar size could not, and distributes the machine's weight over more axles, suiting it to lightly built track.

Why articulate?

As trains grew heavier, railways wanted larger locomotives with more driving wheels. A rigid wheelbase, however, binds on curves and can derail or damage track. By mounting the driving wheels in two (sometimes more) swiveling engine units under a common boiler and frame, designers could combine great tractive effort with flexibility. Flexible steam pipes with ball-and-socket joints carried steam to the moving engines.

Main types

Mallet

The Mallet, patented by Swiss engineer Anatole Mallet, used a compound arrangement: high-pressure cylinders drove the rear engine unit fixed to the main frame, and the exhaust steam fed larger low-pressure cylinders on the swiveling front unit. Later simple articulated locomotives—most famously the Union Pacific Big Boy and Challenger classes and the Norfolk and Western Class A—supplied live steam to all cylinders. Mallets were favored for slow, heavy freight work on mountain grades.

Garratt

The Garratt, patented by Herbert William Garratt and built by Beyer, Peacock and Company, placed the boiler on a cradle frame suspended between two engine units, with fuel and water carried on the units themselves. This freed the boiler from size limits imposed by wheels beneath it, allowing very large boilers and fireboxes. Garratts were widely used in Africa, Australia, South America, and parts of Europe and Asia, especially on narrow-gauge lines.

Meyer and Fairlie

The Meyer type put two powered bogies under a single frame with the boiler above; the related Kitson-Meyer added a rear bunker. The Fairlie—especially Robert Fairlie's double-ended Double Fairlie, used on the Ffestiniog Railway—mounted a double boiler with a central cab and a powered bogie at each end.

Geared locomotives

Geared types such as the Shay, Climax, and Heisler used driveshafts and bevel gears to power swiveling trucks, giving exceptional adhesion on the steep, rough track of logging and industrial railways.

Decline

Articulated steam locomotives were more expensive to build and maintain than rigid engines, with complex flexible steam joints prone to leaks. Diesel-electric traction—whose multiple driven axles and multiple-unit control solved the same problems more simply—displaced them by the mid-twentieth century, though Garratts remained in regular service in southern Africa into the 1990s and articulated steam survives on heritage railways.
