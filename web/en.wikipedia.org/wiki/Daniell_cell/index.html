The Daniell cell is a type of electrochemical (galvanic) cell invented in 1836 by British chemist and meteorologist John Frederic Daniell. It consists of a copper pot filled with copper(II) sulfate solution, in which is immersed an unglazed earthenware container filled with sulfuric acid (or zinc sulfate) and a zinc electrode.

Operation

At the zinc anode, zinc is oxidized: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. At the copper cathode, copper ions are reduced: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The overall reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu produces a potential of about 1.1 volts. In modern school versions, the two half-cells are separated and linked by a salt bridge.

Significance

Unlike the earlier voltaic pile, whose output faded because hydrogen bubbles formed on the copper plate, the Daniell cell delivered a steady current for long periods. In the 1860s it was a standard source of power for telegraph networks. Daniell coined several electrochemical terms, including "electrolyte", in correspondence with Michael Faraday.
