Talus bone

The talus (Latin for ankle or ankle bone; plural tali), also historically called the astragalus, is one of the seven tarsal bones of the foot. It transmits the entire weight of the body from the tibia to the foot. The talus has no muscular attachments; it is held in place by ligaments and the shape of the surrounding bones.

The talus forms the lower part of the ankle joint, articulating above with the tibia and fibula in a mortise, and below with the calcaneus (heel bone) at the subtalar joint. Its head articulates in front with the navicular bone. It is the second largest of the tarsal bones, after the calcaneus.

Structure

The talus is divided into a head, neck, and body. The body has a pulley-like upper surface, the trochlea, which bears the load from the leg. More than half of the talus's surface is covered with articular cartilage; because of this and its lack of muscle attachments, its blood supply is relatively precarious, entering mainly through the neck.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the talar neck, usually from forced dorsiflexion of the foot in falls or motor vehicle collisions, risk interrupting the blood supply and causing avascular necrosis of the body of the talus. In other animals, the talus (as the astragalus or "knucklebone") was historically used as a gaming die, a usage from which the bone takes one of its older names.
