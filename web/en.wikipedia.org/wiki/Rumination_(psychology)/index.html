Rumination (psychology)

Rumination is the focused attention on the symptoms of one's distress, and on its possible causes and consequences, as opposed to its solutions. In the response styles theory proposed by psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema, rumination is defined as "compulsively focused attention on the symptoms of one's distress, and on its possible causes and consequences, as opposed to its solutions."

Relation to depression and anxiety

Rumination is strongly associated with the onset, severity, and duration of depressive episodes, and also plays a role in anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress, binge eating, and substance use. Experimental studies show that induced rumination maintains negative mood and impairs problem solving, whereas distraction provides short-term relief. Rumination is more common in women than in men, a difference Nolen-Hoeksema proposed as a partial explanation of the higher prevalence of depression in women.

Components

Research by Michael McCullough, Wendy Treynor and others distinguishes two components: brooding, a passive comparison of one's current situation with some unachieved standard, which is clearly maladaptive; and reflective pondering, a purposeful turning inward to engage in cognitive problem solving, which shows weaker and sometimes adaptive associations.

Related concepts

Rumination overlaps with worry (which is more future-oriented and verbal), co-rumination (excessively discussing problems with others), and obsessions. Interventions that target rumination include rumination-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, and behavioural activation, which aim to shift the person from abstract, evaluative thinking toward concrete, action-oriented processing.
