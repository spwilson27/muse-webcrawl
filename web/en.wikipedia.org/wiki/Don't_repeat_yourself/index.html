Don't repeat yourself

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Don't repeat yourself (DRY) is a principle of software development aimed at reducing repetition of information and code. It was formulated by Andy Hunt and Dave Thomas in their book The Pragmatic Programmer (1999): "Every piece of knowledge must have a single, unambiguous, authoritative representation within a system."

Rationale

When the same knowledge is duplicated in several places, a change must be made consistently in all of them; any missed copy leaves the system inconsistent and buggy. By keeping each fact in one place — through abstraction, normalisation, code generation, or a single source of truth — modifications become local and safer.

Application and limits

DRY applies not only to code but also to schemas, documentation, build scripts, and business rules. It is often contrasted with "write everything twice" (WET) solutions and with the rule of three, which counsels waiting until a pattern recurs before abstracting it, to avoid premature or wrong abstractions that couple things that merely look similar.
