Gerrymandering in the United States

Gerrymandering is the practice of drawing electoral district boundaries to favour one political party or group over another. In the United States, district maps for Congress and state legislatures are redrawn after each decennial census, usually by state legislatures, giving the governing party an opportunity to entrench itself. The term combines the name of Massachusetts governor Elbridge Gerry with "salamander", after the shape of a district approved under his administration in 1812.

Techniques and effects

The two classic techniques are cracking — splitting an opposing party's voters among many districts so they form a minority in each — and packing — concentrating them into a few districts they win by wasteful margins. Gerrymandering can produce uncompetitive elections, legislatures whose partisan composition diverges from the statewide vote, and primary-driven polarisation. Quantitative measures such as the efficiency gap attempt to detect partisan bias. Racial gerrymandering — drawing lines to dilute minority voting power — is restricted by the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and cases such as Shaw v. Reno (1993).

Legal status and reform

The Supreme Court required equal-population districts in Baker v. Carr (1962) and Reynolds v. Sims (1964), but in Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) it held that partisan gerrymandering claims are nonjusticiable political questions in federal court, leaving the field to state courts, Congress and reformers. Several states — including California, Arizona, Michigan and Colorado — have transferred redistricting to independent commissions. The 2020 census cycle saw aggressive map-drawing in states such as Texas, North Carolina, Illinois and New York, several of which were litigated or struck down under state law.
