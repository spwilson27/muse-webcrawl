Josephinism

Josephinism is a name given to the domestic policies of the Holy Roman Emperor and Habsburg ruler Joseph II (reigned 1765–1790), particularly his attempt to subordinate the Catholic Church to the state and to remodel the Habsburg monarchy as a centralised, rational, enlightened state. Though many of his reforms were reversed after his death, Josephinism left a permanent mark on Austrian law, administration, and church–state relations.

The programme

Becoming sole ruler on the death of Maria Theresa in 1780, Joseph issued a flood of reforming edicts — thousands over the decade. The Patents of Toleration (1781) granted limited religious freedom to Lutherans, Calvinists, Orthodox Christians, and, in separate patents, Jews. The Serfdom Patent of the same year abolished personal serfdom in the hereditary lands. Joseph also abolished the death penalty in ordinary criminal law (1787), reformed the penal code, centralised administration under German as the language of government, and attempted radical tax and land reforms making all landholders liable to taxation.

Church policy

"Josephinism" refers above all to the ecclesiastical settlement: the suppression of contemplative religious orders deemed useless (about a third of the monarchy's monasteries were dissolved and their wealth transferred to a Religious Fund for parishes and schools), state control of seminaries, the requirement of government approval for papal communications, regulation of processions, pilgrimages, and even the number of candles at Mass. Pope Pius VI's unprecedented journey to Vienna in 1782 failed to move him. The aim was a national church useful to the state — priests as, in the critics' phrase, "civil servants in cassocks".

Reaction and legacy

The reforms provoked revolt in the Austrian Netherlands (the Brabant Revolution, 1789) and resistance in Hungary, and Joseph on his deathbed revoked many of his decrees. Yet much endured: religious toleration, the abolition of serfdom's personal bonds, and the state-supervised parish system persisted, and the Austrian concordat tradition retained Josephinist elements until the twentieth century. In historiography Josephinism serves as the classic case of "enlightened absolutism" colliding with the society it sought to remake.
