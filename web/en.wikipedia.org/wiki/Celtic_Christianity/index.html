Celtic Christianity

A high cross of the early medieval Insular tradition.

Celtic Christianity is a term used for certain features of Christianity that developed in the Celtic-speaking regions of Britain and Ireland during the early Middle Ages, roughly the 5th to 8th centuries. Scholars debate how distinct a separate "Celtic Church" ever was; the mainstream view is that the Irish and British churches were part of Western Christianity but retained distinctive practices in some respects.

Distinctive practices

Notable differences from Roman practice included the method of calculating the date of Easter (the Celtic churches long kept an older 84-year cycle), a distinctive form of tonsure, and a strongly monastic organisation in which great monasteries, headed by abbots, were often more influential than diocesan bishops. Irish monasticism produced missionary saints such as Columba, founder of Iona, and Columbanus, who founded monasteries in Francia and Italy.

Synod of Whitby

At the Synod of Whitby in 664, King Oswiu of Northumbria decided in favour of the Roman Easter calculation, a symbolic step toward alignment; Ireland's churches conformed over the following decades. By the 12th century, reforms brought the Irish church fully into the diocesan structure of the Latin Church.

Legacy

The tradition is celebrated for Insular illuminated manuscripts such as the Book of Kells, for penitential literature, and for distinctive nature-focused spirituality. In modern times "Celtic Christianity" has also become a popular spiritual label, sometimes romanticised beyond the historical evidence.
