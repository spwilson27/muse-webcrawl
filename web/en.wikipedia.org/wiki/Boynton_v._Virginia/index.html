Boynton v. Virginia, 364 U.S. 454 (1960), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States which held that racial segregation in public transportation facilities, including terminal restaurants serving interstate travellers, was unlawful under the Interstate Commerce Act. The decision was a major legal victory for the civil rights movement and directly inspired the Freedom Rides of 1961.

Background

In December 1958 Bruce Boynton, a Black law student at Howard University, was travelling by Trailways bus from Washington, D.C., to Alabama. During a stop at the bus terminal in Richmond, Virginia, he entered the terminal restaurant, sat in the section reserved for white customers and ordered food. When he refused to move to the "coloured" section, he was arrested and convicted of trespassing under a Virginia statute, and fined ten dollars. The Virginia courts upheld the conviction, and the U.S. Supreme Court granted review; Boynton was represented by Thurgood Marshall.

Decision

The Court reversed the conviction by a 7–2 majority, in an opinion by Justice Hugo Black. Rather than reach the constitutional questions, the Court held that interstate bus passengers were protected by the Interstate Commerce Act, which forbids "unjust discrimination" by carriers. Because eating facilities at terminals were an integral part of interstate travel, a Black interstate passenger denied equal service was subjected to discrimination the Act prohibited. Justices Whittaker and Clark dissented.

Impact

Although the ruling extended to facilities such as waiting rooms and restrooms as well as restaurants, many Southern terminals simply ignored it. In 1961 the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) organised the Freedom Rides to test compliance; the violence the riders met prompted the Interstate Commerce Commission, at the urging of Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy, to issue explicit regulations banning segregation in interstate bus terminals, effective November 1961.
