Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis is a statistical analysis that combines the results of multiple scientific studies. Meta-analyses can be performed when there are multiple scientific studies addressing the same question, with each individual study reporting measurements that are expected to have some degree of error. The aim is to use approaches from statistics to derive a pooled estimate closest to the unknown common truth based on how this error is perceived. Meta-analytic results are considered the highest level of evidence in evidence-based medicine, above individual randomized controlled trials.

History

The statistical methods underlying meta-analysis have roots in the work of Karl Pearson, who in 1904 combined data from several studies of typhoid inoculation. The modern term was coined by the psychologist Gene V. Glass in 1976, in the context of education research. Meta-analysis was popularised in medicine during the 1980s and is now central to organizations such as the Cochrane Collaboration and to the PRISMA guidelines for reporting systematic reviews.

Methods

Effect sizes and models

Each study contributes an effect size — such as an odds ratio, risk ratio, or standardized mean difference — together with its variance. In a fixed-effect model, all studies are assumed to estimate a single common effect, and studies are weighted by the inverse of their variance. In a random-effects model, the true effect is allowed to vary between studies, and the pooled estimate is the mean of a distribution of effects; the between-study variance is usually denoted τ².

Heterogeneity

Consistency among studies is assessed with statistics such as Cochran's Q and I², which expresses the proportion of variation due to heterogeneity rather than chance. A forest plot graphically displays each study's estimate and confidence interval alongside the pooled result.

Publication bias

Because studies with positive results are more likely to be published, meta-analyses can overestimate effects. Tools for detecting this include funnel plots (a scatter of effect size against precision), Egger's regression test, and the trim-and-fill method. Pre-registration of trials and inclusion of grey literature mitigate the problem.

Advantages and criticisms

Meta-analysis increases statistical power, improves precision of estimates, and can resolve controversies arising from conflicting individual studies. Criticisms include the "apples and oranges" problem of combining dissimilar studies, dependence on the quality of included studies ("garbage in, garbage out"), and sensitivity to publication bias. Critics have joked that meta-analysis mixes studies like "apples and oranges", to which Glass replied that the point is precisely to make fruit salad.
