Appin Murder

The Appin Murder was the killing on 14 May 1752 of Colin Roy Campbell of Glenure, the government factor managing forfeited Jacobite estates in Appin, in the Scottish Highlands. Campbell was shot while riding through the woods at Lettermore. The case became famous for the trial and execution of James Stewart of the Glens (Seumas a’ Ghlinne), who many believed was innocent.

Background

After the Jacobite rising of 1745, estates of Stewart lairds in Appin were forfeited and administered by Campbell appointees, causing deep resentment. James Stewart, a former Jacobite officer, was a leading figure among the dispossessed Stewarts. The actual gunman was widely thought to be Allan Breck Stewart, who fled to France; James Stewart was instead tried as an accessory.

Trial and legacy

Stewart was tried at Inveraray before a jury with many Campbells, presided over by the Duke of Argyll, chief of Clan Campbell. He was convicted and hanged at Ballachulish in November 1752; his body was displayed in chains for years. The verdict remains controversial and is often cited as a miscarriage of justice shaped by clan politics.

The case was fictionalised in Robert Louis Stevenson's novel Kidnapped (1886), where Allan Breck and David Balfour feature in the aftermath of the murder.
