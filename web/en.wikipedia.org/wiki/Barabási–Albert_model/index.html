Barabási–Albert model

The Barabási–Albert (BA) model is an algorithm for generating random scale-free networks using a preferential attachment mechanism. It was proposed by Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert in 1999. Networks generated by the model have a degree distribution that follows a power law, P(k) ~ k−3, resembling the structure observed in many real-world networks such as the World Wide Web, citation networks, and some social networks.

Preferential attachment

The model begins with a small initial network of m0 nodes. New nodes are added one at a time, each bringing m edges (m ≤ m0) that connect it to existing nodes. The probability that a new edge attaches to an existing node i is proportional to that node's current degree ki: nodes with more connections are more likely to gain further connections—"the rich get richer."

Properties

Growth combined with preferential attachment produces a heavy-tailed degree distribution with exponent 3, independent of the parameter m. The average path length grows roughly as ln N / ln ln N, slower than in random graphs, and the clustering coefficient decreases with network size, distinguishing the basic BA model from many real networks with high clustering. Variants such as the fitness model and the model with nonlinear preferential attachment generalize the original scheme.

Significance

The BA model helped launch the modern science of complex networks by providing a simple generative mechanism that reproduces the scale-free property Barabási and Albert reported empirically in the topology of the Web. It has been applied to model citation growth, protein interaction networks, and the spread of epidemics on heterogeneous networks, where hub nodes dominate dynamics.
