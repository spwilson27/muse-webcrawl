Amphetamine

Amphetamine (contracted from alpha-methylphenethylamine) is a potent central nervous system stimulant used medically to treat attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and narcolepsy, and historically for obesity and nasal congestion. It exists as two enantiomers, levoamphetamine and dextroamphetamine, the latter being the stronger central stimulant and the more commonly prescribed form. Amphetamine increases the activity of the monoamine neurotransmitters dopamine and norepinephrine in the brain.

History

Amphetamine was first synthesised in 1887 by Romanian chemist Lazăr Edeleanu in Berlin, but its pharmacology was only explored in the 1920s–30s, when Gordon Alles and others recognised its stimulant effects while seeking an ephedrine substitute. Marketed from 1932 as the Benzedrine inhaler and later in tablets, amphetamines were widely issued to soldiers in the Second World War to combat fatigue, and were used extensively — often excessively — as diet pills and "pep pills" in the postwar decades, leading to tight controls in most countries from the 1970s.

Pharmacology

Amphetamine enters monoamine neurons via the dopamine and norepinephrine transporters, disrupts vesicular storage through VMAT2, and reverses the transporters so that dopamine and norepinephrine are pumped into the synapse; it also weakly inhibits monoamine oxidase and activates the trace amine-associated receptor TAAR1. At therapeutic doses it improves attention and reduces impulsivity in ADHD; at higher doses it produces euphoria, wakefulness and appetite suppression, with risks of dependence, psychosis, cardiovascular events and neurotoxicity in misuse.

Related compounds

Amphetamine is the parent compound of the substituted amphetamines, which include methamphetamine (its N-methyl derivative), MDMA, ephedrine, cathinone and the prodrug lisdexamfetamine. Recreational use remains common worldwide, and the drug is prohibited in sport as a performance-enhancing substance except under therapeutic use exemptions.
