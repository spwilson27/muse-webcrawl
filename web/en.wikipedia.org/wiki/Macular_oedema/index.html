Macular oedema

SpecialtyOphthalmology
Affected structureMacula (central retina)
Common causesDiabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, post-surgical inflammation, uveitis
Key investigationOptical coherence tomography (OCT)

Macular oedema (American spelling: macular edema) is the build-up of fluid in the macula, the central region of the retina responsible for sharp, detailed vision. Leakage from damaged retinal capillaries causes the macula to swell and thicken, distorting central vision. It is a common final pathway of several retinal diseases and a leading cause of visual impairment.

Causes

The most frequent cause worldwide is diabetic macular oedema, a complication of diabetic retinopathy in which chronic hyperglycaemia damages the blood–retinal barrier. Other causes include retinal vein occlusion, inflammation after cataract surgery (Irvine–Gass syndrome), uveitis, age-related macular degeneration treatments, and certain drugs such as prostaglandin analogues and fingolimod. When fluid collects in cyst-like spaces the condition is termed cystoid macular oedema.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Patients notice blurred or wavy central vision (metamorphopsia), faded colours, and difficulty reading, while peripheral vision is usually preserved. Diagnosis is confirmed with optical coherence tomography, which cross-sections the retina and quantifies macular thickness, and fluorescein angiography, which demonstrates the pattern of leakage.

Treatment

Intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF agents (such as ranibizumab, aflibercept and bevacizumab) are first-line therapy for most causes and can substantially restore vision. Corticosteroid implants are used for inflammatory or refractory cases. Focal laser photocoagulation, once the mainstay for diabetic oedema, is now mainly an adjunct. Treating the underlying condition — tight diabetic control, blood-pressure management, or surgery for traction — is essential to prevent recurrence.
