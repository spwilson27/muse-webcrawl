Battle of Berlin

Battle of Berlin
Date16 April – 2 May 1945
LocationBerlin, Nazi Germany
ResultSoviet victory; suicide of Adolf Hitler; unconditional surrender of Germany
BelligerentsSoviet Union, Poland vs. Nazi Germany
CommandersGeorgy Zhukov, Ivan Konev, Konstantin Rokossovsky vs. Gotthard Heinrici, Helmuth Weidling

The Battle of Berlin, designated the Berlin Strategic Offensive Operation by the Soviet Union, was the final major offensive of the European theatre of the Second World War. Between 16 April and 2 May 1945, some 2.5 million Red Army troops, with over 6,000 tanks and 40,000 artillery pieces, encircled and captured the German capital.

Background

By April 1945 Germany was being crushed from east and west. The Western Allies halted at the Elbe, leaving Berlin to the Soviets under inter-Allied agreements. The German defence rested on the Oder–Neisse line, held by Army Group Vistula under Gotthard Heinrici, with perhaps 800,000 troops reinforced by Volkssturm militia and Hitler Youth.

The offensive

The assault opened on 16 April with a massive bombardment. Zhukov's 1st Belorussian Front battered through the Seelow Heights by 19 April, while Konev's 1st Ukrainian Front crossed the Neisse and raced to outflank the city from the south. By 25 April Berlin was completely encircled, and Soviet forces met the Americans at Torgau on the Elbe.

Street fighting inside the city was savage and destructive, fought block by block among the ruins. On 30 April, with Soviet troops a few hundred metres from the Führerbunker, Adolf Hitler killed himself; his successor, Karl Dönitz, authorised surrender negotiations. The Berlin garrison under Helmuth Weidling surrendered on 2 May, and Germany's unconditional surrender followed on 8 May 1945 (9 May in the USSR).

Casualties and aftermath

Soviet casualties were roughly 80,000 dead and 280,000 wounded; German military and civilian deaths were far higher, and the city's population suffered terribly in the fighting and its aftermath. Berlin was divided into four occupation sectors, a partition that hardened into the Cold War division symbolised by the Berlin Wall.
