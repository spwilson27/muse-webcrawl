Neoliberalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Neoliberalism is a term for a set of economic and political ideas and policies, influential from the 1970s onward, associated with free markets, deregulation, privatisation, fiscal restraint, free trade and a reduced economic role for the state. The word was coined in 1938 (at the Colloque Walter Lippmann, Paris) for a renovated liberalism between laissez-faire and planning, developed intellectually by the Mont Pelerin Society (founded 1947; Hayek, Friedman and others), and applied in policy by the Pinochet government in Chile, Thatcher in Britain and Reagan in the United States, then generalised through structural adjustment and the "Washington Consensus" for developing countries.

Contested usage

Few politicians have self-identified as neoliberals; the term is used mainly by critics and scholars, and its precision is debated — deployed variously for an ideology, a policy regime, a phase of capitalism, or a governmental rationality (Foucault's lectures). Defenders of the associated policies credit them with ending 1970s stagflation, extending globalisation and reducing world poverty; critics blame them for rising inequality, financial crises (2008), precarious labour and the hollowing of public services. After 2008, and visibly after COVID-19 and the return of industrial policy in the US, EU and China, commentators repeatedly announced neoliberalism's death — assessments that remain contested, as many of its institutions and defaults persist.
