Video game preservation

Video game preservation is the effort by archivists, museums, libraries, academics, and hobbyists to ensure that video games — as cultural artifacts and works of art — remain playable and accessible for future generations despite the obsolescence of their original hardware and media.

Preservation faces challenges unique among media. Games depend on specific hardware, operating systems, peripherals, and increasingly on online servers; a game whose servers are shut down may become wholly unplayable, and digital-only releases can vanish from storefronts entirely. Physical media also degrade: magnetic disks lose data, optical discs suffer disc rot, and cartridges with battery-backed saves fail as batteries die. A 2023 study by the Video Game History Foundation and the Software Preservation Network found that roughly 87% of classic games released in the United States are commercially unavailable and at risk of being lost.

Methods

  • Emulation — software that imitates original hardware so games can run on modern systems
  • Migration and ports — re-releases, remasters, and backward compatibility
  • Hardware preservation — maintaining original consoles, arcade cabinets, and computers
  • Source code and asset archiving — preserving development materials for study and restoration
  • Documentation — capturing video playthroughs, manuals, magazines, and oral histories

Legal issues

Copyright law, anti-circumvention rules (such as Section 1201 of the U.S. DMCA), and licensing complicate preservation. Libraries and archives have obtained limited exemptions allowing them to circumvent copy protection for preservation of some games, though requests to extend remote access to preserved games were denied by the U.S. Copyright Office in 2024. Institutions active in the field include the Video Game History Foundation, the Strong National Museum of Play, the Internet Archive, and national libraries in several countries.
