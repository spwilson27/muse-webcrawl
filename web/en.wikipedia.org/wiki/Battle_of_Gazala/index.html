Battle of Gazala

The Battle of Gazala was a battle of the Western Desert Campaign of the Second World War, fought from 26 May to 21 June 1942 around Gazala, west of Tobruk in Libya. The Axis forces of Panzerarmee Afrika under Erwin Rommel defeated the British Eighth Army under Neil Ritchie, then stormed Tobruk, capturing some 35,000 prisoners — one of Britain's worst defeats of the war — and advanced deep into Egypt before being halted at El Alamein.

Background

After Operation Crusader in late 1941 drove Rommel back to El Agheila, the Eighth Army advanced into Cyrenaica and dug in along the Gazala Line — a series of defended "boxes" behind extensive minefields, running from the coast at Gazala south to the Free French position at Bir Hakeim. Rommel, resupplied through Tripoli, struck first on 26 May 1942, feinting frontally with Italian infantry while swinging his armour around the southern end of the line.

The battle

The flanking move ran into unexpected trouble: British Grant tanks with their 75 mm guns mauled the panzers, and the Free French at Bir Hakeim held out for over two weeks, disrupting Axis supply. Rommel pulled his armour back into the "Cauldron", a position inside the British minefields, from which — protected by 88 mm guns — he destroyed the British armour in piecemeal counterattacks, culminating in the disaster of 12–13 June ("Black Saturday"), when the Eighth Army lost most of its remaining tanks. Bir Hakeim was evacuated on the night of 10/11 June. With the Gazala Line untenable, Ritchie ordered a retreat; the South African division escaped from Tobruk but the garrison left to hold the port was overwhelmed on 21 June after a one-day assault. Churchill, who received the news in Washington, called Tobruk's fall a bitter blow; Rommel was promoted to field marshal.

Aftermath

Rommel pursued the Eighth Army into Egypt, defeating it again at Mersa Matruh, and was stopped only at the First Battle of El Alamein in July 1942. The Gazala defeat led to Auchinleck taking personal command of the Eighth Army and, in August, to his replacement by Harold Alexander with Bernard Montgomery taking over the Eighth Army. The battle remains a classic study in armoured manoeuvre and in the dangers of dispersing armour against a concentrated opponent.
