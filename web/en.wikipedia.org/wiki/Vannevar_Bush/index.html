Vannevar Bush

Vannevar Bush
Born: 11 March 1890, Everett, Massachusetts, U.S.
Died: 28 June 1974 (aged 84), Belmont, Massachusetts
Known for: Differential analyser, Memex, OSRD, "As We May Think"

Vannevar Bush (11 March 1890 – 28 June 1974) was an American engineer, inventor and science administrator who played a central role in organizing American scientific research during the Second World War. As head of the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD), he coordinated the work of some 6,000 scientists in applying science to warfare, including the early administration of the Manhattan Project. His 1945 report Science, The Endless Frontier laid the groundwork for the National Science Foundation.

Bush directed the American scientific war effort from 1941 to 1947.

Early career and the differential analyser

Bush earned his doctorate from MIT and Harvard in 1916 and joined the faculty of MIT, where he became dean of engineering and vice president. In 1931 he completed the differential analyser, a mechanical analogue computer that could solve differential equations with as many as 18 independent variables; it was used for ballistics calculations and electrical network analysis. He also co-founded the company that became Raytheon.

Wartime administration

In 1940 Bush persuaded President Franklin D. Roosevelt to create the National Defense Research Committee, and in 1941 he became director of the OSRD. Under his leadership, OSRD let contracts to universities and industry rather than building government laboratories, a model that transformed American research. Projects under his oversight included radar development at the MIT Radiation Laboratory, the proximity fuze, and the initial organization of atomic bomb research before it passed to the Army.

"As We May Think" and the Memex

In his influential July 1945 essay "As We May Think", published in The Atlantic, Bush described the Memex, a hypothetical desk-sized device in which an individual would store books, records and communications on microfilm and retrieve them through associative trails — links between related items. The essay is widely credited with inspiring the concepts of hypertext and the personal computer; Douglas Engelbart and Ted Nelson both acknowledged its influence.

Later life

After the war Bush served as president of the Carnegie Institution of Washington and advocated for a permanent federal science agency, realized in 1950 as the National Science Foundation, though with less presidential control than Bush had wanted. He died in Belmont, Massachusetts, in 1974. The National Science Board's Vannevar Bush Award, established in 1980, honours exceptional contributions to science and public service.
