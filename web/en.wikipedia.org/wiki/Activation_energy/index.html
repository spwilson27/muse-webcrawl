Activation energy

Activation energy
SymbolEa
SI unitkJ/mol (also J/mol)
Introduced bySvante Arrhenius, 1889

In the Arrhenius model of reaction rates, activation energy is the minimum amount of energy that must be available to reactants for a chemical reaction to occur. It is usually denoted Ea and given in units of kilojoules per mole. The concept was introduced by the Swedish scientist Svante Arrhenius in 1889.

[Image: Reaction energy profile diagram]

Explanation

A reaction can be visualised as progress along a reaction coordinate from reactants to products, passing through a high-energy transition state. The difference in energy between the reactants and the transition state is the activation energy: only collisions between molecules with at least this much kinetic energy can lead to reaction. Because the fraction of molecules with sufficient energy rises steeply with temperature, reaction rates generally increase rapidly as temperature rises, as described by the Arrhenius equation.

Catalysis

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, thereby increasing the rate of reaction without being consumed. Enzymes are biological catalysts that lower activation energies in living systems.

Negative activation energy

Some reactions, such as certain barrierless radical recombinations, exhibit an apparent negative activation energy, slowing slightly as temperature increases, because they lack a distinct energy barrier.
