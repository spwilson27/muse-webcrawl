History of Montreal

The history of Montreal spans more than four millennia of Indigenous presence followed by French and British colonial periods and its growth into one of Canada's largest cities. The island of Montreal, at the confluence of the Saint Lawrence and Ottawa rivers, was inhabited by the St. Lawrence Iroquoians when Jacques Cartier visited the village of Hochelaga in 1535.

French colonial era

In 1642, Paul de Chomedey de Maisonneuve founded Ville-Marie, a mission settlement that grew into Montreal. The city became a centre of the fur trade and a departure point for French exploration of the interior of North America. Montreal was surrendered to British forces in 1760 during the Seven Years' War, and the Treaty of Paris (1763) ceded Canada to Britain.

Industrial growth

The opening of the Lachine Canal in 1825 and the arrival of railways made Montreal Canada's commercial metropolis through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Immigration, manufacturing, and the port drove rapid growth, and the city hosted landmark events such as Expo 67 and the 1976 Summer Olympics.

Modern era

Postwar Montreal saw the Quiet Revolution, the election of reform mayors, major infrastructure projects, and debates over language and sovereignty in Quebec. Today Montreal is a major centre of culture, education, aerospace, and technology, and the second-most populous city in Canada. See also Montreal.
