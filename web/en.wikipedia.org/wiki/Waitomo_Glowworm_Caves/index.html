Waitomo Glowworm Caves

The Waitomo Glowworm Caves are a cave system on the North Island of New Zealand, in the Waikato region near the town of Waitomo. They are a major tourist attraction, famous for the thousands of glowworms — the larvae of the fungus gnat Arachnocampa luminosa, a species found exclusively in New Zealand — that line the cave walls and ceilings and emit a radiant blue-green light.

History

The caves were known to local Māori for generations. In 1887 the Māori chief Tane Tinorau, together with English surveyor Fred Mace, made the first extensive exploration of the cave system, travelling by raft on the underground stream. Tinorau and his wife Huti began guiding visitors in 1889. The cave was taken over by the government in the early 20th century and returned to the descendants of the original owners in 1989 as part of a Treaty of Waitangi settlement.

Geology

The limestone formations of the Waitomo region began forming some 30 million years ago, when the area lay beneath the sea and accumulated the compressed remains of marine organisms. Rainwater, slightly acidic, dissolved passages through the rock over millions of years, creating caves decorated with stalactites, stalagmites and other speleothems.

The caves and tours

The system includes the main Glowworm Cave, Ruakuri Cave and Aranui Cave. Guided tours of the main cave pass through chambers such as the Cathedral — noted for its acoustics, and a venue where the Māori Queen Dame Te Atairangikaahu and singers including Dame Kiri Te Kanawa have performed — and end with a silent boat ride through the Glowworm Grotto beneath the living lights of the glowworm larvae, whose dangling silk threads trap small insects attracted to their glow.
