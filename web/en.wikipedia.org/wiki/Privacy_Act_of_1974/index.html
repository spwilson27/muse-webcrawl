Privacy Act of 1974

The Privacy Act of 1974 (5 U.S.C. § 552a) is a United States federal law enacted on December 31, 1974, that establishes a code of fair information practices governing the collection, maintenance, use and dissemination of personally identifiable information held in systems of records by federal agencies.

Provisions

The Act generally prohibits agencies from disclosing records about an individual without that person's written consent, subject to twelve statutory exceptions, including disclosures for law enforcement, statistical research under safeguards, and "routine uses" published in the Federal Register. It gives individuals the right to access records about themselves and to request amendment of records that are not accurate, relevant, timely or complete. Agencies must maintain only information relevant and necessary to their purposes, keep an accounting of disclosures, and safeguard records against misuse.

Background and amendments

The Act was passed in the wake of the Watergate scandal and concerns about federal databanks and computerised files. Later amendments addressed computer matching programmes (the Computer Matching and Privacy Protection Act of 1988). Its rights extend to U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents. The Act has been criticised for broad exemptions and limited remedies, and much federal privacy protection remains sector-specific.
