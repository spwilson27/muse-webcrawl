The International Military Tribunal (IMT) was a court convened by the Allied powers after the Second World War to try the surviving leaders of Nazi Germany. Sitting in Nuremberg, Germany, it conducted the most famous of the Nuremberg trials, formally the Trial of the Major War Criminals before the International Military Tribunal, from 20 November 1945 to 1 October 1946.

Legal basis

The Tribunal was created by the London Agreement and Charter of 8 August 1945 between the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union and France. The Charter defined three categories of crime over which the Tribunal had jurisdiction: crimes against peace (planning and waging aggressive war), war crimes (violations of the laws and customs of war), and crimes against humanity (murder, extermination, enslavement, persecution and other inhumane acts against civilian populations). The Charter rejected the defences of official position and superior orders.

The trial

Twenty-four men were indicted, among them Hermann Göring, Rudolf Hess, Joachim von Ribbentrop, Wilhelm Keitel, Alfred Jodl, Albert Speer, Julius Streicher and Ernst Kaltenbrunner; Robert Ley committed suicide before trial and Gustav Krupp was found medically unfit. Martin Bormann was tried in absentia. The prosecution, led on the American side by Supreme Court Justice Robert H. Jackson, relied heavily on the Nazis' own documents and film evidence of the concentration camps. Simultaneous interpretation in English, French, Russian and German pioneered the technology of instant translation.

Verdicts

Judgment was delivered on 30 September–1 October 1946. Twelve defendants were sentenced to death by hanging (Bormann in absentia), three to life imprisonment (Hess, Walther Funk and Erich Raeder), four to prison terms of 10–20 years, and three were acquitted (Hjalmar Schacht, Franz von Papen and Hans Fritzsche). Göring killed himself with a cyanide capsule the night before his scheduled execution; the remaining death sentences were carried out on 16 October 1946. Several Nazi organisations, including the SS, Gestapo and the Leadership Corps of the Nazi Party, were declared criminal.

Legacy

The IMT established precedents that shaped modern international criminal law: individual responsibility for aggression and atrocity, the rejection of sovereign immunity for such crimes, and the Nuremberg principles later affirmed by the United Nations. Its work led through the Tokyo tribunal, the ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda, to the permanent International Criminal Court. Critics have charged the trial with victors' justice and retroactive lawmaking, while defenders argue it gave the defendants a fairer hearing than they had afforded their victims and documented Nazi crimes for history.
