Antoine-Louis Barye

Born: 24 September 1796, Paris, France
Died: 25 June 1875, Paris, France
Known for: Sculpture, animalier bronze

Antoine-Louis Barye (24 September 1796 – 25 June 1875) was a French sculptor most famous for his work as an animalier, a sculptor of animals. He is regarded, along with the painter Eugène Delacroix and the sculptor Auguste Préault, as one of the founders of the Romantic movement in French sculpture.

Life and work

Barye trained as a goldsmith and engraver before turning to sculpture, studying at the École des Beaux-Arts and dissecting animals at the Jardin des Plantes to master their anatomy. His breakthrough came at the Salon of 1831 with his plaster group Tiger Devouring a Gavial, whose violent drama announced a new direction in sculpture. In 1833 he exhibited the monumental Lion Crushing a Serpent, later cast in bronze for the Tuileries gardens.

Rejected by the Salon jury in 1837, Barye withdrew from official exhibitions and made his living producing small, exquisitely finished bronze editions of animals for private collectors, founding his own foundry to control quality. Official recognition returned with the July Monarchy and the Second Empire; he was appointed professor of zoological drawing at the Muséum d'Histoire Naturelle in 1854 and became one of the most honoured sculptors in France.

Legacy

Barye's bronzes, characterised by exact anatomical observation and Romantic intensity, were collected across Europe and America and had a lasting influence on animal sculpture. Large holdings of his work are in the Louvre, the Musée d'Orsay, and the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore.
