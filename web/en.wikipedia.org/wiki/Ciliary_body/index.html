The ciliary body is a part of the eye that includes the ciliary muscle, which controls the shape of the lens, and the ciliary epithelium, which produces the aqueous humour. It is a ring of tissue forming the middle layer of the eye's wall (the uvea), lying between the choroid behind and the iris in front, encircling the lens just behind the limbus. The aqueous humour produced here nourishes the lens and cornea and determines intraocular pressure.

Anatomy

The ciliary body is roughly triangular in cross-section and about 5–6 mm wide. Its anterior portion, the pars plicata, bears some 70–80 radial folds called ciliary processes, from which the zonular fibres (zonules of Zinn) extend to suspend the lens. The flatter posterior portion, the pars plana, extends back to the ora serrata where the retina begins. The bulk of the structure is the ciliary muscle, a ring of smooth muscle with longitudinal, radial and circular fibres under parasympathetic control from the oculomotor nerve. A double-layered epithelium — pigmented outside, non-pigmented inside — covers its inner surface.

Functions

Accommodation

When the ciliary muscle contracts, the ring constricts and tension on the zonules slackens, allowing the elastic lens to round up and increase its optical power for near vision — the process of accommodation. When the muscle relaxes, zonular tension flattens the lens for distance vision. Loss of lens elasticity with age (presbyopia) limits this process.

Aqueous humour production

The non-pigmented ciliary epithelium actively secretes aqueous humour into the posterior chamber at roughly 2–3 µL per minute. The fluid passes through the pupil into the anterior chamber and drains through the trabecular meshwork and canal of Schlemm. The balance of production and drainage sets intraocular pressure; obstruction of outflow causes the pressure rise of glaucoma. Drugs that reduce aqueous production (beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors) act on the ciliary epithelium, and the blood–aqueous barrier formed by its tight junctions keeps the fluid clear.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the ciliary body (cyclitis, a form of intermediate/anterior uveitis) causes pain, photophobia and blurred vision. Blunt trauma can detach the ciliary body (cyclodialysis), causing low pressure (hypotony). Tumours such as ciliary body melanoma and medulloepithelioma arise here, hidden behind the iris and often detected late. Surgical and laser procedures on the ciliary processes (cyclophotocoagulation) are used to reduce aqueous production in refractory glaucoma.
