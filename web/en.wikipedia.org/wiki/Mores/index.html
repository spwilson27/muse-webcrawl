Mores

Mores (pronounced "mor-ays"; from Latin mōrēs, plural of mōs, "custom") are the norms, conventions, and moral customs of a society that are regarded as essential to its welfare and values. The term was popularised in sociology by William Graham Sumner in his book Folkways (1906).

Folkways, mores, and taboos

Sumner distinguished folkways — routine customs whose violation draws mild disapproval — from mores, which carry moral significance: violating them provokes strong sanctions because the community believes they embody right and wrong. Strongly held mores may harden into taboos, prohibitions (for example against incest or cannibalism) that arouse horror rather than mere disapproval. Mores may eventually be codified into law, but many remain powerful unwritten rules enforced socially.

Examples

Mores commonly concern marriage and sexuality, treatment of the dead, honesty in basic dealings, and respect owed to parents and elders. Because they are tied to a group's moral self-understanding, mores vary between cultures and change slowly, often lagging behind technological and economic change — a phenomenon sociologists call cultural lag.
