Lieutenant governor (United States)

A lieutenant governor is an official in state governments of 45 out of 50 United States. In most cases, the lieutenant governor is the highest officer of state after the governor, standing in for that officer when they are absent from the state or temporarily incapacitated. In many states the office is the first in the line of succession to the governorship.

States without the office

Five states do not have a lieutenant governor: Arizona, Maine, New Hampshire, Oregon, and Wyoming. In those states, succession and the duties of the office fall to another official, such as the secretary of state or the president of the state senate.

Duties and powers

The powers of lieutenant governors vary widely. Common features include:

  • Serving as president of the state senate, often with a tie-breaking vote (as in the U.S. vice president's role)
  • Succeeding to the governorship upon vacancy
  • Acting as governor when the governor is out of state or incapacitated
  • Assignments given by statute or by the governor, such as leading trade missions or commissions

In Texas, the lieutenant governor's control over the Senate makes the office arguably more powerful than the governorship. In Alaska, Hawaii and Utah, the lieutenant governor serves as the state's chief elections officer, equivalent to a secretary of state.

Election

In 26 states, the governor and lieutenant governor are elected together on a joint ticket, as with the U.S. president and vice president. In 17 states they are elected separately, which can result in the two offices being held by members of different parties. In Tennessee and West Virginia, the title is bestowed on the president of the state senate.

History

The office descends from colonial lieutenant governors who deputised for crown-appointed governors. Many early state constitutions included the office, and it spread with statehood across the 19th century.
