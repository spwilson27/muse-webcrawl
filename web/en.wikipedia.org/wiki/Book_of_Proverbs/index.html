The Book of Proverbs (Hebrew: מִשְׁלֵי, Mishlei) is a book in the third section (Ketuvim) of the Hebrew Bible and a book of the Christian Old Testament. It is a collection of moral and religious instruction in the form of pithy sayings, poems, and short discourses, traditionally attributed largely to King Solomon.

Structure

The book opens with a prologue (chapters 1–9) contrasting the paths of wisdom and folly, personifying Wisdom as a woman calling in the streets. The main body consists of collections of individual proverbs attributed to Solomon (chapters 10–22 and 25–29), the "words of the wise", and short appendices including the words of Agur and Lemuel. The final chapter contains the famous acrostic poem of the "wife of noble character" (Proverbs 31).

Themes

The book's governing theme is that "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 9:10). Its sayings address diligence, honesty, humility, speech, family life, poverty and wealth, and the consequences of foolishness.

Composition

Scholars generally date the collection's final form to the Persian or early Hellenistic period, though many individual proverbs are considerably older and show parallels with ancient Egyptian wisdom literature such as the Instruction of Amenemope.
