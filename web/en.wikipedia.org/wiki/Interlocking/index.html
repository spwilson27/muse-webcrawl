Interlocking

In railway signalling, an interlocking is an arrangement of signal apparatus that prevents conflicting movements through an arrangement of tracks such as junctions or crossings. The signalling appliances and tracks are sometimes collectively referred to as an interlocking plant. An interlocking is designed so that it is impossible to display a signal to proceed unless the route to be used is proven safe.

In North American usage, the official definition is: "An arrangement of signals and signal appliances so interconnected that their movements must succeed each other in proper sequence."

History

Interlocking was pioneered in Britain in the 1850s, notably by John Saxby, who patented a mechanical system linking points and signals in 1856. Before interlocking, pointsmen and signalmen worked independently, and conflicting signals could be — and were — given, causing collisions. Early accidents, and the investigations that followed, drove the adoption of interlocking as standard practice; in the United Kingdom the Regulation of Railways Act 1889 made interlocking of points and signals effectively compulsory on passenger lines.

Types

Mechanical interlocking uses a system of rods, tappets and locking bars in the signal box so that levers physically cannot be moved into conflicting positions. Electro-mechanical and relay interlocking replaced physical linkage with electrical circuits while preserving the same logical principles. Modern electronic (solid-state) interlocking implements the logic in software on fail-safe computer hardware, and computer-based control allows large areas to be supervised from a single operations centre.

Principles

The core functions of an interlocking include: setting and locking points (switches) in the required position; detecting that the track ahead is unoccupied, typically by track circuits or axle counters; locking the route so no conflicting movement can be authorised; and only then clearing the signal. Once a train has entered the route, the interlocking releases the locking progressively behind it.

Other uses

The term is also used more generally for any safety mechanism in which devices are interdependent so that an unsafe sequence cannot occur — for example in machinery guards, electrical switchgear and pipeline valves.
