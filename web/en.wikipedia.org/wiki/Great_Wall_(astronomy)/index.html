The Great Wall (sometimes called the CfA2 Great Wall) is an immense galaxy filament, one of the largest known structures in the observable universe. It was discovered in 1989 by Margaret Geller and John Huchra of the Harvard–Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, from data of the CfA Redshift Survey.

Description

The wall measures roughly 500 to 750 million light-years long, about 200–300 million light-years wide and some 15 million light-years thick, lying at distances of about 200 to 300 million light-years. It contains several rich galaxy superclusters, including the Coma Cluster and the Hercules Superclusters. Its discovery startled astronomers: no structure of that size had been expected, and it raised questions about the scale at which the universe becomes homogeneous — a basic assumption of the cosmological principle in the standard Big Bang model.

Context

The Great Wall was the first of the giant structures charted by redshift surveys, later surpassed by the Sloan Great Wall (announced 2003, ~1.4 billion light-years long) and the Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall (claimed in 2013–14). Modern surveys and simulations of cosmic structure, such as the Millennium Simulation, show such filaments to be natural outcomes of gravitational growth of initial density fluctuations with dark matter and dark energy — though the largest claimed structures continue to be debated in relation to the assumed "End of Greatness" scale of cosmic homogeneity.
