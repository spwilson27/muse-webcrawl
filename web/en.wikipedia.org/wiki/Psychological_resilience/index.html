Psychological resilience

Psychological resilience is the ability to cope mentally and emotionally with a crisis, adapt to adversity, trauma or significant stress, and return to a pre-crisis state of functioning — sometimes summarised as "bouncing back". Resilience is not a fixed trait possessed by a few, but a dynamic process involving behaviours, thoughts and relationships that can be learned and developed.

Research

Modern resilience research began with longitudinal studies of children at risk, notably Emmy Werner's Kauai study, which followed children born in 1955 and found that a substantial minority of those facing poverty, family instability and perinatal stress nevertheless developed into competent, caring adults. Researchers such as Norman Garmezy, Michael Rutter and Ann Masten — who called resilience "ordinary magic" — identified protective factors including a close relationship with a competent adult, cognitive and self-regulation skills, and schools and communities that provide structure and support.

Factors

Contributors to resilience include realistic optimism, emotional regulation, problem-solving skills, social support, a sense of purpose, and cultural and spiritual resources. George Bonanno's research on bereavement and trauma showed that a stable trajectory of healthy functioning after loss is more common than chronic dysfunction — resilience is the modal human response to adversity.

Applications

Resilience-building programmes are used in education, the military (for example the U.S. Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness), disaster response and clinical psychology, though critics caution against "responsibilising" individuals for structural problems and note the risk of pathologising normal distress.
