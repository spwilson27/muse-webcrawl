Cyprus dispute

The Cyprus dispute, also known as the Cyprus conflict, the Cyprus issue or the Cyprus problem, is a long-running territorial and constitutional dispute between the Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot communities of the island of Cyprus. The island has been effectively divided since 1974, when a coup d'état backed by the Greek military junta, aiming at union (enosis) with Greece, prompted a Turkish military intervention. The internationally recognised Republic of Cyprus controls the south, while the self-declared Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus, recognised only by Turkey, controls the north; a UN-patrolled buffer zone separates them.

Background

Cyprus came under British rule in 1878 and was annexed in 1914. Greek Cypriots long sought enosis (union with Greece); the armed campaign of EOKA (1955–1959) ended with the Zurich and London Agreements, and Cyprus became an independent republic in 1960 under a power-sharing constitution, with Britain, Greece and Turkey as guarantor powers. Britain retained the Sovereign Base Areas of Akrotiri and Dhekelia.

Violence and division, 1963–1974

Constitutional breakdown in December 1963 led to intercommunal fighting ("Bloody Christmas"), the withdrawal of Turkish Cypriots into enclaves, and the deployment of UNFICYP in 1964. In 1967 further clashes brought Greece and Turkey close to war. On 15 July 1974 the Greek junta sponsored a coup that installed Nikos Sampson and proclaimed the aim of enosis. Turkey intervened militarily on 20 July and, in a second offensive in August, took control of about 36% of the island. Some 160,000–200,000 Greek Cypriots fled or were expelled south, and Turkish Cypriots moved north; Nicosia remains divided by the "Green Line".

Aftermath and negotiations

In 1983 the Turkish Cypriot side unilaterally declared independence as the "Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus", recognised only by Turkey and declared invalid by the UN Security Council. The Republic of Cyprus joined the European Union in 2004, days after Greek Cypriot voters rejected the UN's Annan Plan for reunification in a referendum (Turkish Cypriots approved it). The crossing points in the buffer zone partially opened in 2003. Talks at Crans-Montana collapsed in 2017; the dispute also involves property claims, missing persons, Turkish settlers, and competing claims to offshore natural gas. The status quo remains one of the world's longest-running frozen conflicts.
