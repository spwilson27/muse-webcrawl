Blichfeldt's theorem is a mathematical theorem in the geometry of numbers, proved by Hans Frederick Blichfeldt in 1914. It states that any measurable set in Euclidean space whose volume exceeds the volume of the fundamental parallelepiped of a lattice contains two distinct points whose difference belongs to the lattice; equivalently, the set can be translated so that it contains a non-zero lattice point in a strengthened sense.

Statement

Let Λ be a full-rank lattice in Rn with determinant d(Λ) (the volume of its fundamental parallelepiped). If X ⊂ Rn is a measurable set with volume greater than d(Λ), then there exist two distinct points x, y ∈ X such that x − y ∈ Λ. Equivalently, there is a non-zero vector v ∈ Λ such that the intersection X ∩ (X + v) is non-empty. In the classical case Λ = Zn, the condition is simply that X has volume greater than 1.

Proof idea

The proof is an application of the pigeonhole principle. The set X is cut into pieces by the translates of the fundamental parallelepiped, and the pieces are translated back into a single fundamental parallelepiped. If no two pieces overlapped, the total volume would be at most d(Λ), contradicting the hypothesis. Hence two points of X land on the same point under translation by lattice vectors, and their difference is a lattice vector.

Applications

Blichfeldt's theorem is the key lemma in the standard proof of Minkowski's theorem on convex bodies: applying the theorem to a centrally symmetric convex set of volume greater than 2nd(Λ), after shrinking by a factor of two and using convexity and symmetry, produces a non-zero lattice point inside the set. Consequences include proofs of Lagrange's four-square theorem and results on simultaneous Diophantine approximation.
