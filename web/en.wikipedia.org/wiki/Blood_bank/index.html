FunctionCollection, processing, testing and storage of blood and blood products

A blood bank is a centre where blood gathered as a result of blood donation is stored and preserved for later use in blood transfusion. The term usually refers to a division of a hospital where blood products are stored and where testing is performed to reduce the risk of transfusion-related adverse events. Blood banks also collect blood from donors, separate it into components, and distribute it to hospitals.

History

The first blood banks were established in the 1930s. During the Spanish Civil War, Frederic Durán-Jordà organised a blood service in Barcelona that collected, tested, refrigerated and transported blood to front-line hospitals. In the Soviet Union, Sergei Yudin pioneered the use of cadaveric blood. In Britain, the wartime Emergency Blood Transfusion Service, organised by Janet Vaughan, created a national system of depots. In the United States, Bernard Fantus established the first hospital blood bank at Cook County Hospital in Chicago in 1937, coining the term "blood bank".

Collection and processing

Whole blood collected from donors is usually separated by centrifugation into its components: packed red blood cells, plasma and platelets. Plasma may be further processed into cryoprecipitate and fractionated products such as albumin and immunoglobulins. Each donation is tested for blood group (ABO and Rh) and screened for transfusion-transmissible infections including HIV, hepatitis B and C, and syphilis.

Storage

Red blood cells are refrigerated at 2–6 °C and can typically be stored for up to 42 days depending on the anticoagulant-preservative solution. Plasma is frozen and may be kept for a year or more. Platelets are stored at room temperature with gentle agitation and have a shelf life of only five to seven days, which makes platelet supply a persistent logistical challenge. Before issue, units are crossmatched or electronically checked against the intended recipient's sample to ensure compatibility.
