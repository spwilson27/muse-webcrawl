British anti-invasion preparations of the Second World War were the extensive efforts made in 1940–41 to defend Britain against a feared German invasion, Operation Sea Lion, following the fall of France. In a matter of months, Britain's coasts and countryside were transformed with tens of thousands of fortifications, anti-tank obstacles and defended localities, many of which — above all the humble concrete pillbox — remain familiar features of the landscape today.

Background

After the evacuation from Dunkirk in May–June 1940, Britain's regular army had lost most of its heavy equipment, and invasion seemed imminent while the Battle of Britain raged overhead. General Edmund Ironside, as Commander-in-Chief Home Forces, adopted a strategy of static defence: a "coastal crust" of pillboxes and obstacles, backed by inland "stop lines" intended to delay German armour until mobile reserves could counter-attack. His successor, Alan Brooke, from July 1940 placed greater emphasis on mobile reserves, but the building programme continued.

Defences

More than 28,000 pillboxes and other hardened positions were built, alongside beach scaffolding, anti-tank ditches, minefields, concrete "dragon's teeth", roadblocks and flame traps (including petrol-filled drums that could ignite roads). Signposts and station name boards were removed to confuse invaders, church bells were reserved as an invasion signal, and airfields and open ground were obstructed with poles, wires and old cars to prevent glider landings. The Home Guard — over 1.5 million volunteers at its height — guarded vulnerable points and would have reinforced the regular army. Secret Auxiliary Units were trained to go to ground and wage guerrilla warfare after an occupation, and plans existed, under Operation Sea Lion's expected counterpart, to evacuate the government and leave stay-behind parties.

Aftermath

Germany postponed Sea Lion indefinitely in the autumn of 1940 and turned east in 1941; the defences were gradually run down from 1942, freeing men and materials for the offensive war. Thousands of pillboxes survive as a memorial of the "finest hour" generation, and the preparations remain a rich subject for archaeology and popular history.
