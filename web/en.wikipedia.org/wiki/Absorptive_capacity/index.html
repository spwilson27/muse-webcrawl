Absorptive capacity

In business administration and organisational theory, absorptive capacity is an organisation's ability to recognise the value of new, external information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends. The concept was introduced by Wesley Cohen and Daniel Levinthal in their 1990 paper "Absorptive Capacity: A New Perspective on Learning and Innovation".

Dimensions

Cohen and Levinthal argued that a firm's prior related knowledge — built largely through its own research and development — determines its ability to exploit external knowledge, giving R&D a "two faces" role: generating innovation and enabling learning from others. Later work, notably by Zahra and George (2002), divided absorptive capacity into potential capacity (acquisition and assimilation of knowledge) and realised capacity (transformation and exploitation).

Effects and applications

Absorptive capacity has been linked to innovation performance, technology transfer, alliance learning, and the ability of regions and nations to benefit from foreign direct investment and knowledge spillovers. It is a central construct in research on dynamic capabilities and organisational learning.

Criticism

Scholars have criticised the concept's reification and the wide variety of measures used to operationalise it, prompting several reconceptualisations that emphasise processes, routines and power relations within firms.
