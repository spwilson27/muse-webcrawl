Spiral ganglion

The spiral ganglion is the group of nerve cells that serve the sense of hearing; it consists of the cell bodies of the bipolar neurons whose peripheral processes innervate the hair cells of the organ of Corti and whose central processes form the cochlear division of the vestibulocochlear nerve (cranial nerve VIII).

Location and structure

The ganglion lies within the modiolus, the central bony core of the cochlea, occupying the spiral canal of the modiolus (Rosenthal's canal), which winds about two and a half turns in parallel with the cochlear duct. In humans it contains roughly 30,000–35,000 neurons. Type I neurons (about 90–95 percent) innervate inner hair cells and carry the great majority of auditory information to the brain; type II neurons innervate outer hair cells.

Function

Hair cell stimulation causes the afferent fibres of spiral ganglion neurons to fire, transmitting coded information about sound frequency and intensity to the cochlear nuclei of the brainstem. Because neurons serving the base of the cochlea (high frequencies) and apex (low frequencies) are arranged tonotopically, the ganglion preserves the frequency map of the cochlea.

Clinical relevance

Survival of spiral ganglion neurons is essential for cochlear implants, whose electrodes directly stimulate these cells. Degeneration of the ganglion can follow hair cell loss, noise injury, ototoxic drugs and ageing, contributing to sensorineural hearing loss.
