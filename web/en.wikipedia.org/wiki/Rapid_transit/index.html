Rapid transit

For other uses, see Metro (disambiguation).

Rapid transit or mass rapid transit (MRT), also known as a metro, subway, underground or U-Bahn, is a type of high-capacity public transport generally found in urban areas. A rapid transit system that is primarily or traditionally underground is known as a metro. Unlike buses or trams, rapid transit systems operate on an exclusive right-of-way which cannot be accessed by pedestrians or other vehicles, and which is often grade-separated in tunnels or on elevated railways or bridges.

Modern services on rapid transit systems are provided on designated lines between stations, typically using electric multiple units on rail tracks. The stations typically have elevators or escalators, and are usually longer and wider than ordinary train stations. Some systems are fully automated and driverless.

History

The world's first rapid transit system was the partly underground Metropolitan Railway, which opened in London in 1863 with steam locomotives and now forms part of the London Underground. In 1868, New York opened the elevated West Side and Yonkers Patent Railway, initially a cable-hauled line using static steam engines.

Characteristics

Rapid transit systems generally have high capacity, frequent service, and stations spaced roughly one kilometre apart in city centres. They use dedicated tracks, electric traction, and signalling systems that permit trains to run at short headways. Fares are usually collected at station gates rather than on board.

Largest systems

The largest rapid transit systems in the world by number of stations and length of track include the Shanghai Metro, the Beijing Subway, the New York City Subway and the Moscow Metro.
