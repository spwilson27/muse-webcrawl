CORONA was the first operational United States photo-reconnaissance satellite programme, operated by the Central Intelligence Agency with the support of the US Air Force from 1959 to 1972. Publicly disguised as the "Discoverer" scientific research programme, Corona satellites photographed the Soviet Union, China and other denied areas during the Cold War, returning film to Earth in re-entry capsules that were snatched in mid-air by recovery aircraft.

History

The programme was approved in 1958 after the failure of early efforts and the growing need to monitor Soviet bomber and missile deployments. After twelve failed or partially failed Discoverer missions, Discoverer 13 returned its capsule from orbit in August 1960 — the first object ever recovered from orbit — and Discoverer 14, launched on 18 August 1960 and recovered the next day, carried the first successful Corona film, imaging more Soviet territory in a single mission than all previous U-2 flights combined.

System

Corona satellites (designated KH-1 through KH-4B, for "KeyHole") were built by Lockheed using the Agena upper stage as the spacecraft bus. Cameras built by Itek took panoramic photographs on 70 mm film. After a mission of days to weeks, film return vehicles ("buckets") separated, re-entered, and deployed parachutes; a specially equipped C-119 (later JC-130) aircraft caught the descending capsule by its parachute, or it was recovered from the ocean. Resolution improved from about 12 metres in early systems to roughly 1.8 metres in the KH-4B.

Legacy

Corona imagery resolved the "missile gap" debate, mapped Soviet missile fields and provided the basis for arms-control verification. President Bill Clinton declassified the programme in 1995, and its more than 800,000 images became a major resource for archaeology, environmental monitoring and historical geography. Data from Corona underlies services such as the CORONA Atlas of the Middle East.
