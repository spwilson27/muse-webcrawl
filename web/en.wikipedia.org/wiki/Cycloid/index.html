Cycloid

In geometry, a cycloid is the curve traced by a point on a circle as it rolls along a straight line without slipping. A cycloid is a specific form of trochoid and is an example of a roulette, a curve generated by a curve rolling on another curve.

Properties

The cycloid through the origin, with a horizontal base (the x-axis), generated by a circle of radius r, consists of the points (x, y) with x = r(t − sin t), y = r(1 − cos t), where t is a real parameter corresponding to the angle through which the rolling circle has rotated. The area under one arch is 3πr², three times the area of the generating circle, a result first proved by Roberval; the arc length of one arch is exactly 8r, proved by Christopher Wren in 1658.

Brachistochrone and tautochrone

The cycloid is the solution to the brachistochrone problem — the curve of fastest descent under gravity — as shown by Johann Bernoulli in 1696. An inverted cycloid is also the tautochrone: a bead sliding frictionlessly to the bottom does so in the same time regardless of its starting point, a property used by Christiaan Huygens in designing pendulum clocks with cycloidal cheeks.

History

The curve was studied by Nicholas of Cusa and Charles de Bovelles in the early modern period, named by Galileo in 1599, and investigated by Mersenne, Roberval, Pascal, Huygens and the Bernoullis. Pascal's 1658 contest on the cycloid, issued under the pseudonym Amos Dettonville, spurred important developments in infinitesimal methods.
