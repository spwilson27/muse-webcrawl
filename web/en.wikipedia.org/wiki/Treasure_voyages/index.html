Ming treasure voyages

Commissioned byYongle Emperor; Xuande Emperor
CommanderAdmiral Zheng He

The Ming treasure voyages were seven great naval expeditions undertaken by the treasure fleet of Ming-dynasty China between 1405 and 1433. Commanded by the Muslim eunuch admiral Zheng He, the fleets — numbering, on the largest voyages, hundreds of ships and more than 27,000 men — projected Chinese power across the Indian Ocean, visiting Southeast Asia, India, the Persian Gulf, Arabia and the coast of East Africa decades before European ships rounded the Cape of Good Hope.

Purposes and ships

Commissioned by the Yongle Emperor, who had usurped the throne in 1402, the voyages proclaimed the emperor's legitimacy, enrolled foreign rulers in the tributary system, secured trade routes, and suppressed piracy — Zheng He destroyed the pirate fleet of Chen Zuyi at Palembang on the first voyage and intervened in Ceylon and Sumatra. The fleet's flagships, the "treasure ships" (baochuan), are described in Ming sources with dimensions implying lengths of over 120 metres; modern scholars debate the true figures, but they were by any account among the largest wooden ships ever built.

Routes and diplomacy

Following established monsoon routes via Champa, Java, Malacca and Ceylon to Calicut, later voyages reached Hormuz, Aden and Jeddah, and on the fourth to seventh voyages detachments visited the Swahili coast — Mogadishu, Brava and Malindi. Exotic tribute flowed back to the Ming court, most famously a giraffe from Bengal in 1414, presented as the auspicious qilin. Zheng He's inscriptions, such as the trilingual stele erected at Galle in 1409, record his devotion to the goddess Tianfei and his respect for local religions.

End of the voyages

The voyages ceased after 1433. Their expense, the hostility of Confucian officials to eunuch-led extravagance, the Yongle Emperor's death in 1424 and the Xuande Emperor's in 1435, and the growing Mongol threat on the northern frontier all redirected Ming priorities inward. The fleet was allowed to decay and maritime prohibitions (haijin) tightened, leaving the Indian Ocean to others — though private Chinese trade continued. Zheng He died in 1433, traditionally at sea off Calicut on the final voyage, and is commemorated today in China and throughout Southeast Asia.
