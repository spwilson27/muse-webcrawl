Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy

Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES), also called inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), is an analytical technique used to detect and quantify chemical elements in a sample. It uses an inductively coupled plasma — a very hot, partially ionised gas, usually argon, reaching temperatures of roughly 6,000–10,000 K — to atomise and excite the sample. As the excited atoms and ions return to lower energy states, they emit light at wavelengths characteristic of each element; measuring the intensity of this light allows the concentration of each element to be determined.

How it works

A liquid sample is typically nebulised into a fine aerosol and carried by argon gas into the plasma torch. The plasma is sustained by a radio-frequency induction coil that heats the argon by electromagnetic induction. A spectrometer separates the emitted light by wavelength, and detectors (historically photomultipliers, now usually charge-coupled or charge-injection devices) measure the emission lines. Because the plasma excites many elements simultaneously, ICP-AES can measure dozens of elements in a single run.

Advantages and limitations

ICP-AES offers low detection limits (typically in the parts-per-billion to parts-per-million range for many elements), a wide linear dynamic range, and relatively low chemical interference because of the high plasma temperature. Its main limitations are spectral interferences — overlapping emission lines from different elements — and higher running costs than flame techniques, chiefly the consumption of argon. For even lower detection limits, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) is often used instead.

Applications

The technique is widely used in environmental analysis (water, soil, and waste testing), geochemistry, metallurgy, agriculture, food safety, and clinical and forensic laboratories, wherever multi-element trace analysis is required.
