Cyberweapon

A cyberweapon is a malware agent employed for military, paramilitary, or intelligence purposes as part of a cyberattack. The term is generally used for software (and occasionally hardware) designed and deployed by or on behalf of a state or state-sponsored actor to damage, disrupt, spy upon, or gain control of an adversary's computer systems.

Characteristics

Scholars and practitioners have proposed criteria that distinguish a cyberweapon from ordinary criminal malware: it is developed or deployed by a state actor or its proxies; it is aimed at a specific political or military objective; and it is designed to cause effects beyond mere data theft — ranging from espionage through sabotage to physical destruction of equipment controlled by computers. Like conventional weapons, a cyberweapon's value can collapse once it is used, because defenders can analyse it, patch the vulnerabilities it exploits, and render it ineffective.

Examples

  • Stuxnet (discovered 2010) — a worm targeting Siemens industrial control systems, widely attributed to the United States and Israel, which sabotaged centrifuges at Iran's Natanz uranium enrichment facility.
  • Flame (2012) — a large espionage toolkit found across the Middle East.
  • NotPetya (2017) — a destructive wiper disguised as ransomware, attributed to Russia, initially aimed at Ukraine but causing billions of dollars of collateral damage worldwide.
  • WannaCry (2017) — a ransomware worm attributed to North Korea that disrupted hospitals and businesses globally.

Proliferation and law

Because cyberweapons are software, they can be copied, leaked, and reused; the 2017 leak of intelligence-agency exploits fed directly into the WannaCry and NotPetya outbreaks. Legal scholars debate how international law, including the law of armed conflict, applies to such weapons, a debate summarised in the Tallinn Manual process.
