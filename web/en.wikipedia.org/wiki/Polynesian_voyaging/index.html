Polynesian voyaging

Polynesian voyaging (also covered under Polynesian navigation) describes the settlement of the vast Pacific Ocean by the Polynesian peoples using double-hulled voyaging canoes and a sophisticated system of non-instrument wayfinding. Between roughly 3000 BCE (for the Lapita ancestors) and about 1250–1300 CE, voyagers settled the "Polynesian Triangle" bounded by Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui (Easter Island), and Aotearoa (New Zealand) — one of the largest pre-industrial maritime expansions in human history.

The canoes

Voyaging canoes (waka, vaʻa, waʻa) were double-hulled vessels lashed together with coconut-fibre cordage, carrying a crab-claw or Oceanic lateen sail of woven pandanus. Large examples could exceed 30 metres and carry dozens of settlers along with plants (taro, breadfruit, banana, coconut) and animals (pigs, dogs, chickens) needed to found new colonies.

Navigation methods

Master navigators steered by a star compass: memorizing the rising and setting points of stars on the horizon, organized into "houses." They read ocean swells refracted and reflected by distant islands, followed bird flight (land-based birds return to roost at night), observed cloud formations that gather over islands, and used a conceptual reference system of etak (moving reference islands) in the Caroline Islands tradition. Knowledge was transmitted orally through long apprenticeships and chants.

Revival

Long-distance voyaging declined after European contact, and some scholars once doubted intentional settlement. In 1976 the replica canoe Hōkūleʻa, built by the Polynesian Voyaging Society, sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti navigated without instruments by Micronesian master navigator Mau Piailug, proving the feasibility of deliberate two-way voyaging. Hōkūleʻa went on to complete a worldwide voyage (2014–2017), and voyaging societies across the Pacific have revived traditional navigation as a cultural renaissance.
