Nymphaea caerulea

Nymphaea caerulea, known in English as the blue lotus, blue water lily, or sacred blue lily, is a water lily in the family Nymphaeaceae. It is native to eastern Africa and was spread in ancient times along the Nile; the species is now widely cultivated and has been introduced elsewhere in the world.


The blue lotus flower, a symbol of the sun and rebirth in ancient Egypt.

Description

The plant grows from a rhizome anchored in mud, with round floating leaves up to about 40 cm across. The flowers, 10–15 cm in diameter, have pointed pale blue to violet petals fading to white at the base and a golden centre; they open in the morning and close in the afternoon over a period of about three days.

Cultural significance

The blue lotus was one of the most revered plants of ancient Egypt, depicted constantly in tomb paintings, jewellery, and architecture. It symbolised the sun, creation, and rebirth — the flower opens with the sunrise and closes at night — and was associated with the gods Nefertem and Ra. It appears in the Egyptian Book of the Dead, and its image was used in the design of columns and the "lotus chalice" of Tutankhamun.

Properties

The flowers contain the alkaloids apomorphine and nuciferine, and the plant has mild psychoactive properties; it was reportedly steeped in wine in antiquity and is sold today in herbal products, though evidence for strong effects is limited and its legal status varies by jurisdiction.
