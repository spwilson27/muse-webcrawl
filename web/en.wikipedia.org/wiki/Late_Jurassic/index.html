The Late Jurassic is the third epoch of the Jurassic Period, and it spans the geologic time from 161.5 ± 1.0 to 143.1 ± 0.6 million years ago (Ma), which is preserved in the Upper Jurassic strata. In Europe this epoch was historically called the Malm, a term still occasionally used for the lithostratigraphic units of that age.

Subdivisions

The Late Jurassic is divided into three ages or stages, from oldest to youngest: the Oxfordian (161.5–154.8 Ma), the Kimmeridgian (154.8–149.2 Ma), and the Tithonian (149.2–143.1 Ma). Their boundaries are defined by Global Boundary Stratotype Sections and Points (GSSPs), largely based on ammonite biostratigraphy.

Palaeogeography and climate

During the Late Jurassic the supercontinent Pangaea continued to break apart: the central Atlantic widened, the Tethys Ocean expanded, and rifting separated Gondwana fragments. Sea levels were high, flooding continental interiors with shallow epicontinental seas such as the Sundance Sea in North America. The global climate was warm and generally equable, with no polar ice caps, though there is evidence for increasing aridity in some continental interiors.

Fauna

The Late Jurassic saw the dinosaurs reach enormous sizes and diversity. Sauropods such as Diplodocus, Brachiosaurus, Apatosaurus, and Camarasaurus dominated terrestrial herbivore niches, alongside stegosaurs and early ankylosaurs; large theropods such as Allosaurus, Ceratosaurus, and Torvosaurus were the apex predators. In the seas, ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs, and marine crocodyliforms thrived, and ammonites were diverse and abundant. The first undisputed birds, represented by Archaeopteryx from the Tithonian Solnhofen Limestone of Germany, appeared in this epoch, as did many early mammal groups.

Famous fossil localities

Key Late Jurassic formations include the Morrison Formation of the western United States (Kimmeridgian–Tithonian), source of many iconic dinosaurs; the Solnhofen Limestone of Bavaria, a Konservat-Lagerstätte preserving soft tissues and delicate organisms; the Kimmeridge Clay of England; and the Tendaguru Formation of Tanzania, rich in sauropods.
