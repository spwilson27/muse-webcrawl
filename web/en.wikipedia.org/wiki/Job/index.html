Job (Hebrew: אִיּוֹב, ʾIyyōv) is the central figure of the Book of Job in the Hebrew Bible and the Old Testament. He is portrayed as a wealthy and righteous man, "blameless and upright", whose faith is tested when Satan, with God's permission, destroys his property, kills his children and afflicts him with painful sores. The book consists mainly of poetic dialogues between Job and his friends, who urge him to confess sins they assume must explain his suffering, and it closes with speeches from God and the restoration of Job's fortunes.

The narrative

In the prose prologue, God praises Job's righteousness; the satan (the accuser) suggests Job is faithful only because he has been blessed. God permits the test, and Job loses his livestock, servants and children in a series of calamities. When he remains steadfast, he is struck with loathsome sores. His wife urges him to "curse God and die", but Job refuses.

Three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar — come to comfort him and argue that suffering must be punishment for sin. Job rejects their theology, maintains his innocence and demands an audience with God. A younger figure, Elihu, speaks after them. Finally God answers Job "out of the whirlwind" with speeches about the vastness and wildness of creation, and rebukes the friends for having spoken wrongly. Job is restored to health and given twice his former wealth and a new family, living 140 more years.

Interpretation and legacy

The Book of Job is a central text of theodicy writing on the problem of innocent suffering, and scholars generally regard Job as a literary or legendary figure rather than a historical person; he is also mentioned in Ezekiel alongside Noah and Daniel. In Islam, Job (Ayyub) is a prophet famed for patience. The story has inspired works by writers and artists from medieval theologians to William Blake, whose engravings for the book are famous, and the phrase "the patience of Job" passed into common speech.
