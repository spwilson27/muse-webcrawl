Pisco
TypeBrandy (grape spirit)
OriginPeru and Chile
ABV38–48% (Peruvian); 30–50% (Chilean)
Named afterPort of Pisco, Peru

Pisco is a colourless to yellowish-to-amber coloured brandy produced in the winemaking regions of Peru and Chile. Made by distilling fermented grape juice into a spirit, it developed from the aguardiente brought by Spanish settlers and takes its name from the Peruvian port of Pisco, from which it was exported in the colonial era.

Peruvian pisco

Peruvian pisco is distilled to proof in a single distillation from fully fermented fresh must of specified grape varieties (Quebranta, Negra Criolla, Mollar, Uvina, Albilla, Italia, Moscatel, and Torontel) grown in five coastal valleys. By regulation it may not be diluted after distillation, aged in wood, or have anything added. Styles include puro (single variety), acholado (blend), and mosto verde (distilled from partly fermented must).

Chilean pisco

Chilean pisco, produced mainly in the Elqui and Limarí valleys of the Coquimbo and Atacama regions, is typically made from Muscat varieties, may be distilled multiple times, diluted to drinking strength, and aged in oak, giving styles from transparent to amber reservas and gran piscos.

The denomination dispute

Peru and Chile have a long-running dispute over the exclusive right to the name "pisco" as an appellation of origin; both countries claim it as a national spirit, and different jurisdictions recognise one or both. Pisco is the base of the pisco sour, a cocktail claimed by both countries and celebrated with national holidays in each.
