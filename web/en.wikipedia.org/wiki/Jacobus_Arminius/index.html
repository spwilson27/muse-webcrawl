Jacobus Arminius

Jacobus Arminius (Jakob Hermanszoon)

Born: October 10, 1560, Oudewater, Netherlands

Died: October 19, 1609, Leiden, Netherlands

Occupation: Theologian, professor at Leiden University

Known for: Arminianism; opposition to strict Calvinist predestination

Jacobus Arminius (born Jakob Hermanszoon; October 10, 1560 – October 19, 1609) was a Dutch Reformed theologian whose questioning of the Calvinist doctrine of predestination gave rise to Arminianism, one of the most influential currents in Protestant theology.

Life

Arminius was born in Oudewater; his father died while he was an infant, and the town's minister, Theodorus Æmilius, adopted and educated him. When Spanish troops massacred much of Oudewater's population in 1575, including his mother and siblings, benefactors sent him to study at the new University of Leiden and then at Geneva under Theodore Beza, Calvin's successor. Ordained in Amsterdam in 1588, he became a popular preacher. Asked to refute the anti-predestinarian writings of Dirck Coornhert, Arminius instead found himself persuaded that the orthodox account needed revision. In 1603 he was appointed professor of theology at Leiden, where his lectures on predestination provoked an escalating feud with his colleague Franciscus Gomarus, drawing in the States of Holland and dominating Dutch public life until his death from tuberculosis in 1609.

Theology

Arminius taught that God's decrees of election are conditional: God elects those who, by grace, believe in Christ, and foreknows — but does not unilaterally cause — human responses to grace. He emphasized that Christ died for all, that grace precedes and enables faith (prevenient grace), and that God's decrees are ordered so that predestination is grounded in Christ. He denied being a Pelagian and professed loyalty to the Reformed confessions, asking the church to resolve the dispute by scripture and a synod.

Legacy

After his death his followers, the Remonstrants, published the Five Articles of Remonstrance (1610); the Synod of Dort (1618–19) condemned their views and formulated the Canons of Dort, the five points often summarized as TULIP, in response. Arminian ideas nonetheless spread widely, shaping John Wesley and Methodism, the General Baptists, and much of later evangelical, Pentecostal, and free-will theology, and remaining the principal alternative to Calvinist soteriology.
