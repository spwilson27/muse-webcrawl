Classification of finite simple groups

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the classification of finite simple groups is a theorem stating that every finite simple group belongs to one of a small number of families: cyclic groups of prime order; alternating groups of degree at least five; groups of Lie type; or one of 26 exceptional "sporadic" groups. Finite simple groups are the basic building blocks of all finite groups, much as prime numbers are for the integers, so the theorem completes a central problem of group theory.

The proof

The proof is arguably the largest collaborative effort in mathematical history: tens of thousands of journal pages by more than 100 authors, published between roughly 1955 and 2004. Key milestones included the Feit–Thompson odd-order theorem (1963), which showed every non-abelian finite simple group has even order, and the work of Daniel Gorenstein, who organised the classification programme in the 1970s. The largest sporadic group, the Monster, was constructed by Robert Griess in 1982 and is connected, through "monstrous moonshine", to modular functions — a link clarified by Richard Borcherds.

The families

Beyond the cyclic and alternating groups, the groups of Lie type include the classical groups (such as PSL(n,q)) and the exceptional Chevalley and twisted groups arising from Lie algebras over finite fields. The 26 sporadic groups — five Mathieu groups, the Janko groups, the Conway groups, the Fischer groups, and others, culminating in the Monster — fit no infinite pattern.

Verification and second-generation proof

Because the original proof was scattered across some 500 articles, a "second-generation" programme led by Gorenstein, Richard Lyons, and Ronald Solomon has been systematically re-proving and consolidating the theorem in a more accessible, self-contained form — a project still in progress. The classification is now routinely used as a black box throughout finite group theory and combinatorics.
