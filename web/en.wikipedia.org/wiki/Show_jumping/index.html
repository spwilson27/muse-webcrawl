Show jumping

Show jumping is a part of a group of English riding equestrian events that also includes dressage, eventing, and hunt seat. Show jumping, as an internationally governed sport, tests the ability of horse and rider to jump a course of obstacles within a designated arena, combining speed, precision, and obedience. It is governed internationally by the Fédération Équestre Internationale (FEI) and has been an Olympic sport since the 1900 Games in Paris.

Competition format

A course typically consists of 10 to 16 jumping efforts, including combinations of fences such as verticals, oxers (spread fences), walls, gates, and water jumps. Faults are scored for knocking down a rail (four faults), a refusal (four faults), or exceeding the time allowed. Riders who complete the first round without faults advance to a timed jump-off over a shortened course, in which the fastest clear round wins.

Horses must be at least seven years old for the highest international levels, and in top-level (CSI5* and championship) competition fences may reach 1.60 metres in height with spreads of up to 2 metres. Elite horses can clear heights well above their withers and are highly trained athletes in their own right.

History

Show jumping grew out of fox hunting and cavalry training in the 18th and 19th centuries. Enclosure acts in England created fenced fields that required horses and riders to jump obstacles in the hunting field, while cavalry schools in Italy, under Federico Caprilli, developed the forward seat that made jumping safer and more efficient. The sport was formalized at horse shows across Europe and North America in the late 19th century and became a fixture of the Olympic Games from 1900 onward, though in its modern format from Stockholm 1912.

Major events

The FEI Jumping World Cup, inaugurated in 1978, is the sport's premier indoor series. The World Equestrian Games and the European and Pan American Championships are held on multi-year cycles. Prestigious Grand Prix events include Aachen (Germany), Spruce Meadows (Canada), Wellington (United States), and the shows of the Longines Global Champions Tour. The Olympic team and individual titles remain the most coveted honours.
