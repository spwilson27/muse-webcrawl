Crime prevention through environmental design

CPTED seeks to reduce crime through the design of the built environment.

Crime prevention through environmental design (CPTED) is an agenda for manipulating the built environment to reduce crime and the fear of crime. The term was coined by criminologist C. Ray Jeffery in his 1971 book of the same name, and the approach was later promoted and popularised by architect Oscar Newman, whose concept of "defensible space" (1972) influenced housing design, particularly in the United States.

Principles

CPTED strategies are commonly grouped into several principles:

  • Natural surveillance — designing spaces so that users are visible to others, for example through window placement, lighting, and the removal of hiding places.
  • Natural access control — guiding movement through a space using entrances, fencing, landscaping and signage, so that unauthorised access is discouraged.
  • Territorial reinforcement — features that express ownership and distinguish public from private space, encouraging residents to monitor their surroundings.
  • Maintenance — keeping spaces in good repair, on the basis that visible disorder signals a lack of control and invites offending.

A later "second generation" CPTED added social strategies such as community cohesion and connectivity alongside physical design measures.

Applications and criticism

CPTED principles have been applied to housing estates, schools, car parks, public transport and commercial premises, and are embedded in planning guidance in several countries. Critics argue that the approach can be used to justify exclusionary design, displacement of crime to other areas, and excessive surveillance, and that evidence for its effectiveness varies by context.
