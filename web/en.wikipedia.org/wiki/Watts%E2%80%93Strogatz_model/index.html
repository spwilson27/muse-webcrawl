Watts–Strogatz model

The Watts–Strogatz model, proposed by Duncan J. Watts and Steven Strogatz in a landmark 1998 paper in Nature, is a random graph generation model that produces graphs with small-world properties, including short average path lengths and high clustering. It helped launch the modern science of complex networks.

Algorithm

The construction begins with a regular ring lattice: N nodes arranged in a ring, each connected to its K nearest neighbours. Each edge is then "rewired" with probability β by moving one of its endpoints to a randomly chosen node. For β = 0 the graph remains a regular lattice; for β = 1 it becomes essentially a random graph.

Properties

For intermediate values of β, the rewired shortcuts dramatically shorten the average path length while the clustering coefficient stays close to that of the original lattice. This combination — high local clustering with globally short paths — matches the structure observed in many real systems, including neural networks, power grids and social networks, and relates to the "six degrees of separation" phenomenon explored earlier by Stanley Milgram.

Influence

The model provided the first simple mechanism explaining the ubiquity of small-world networks in nature and society, and was soon complemented by the Barabási–Albert model of scale-free networks generated by preferential attachment.
