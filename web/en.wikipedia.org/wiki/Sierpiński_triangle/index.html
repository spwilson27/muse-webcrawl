Sierpiński triangle

The Sierpiński triangle, a self-similar fractal.

The Sierpiński triangle (also called the Sierpiński gasket or sieve) is a fractal with the overall shape of an equilateral triangle, subdivided recursively into smaller equilateral triangles. It is named after the Polish mathematician Wacław Sierpiński, who described it in 1915, although the pattern appears in art centuries earlier, including in the mosaics of medieval Italian churches. It is one of the most familiar examples of a self-similar set.

Construction

Begin with a solid equilateral triangle. Connect the midpoints of its sides to form four congruent triangles and remove the central inverted one. Repeat the process for each of the three remaining upright triangles, indefinitely. After n steps there are 3n triangles of side 2−n relative to the original. The same figure arises in other ways: shading the odd entries of Pascal's triangle, or playing the "chaos game" — repeatedly moving halfway from a random vertex to a random starting point — produces points that accumulate on the triangle.

Properties

The limiting set has zero area, since the retained fraction (3/4)n tends to zero, and its Hausdorff dimension is log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585. The triangle is exactly self-similar: it consists of three copies of itself scaled by one half. It can also be generated by an iterated function system of three contractions, or as the attractor of a Lindenmayer system.

Generalizations

The three-dimensional analogue, built from a tetrahedron, is the Sierpiński tetrahedron or tetrix. Arrowhead-curve constructions, cellular automaton Rule 60, and the Tower of Hanoi state graph all reproduce the same pattern, making the triangle a recurring example across mathematics and computer science.
