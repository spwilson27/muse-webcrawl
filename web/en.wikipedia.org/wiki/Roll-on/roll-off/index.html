Roll-on/roll-off

TypeCargo ship design
CargoWheeled vehicles: cars, trucks, trailers, rail cars
AccessStern, bow or side ramps
VariantsPCC, PCTC, RoPax, ConRo
Capacity measureLane metres; CEU (car-equivalent units)

Roll-on/roll-off (RoRo or ro-ro) ships are cargo vessels designed to carry wheeled cargo, such as cars, motorcycles, trucks, semi-trailer trucks, buses, trailers and railroad cars, that is driven on and off the ship on its own wheels or using a platform vehicle. This contrasts with lift-on/lift-off (LoLo) vessels, which use cranes to load and unload. RoRo ships have built-in or shore-based ramps and multi-level internal car decks, allowing vehicles to be loaded and discharged quickly without cranes.

Design

A typical RoRo ship is a boxy, multi-deck vessel with a large enclosed superstructure running nearly the full length of the hull. Vehicles enter through a stern ramp — sometimes supplemented by bow visors or side doors — and move between decks on internal ramps or lifts. Pure car carriers (PCC) and pure car and truck carriers (PCTC) carry thousands of automobiles on a dozen or more decks; their capacity is measured in car-equivalent units (CEU). RoPax vessels combine vehicle decks with passenger accommodation and form the backbone of short-sea ferry routes. ConRo ships carry containers on deck above RoRo spaces below.

History

The concept dates to nineteenth-century train ferries, which carried railway wagons across rivers and seas. Purpose-built vehicle ferries expanded rapidly after the Second World War, and from the 1960s specialised ocean-going car carriers transformed the logistics of the global automobile industry, allowing manufacturers to export finished vehicles in bulk. The introduction of high-sided, fully enclosed carriers improved protection of the cargo and fuel efficiency.

Operations and trade

RoRo terminals are essentially large car parks: vehicles are driven aboard by teams of drivers and parked bumper-to-bumper in lanes, so a large carrier can be loaded or discharged in hours. The method suits cars, agricultural and construction machinery, and project cargo on mafi trailers, but the large enclosed vehicle decks are less space-efficient than container stowage, so RoRo is used mainly where speed and the self-propelled nature of the cargo matter.

Safety

The long, undivided vehicle decks of RoRo ships make them vulnerable to the free-surface effect if water enters, and several disasters — the capsizing of the Herald of Free Enterprise in 1987 after sailing with its bow doors open, and the loss of the ferry Estonia in 1994 — led to stricter rules on bow visors, watertight subdivision, stability monitoring and door indicators. Fires in vehicle decks, including those involving electric-vehicle batteries, remain an active safety concern.
