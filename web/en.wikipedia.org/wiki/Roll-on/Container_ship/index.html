Roll-on/roll-off

Roll-on/roll-off (RORO or ro-ro) ships are cargo ships designed to carry wheeled cargo, such as cars, motorcycles, trucks, semi-trailer trucks, buses, trailers, and railroad cars, that are driven on and off the ship on their own wheels or using a platform vehicle, such as a self-propelled modular transporter. This is in contrast to lift-on/lift-off (LoLo) vessels, which use a crane to load and unload cargo.

Design

RORO vessels have built-in or shore-based ramps or ferry slips that allow cargo to be efficiently rolled on and off the vessel when in port. Pure car carriers (PCC) and pure car and truck carriers (PCTC) are specialised RORO ships with box-like superstructures spanning the full length of the hull, carrying thousands of automobiles. Ro-pax ferries combine vehicle decks with passenger accommodation on short sea routes.

History and safety

The concept descends from nineteenth-century train ferries and from wartime landing craft. Safety of ro-ro ferries drew attention after disasters such as the capsizing of the Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) and the sinking of the Estonia (1994), which led to stricter rules on bow doors, free-surface effect, and stability standards.
