Containerisation

Containerisation is a system of intermodal freight transport using standardised shipping containers (ISO containers), which can be transferred between ships, trains and trucks without unloading the cargo itself. Developed in the 1950s and standardised in the 1960s, containerisation slashed the cost of shipping — loading a ship fell from several dollars per ton in loose cargo to a few cents per ton — and is widely credited with enabling the post-war boom in world trade and globalisation.

History

Early container systems were used on railways and by the US military (the CONEX box of the Korean War era). The breakthrough came from American trucking entrepreneur Malcom McLean: on 26 April 1956 his converted tanker Ideal X sailed from Newark, New Jersey to Houston carrying 58 metal containers. McLean's Sea-Land Service and Matson Navigation developed purpose-built container ships and port cranes, and in 1968–1970 the International Organization for Standardization fixed the standard sizes and corner fittings (ISO 668 and related standards), based largely on Sea-Land and Matson patents released for the standard.

The container

The standard unit is the TEU (twenty-foot equivalent unit); containers come mainly in 20-foot and 40-foot lengths, with 8-foot width and 8 ft 6 in (or "high cube" 9 ft 6 in) height. Corrugated steel boxes with twistlock corner castings can be stacked many high on ships that now carry over 24,000 TEU, and handled by ship-to-shore gantry cranes in minutes per box. Variants include refrigerated (reefer), tank, open-top and flat-rack containers.

Effects

Containerisation eliminated the break-bulk handling that had made ports labour-intensive, decimating traditional dockworker employment while concentrating traffic in a few deep-water hub ports. It underpinned just-in-time manufacturing and the integration of East Asian manufacturing with Western markets. Security and tracking systems, the BIC/ISO container numbering scheme and conventions such as the 1972 Container Safe Convention govern the global fleet of tens of millions of containers.
