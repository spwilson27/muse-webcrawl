Intermodal freight transport

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Intermodal freight transport involves the transportation of freight in an intermodal container or vehicle, using two or more modes of transport (rail, ship, aircraft, and truck), without any handling of the freight itself when changing modes. The method reduces cargo handling, and so improves security, reduces damage and loss, and allows freight to be transported faster.

Containerisation

The modern intermodal system grew from containerisation in the 1950s, pioneered by Malcom McLean's Sea-Land Service, whose first container ship sailed in 1956. Standardised ISO containers — principally 20-foot (TEU) and 40-foot (FEU) units — can be transferred between ships, trains, and trucks by gantry cranes in minutes. Roll-on/roll-off shipping is an intermodal variant in which the cargo itself is wheeled: trailers, truck-tractor combinations, and containers on chassis are driven aboard ferries and ro-ro vessels, common on short sea routes.

Infrastructure and operations

Intermodal transport depends on container ports, inland terminals, double-stack rail services, and freight corridors. Reduced handling lowers labour costs and theft while enabling seamless global supply chains; by volume, the great majority of international manufactured goods now move in containers at some point in their journey.
