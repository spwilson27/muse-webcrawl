Roll-on/roll-off (RORO or ro-ro) ships are cargo vessels designed to carry wheeled cargo — such as cars, motorcycles, trucks, semi-trailer trucks, buses, trailers, and railroad cars — that is driven on and off the ship on its own wheels or on platform vehicles. This contrasts with lift-on/lift-off (LoLo) vessels, which use cranes to load and unload.

Design

RORO vessels have built-in or shore-based ramps, often at the stern and sometimes via a bow visor, leading to multiple enclosed vehicle decks resembling large car parks. Pure car carriers (PCCs) and pure car and truck carriers (PCTCs) are specialised types that move new automobiles between manufacturing plants and markets worldwide; ROPAX vessels combine vehicle decks with passenger accommodation on ferry routes.

History and safety

The principle descends from 19th-century train ferries and was transformed by converted tank landing ships and purpose-built car carriers after the Second World War. Several high-profile ferry disasters — including the Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) and the Estonia (1994) — led to stricter rules on bow doors, subdivision, and stability for ro-ro passenger ferries.
