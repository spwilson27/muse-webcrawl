Intermodal container
Also called: shipping container, ISO container
Standard sizes: 20 ft and 40 ft (TEU/FEU)
Standard: ISO 668

An intermodal container, often called a shipping container, is a large standardised reusable steel box used to store and move products and raw materials between locations and countries. Designed to be transferred between different modes of transport — ship, rail and truck — without unloading and reloading the cargo, it is the basic unit of the containerisation system that underpins global trade.

Specifications

Containers are built to ISO standards, principally 20-foot (6.06 m) and 40-foot (12.19 m) lengths, 8 feet (2.44 m) wide and 8 ft 6 in (2.59 m) high, with "high cube" versions at 9 ft 6 in. Capacity is measured in twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU). Each corner has a cast steel fitting allowing the box to be lifted by cranes and locked together or to vehicles with twistlocks. Walls are corrugated weathering (Corten) steel; doors at one end are sealed and locked, and each unit carries a unique 11-character identifier administered by the Bureau International des Containers (BIC).

Types

Beyond the standard dry-freight "general purpose" box, specialised types include refrigerated containers (reefers) with integral cooling units, insulated and ventilated containers, tank containers for liquids, open-top and flat-rack units for oversized cargo, and half-height and bulk containers for dense or loose goods. Swap bodies are a related European road–rail unit that is not stackable to shipping standards.

Use and reuse

Around 90% of the world's non-bulk cargo moves by containerised ship at some point. Empty containers are repositioned at great cost along imbalanced trade routes. Retired containers are widely reused for storage, site offices and container architecture, including housing and retail developments, because of their strength, modularity and low cost. Handling equipment includes ship-to-shore gantry cranes, reach stackers, straddle carriers and container handlers at intermodal terminals.

History

The container was commercialised in 1956 by Malcom McLean's Sea-Land service, and international standardisation in the late 1960s allowed the system to spread worldwide, displacing break-bulk shipping and transforming ports and logistics.
