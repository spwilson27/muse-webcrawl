Cruiseferry

A cruiseferry is a ship that combines the features of a cruise ship with those of a roll-on/roll-off (ro-pax) ferry: it carries passengers, their cars and freight vehicles on regular scheduled routes, while offering cruise-like amenities such as multiple restaurants, bars, nightclubs, shops, cinemas and conference facilities. The concept is most highly developed in Northern Europe, above all in the Baltic Sea, where large cruiseferries connect Finland, Sweden, Estonia, Åland and Germany.

Development

Cruiseferries grew out of the luxurious Baltic ferry services of the late 1950s and 1960s, when Viking Line and Silja Line competed on the Finland–Sweden routes with ever grander ships. In 1980–1981 Silja's Finlandia and Silvia Regina set a new standard in size and comfort; the race culminated in ships such as Mariella (1985), Symphony and Serenade (1990–1991) with central promenade decks modelled on cruise ships, and later vessels including Color Fantasy (2004) on the Oslo–Kiel route, among the largest cruiseferries in the world. Duty- and tax-free sales — long preserved by Åland's special status in the EU — underpinned the onboard shopping and entertainment economy.

Design and operation

Typical cruiseferries carry 2,000–3,000 passengers and 400–1,000 cars or equivalent lane metres of freight. They operate overnight or short crossings on fixed timetables, loading vehicles through bow and stern doors or side ramps. Amenities resemble those of cruise ships but voyages are point-to-point transport rather than round trips, though mini-cruises are heavily marketed. Similar though generally less elaborate ro-pax ferries operate in the Mediterranean, the North Sea, Japan and North America.

Safety

The ro-ro design's vulnerability to flooding on the vehicle deck drove major safety reforms after disasters such as Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) and Estonia (1994), the latter a cruiseferry lost in the Baltic with 852 lives, leading to the Stockholm Agreement on ro-ro stability standards.
