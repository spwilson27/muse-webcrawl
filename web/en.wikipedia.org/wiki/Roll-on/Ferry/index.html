Ferry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ferry is a vessel used to transport passengers and, in many cases, their vehicles and cargo across bodies of water on regular scheduled services. Roll-on/roll-off ferries (ropax vessels when they combine passenger accommodation with vehicle decks) allow cars, coaches, and freight trucks to drive aboard through bow or stern doors and visors, making them essential links in road networks separated by seas, straits, lakes, and rivers.

Types

Ferries range from small foot-passenger boats and cable ferries to large cruiseferries with cabins, restaurants, and shops operating overnight routes. Double-ended ferries can shuttle back and forth without turning, while catamarans and hydrofoils provide high-speed services. Train ferries carry railway carriages on rails laid on their decks.

Operations and safety

Major ferry networks operate in the Baltic Sea, the Mediterranean, the English Channel, Japan, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific Northwest, where the Washington State Ferries and BC Ferries systems are among the world's largest. Roll-on/roll-off ferries have distinctive safety concerns: open vehicle decks can be vulnerable to flooding, as shown by disasters such as the Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) and the Estonia (1994), which led to stricter regulations on bow doors, subdivision, and stability.
