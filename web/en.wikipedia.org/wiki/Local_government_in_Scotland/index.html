Local government in Scotland

Local government in Scotland comprises thirty-two unitary local authorities responsible for providing a range of public services to the people of Scotland. Scotland's local government system is one of the oldest in the world, with a history stretching back to the burghs of the medieval period.

Scotland's 32 council areas, in place since 1996.

History

Scotland's first local authorities were the burghs, which were established from the 12th century. From 1890, counties were administered by county councils. The Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973 introduced a two-tier system of regions and districts (along with island councils), which lasted from 1975 until 1996. The Local Government etc. (Scotland) Act 1994 replaced it with the current single-tier system of 32 council areas.

Structure

Each council area is governed by a council made up of councillors elected every five years under the single transferable vote form of proportional representation, as introduced by the Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004. The council areas vary greatly in size and population, from Glasgow (population around 635,000) to Orkney (around 22,000).

Councils are responsible for services including education, social work, housing, roads, planning, environmental protection, waste collection, libraries, and trading standards. Some services such as police and fire are provided nationally through Police Scotland and the Scottish Fire and Rescue Service.

Finance

Local government in Scotland is funded partly by the Scottish Government block grant and partly by locally raised taxes, principally the Council Tax (a property tax frozen or capped for much of the period since 2007) and non-domestic rates. COSLA (the Convention of Scottish Local Authorities) represents the collective voice of local government in dealings with the Scottish Government.

Community councils

Below the council areas, there are around 1,200 community councils, which have a statutory right to be consulted on planning and licensing matters but have few formal powers.
