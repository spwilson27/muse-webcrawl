Bandsaw

A vertical bandsaw in a woodworking shop.

A bandsaw (also written band saw) is a power saw with a long, sharp blade consisting of a continuous band of toothed metal stretched between two or more wheels. The blade moves in one direction only, giving a continuous cutting action. Bandsaws are used principally in woodworking, metalworking, and lumbering, but can cut a variety of materials; they are especially valued for cutting irregular shapes and curves, and for resawing thick stock into thinner boards.

Types

Vertical bandsaws are the most common in workshops: the blade runs vertically through a table on which the workpiece rests, and the operator feeds the work into the blade by hand. Horizontal bandsaws pivot or lower the blade through stationary work and are the standard machine for cutting off metal stock to length in industry; portable handheld versions are widely used by plumbers and electricians. Large head rigs in sawmills are industrial bandsaws used to break down logs.

Blades

Blade performance depends on width, thickness, tooth pitch (teeth per inch) and tooth form. Narrow blades cut tight curves; wide blades resaw and rip. Bi-metal blades, with high-speed steel teeth electron-beam welded to a flexible alloy back, dominate metal cutting, while carbide-tipped and diamond-grit blades serve harder materials.

History

The bandsaw was patented in England in 1809 by William Newberry, but it did not become practical until reliable methods of welding a blade into a continuous loop were developed in France around the 1840s, notably by Anne Paulin Crepin. Bandsaws were soon adopted in furniture making and later transformed the sawmill industry.
