Disease vector

In epidemiology, a disease vector is any living agent that carries and transmits an infectious pathogen such as a bacterium or virus to another living organism. Disease vectors can be considered a type of disease agent. The most common vectors are blood-feeding arthropods such as mosquitoes, ticks, and fleas, though other animals and even plants can serve as vectors for some pathogens.

Types of transmission

Vector transmission is commonly divided into two main types. In mechanical transmission, the vector carries the pathogen on or in its body without the pathogen undergoing any developmental change — for example, flies transferring bacteria to food. In biological transmission, the pathogen develops or multiplies within the vector before being transmitted; the malaria parasite Plasmodium, which undergoes part of its life cycle inside Anopheles mosquitoes, is a classic example.

Common vectors and diseases

  • Mosquitoes — malaria, dengue fever, Zika fever, yellow fever, West Nile fever, chikungunya, lymphatic filariasis;
  • Ticks — Lyme disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, tick-borne encephalitis, babesiosis;
  • Sandflies — leishmaniasis;
  • Tsetse flies — African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness);
  • Fleas — plague, murine typhus;
  • Triatomine bugs — Chagas disease;
  • Freshwater snails — schistosomiasis (as intermediate hosts).

Vector control

Vector control is a central element of public health strategies against vector-borne diseases, which account for more than 17% of all infectious diseases and cause hundreds of thousands of deaths annually according to the World Health Organization. Methods include insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, elimination of larval breeding sites, biological control agents, and newer approaches such as releasing mosquitoes infected with Wolbachia bacteria or genetically modified sterile males. Climate change and global travel are expanding the geographic ranges of many vectors, bringing diseases such as dengue into previously unaffected regions.
