The Battle of Tewkesbury, fought on 4 May 1471, was one of the decisive battles of the Wars of the Roses. Edward IV's Yorkist army intercepted and destroyed the Lancastrian army of Queen Margaret of Anjou and her son Edward of Westminster, Prince of Wales, who was killed in the battle or its immediate aftermath — the only Prince of Wales ever to die in battle. With the Lancastrian field army annihilated and Henry VI (murdered in the Tower days later) extinguished, Edward IV's throne was secure for the rest of his reign.

Background

Restored briefly to the throne in 1470 by the Earl of Warwick, Henry VI's cause collapsed when Edward IV returned from exile, and Warwick was killed at Barnet on 14 April 1471. That same day Margaret landed at Weymouth with her son and a French-backed army. Marching to join forces in Wales and the north-west, she was refused entry to Gloucester and its Severn crossing, and was forced on to Tewkesbury, where her exhausted army — pursued relentlessly by Edward — turned to fight with the rivers Severn and Avon at its back.

The battle

The Lancastrians took a strong position in the hedged enclosures south of the town, Edmund Beaufort, Duke of Somerset, commanding the right. Yorkist artillery and archery provoked Somerset into attacking Edward's left; he drove down into a lane but found himself unsupported — the Earl of Devon's and Prince Edward's divisions did not advance (whether by misjudgement or, as Yorkist writers claimed, treachery). Edward's men counter-attacked, while a hidden detachment of 200 spearmen, posted in a wood on the Lancastrian flank, fell on Somerset's rear. The Lancastrian right collapsed into the "Bloody Meadow"; the whole army broke, and many drowned or were cut down at the mills and river crossings. Prince Edward was killed — in the rout, or, in hostile accounts, after capture. Somerset and other leaders who took sanctuary in Tewkesbury Abbey were dragged out and executed two days later; Margaret was captured nearby.

Aftermath

Henry VI died in the Tower of London on the night of 21 May, officially "of pure displeasure and melancholy." The direct Lancastrian line was extinct, and England enjoyed a decade of stable Yorkist rule until Edward IV's death in 1483 reopened the succession crisis that ended at Bosworth.
