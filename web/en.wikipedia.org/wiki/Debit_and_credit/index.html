Debit and credit

Debits and credits are the two sides of every entry in double-entry bookkeeping, the standard system of accounting in use since at least the fifteenth century. Every financial transaction is recorded in at least two accounts, with total debits equal to total credits, so that the accounting equation — assets = liabilities + equity — always balances.

Meaning of the terms

From the Latin debere ("to owe") and credere ("to entrust"), a debit (abbreviated Dr) is conventionally recorded on the left of an account and a credit (Cr) on the right. Whether a debit increases or decreases an account depends on the account type: debits increase assets and expenses and decrease liabilities, equity and revenue; credits do the opposite. Thus receiving cash increases (debits) the cash asset account, while earning revenue increases (credits) the revenue account. A common mnemonic in English-speaking teaching is that the effect is determined by the category of account rather than by "good" or "bad" movements — a frequent source of confusion, since in everyday banking language a "credit" to one's bank account increases it (because, from the bank's perspective, the deposit is a liability owed to the customer).

History

The system was codified by Luca Pacioli in his Summa de arithmetica of 1494, which described the Venetian method of merchants, including journals, ledgers and the rule that no debit exists without a corresponding credit.
