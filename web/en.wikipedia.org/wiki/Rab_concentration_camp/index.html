The Rab concentration camp (Italian: campo di concentramento di Arbe) was an Italian concentration camp established during the Second World War on the island of Rab in the Adriatic, near the village of Kampor. It was one of the largest and most notorious of the concentration camps run by Fascist Italy in the annexed and occupied territories of Yugoslavia.

History

The camp was opened in July 1942 as part of the Italian policy of repression and Italianisation in the Province of Ljubljana and the Dalmatian coast, spearheaded by General Mario Roatta's notorious "Circolare 3C", which ordered mass internment of the Slovene and Croat civilian population suspected of supporting the partisan resistance. Internees — men, women and children — were held in tents and later rudimentary barracks, suffering from severe overcrowding, malnutrition, disease and exposure, especially during the winter of 1942–43.

A separate section of the camp held Jewish internees, who were paradoxically afforded somewhat better conditions because the Italian authorities refused German and Ustaša demands to hand them over for extermination.

After the Italian armistice of September 1943, the surviving inmates were liberated by Yugoslav partisans; many of the Jewish internees joined or were evacuated by the resistance, and a Rab battalion was formed from former prisoners. Estimates of the death toll vary: at least 1,000 to 1,500 documented deaths, with Slovene and Croatian sources often citing higher figures, around 3,500 or more.

Memorial

A memorial cemetery and park designed by Slovenian architect Edvard Ravnikar was built at the site in 1953. The camp is remembered in Slovenia and Croatia as a symbol of Italian fascist occupation crimes, which received far less attention after the war than German atrocities, partly because Italy did not prosecute its own war criminals.
