AB language

The AB dialect is preserved in manuscripts from religious houses for women in the West Midlands.

The AB language is the name given by the philologist J. R. R. Tolkien to a remarkably consistent written dialect of early Middle English found in two groups of texts from the West Midlands of England, written in the first half of the 13th century. The term comes from the two principal manuscript witnesses: "A", the Corpus Christi College, Cambridge manuscript of the Ancrene Wisse (MS 402), and "B", the Bodleian Library manuscript (MS Bodley 34) containing the lives of saints Katherine, Margaret and Juliana, together with Hali Meiðhad and Sawles Warde.

Characteristics

Tolkien's 1929 essay "Ancrene Wisse and Hali Meiðhad" demonstrated that the two manuscripts shared not merely a dialect but a disciplined scribal spelling system — a "standard language" of the early Middle English period, based on the spoken West Midland dialect but preserved with unusual regularity. Features of the AB language include the retention of Old English rounded vowels (written eo and u), distinctive verb endings such as the plural present -eð, the pronoun forms ha (she/they) and hare (her/their), and a rich vocabulary of native words later lost from standard English, including the intimate second-person forms used in devotional address.

Significance

The AB language matters because it shows that, contrary to the usual picture of chaotic Middle English spelling after the Norman Conquest, a regional literary standard could be maintained by a network of scribes — probably linked to Augustinian houses in the Welsh Marches — over decades. The texts themselves were composed for enclosed religious women (anchoresses), making the AB corpus one of the most important bodies of writing for women readers in medieval England. Scholars including E. J. Dobson and Bella Millett have refined Tolkien's account, debating the precise localisation of the dialect and the degree to which it represents a spoken variety rather than a purely written convention.
