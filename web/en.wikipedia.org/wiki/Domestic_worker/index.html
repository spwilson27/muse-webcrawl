Domestic worker

A domestic worker is a person who works within the scope of a residence. The term "domestic service" applies to the equivalent occupational category. In traditional British English, such a person was called a "domestic servant." Domestic workers perform a variety of household services for an individual, family or household, including cleaning, cooking, laundry, childcare (as a nanny), elder care, gardening and household maintenance.

Scale and conditions

The International Labour Organization (ILO) estimates that there are at least 75 million domestic workers worldwide, the large majority of them women, and many of them migrants. Domestic work is among the occupations least protected by labour law: in many countries domestic workers are excluded in whole or part from minimum wage laws, working-time limits, social security and the right to unionize. Live-in workers are particularly vulnerable to excessive hours, withheld wages and abuse, and migrant domestic workers in some Gulf states work under the kafala sponsorship system.

Legal protections

In 2011 the ILO adopted the Domestic Workers Convention (Convention No. 189), the first international standard recognizing domestic workers' rights to decent work, including rest days, minimum wage coverage and protection from abuse. Dozens of countries have since ratified it or reformed national laws. In the United States, domestic workers were excluded from the National Labor Relations Act and originally from the Fair Labor Standards Act, though several states have enacted a "Domestic Workers' Bill of Rights," beginning with New York in 2010.
