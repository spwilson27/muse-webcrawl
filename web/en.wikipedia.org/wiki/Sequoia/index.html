Sequoia

Sequoia

Kingdom: Plantae
Division: Pinophyta
Class: Pinopsida
Order: Pinales
Family: Cupressaceae
Subfamily: Sequoioideae
Genus: Sequoia Endl.
Living species: Sequoia sempervirens

Sequoia is a genus of redwood coniferous trees in the subfamily Sequoioideae of the cypress family Cupressaceae. The only extant species of the genus is Sequoia sempervirens, the coast redwood, native to the coastal forests of northern California and southwestern Oregon. The name Sequoia is also commonly used as a general term for the whole redwood subfamily, which includes the giant sequoia (Sequoiadendron giganteum) of California's Sierra Nevada and the dawn redwood (Metasequoia glyptostroboides) of China. Coast redwoods include the tallest living trees on Earth, exceeding 115 metres (379 ft) in height.

Taxonomy and naming

The genus was described by the Austrian botanist Stephan Endlicher in 1847. The origin of the name is disputed: it is traditionally said to honor Sequoyah (c. 1770–1843), the Cherokee silversmith who created the Cherokee syllabary, though some scholars argue Endlicher, who also worked on linguistics, derived the name from the Latin sequor ("I follow"), in reference to the number of seeds per cone following a pattern in related genera. Fossil species assigned to Sequoia show that the genus was once widespread across the Northern Hemisphere during the Mesozoic and Cenozoic eras.

The living species

Sequoia sempervirens is an evergreen, long-lived tree, commonly living 500–700 years and occasionally more than 2,000. It has fibrous, reddish-brown bark up to 30 cm thick that resists fire and insects, small scale-like or needle-like leaves, and small woody cones. The tallest known living specimen, named Hyperion and discovered in Redwood National Park in 2006, stands about 115.9 metres (380 ft) tall. Coast redwoods reproduce both by seed and by sprouting from the root crown or trunk, so many trees are clonal offshoots of a parent stump.

Distribution and conservation

The natural range is a narrow coastal strip roughly 750 km long, where winter rains and summer fog supply the moisture the trees require. Logging from the nineteenth century onward removed the great majority of the old-growth forest — an estimated 95 percent or more — before conservation efforts, led in part by the Save the Redwoods League (founded 1918), protected remaining groves in state and national parks, including Redwood National and State Parks, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Redwoods are widely planted as ornamentals and for timber elsewhere in the world. Climate change, particularly declining fog and increasing drought stress, is a focus of current research on the species' future.

Related redwoods

The giant sequoia, Sequoiadendron giganteum, grows only in groves on the western slopes of the Sierra Nevada and includes the General Sherman Tree, the largest known living single-stem tree by volume. The dawn redwood, Metasequoia glyptostroboides, was described from fossils in 1941 and discovered alive in China a few years later; unlike the other redwoods it is deciduous. Together the three genera are the surviving members of a once much more diverse and widespread subfamily.
