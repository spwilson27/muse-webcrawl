A clerkship is a period of clinical training in medical education during which medical students work directly with patients in hospitals and clinics under the supervision of attending physicians and residents. In the United States, clerkships constitute the core of the third and fourth years of medical school, following the pre-clerkship (classroom and laboratory) phase. In the United Kingdom and Commonwealth countries, equivalent rotations are often called clinical attachments or firms.

Core clerkships

Third-year students typically rotate through the major specialties:

  • Internal medicine
  • General surgery
  • Pediatrics
  • Obstetrics and gynecology
  • Psychiatry
  • Family medicine
  • Neurology (at many schools)

Each rotation lasts roughly four to twelve weeks. Students take patient histories, perform physical examinations, write notes, present cases on rounds, assist in procedures and operations, and begin to develop clinical reasoning. Fourth year is largely devoted to electives and "sub-internships" (acting internships) in the student's chosen specialty, along with residency interviews.

Assessment

Clerkship performance typically combines supervisor evaluations with standardized examinations — in the U.S., the NBME subject ("shelf") examinations taken at the end of each rotation. Objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs) assess practical skills with standardized patients. Clerkship grades are an important component of residency applications, along with USMLE Step 2 CK scores.

Role in training

The clerkship phase marks the transition from student to clinician: students join care teams, take graduated responsibility for patients, and choose their specialty based on their rotation experiences. The intensity and apprenticeship character of clerkships, including call nights and long hours, are a defining feature of medical training worldwide.
