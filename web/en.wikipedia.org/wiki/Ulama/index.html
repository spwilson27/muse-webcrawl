Ulama

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ulama (also ulema; singular ʿālim) are the scholars of Islamic law and theology — the jurists, judges, teachers and muftis who preserve, interpret and transmit the religious sciences in Muslim societies. Educated traditionally in madrasas in Quran, Hadith, jurisprudence and Arabic, and licensed by teachers through the ijaza system, the ulama historically formed a decentralised clerisy without a church hierarchy in Sunni Islam: their authority rested on learning and reputation, and their institutions (endowed mosques and colleges, the qadi courts) gave them a corporate presence partly independent of rulers.

In Twelver Shi'ism the ulama developed a graded clerical hierarchy (culminating in the marja' al-taqlid, sources of emulation, and titles such as Ayatollah), and in the Islamic Republic of Iran the doctrine of velayat-e faqih (guardianship of the jurist) placed a senior jurist at the head of the state. In the modern period the ulama's monopoly on religious authority has been challenged by state educational systems, codified law, new media preachers and lay Islamist movements, yet institutions such as al-Azhar in Cairo and the seminaries of Qom and Najaf remain influential.
