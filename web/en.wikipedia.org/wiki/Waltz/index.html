Waltz

The waltz (from German Walzer) is a ballroom and folk dance, normally in triple time (3/4), performed primarily in closed position by a couple who turn continuously around the room. It is also the name of the musical form that accompanies it, characterised by a strong first beat and two lighter beats — often described as "oom-pah-pah" — in the bass.

A couple performing the Viennese waltz

History

The waltz developed in the late 18th century from Austrian and German peasant dances such as the Ländler, danced in the suburbs of Vienna. Its close hold and rapid turning were considered scandalous when it first reached fashionable ballrooms around 1800, but it soon conquered Europe and America. In Vienna, composers Joseph Lanner and Johann Strauss I, and above all Johann Strauss II, the "Waltz King", made the Viennese waltz world-famous with pieces such as The Blue Danube (1866). Waltzes also entered the concert repertoire through Chopin, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, and Ravel, whose La valse (1920) comments on the form's decline.

Styles

The faster original form is the Viennese waltz; the slower version codified in England became the modern (international standard) waltz of competitive ballroom dancing. American style waltz allows open figures and separation. Related dances include the hesitation waltz, the French valse musette, and the Scandinavian gammaldans.
