Sabellianism

In Christian theology, Sabellianism is the belief that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are not three distinct persons but different modes or aspects of the one God — a doctrine also known as modalistic monarchianism or simply modalism. It is named after Sabellius, a third-century priest and theologian who taught in Rome in the early 200s AD.

According to modalism, God is a single person who manifests himself in different modes at different times — as the Father in creation and lawgiving, as the Son in redemption, and as the Holy Spirit in sanctification — rather than three co-eternal persons. The doctrine was condemned as heresy by the early church, since it implied, among other things, that the Father himself suffered on the cross (a consequence its critics called Patripassianism).

History

Ideas resembling modalism were taught earlier by Noetus of Smyrna and Praxeas, whom Tertullian attacked in his treatise Against Praxeas, coining the formula "one substance, three persons" in response. Sabellius, teaching in Rome around 215 AD, gave the doctrine its most systematic early form and was excommunicated by Pope Callixtus I. The controversy continued in the East, where modalist language about the Son influenced later Trinitarian debates resolved at the Council of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381).

Modern usage

The term "modalism" is applied today to Oneness Pentecostal theology, which likewise rejects the traditional doctrine of the Trinity in favour of one God manifesting in different modes and baptises in the name of Jesus only. Mainstream Trinitarian churches regard Sabellianism as a denial of the personal distinctions affirmed in the Nicene Creed.
