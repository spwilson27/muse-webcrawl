Contrapposto (Italian: [kontrapˈposto]) is an Italian term used in the visual arts to describe a human figure standing with most of its weight on one foot so that its shoulders and arms twist off-axis from the hips and legs, giving the figure a more dynamic or relaxed appearance.

History

Contrapposto first appeared in early Classical Greek sculpture around 480 BC, replacing the rigid frontal pose of Archaic kouroi. The Kritios Boy (c. 480 BC) is one of the earliest surviving examples.

Ancient Greece

Polykleitos' Doryphoros (Spear Bearer, c. 440 BC) codified the pose in his treatise the Canon: the weight-bearing leg tenses while the free leg relaxes, with shoulders counter-tilting against the hips.

Renaissance

Renaissance artists such as Donatello and Michelangelo revived contrapposto; Michelangelo's David (1501–1504) is its most famous expression.
