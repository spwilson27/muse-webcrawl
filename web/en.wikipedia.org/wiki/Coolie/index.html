Coolie

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coolie is a term historically applied to manual labourers from Asia, particularly from China and India, who worked in European colonies during the 19th and early 20th centuries, often under indentured contracts. The word is now widely regarded as derogatory and offensive when applied to people of Asian descent.

Etymology

The term entered English in the 17th century, probably from the Hindi qulī or Urdu qulī meaning a hired labourer or slave, possibly influenced by a similar Tamil word kūli meaning wages. It was also folk-associated with the Koli people of Gujarat. Chinese usage rendered the term as 苦力 (kǔlì), literally "bitter labour".

History

After the abolition of slavery in the British Empire in the 1830s, plantation owners in the Caribbean, Mauritius, Fiji, Malaya and elsewhere turned to indentured labour from India and China. The "coolie trade" shipped hundreds of thousands of workers across the oceans; conditions on the voyages and plantations were often brutal and the system was condemned by critics as "a new system of slavery". Chinese labourers also built railways in North America and worked guano islands off Peru, while Indian indentured labourers reshaped the demographics of Mauritius, Guyana, Trinidad, Suriname, Natal and Fiji.

Abuses led to regulation and eventually abolition of indenture in the early 20th century; the last indenture systems ended around 1917–1920.

Legacy

Descendants of indentured labourers form substantial communities in many countries, and their culture — food, language varieties such as Fiji Hindi, and festivals — is a lasting legacy. In modern usage, outside historical contexts, the word is considered an ethnic slur, though in parts of South Asia related words for porterage may still be used descriptively.
