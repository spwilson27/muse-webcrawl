Therigatha

The Therīgāthā ("Verses of the Elder Nuns"), often translated as Poems of Early Buddhist Nuns, is a Buddhist text of the Pali Canon, part of the Khuddaka Nikāya of the Sutta Piṭaka. It consists of 73 poems in 494 verses (in the usual count) attributed to the therīs, the elder bhikkhunīs (fully ordained nuns) of the early Buddhist community, many of them contemporaries of the Buddha. Together with its companion volume for monks, the Theragāthā, it is among the earliest collections of religious poetry in India and one of the earliest bodies of literature in the world attributed to named women.

Contents

The poems are arranged by length, from single verses to long narrative pieces of over seventy verses. Their authors include Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī, the Buddha's aunt and foster mother and first bhikkhunī; Khemā and Uppalavaṇṇā, the Buddha's two chief female disciples; Paṭācārā, driven nearly mad by the deaths of her family, whose verses celebrate her recovery of peace; Kisāgotamī, who came to the Buddha carrying her dead child; and Ambapālī, the former courtesan of Vesālī, whose famous verses contrast the beauty of her youth with her aged body. Common themes are the griefs of women's lives — bereavement, unwanted marriage, the loss of beauty, the deaths of children — and the liberation found in renunciation and insight: many poems end with the refrain of having become "cooled" and free.

History and significance

The verses were transmitted orally for centuries and written down with the rest of the Pali Canon in Sri Lanka in the first century BCE; the fifth-century commentator Dhammapāla supplied prose stories of the nuns' past lives. The text was among the first Pali works published by the Pali Text Society (1883, edited by Richard Morris), and was translated into English by Caroline Rhys Davids (1909, as Psalms of the Early Buddhists — Psalms of the Sisters), with later translations by K. R. Norman (1971) and Charles Hallisey (2015). Scholars value the Therīgāthā as unique testimony to women's religious experience in ancient India and to the early bhikkhunī order, whose full ordination lineage later died out in the Theravada world and has been controversially revived in modern times.
