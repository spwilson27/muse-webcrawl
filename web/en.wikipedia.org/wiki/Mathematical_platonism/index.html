Mathematical platonism

Mathematical platonism is a form of realism in the philosophy of mathematics that holds that mathematical entities — numbers, sets, functions, geometric shapes — exist independently of human minds, languages, and practices, in an abstract realm outside space and time. On this view, mathematical truths are discovered rather than invented, and mathematical statements have definite truth values in virtue of the properties of these abstract objects.

Historical background

The view is named after Plato, whose theory of Forms held that the objects of knowledge are eternal, unchanging archetypes apprehended by reason rather than the senses. In modern philosophy of mathematics, platonism was defended by Gottlob Frege and, in the twentieth century, by Kurt Gödel, who argued that the axioms of set theory force themselves upon us as true descriptions of an independent reality. W. V. O. Quine and Hilary Putnam defended a related position through the indispensability argument: since mathematics is indispensable to our best scientific theories, we should be ontologically committed to mathematical entities just as we are to physical ones.

Arguments and objections

The most famous objection is Paul Benacerraf's epistemological challenge: if mathematical objects are abstract and causally inert, how can human beings know anything about them? Benacerraf also posed an identification problem, noting that numbers can be reduced to sets in multiple incompatible ways. Platonists respond with accounts of mathematical intuition, holistic confirmation in science, or by rejecting causal theories of knowledge. Other objections concern the ontological parsimony claimed by nominalists, who seek to paraphrase mathematics without commitment to abstract objects (for example, Hartry Field's program in Science Without Numbers).

Variants

"Full-blooded platonism," defended by Mark Balaguer, holds that every mathematically consistent object exists. Set-theoretic realism, object realism, and plenitudinous platonism are other variants; platonism contrasts with formalism, intuitionism, structuralism, and fictionalism.
