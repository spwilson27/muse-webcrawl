Sacred fig

Sacred fig (Ficus religiosa)
FamilyMoraceae
Native rangeIndian subcontinent and Indochina
Common namesPeepal, bodhi tree, ashwattha
Notable specimenJaya Sri Maha Bodhi, Anuradhapura (planted 288 BC)

The sacred fig (Ficus religiosa) is a species of fig tree native to the Indian subcontinent and Indochina. It is sacred in Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism: according to Buddhist tradition, Siddhartha Gautama attained enlightenment while meditating beneath a tree of this species at Bodh Gaya, which has given it the alternative name bodhi tree.

The sacred fig's leaves have a long, tapering drip tip.

Description

The sacred fig is a large, fast-growing deciduous or semi-evergreen tree reaching up to 30 metres, often beginning life as an epiphyte. Its leaves are distinctive: broadly heart-shaped with a long drawn-out tip ("drip tip") on a slender stalk, so that they tremble in the slightest breeze. The small figs ripen from green to purple-black and are eaten by birds and bats.

Religious significance

In Hinduism the tree (ashvattha) is associated with the Trimurti and is worshipped, especially on Saturdays; its form is described in the Bhagavad Gita as an image of the cosmos. Buddhists venerate the Bodhi Tree at Bodh Gaya and its descendants, including the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi in Sri Lanka, planted in 288 BC and among the oldest living human-planted trees with a recorded planting date. In Jainism the tree is connected with the enlightenment of tirthankaras.
