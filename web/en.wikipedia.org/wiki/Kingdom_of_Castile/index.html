The Kingdom of Castile (Spanish: Reino de Castilla) was a large and powerful state on the Iberian Peninsula during the Middle Ages. Its name comes from the host of castles constructed in the region. It began in the 9th century as the eastern frontier county of the Kingdom of León and became the dominant Christian power of the Reconquista, ultimately forming, with the Crown of Aragon, the basis of modern Spain.

Origins

Castile emerged as a frontier county (the county of Castile, founded by Rodrigo c. 850) defending León against Muslim raids. Count Fernán González made it effectively hereditary and autonomous in the 10th century. In 1037 Ferdinand I of León acquired Castile and raised it to a kingdom; on his death in 1065 Castile passed as a separate kingdom to his son Sancho II.

Union with León and expansion

After periods of separation and civil war, Ferdinand III definitively united Castile and León in 1230. Under Ferdinand III and Alfonso X "the Wise", Castile conquered Córdoba (1236), Seville (1248), and most of al-Andalus except the Emirate of Granada, and developed a major legal and literary culture, including the Siete Partidas law code and the promotion of Castilian over Latin.

Late Middle Ages

The 14th and 15th centuries saw the Trastámara dynasty, civil wars between Peter the Cruel and Henry II, powerful nobles, and the growth of the Cortes and the wool trade of the Mesta. Isabella I of Castile's marriage to Ferdinand II of Aragon (1469) united the two crowns dynastically; their reign completed the conquest of Granada (1492) and sponsored Columbus's voyage, founding the Spanish Empire in the Americas. Castile, the more populous and wealthy partner, provided most of the empire's settlers and administration, and its language became Spanish.

The Crown of Castile persisted as a constituent of the Spanish monarchy until the territorial reorganization of 1833 dissolved the old kingdoms into provinces.
