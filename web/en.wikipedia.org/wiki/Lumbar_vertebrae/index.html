Lumbar vertebrae

The lumbar vertebrae are the five vertebrae between the rib cage and the pelvis, designated L1 to L5. They are the largest segments of the vertebral column, adapted to bear the weight of the upper body, and are characterised by the absence of the transverse foramen (found in cervical vertebrae) and of facets on the sides of the body (found in thoracic vertebrae).

Characteristics

Lumbar vertebrae have massive, kidney-shaped bodies; short, thick spinous processes that project straight backwards; and long, slender transverse processes. The fifth lumbar vertebra (L5) is wedge-shaped, contributing to the lumbosacral angle where the spine meets the sacrum. Together the lumbar vertebrae form the inward curve of the lower back known as lumbar lordosis.

Clinical significance

The lumbar region is the most common site of back pain. Disorders include disc herniation ("slipped disc"), spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis and compression fractures, especially with osteoporosis. Lumbar puncture (spinal tap) is performed between L3/L4 or L4/L5, below the end of the spinal cord. Transitional vertebrae — sacralisation of L5 or lumbarisation of S1 — occur in a minority of people and are usually harmless variants.

In other animals

Most mammals have seven cervical vertebrae but the number of lumbar vertebrae varies; humans normally have five, though four or six occur.
