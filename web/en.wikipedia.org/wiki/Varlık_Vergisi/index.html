The Varlık Vergisi ("wealth tax" or "capital tax") was a Turkish tax levied in 1942–44, ostensibly to raise revenue and curb wartime profiteering during the Second World War, in which Turkey remained neutral for most of the conflict. In practice it was assessed in a sharply discriminatory manner against the country's non-Muslim minorities — Greeks, Armenians and Jews — and is widely regarded as an instrument for transferring wealth to Muslim Turks and building a "national" bourgeoisie.

Implementation

The law was passed on 11 November 1942. Local commissions assessed liabilities arbitrarily, with no right of appeal, and classified taxpayers by coded categories: M for Muslims, G for non-Muslims (gayrimüslim), D for dönme (converts from Judaism), and E for foreigners. Non-Muslims were assessed at many times the rate applied to Muslims with equivalent wealth — frequently more than their entire assets — and payment was demanded within fifteen days, with a short grace period at punitive interest.

Consequences

Those unable to pay were forced to sell businesses and property, often at fire-sale prices to Muslim buyers, and about 1,400 defaulters — almost all non-Muslims — were deported to labour camps at Aşkale in eastern Anatolia, where they broke stones and cleared snow; some died there. In total the tax collected roughly 324 million Turkish lira, the great majority from non-Muslims, although they formed a small minority of the population. Under Allied diplomatic pressure and criticism abroad, the government released the Aşkale deportees in late 1943 and repealed the law on 15 March 1944, remitting uncollected assessments; the sums already taken were never refunded.

Legacy

The Varlık Vergisi devastated Istanbul's remaining Greek, Armenian and Jewish commercial classes, accelerated minority emigration, and remains a central episode in debates about the Turkification of the economy and minority rights in the early Turkish Republic. It has been the subject of historical studies and of the novel and film adaptations of Yılmaz Karakoyunlu's Salkım Hanım'ın Taneleri ("Mrs. Salkım's Diamonds").
