Fauna of Madagascar

The fauna of Madagascar is among the most distinctive on Earth. The island split from Africa roughly 160 million years ago and from India about 88 million years ago, and its wildlife has evolved in isolation ever since. Around 90 percent of Madagascar's species are found nowhere else, and many belong to lineages that are extinct or absent on the African mainland.

Mammals

All of Madagascar's native terrestrial mammals appear to descend from a small number of colonisation events in which ancestors crossed from Africa, probably by rafting on vegetation. The most famous are the lemurs, a radiation of more than 100 primate species — from the tiny mouse lemurs, the world's smallest primates, to the indri — all endemic to the island. Other endemic groups include the tenrecs, a diverse family filling niches from hedgehog-like to otter-like forms; the euplerid carnivorans such as the cat-like fossa, the island's largest predator; native rodents; and a rich bat fauna, the one group able to arrive by flight.

Birds, reptiles, and other groups

About 280 bird species are recorded, over half of them endemic, including entire endemic families such as the mesites, ground rollers, and asities. Madagascar is a world centre for chameleons, with roughly half of all known species, and also harbours day geckos, radiated and ploughshare tortoises, and boas — a group otherwise associated with South America. Frogs number in the hundreds of species, almost all endemic; remarkably, the island has no native toads. Freshwater fish are highly endemic and highly threatened, while the invertebrate fauna includes the comet moth and giraffe weevil.

Extinction and conservation

Humans arrived within roughly the last two thousand years, and a megafauna of giant lemurs, elephant birds — the largest birds known to have lived — pygmy hippopotamuses, and giant tortoises vanished, probably through hunting and habitat change. Madagascar is classified as a biodiversity hotspot, yet most of its original forest has been cleared, mainly for shifting cultivation, and a large fraction of its lemurs are now threatened with extinction. National parks such as Ranomafana, Andasibe-Mantadia, and Tsingy de Bemaraha protect some of the richest remaining habitats.
