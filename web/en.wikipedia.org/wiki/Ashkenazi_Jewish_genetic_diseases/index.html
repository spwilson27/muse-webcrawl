Ashkenazi Jewish genetic diseases

Several genetic disorders occur at markedly higher frequency among Ashkenazi Jews — Jews of Central and Eastern European descent — than in the general population. These conditions are typically autosomal recessive and are attributed to the population's history: a small founding population (genetic bottleneck) in medieval Europe, followed by rapid expansion with a high degree of endogamy, allowed genetic drift to raise certain rare alleles to relatively high frequencies (the founder effect).

Principal disorders

Well-known examples include:

Tay–Sachs disease, a fatal neurodegenerative disorder of infancy caused by hexosaminidase A deficiency; carrier frequency among Ashkenazi Jews is roughly 1 in 30, versus about 1 in 300 in the general population. Gaucher disease, the most common, with a carrier rate around 1 in 15–18, usually presenting in its milder type 1 form. Niemann–Pick disease (type A), mucolipidosis IV, Canavan disease, familial dysautonomia, Bloom syndrome, Fanconi anemia (group C), and familial hyperinsulinism are also enriched. Certain hereditary cancer mutations are also more common: the BRCA1 and BRCA2 founder mutations 185delAG, 5382insC, and 6174delT together are carried by roughly 1 in 40 Ashkenazi Jews, substantially raising lifetime breast and ovarian cancer risk.

Causes debated

Most researchers attribute the pattern to bottleneck and drift. A debated hypothesis holds that some alleles (e.g., those causing Tay–Sachs or Gaucher disease) conferred a heterozygote advantage — for example resistance to tuberculosis or increased intelligence — but this remains contested and is not the mainstream explanation for the overall pattern.

Screening

Carrier screening for these conditions is one of medicine's success stories. Community-based Tay–Sachs screening programs begun in the 1970s, using enzyme assays and later DNA testing, reduced the incidence of the disease in Jewish populations in North America and Israel by more than 90%. Premarital confidential screening organizations such as Dor Yeshorim test young people and advise on matching. Modern expanded carrier screening panels offered before or during pregnancy routinely test for dozens of Ashkenazi Jewish genetic diseases, and screening recommendations are now extended to pan-ethnic panels.
