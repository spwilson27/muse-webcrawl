Australian English phonology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Australian English accent is a non-rhotic variety most similar, among other accents, to that of New Zealand and southeastern England. Its phonology is distinctive for its vowel system: long vowels and diphthongs with wide trajectories, a fronted and raised vowel space, and widespread use of schwa in unstressed syllables.

Vowels

Australian English preserves the phonemic length contrasts of southern British English but realises them with characteristic qualities. The vowel of face is a wide diphthong beginning low and front; the vowel of price may begin far back, giving rise to the well-known confusion between "day" and "die" for outsiders. The near and square vowels have merged for most speakers, both realised as a long monophthong. Vowel length is a primary cue to the distinction between pairs such as ferry and fairy in varieties where they are not merged.

Consonants

Like other non-rhotic varieties, Australian English drops /r/ except before vowels, and has both linking and intrusive /r/. Intervocalic /t/ is commonly flapped, as in city, and word-final stops are often unreleased. The dark /l/ may vocalise to a vowel in words like milk and feel, a change increasingly common among younger speakers.

Variation

Mitchell and Delbridge (1965) classified the accent along a continuum from Broad through General to Cultivated, correlated with class and formality. Later research suggests the Broad–Cultivated distinction has weakened, while ethnic varieties — sometimes termed "ethnocultural dialects" — have developed in Sydney and Melbourne. Intonation is often noted for the high rising terminal, or "uptalk," also found in New Zealand English.
