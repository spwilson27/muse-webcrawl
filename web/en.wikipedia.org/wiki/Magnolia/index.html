Magnolia is a large genus of about 210–340 flowering plant species in the subfamily Magnolioideae of the family Magnoliaceae. It is named after the French botanist Pierre Magnol. Magnolias are ancient plants: fossilised specimens of species such as Magnolia acuminata have been dated to 20 million years ago, and plants of the family Magnoliaceae are known from 95 million years ago. Having evolved before bees appeared, magnolia flowers are thought to have developed to encourage pollination by beetles, and they lack true nectar and distinct sepals and petals, bearing instead undifferentiated tepals.

Distribution

The natural range of Magnolia species is a disjunct distribution, with a main centre in east and southeast Asia and a secondary centre in eastern North America, Central America, the West Indies, and some species in South America.

Description

Magnolias are trees or shrubs, deciduous or evergreen, with large, often fragrant, bowl- or star-shaped flowers that are bisexual and usually white, pink, purple, green, or yellow. The fruit is a cone-like aggregate of follicles, typically exposing bright red or orange seeds. Well-known cultivated species include the southern magnolia (Magnolia grandiflora), the saucer magnolia (Magnolia × soulangeana), the star magnolia (M. stellata), and the cucumber tree (M. acuminata).

Uses

Many species and hybrids are widely planted as ornamentals in parks and gardens for their showy flowers, which often appear before the leaves in spring. The bark and flower buds of Magnolia officinalis (hou po) have long been used in traditional Chinese medicine. The magnolia is the state flower of Mississippi and Louisiana, and is a prominent cultural symbol in the southern United States and in East Asia.
