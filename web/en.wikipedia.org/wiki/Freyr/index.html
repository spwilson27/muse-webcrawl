Freyr

Freyr (Old Norse: "Lord"), sometimes anglicised as Frey, is one of the most widely venerated gods in Norse mythology. A member of the Vanir, he is the son of the god Njord and twin brother of the goddess Freyja. Freyr is associated with sacral kingship, virility, fertility, sunshine and fair weather, and rules over Álfheimr, the realm of the elves.

Mythology

In the poem Skírnismál, Freyr falls in love with the beautiful jötunn Gerðr, whom he spies from Odin's high seat Hliðskjálf, and sends his servant Skírnir to woo her, surrendering his magic sword — which fights on its own — as a reward. Freyr's lack of the sword is fateful: at Ragnarök he will fight the fire giant Surtr and fall. Freyr owns the ship Skíðblaðnir, the best of all ships, which can be folded up and carried in a pouch, and the golden-bristled boar Gullinbursti, made by the dwarven smiths Brokkr and Sindri.

Worship

Adam of Bremen's account of the temple at Uppsala describes a statue of Freyr (Fricco) depicted with an immense phallus, worshipped alongside Thor and Odin. Tacitus's earlier account of the goddess Nerthus has been connected by scholars to a possible precursor of Njord and Freyr. Icelandic sagas describe Freyr as the ancestral god of Swedish kings — the Yngling dynasty claimed descent from him under the name Yngvi-Freyr — and mention sacrifices to him for good seasons and peace (til árs ok friðar).

Legacy

Freyr's name survives in Scandinavian place names and personal names. Archaeological finds, including small phallic figurines such as the Rällinge statuette, are often interpreted as images of the god. He remains a central figure in modern Germanic pagan practice.
