Anti-globalization movement

Protests against the WTO in Seattle in 1999 brought the movement to global attention.

The anti-globalization movement, or counter-globalization movement, is a social movement critical of economic globalization and the power of multinational corporations and institutions such as the World Trade Organization (WTO), the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. Participants often prefer the label alter-globalization ("another globalization"), arguing that they support international cooperation but oppose a model of globalization driven by corporate interests. In French the movement is known as altermondialisme.

History

The movement coalesced in the 1990s from labour unions, environmentalists, farmers' organisations, indigenous movements, anarchists and students. Early milestones included the Zapatista uprising in Chiapas, Mexico, launched on 1 January 1994 in protest at NAFTA, and the founding of networks such as People's Global Action. The movement became world-famous with the protests against the WTO Ministerial Conference in Seattle in 1999, when some 50,000 demonstrators helped shut down the meeting — the "Battle in Seattle." Large counter-summits followed at Prague (2000), Quebec City (2001) and Genoa (2001), where the protester Carlo Giuliani was shot dead by police. The World Social Forum, first held in Porto Alegre, Brazil in 2001 under the slogan "Another world is possible," became the movement's principal gathering.

Issues and criticism

The movement campaigns on trade rules and farmers' livelihoods, sweatshop labour, debt relief for developing countries, environmental protection, food sovereignty and opposition to the privatisation of public services. Critics, including many economists, argue that the movement is protectionist, that trade liberalisation has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty, and that the movement's diversity conceals incompatible goals. After 2001 the movement was partly eclipsed by the global anti-war mobilisations against the Iraq War, and many of its themes reappeared in later movements such as Occupy Wall Street (2011).
