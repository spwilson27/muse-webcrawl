Pidyon haben

The pidyon haben (Hebrew: פדיון הבן) or redemption of the first-born son is a mitzvah in Judaism whereby a Jewish firstborn son is "redeemed" from the service of the Temple by use of silver coins paid to a kohen (a Jewish priest), whose descendants' role this preserves. It is one of the 24 kohanic gifts. The source for the mitzvah is in the Torah: "You shall give Me the first-born among your sons" and the redemption price of five shekels set out in the Book of Numbers (18:15–16).


The infant is presented on a tray, often decorated, alongside the five silver coins of the redemption.

Principles

The obligation falls on the father to redeem his son 30 days after birth, when the son is the firstborn (bechor) who "opened the womb" of his Jewish mother — i.e., the mother's first child, born by natural (vaginal) delivery. If the father does not redeem him, the son must redeem himself when he reaches majority. The mitzvah does not apply if the father is a kohen or a Levite, or if the mother is the daughter of one, since those families are exempt as connected to the priestly service; nor does it apply to a child born by caesarean section or following certain earlier pregnancies or miscarriages.

The redemption is effected with five silver coins given to a kohen. In practice, coins of certified silver content are used — traditionally five American silver dollars dating from before 1965 or special coins minted for the purpose by the Israel Mint — representing the five shekels of the Torah. After the ceremony the kohen commonly returns the coins to the family as a gift, though this is not obligatory.

Ceremony

The ceremony is traditionally held at a festive meal (seudat mitzvah). The father presents the child to the kohen and, in a fixed dialogue in Aramaic and Hebrew, the kohen offers the choice between the child and the five shekels; the father chooses the coins and pays them, reciting two blessings, including Shehecheyanu. The kohen then blesses the child with the priestly blessing.

Because the child must be 30 days old, and the ceremony is not performed on Shabbat or major festivals, its timing can shift; it is a relatively rare ceremony, since it applies only to a minority of firstborn sons (estimates suggest fewer than one in ten Jewish boys qualify).
