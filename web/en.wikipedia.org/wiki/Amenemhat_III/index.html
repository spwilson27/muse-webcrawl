Amenemhat III (Ancient Egyptian: Ỉmn-m-ḥꜢt, meaning "Amun is at the forefront") was a pharaoh of the Twelfth Dynasty of ancient Egypt, ruling for about 46 years in the 19th century BC (c. 1860–1814 BC). His reign is regarded as the apex of the Middle Kingdom's prosperity, marked by extensive building works, irrigation projects in the Faiyum, and expeditions for mining and trade.

Reign

The son and successor of Senusret III, Amenemhat III may first have served as junior co-regent for as long as twenty years. Ancient sources such as the Turin Canon credit him with a reign of more than 40 years. Under his rule Egypt enjoyed administrative stability and great wealth. He intensified the exploitation of the Faiyum oasis, a programme begun by his predecessors, with large-scale hydraulic works around Lake Moeris that expanded the amount of cultivated land.

Building projects

Amenemhat III built two pyramids — an unusual distinction. His first, the "Black Pyramid" at Dahshur, suffered structural problems caused by its low-lying position and cracking in its chambers, and was abandoned as a royal tomb. His second pyramid was built at Hawara in the Faiyum, where he was probably buried. Adjoining it stood a vast mortuary temple that Greek and Roman writers identified as the famous "Labyrinth", praised by Herodotus and Strabo as a marvel exceeding the pyramids themselves.

He also built at Biahmu, where colossal statues of the king stood, and sponsored temples and monuments throughout Egypt, including at Thebes, Heracleopolis, and Memphis, as well as mining expeditions to Sinai and quarrying in Nubia and the Eastern Desert.

Legacy

Statues of Amenemhat III are noted for their sombre, care-worn realism, continuing a style begun under Senusret III. After his death he was succeeded briefly by Amenemhat IV and then by Sobekneferu, the last ruler of the Twelfth Dynasty. In the Faiyum he was long venerated as a god in the Ptolemaic period.
