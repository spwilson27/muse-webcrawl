Small hydro

Small hydro is the generation of hydroelectric power on a small scale, typically from installations with a generating capacity of up to about 10 megawatts (MW), although definitions vary by country. Small hydro projects usually have correspondingly low environmental impact compared with large dams, particularly run-of-the-river schemes which require little or no water storage.

Definition

There is no universally accepted upper limit for "small" hydro. Common classifications include:

CategoryTypical capacity
Large hydromore than 30–100 MW
Small hydroup to 10 MW (up to 30 MW in some countries, e.g. China)
Mini hydroup to 1 MW
Micro hydroup to 100 kW
Pico hydroup to 5 kW

The European Small Hydropower Association and many national programmes use 10 MW as the threshold; Canada and China have at times used higher limits (30–50 MW) in support programmes.

Technology

A small hydro plant diverts part of a stream or river through an intake and penstock to a turbine — commonly a Pelton, Francis, Kaplan or crossflow (Banki–Michell) machine depending on head and flow — driving a generator. Many schemes are run-of-the-river, using a low weir rather than a large dam, and return the water downstream after generation.

Because civil works dominate costs, small hydro is often attractive where infrastructure already exists: disused mill sites, irrigation canals, water-supply pipelines and the outlets of existing dams.

Advantages and disadvantages

Small hydro offers a mature, reliable renewable technology with high capacity factors, long plant lifetimes (often 50 years or more), and minimal operating emissions. Against this, output depends on stream flow, suitable sites are dispersed, and per-kilowatt capital costs can be higher than for large hydro. Even run-of-the-river schemes must manage effects on fish passage and minimum environmental flows.

Deployment

Worldwide small hydro capacity is estimated at well over 75 GW, with the largest fleets in China, where rural electrification programmes built tens of thousands of small stations. Significant capacity also exists in Europe, India, Japan and North America. Small hydro is widely used for off-grid and mini-grid electrification in mountainous regions such as Nepal and the Andes.
