Epirus

Epirus (Greek: ἠπειρος, Ēpeiros) is a geographical and historical region in southeastern Europe, now shared between Greece and Albania. It lies between the Pindus Mountains and the Ionian Sea, stretching from the Bay of Vlorë in the north to the Ambracian Gulf in the south. The modern Greek region of Epirus has its capital at Ioannina, and the greater historical region is sometimes divided into a Greek south and an Albanian north known in Greek as Northern Epirus.

Geography

Epirus is a rugged, mountainous region. Much of it lies on the Pindus mountain range, the "spine of Greece", whose peaks include Mount Smolikas, the second-highest mountain in Greece. The Vikos Gorge, one of the deepest gorges in the world relative to its width, lies in the Vikos–Aoös National Park. Rivers such as the Acheron, Arachthos and Aoös (Vjosë) rise in Epirus; the Acheron was famed in antiquity as a river of the underworld.

Ancient history

In classical antiquity Epirus was inhabited by Greek tribes including the Chaonians, Molossians and Thesprotians, organised into a federation, the Epirote League. The oracle of Zeus at Dodona, among the oldest in Greece, stood in Molossian territory. Under King Pyrrhus of Epirus (319/318–272 BC), a cousin of Alexander the Great, the Molossian kingdom reached its greatest power; Pyrrhus campaigned in Italy against Rome, winning battles at such cost that his name gave rise to the phrase "Pyrrhic victory". In 168 BC Epirus was conquered by the Roman Republic after the Third Macedonian War, and tens of thousands of its inhabitants were enslaved.

Medieval and Ottoman periods

After 1204, following the Fourth Crusade, Michael I Komnenos Doukas founded the Despotate of Epirus, one of the Byzantine successor states, which at times extended over much of western Greece. The region passed to Ottoman rule in the 15th century and remained under it for nearly five centuries; under Ali Pasha of Ioannina (r. 1788–1822) Epirus enjoyed a quasi-autonomous and culturally flourishing period.

Modern history

The southern part of Epirus was incorporated into Greece after the First Balkan War (1912–13). The northern part was awarded to the newly created Albania, a settlement contested by the local Greek population and by Greece, which claimed it as "Northern Epirus"; an autonomous Greek administration briefly existed in 1914. The Greco-Italian War of 1940–41 was fought largely on Epirote soil. Today the Greek periphery of Epirus is one of the least densely populated regions of Greece, known for the stone villages of Zagori, the monasteries of Meteora lying just beyond its edge, and the port of Igoumenitsa.
