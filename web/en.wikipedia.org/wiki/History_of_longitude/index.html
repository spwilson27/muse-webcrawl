Overview

The problem of finding longitude — how far east or west a ship had sailed — was the great unsolved navigational problem of the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. Latitude could be read from the height of the Sun or the pole star, but without knowing the time at a reference meridian a ship's east–west position was a matter of dead reckoning and guesswork, with wrecked fleets and lost lives as the price. The 1707 wreck of four Royal Navy ships on the Scilly Isles, with some 1,400–2,000 sailors drowned, helped spur Parliament to act.

The Longitude Act of 1714 offered rewards of up to £20,000 — a fortune — for a method of finding longitude to within half a degree, judged by a new Board of Longitude.

The two solutions

Astronomers proposed the lunar-distance method: the Moon moves against the stars like the hand of a clock, so measuring its position, and consulting printed tables such as Nevil Maskelyne's Nautical Almanac (from 1767), gives the time at Greenwich, and comparing that with local time gives longitude.

The clockmaker John Harrison pursued the rival method: carry the reference time aboard on a precision timekeeper. After decades of work his fourth timekeeper, H4, lost only seconds on an Atlantic crossing in 1761–62 — a marine chronometer accurate enough to win the prize in spirit, though the Board paid out fully only after long resistance, an Act of Parliament and the intervention of George III. By the early nineteenth century chronometers were standard equipment, and the longitude problem was solved.
