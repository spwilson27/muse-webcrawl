Price discrimination

Price discrimination is a pricing strategy in which a seller charges different prices to different buyers for the same or similar goods or services, where the differences are not explained by differences in cost. It requires that the seller has some market power, that buyers have different willingness to pay, and that resale (arbitrage) between buyers can be prevented.

Degrees

Following the economist A. C. Pigou, three degrees are distinguished. First-degree (perfect) price discrimination charges each customer the maximum they are willing to pay, capturing all consumer surplus. Second-degree discrimination offers price schedules based on quantity or version — bulk discounts, or premium and basic editions — letting customers self-select. Third-degree discrimination charges different prices to identifiable groups, such as student and senior discounts, geographic pricing, or matinee ticket prices.

Examples and effects

Examples include airline and hotel yield management, software versioning, coupons, pharmaceutical pricing across countries, and personalized online pricing based on data about shoppers. Economically, price discrimination can increase output and total welfare by serving customers who would not buy at a single high price, but it may also transfer surplus from consumers to producers and is regulated in some contexts — for instance, the U.S. Robinson–Patman Act restricts certain price differences in goods sold to competing businesses.
