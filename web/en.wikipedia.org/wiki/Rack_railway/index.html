Other namesCog railway, cog railroad
SystemsRiggenbach, Abt, Strub, Locher, Lamella (von Roll)

A rack railway (also called a cog railway or cog railroad) is a steep-grade railway with a toothed rack rail, usually positioned between the running rails. Trains are fitted with one or more cog wheels or pinions that mesh with this rack rail, allowing them to climb gradients far steeper than the roughly 6–8% limit of ordinary adhesion railways.

Technology

Several rack systems were developed in the 19th and 20th centuries. The Riggenbach system, designed by Niklaus Riggenbach in the 1860s, uses a ladder-like rack of steel plates. The Abt system, by Roman Abt, uses vertical rack bars with offset teeth for smoother engagement and is the most widely used worldwide. The Strub system uses a flat-bottomed rolled rail with teeth milled into the top, while the Locher system — used on the Pilatus Railway in Switzerland, the steepest rack railway in the world at 48% — uses horizontal teeth on the sides of a double rack to prevent the pinions from climbing out.

Examples

Famous rack railways include the Mount Washington Cog Railway in New Hampshire (opened 1868, the world's first mountain-climbing cog railway), the Manitou and Pike's Peak Railway in Colorado, the Jungfrau Railway and Gornergrat Railway in Switzerland, the Snowdon Mountain Railway in Wales, and numerous Alpine and Andean lines. Many rack lines are tourist attractions; others, such as the rack sections of the Bernina line, form part of working railway networks where steam or diesel locomotives work both rack and adhesion sections.
