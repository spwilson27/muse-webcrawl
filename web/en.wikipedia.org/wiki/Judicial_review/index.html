Judicial review

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Judicial review is the power of a judiciary to examine the actions of the legislative and executive branches and to declare them invalid — above all, to strike down laws that violate a constitution. It is the institutional teeth of constitutional supremacy: without it, constitutional limits are advice; with it, unelected judges can override democratic majorities, which is why judicial review is everywhere both defended as the guardian of rights and attacked as the "counter-majoritarian difficulty".

Models and history

The famous founding act is the United States Supreme Court's decision in Marbury v. Madison (1803), where Chief Justice John Marshall claimed for the courts the power to declare acts of Congress unconstitutional — the "diffuse" model, in which any court may refuse to apply an unconstitutional law, spread through the Americas. Europe, distrusting ordinary judges, largely adopted Hans Kelsen's "concentrated" model after 1920: a single specialised constitutional court (Austria 1920, Germany and Italy after 1945, France's Constitutional Council) monopolises annulment. After the Second World War and again after 1989, judicial review expanded worldwide — India's Supreme Court (with its "basic structure" doctrine), South Africa's Constitutional Court, the European courts — turning judges into central political actors and provoking recurring debates over judicial activism versus restraint, appointment politics, and court-packing, from Roosevelt's 1937 plan to the rule-of-law crises in Poland and Hungary.
