The Siege of Leiden was a Spanish siege of the rebel Dutch city of Leiden during the Eighty Years' War. Spanish troops under Francisco de Valdés first invested the city in late 1573, withdrew in March 1574 to meet an invasion by Louis of Nassau, and returned in May to besiege it again. The second siege ended on 3 October 1574, when a Dutch relief fleet sailed across land deliberately flooded by cutting the dikes and relieved the starving city — an episode celebrated annually in Leiden as Leidens Ontzet (the Relief of Leiden).

Background

After the Spanish recapture of several Holland towns and the brutal sieges of Haarlem (1572–73) and Alkmaar (1573), Leiden held out as a key rebel stronghold. William of Orange, Prince of Orange, directed the resistance from Delft. The first siege was lifted when Valdés marched south to confront Louis of Nassau, whose army was destroyed at the Battle of Mookerheyde in April 1574; Valdés then returned to Leiden with a free hand.

The second siege and the relief

From May 1574 the Spanish ringed Leiden with fortified positions, cutting off supplies. Inside the city, food ran out; famine and plague killed a large share of the population — estimates commonly put the dead at around a third of the city's roughly 15,000–18,000 inhabitants — yet the magistrates, led by burgomaster Pieter Adriaansz. van der Werff, refused to surrender. A famous (if embellished) tradition holds that Van der Werff offered his own body as food to the starving citizens rather than yield.

To relieve the city, the States of Holland took the drastic decision to breach the dikes of the Maas and IJssel rivers and flood the low-lying polders between Rotterdam and Leiden. Rising water and autumn storms slowly drove out the Spanish from their lower camps. A fleet of flat-bottomed barges — the "Sea Beggars" under Admiral Louis de Boisot, carrying soldiers, herring and white bread — fought its way across the flooded land, engaging Spanish positions at the remaining dry points. In the night of 2–3 October the Spanish abandoned their last strongpoints, and on the morning of 3 October 1574 the fleet entered Leiden.

Aftermath and legacy

The relief was a turning point in morale for the Dutch Revolt in Holland. In gratitude, William of Orange offered the city a choice between a tax exemption and a university; Leiden chose the university, and Leiden University was founded in 1575 — the oldest university in the Netherlands. The relief is still commemorated every 3 October with traditional meals of herring and white bread (haring en wittebrood) and hutspot, said to be modelled on a pot of stew found in an abandoned Spanish camp.
