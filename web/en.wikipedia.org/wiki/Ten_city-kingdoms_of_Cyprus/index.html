Ten city-kingdoms of Cyprus

The ten city-kingdoms of Cyprus were the Greek, Phoenician, and Eteocypriot polities that dominated the island of Cyprus during the Iron Age, from roughly the eighth century BC until their abolition by Ptolemy I of Egypt around 312 BC. The canonical list of ten is preserved in an inscription of the Assyrian king Esarhaddon (673/2 BC), which names ten kings of the land of Iatnana (Cyprus) who sent materials for his palace at Nineveh.

The kingdoms

The ten kingdoms named in the Assyrian record are conventionally identified as:

  • Salamis — Greek kingdom on the east coast, traditionally founded by Teucer
  • Kition — Phoenician kingdom, modern Larnaca
  • Amathus — Eteocypriot city on the south coast
  • Kurion (Curium) — Greek kingdom on the south coast
  • Paphos — Greek, famed for the sanctuary of Aphrodite
  • Soloi — Greek, on the northwest coast
  • Tamassos — in the copper-rich interior
  • Ledra — inland, on the site of modern Nicosia
  • Idalion — inland, later annexed by Kition
  • Chytroi — inland kingdom in the Mesaoria

Other kingdoms, such as Lapithos and Kyrenia in the north and Golgoi, existed at various times; Marion rose to prominence later. The exact roster thus shifted across the centuries, but the ten of Esarhaddon's stele remained the traditional count.

History

The kingdoms emerged as Cyprus recovered from the upheavals at the end of the Bronze Age, enriched by the island's copper trade. They successively acknowledged the overlordship of Assyria, Egypt, and the Achaemenid Persian Empire, while retaining their own kings, who styled themselves in inscriptions and on coinage. Cypriot kings supplied ships to Persian fleets but also joined revolts, most notably the revolt of Evagoras of Salamis against Persia (391–380 BC) and the general Cypriot uprising during the Ionian Revolt (498 BC).

After Alexander the Great's conquest, the kings allied with his successors in shifting combinations. Ptolemy I finally suppressed the kingdoms around 312 BC: the last kings were deposed, executed, or exiled, and Cyprus was governed as a unified Ptolemaic province from a capital at Salamis, later New Paphos.

Culture

The kingdoms were culturally diverse. Greek kingdoms wrote in the Cypriot syllabary and later the Greek alphabet; Kition used Phoenician; Amathus preserved the undeciphered Eteocypriot language into the fourth century BC. Their art blended Greek, Phoenician, Egyptian, and Near Eastern elements, and their sanctuaries — above all the sanctuary of Aphrodite at Palaepaphos — drew pilgrims from across the Mediterranean.
