Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamics (sometimes abbreviated PD) is the branch of pharmacology concerned with the effects of drugs on the body — summarised as "what the drug does to the body". It studies the biochemical and physiological interactions between a drug and its targets, and the resulting effects, in contrast to pharmacokinetics, which concerns what the body does to the drug.

Mechanisms

Most drugs act by binding to specific targets, commonly receptors, but also enzymes, ion channels, transporters and nucleic acids. A drug that activates a receptor is an agonist; one that blocks activation is an antagonist; partial agonists produce a submaximal response. Binding initiates signalling that may change cell function directly or through second messengers. The relationship between drug concentration and effect is described by the dose–response curve, characterised by potency (often expressed as EC50, the concentration producing half the maximal effect) and efficacy (the maximal effect achievable).

Clinical relevance

Pharmacodynamic concepts explain therapeutic windows, tolerance and tachyphylaxis, receptor desensitisation, and drug interactions in which one medicine alters the effect of another at its site of action. Together with pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics guides the choice of drug and dose for individual patients, and its principles are applied in fields from anaesthesia to antimicrobial therapy, where measures such as minimum inhibitory concentration link exposure to effect.
