Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Berkeley Lab) is a federally funded research and development center in the hills above Berkeley, California, United States. Founded in 1931 by Ernest Orlando Lawrence as the Radiation Laboratory of the University of California, Berkeley, it is the oldest of the United States Department of Energy's national laboratories still operated by its founding university. Berkeley Lab is managed by the University of California for the Department of Energy's Office of Science.

History

Lawrence founded the laboratory around his invention of the cyclotron, pioneering the model of large, team-based "big science" organised around major instruments. During the Second World War the lab contributed to the Manhattan Project, including electromagnetic isotope separation work led by Lawrence and the chemistry of plutonium by Glenn Seaborg's group. In 1952 the growing weapons-related work was split off into a new laboratory at Livermore (now Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory), while Berkeley Lab returned to basic research. The laboratory was renamed for Ernest Lawrence after his death in 1958 (and later for both Lawrence brothers' legacy).

Research and discoveries

Berkeley Lab scientists have discovered or co-discovered numerous chemical elements, including berkelium and californium (named for the lab's home), lawrencium, and seaborgium, as well as technetium and astatine in its early years. Sixteen Nobel Prizes are associated with the laboratory, including those of Lawrence, Seaborg, Edwin McMillan, Luis Alvarez, Melvin Calvin, and Saul Perlmutter, whose team at Berkeley Lab found evidence for the accelerating expansion of the universe (dark energy). Major facilities include the Advanced Light Source synchrotron, the National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC), the Molecular Foundry nanoscience center, and the Joint Genome Institute. The laboratory's research spans physics, materials and chemical sciences, energy technologies, earth and environmental science, bioscience, and computing.
