Ōbaku (Japanese: 黄檗宗, Ōbaku-shū) is the third and smallest of the historic schools of Zen Buddhism in Japan, after Rinzai and Sōtō. It was introduced in the mid-17th century by the Chinese Linji master Yinyuan Longqi (Japanese: Ingen Ryūki), who arrived at Nagasaki in 1654 with a party of Chinese monks and craftsmen.

History

Ingen's reputation reached the shogunate, and in 1661 he was granted land at Uji, south of Kyoto, where he founded Manpuku-ji, built in contemporary Chinese Ming dynasty style — a striking contrast to earlier Japanese temple architecture. The school takes its Japanese name from Mount Huangbo (Ōbaku) in Fujian, Ingen's home mountain, and in turn echoes the name of the great Tang-dynasty master Huangbo Xiyun.

Practice and influence

Ōbaku practice blends Zen meditation with Pure Land elements such as recitation of the nembutsu, following later Chinese custom, and its liturgy long retained Chinese pronunciation. Beyond religion, Ōbaku monks profoundly influenced Edo-period culture: they popularised sencha (steeped-leaf tea) in place of powdered matcha ceremony culture, introduced Ming-style calligraphy, seal carving and portraiture, and are credited with bringing foods and customs — including the fucha vegetarian temple cuisine — that entered wider Japanese life.
