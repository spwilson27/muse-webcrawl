Compulsive hoarding

Hoarding disorder
Specialty: Psychiatry, clinical psychology
Symptoms: Persistent difficulty discarding possessions, excessive clutter impairing use of living spaces
Usual onset: Adolescence, worsening with age
Treatment: Cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for hoarding

Compulsive hoarding, clinically recognised as hoarding disorder, is a mental disorder characterised by a persistent difficulty discarding or parting with possessions, regardless of their actual value, because of a perceived need to save them and distress associated with discarding them. The resulting accumulation of clutter compromises the use of living areas and causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other areas of functioning, including maintaining a safe environment.

Diagnosis

Hoarding disorder was listed as a distinct diagnosis in the DSM-5 (2013), having previously been considered a symptom or subtype of obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) and of obsessive–compulsive personality disorder. Diagnostic specifiers note whether the disorder is accompanied by excessive acquisition and the person's level of insight. Hoarding must be distinguished from normal collecting, in which possessions are organised and do not impair functioning, and from accumulation caused by other conditions such as brain injury, dementia, or the negative symptoms of schizophrenia.

Prevalence and course

Clinically significant hoarding is estimated to affect roughly 2–6% of the population, with some prevalence studies converging around 2.5%. Symptoms typically begin in adolescence and worsen with each decade of life; diagnosis is most common in older adults. The disorder often co-occurs with depression, anxiety disorders, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (particularly the inattentive presentation).

Mechanisms and treatment

Research associated with psychologists Randy Frost and Gail Steketee, authors of Stuff: Compulsive Hoarding and the Meaning of Things (2010), links hoarding to strong emotional attachment to objects, beliefs about waste and responsibility, and deficits in information-processing such as categorisation and decision-making. First-line treatment is a specialised form of cognitive behavioural therapy involving motivational interviewing, skills training in organising and problem solving, and graduated exposure to discarding; antidepressant medication used for OCD is less effective for hoarding itself. Animal hoarding is a related manifestation in which large numbers of animals are kept without adequate care.
