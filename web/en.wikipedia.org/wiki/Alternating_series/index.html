Alternating series

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, an alternating series is an infinite series whose terms alternate in sign: Σ (−1)n an (or Σ (−1)n+1 an) with an ≥ 0. The alternating structure produces partial cancellation between consecutive terms, so such series often converge even when the series of absolute values diverges.

Alternating series test

The alternating series test (or Leibniz test, after Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz) states that an alternating series converges if the sequence an is monotonically decreasing and tends to zero: an+1 ≤ an for all n, and an → 0. The proof observes that the odd and even partial sums form monotone bounded sequences squeezing the same limit.

Error bound

A distinctive and practical feature: if the test's hypotheses hold, the truncation error is bounded by the first omitted term, |S − SN| ≤ aN+1, and the error has the sign of that term. The partial sums alternately overshoot and undershoot the true sum, closing in on it — hence the name of the estimation theorem.

Examples

  • The alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯ converges (conditionally) to ln 2.
  • Σ (−1)n/n² converges absolutely to −π²/12.
  • The Taylor series for ln(1 + x), e−x, sin x, and cos x are alternating for x > 0, with the Leibniz series 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − ⋯ = π/4 as the celebrated special case of the arctangent series.

Conditional convergence and rearrangement

An alternating series whose terms decrease to zero but whose absolute series diverges is conditionally convergent. By the Riemann series theorem, such a series can be rearranged to converge to any prescribed value, or to diverge — a pathology impossible for absolutely convergent series. Acceleration methods such as Euler's transform can dramatically speed up the slow convergence typical of alternating series, whose error after N terms is often of order aN.
