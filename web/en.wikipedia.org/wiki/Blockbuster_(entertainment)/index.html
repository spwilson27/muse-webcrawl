Blockbuster (entertainment)

Blockbuster
Origin of termSecond World War "blockbuster" bombs; applied to films from the 1950s
Archetypal examplesJaws (1975), Star Wars (1977)

A blockbuster is a work of entertainment — typically a feature film, but also other media — that is highly popular and financially successful. The term originally described very large aerial bombs in the Second World War and came to be applied to plays and films that achieved extraordinary popular success.

The blockbuster era

Although hits such as Gone with the Wind predate the term's cinematic use, the modern blockbuster era is usually dated from Steven Spielberg's Jaws (1975), whose wide release and heavy television advertising created the summer movie season, and George Lucas's Star Wars (1977), which demonstrated the merchandising potential of a hit. Studios thereafter concentrated on high-concept, heavily marketed "event" films, sequels and franchises designed for global audiences.

Economics

Blockbuster strategy involves large production and marketing budgets recouped through massive opening weekends, international box office, and ancillary revenues. Critics argue the model crowds out mid-budget films and favours spectacle over originality, while the industry points to the hits' role in subsidising riskier work.

Other uses

The word is also used for bestselling books, hit stage musicals, pharmaceutical drugs with over a billion dollars in annual sales, and, historically, a video rental chain named Blockbuster.
