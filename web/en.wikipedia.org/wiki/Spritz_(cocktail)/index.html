Spritz (cocktail)

Spritz
TypeWine-based cocktail (aperitivo)
ServedOn the rocks in a wine glass
Standard garnishOrange slice
Common ingredientsProsecco, a bitter liqueur (Aperol or Campari), soda water
OriginVeneto, Italy

A spritz is an Italian wine-based cocktail, commonly served as an aperitivo in northeastern Italy, especially in the Veneto. It typically consists of prosecco, a bitter liqueur such as Aperol or Campari, and a splash of soda water, served over ice in a large wine glass and garnished with a slice of orange.

History

The name derives from the German spritzen, "to spray," and the practice of Habsburg soldiers in the nineteenth-century Veneto, then under Austrian rule, who diluted local wines they found too strong with a splash of soda water. In the early twentieth century, bitter liqueurs began to be added: Aperol was created in Padua in 1919 by the Barbieri brothers, and the combination of Aperol, prosecco, and soda — the Aperol spritz — became a fixture of Venetian café culture before conquering international markets in the 2000s and 2010s.

Variations

  • Aperol spritz – the best-known form: roughly three parts prosecco, two parts Aperol, one part soda. It is an IBA official cocktail in the "contemporary classics" list.
  • Campari spritz – substitutes the more bitter Campari, giving a deeper red colour.
  • Spritz bianco – white wine and soda with a lemon garnish, closer to the original Venetian style.
  • Select spritz – made with Select, another Venetian bitter aperitivo dating to 1920, garnished with an olive.
  • Hugo – a South Tyrolean variant with elderflower syrup or cordial and mint, without a bitter liqueur.

Preparation

The drink is built in the glass: fill a large wine glass with ice, add prosecco, then the liqueur, then a splash of soda, and stir gently. Adding the prosecco first is said to keep the liqueur from settling at the bottom. At about 8–11% alcohol by volume depending on proportions, the spritz is relatively light and is emblematic of the Italian aperitivo hour, accompanied by small snacks called cicchetti in Venice.
