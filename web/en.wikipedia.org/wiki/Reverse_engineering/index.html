Reverse engineering

Reverse engineering is the process of analyzing a finished product, device, software system or structure to understand its design, architecture, materials or behavior, often so that it can be reproduced, repaired, improved or made interoperable with other systems.

Typical steps
Observe behavior → inspect structure → measure and model → infer design principles → test the reconstruction.

Applications

Engineers reverse engineer legacy equipment when drawings have been lost, replacement parts are unavailable or an old system must be maintained. In electronics, integrated circuits may be photographed layer by layer to reconstruct their layout. Archaeologists and historians use comparable methods to infer how ancient tools and buildings were made.

Software

Software reverse engineering examines executable code through disassembly, decompilation, debugging, packet analysis and observation of inputs and outputs. Legitimate purposes include malware analysis, security auditing, preservation, interoperability and recovering lost source code. Techniques range from static inspection to running the program in a controlled environment.

Legal and ethical issues

The legality of reverse engineering varies by jurisdiction and purpose. Copyright law may permit analysis for interoperability, while contracts, anti-circumvention rules, patent rights and trade-secret law can impose restrictions. It differs from ordinary theft of design documents because the analyst works from a lawfully obtained artifact, but unauthorized access or circumvention can change the legal analysis.

Relation to science

Reverse engineering resembles the scientific method: observations generate hypotheses about internal mechanisms, which are tested against new behavior. Unlike forward engineering, however, the original requirements and design trade-offs are usually unknown and must be inferred.
