Landing craft
RoleTransport troops and vehicles from ship to shore
Key typesLCVP, LCM, LCU, LCAC (air-cushioned)
First large-scale useSecond World War amphibious landings

Landing craft are small and medium-sized seagoing vessels used to convey troops, vehicles and cargo from transport ships directly onto a shore during an amphibious assault. Characterised by shallow draft, flat bottoms and bow ramps that lower for rapid unloading, they were developed in their modern form between the world wars and became indispensable in the Second World War.

Second World War types

The most famous is the American LCVP (Landing Craft, Vehicle, Personnel), the "Higgins boat", designed by Andrew Higgins from Louisiana swamp boats. Nearly 24,000 were built; it carried a platoon of 36 men or a jeep and could retract off the beach under its own power. Larger types included the LCM (Landing Craft, Mechanized), able to land a tank, and British designs such as the LCA (Landing Craft, Assault). General Eisenhower credited Higgins boats with winning the war for the Allies, so central were they to the Normandy landings of 6 June 1944 and the Pacific island-hopping campaign.

Modern landing craft

Postwar navies developed larger utility landing craft (LCU), roll-on/roll-off connectors, and the air-cushioned LCAC, which can travel at over 40 knots and ride up onto beaches, accessing far more of the world's coastline than displacement craft. Landing craft operate from amphibious transport docks and assault ships with well decks.
