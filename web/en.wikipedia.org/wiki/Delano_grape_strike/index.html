Delano grape strike

Striking grape workers and supporters in Delano, California

The Delano grape strike was a labor strike organized by the Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee (AWOC), a predominantly Filipino and Chicano farm labor union, against grape growers in Delano, California, which began on September 8, 1965. It became a pivotal event in the history of the American farmworker movement and the broader Chicano movement.

Background

On September 8, 1965, Filipino American members of AWOC, led by Larry Itliong, Philip Vera Cruz, Benjamin Gines and Pete Velasco, voted at Filipino Community Hall in Delano to strike against the Delano grape growers. The Filipino workers demanded wages equal to the federal minimum wage. Eight days later, on September 16, the predominantly Mexican American National Farm Workers Association (NFWA), led by César Chávez and Dolores Huerta, voted to join the strike. The two unions later merged to form the United Farm Workers (UFW).

The strike and boycott

When growers brought in strikebreakers, the union expanded its strategy to a nationwide consumer boycott of California table grapes. Union organizers traveled across the United States and Canada drumming up support. In 1968 Chávez undertook a 25-day fast to rededicate the movement to nonviolence. The strike and boycott attracted national attention and support from civil rights, religious and labor organizations.

Outcome

On July 29, 1970, the strike ended when the major Delano grape growers signed union contracts granting workers improved pay and benefits, including contributions to health and welfare funds and protections against pesticides. The strike lasted five years and is regarded as the spark of the farmworkers' labor movement in the United States.
