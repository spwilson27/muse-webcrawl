In mathematics, a nowhere differentiable function is a real-valued function that is continuous on its whole domain but differentiable at no point. Such functions shattered the intuitive 18th- and 19th-century assumption — held, for example, by Ampère — that every continuous function must be differentiable except at isolated points.

The Weierstrass function

Karl Weierstrass presented the first famous example in 1872:

f(x) = Σn=0∞ an cos(bn πx)

where 0 < a < 1, b is a positive odd integer, and ab > 1 + 3π/2. Uniform convergence makes f continuous, but the sum oscillates at every scale, so no tangent exists anywhere. The graph of the function is a fractal with Hausdorff dimension greater than 1 (proved in full generality only in the 21st century). Earlier, Bolzano (c. 1830) and Riemann had constructed or suggested related examples that went unpublished or unproven at the time.

Other examples

  • The Blancmange (Takagi) function, built from sums of rescaled sawtooth waves, is continuous and nowhere differentiable and is easier to analyse than Weierstrass's.
  • Van der Waerden's function (1930), a simple classroom example based on distance to the nearest integer.
  • The paths of Brownian motion (Wiener process) are almost surely continuous and nowhere differentiable — a fact proved by Paley, Wiener and Zygmund (1933), giving the phenomenon physical significance.

How typical are they?

Counter-intuitively, nowhere differentiable functions are the rule rather than the exception. Banach proved in 1931 that the set of continuous functions on [0, 1] that have a finite derivative at even one point forms a meagre (first-category) set; in the Baire category sense, the "generic" continuous function is nowhere differentiable. In the prevalence (measure-theoretic) sense of Hunt, Sauer and Yorke, the same conclusion holds: almost every continuous function, in the sense of prevalence, is nowhere differentiable.
