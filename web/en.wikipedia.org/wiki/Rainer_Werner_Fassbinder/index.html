Rainer Werner Fassbinder (31 May 1945 – 10 June 1982) was a German filmmaker, actor, and playwright, and one of the leading figures of the New German Cinema movement. In a career of just sixteen years he completed over forty feature films, two television series, twenty-four plays, and numerous other works — a productivity unmatched in postwar European cinema.

Career

Fassbinder began in avant-garde theatre in Munich, co-founding the Action Theater and then the Antitheater, whose members became his core acting troupe — including Hanna Schygulla, his most famous leading lady. His early films, such as Katzelmacher (1969) and Love Is Colder Than Death (1969), drew on Brechtian theatre and American genre cinema. His international breakthrough came with Ali: Fear Eats the Soul (1974), a melodrama about a romance between an elderly German cleaning woman and a young Moroccan guest worker, and reached its commercial peak with The Marriage of Maria Braun (1979). His 14-part television adaptation of Döblin's Berlin Alexanderplatz (1980) is considered a landmark of European filmmaking.

Death and legacy

Fassbinder died in 1982, aged 37, from a lethal interaction of cocaine and sleeping pills while working on the edit of Querelle. His films — preoccupied with exploitation, desire, class, race, and postwar German identity — remain central to the study of European art cinema, and archival restorations of his work continue to appear at major festivals.
