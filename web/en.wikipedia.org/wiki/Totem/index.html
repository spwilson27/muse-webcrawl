Totem
Origin of termOjibwe doodem / ototeman
Associated peoplesIndigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest, Ojibwe, and others worldwide

A totem (from Ojibwe doodem, "his kinship group") is a spirit being, sacred object, or symbol that serves as an emblem of a group of people, such as a family, clan, lineage, or tribe. Totemism is a system of belief in which humans are said to have kinship or a mystical relationship with a spirit-being, such as an animal or plant.

Totem poles

Totem poles are monumental carvings created by Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast of North America, including the Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, and Kwakwaka'wakw. The poles, typically carved from western red cedar, bear crests — stylised animals, supernatural beings, and figures from family histories — that record lineage rights, myths, and memorial events. Contrary to common assumption, most totem poles were not religious idols but records of family identity and history.

Anthropological study

Totemism became a central topic of nineteenth- and twentieth-century anthropology. Émile Durkheim treated totemism as the most elementary form of religion in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912), arguing that the totem is a material expression of society itself. Sigmund Freud addressed the subject in Totem and Taboo (1913). Claude Lévi-Strauss later argued, in Totemism (1962), that totemic classifications are a way of organising thought — natural species are chosen not because they are "good to eat" but because they are "good to think" with.

Group totems

Among the Ojibwe, clans (doodemag) are identified by animal symbols such as bear, loon, crane, deer, and fish, which govern marriage rules and social responsibilities. Similar clan-animal systems are found among many peoples in North America, Australia, and Africa.
