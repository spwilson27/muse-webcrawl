Duchy of Athens

The Duchy of Athens (Greek: Δουκᾶτον Ἀθηνῶν, Doukaton Athinon; Catalan: Ducat d'Atenes) was one of the Crusader states set up in Greece after the Fourth Crusade conquered the Byzantine Empire in 1204. Its capital was Athens, and its territory included Attica and Boeotia. It survived until its conquest by the Ottoman Empire in 1458–1460.

History

Burgundian dukes (1205–1311)

The duchy was created in 1205 when the Burgundian knight Otho de la Roche, who had seized Athens and Thebes during the partition of the Byzantine Empire, took the title of "Lord of Athens" (sire d'Athènes); his successors used the title of duke from the 1260s. The de la Roche dukes made Athens a prosperous court, and Guy II, the last of the line, was one of the leading princes of Frankish Greece.

Catalan rule (1311–1388)

In 1311 the mercenaries of the Catalan Company destroyed the chivalry of Frankish Greece at the Battle of Halmyros (Cephissus), killing Duke Walter V of Brienne, and seized the duchy. For over seventy years Athens was ruled from Sicily as part of the Crown of Aragon, and Catalan became the language of government; the Acropolis was fortified as the city's castle.

Florentine Acciaioli and Ottoman conquest

In 1388 the Florentine Nerio I Acciaioli captured Athens, and his family held the duchy (with an interruption by Venice and a brief Ottoman vassalage) until 1456–1458, when the last duke, Francesco II, surrendered the Acropolis to the forces of Mehmed II. The duchy was formally extinguished by 1460, and the Parthenon was converted into a mosque.

Legacy

The title "Duke of Athens" passed into literary fame through Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream and Timon of Athens, and was later claimed among Spanish royal titles. The Frankish tower on the Acropolis, associated with the dukes, was demolished in 1874–75.
