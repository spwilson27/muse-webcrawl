Dutch elm disease

Dutch elm disease (DED) is a devastating disease of elm trees caused by ascomycete fungi of the genus Ophiostoma, principally Ophiostoma ulmi and the more aggressive Ophiostoma novo-ulmi. It is spread by elm bark beetles (such as the European elm bark beetle, Scolytus multistriatus) and through root grafts between neighbouring trees. The fungus blocks the tree's xylem vessels, causing wilting and death, typically within a season or a few years.

History

The disease was first identified in Europe in the early 20th century—it was named "Dutch" because much of the pioneering research was done by Dutch phytopathologists such as Bea Schwarz and Christine Buisman, not because of a Dutch origin; the fungus is thought to originate in Asia. A first pandemic of O. ulmi subsided by the 1940s, but a second, far more destructive pandemic caused by O. novo-ulmi spread across Europe and North America from the 1960s, killing tens of millions of elms and destroying the American elm as a dominant street tree and the English elm in the British landscape.

Management

Control relies on sanitation felling and prompt removal of diseased elms, disruption of root grafts, insecticide or fungicide injections, and breeding programmes that propagate tolerant cultivars—including resistant American elm selections such as 'Princeton' and 'Valley Forge' and hybrid Asian–European elms now widely planted.
