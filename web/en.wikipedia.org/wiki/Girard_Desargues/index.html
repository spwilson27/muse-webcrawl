Girard Desargues

Girard Desargues (21 February 1591 – September 1661) was a French mathematician and engineer, regarded as one of the founders of projective geometry. An architect and military engineer from Lyon, he moved in the circle of Marin Mersenne and was known to Descartes, Pascal and Fermat.

Work

Desargues's principal mathematical work, the Brouillon project d'une atteinte aux événements des rencontres du cône avec un plan ("Rough draft of an attempt to deal with the outcome of a meeting of a cone with a plane", 1639), treated the conic sections uniformly as projections of a circle, introducing points at infinity and the concept of involution. His name attaches to Desargues's theorem: if two triangles are in perspective from a point, then the intersections of corresponding sides are collinear, and conversely. He also wrote practical treatises on perspective for artists and craftsmen, on stonecutting (stéréotomie) and on sundials, and designed water-raising machinery and buildings in and around Paris.

Recognition

Only a few copies of the Brouillon project were printed, in an idiosyncratic vocabulary borrowed from botany, and the work was nearly lost; it survived through a manuscript copy made by Philippe de La Hire, rediscovered in the 19th century, by which time projective geometry had been recreated by Poncelet and others. The lunar crater Desargues is named after him.
