Polyphenism

A polyphenism is the trait in which multiple phenotypes can arise from a single genotype as a consequence of differing environmental conditions. It is thus a special case of phenotypic plasticity. The term polyphenism was coined by the evolutionary biologist Ernst Mayr. In contrast to polymorphism, where the different forms are genetically determined, polyphenic forms are produced by the same genome in response to environmental cues such as temperature, photoperiod, nutrition, or population density.

Types

Polyphenisms are usually discrete, with an organism developing into one of two or more distinct forms rather than a continuum. The switch between forms is typically controlled by hormones whose release is triggered by an environmental signal during a sensitive period of development.

Examples

Seasonal polyphenism in butterflies

Many butterflies, famously the African satyrine Bicyclus anynana studied by Paul Brakefield, produce wet-season forms with large ventral eyespots and dry-season forms with reduced eyespots, depending on the temperature experienced by the pupa. The map butterfly (Araschnia levana) has an orange spring form and a black-and-white summer form, once mistaken for separate species.

Caste in social insects

In ants, bees, and termites, genetically similar females develop into workers, soldiers, or queens depending on nutrition and pheromonal environment. In honey bees, larvae fed royal jelly throughout development become queens, mediated by epigenetic mechanisms including DNA methylation.

Other examples

Other examples include the winged and wingless morphs of aphids produced under crowding, the helmet-like defensive structures (cyclomorphosis) induced in Daphnia by predator chemicals, the spadefoot toad tadpoles that develop into either omnivore or carnivore morphs, horned and hornless male dung beetles determined by larval nutrition, and the temperature-dependent sex determination of many reptiles, sometimes treated as a polyphenism.

Mechanism and evolution

Polyphenic development is controlled by developmental switch mechanisms in which environmental signals alter hormone titres (such as ecdysteroids and juvenile hormone in insects), which in turn activate alternative gene-expression programmes. Polyphenisms are thought to evolve from continuous plasticity through genetic accommodation, and they allow a single genotype to match its phenotype to variable environments.
