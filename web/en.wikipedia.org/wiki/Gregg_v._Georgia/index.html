Gregg v. Georgia

Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States. The Court upheld the use of the death penalty in the United States, holding that capital punishment per se does not violate the Eighth Amendment's prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment, and that Georgia's new "guided discretion" sentencing scheme was constitutional. The decision effectively ended the de facto moratorium on executions that had followed Furman v. Georgia (1972).

Background

Troy Leon Gregg was convicted in Georgia of armed robbery and the murders of two men, Fred Edward Simmons and Bob Moore, whom he had picked up hitchhiking in November 1973. Under Georgia's revised post-Furman statute, his trial was bifurcated: after a guilty verdict, a separate sentencing hearing considered aggravating and mitigating circumstances, and the jury imposed death sentences. Gregg challenged the sentence as cruel and unusual punishment.

Decision

In a per curiam opinion joined in its key judgment by a 7–2 majority (with Justices Stewart, Powell and Stevens announcing the judgment of the Court), the Supreme Court held that the punishment of death does not invariably violate the Constitution, reasoning that the Eighth Amendment must be interpreted in light of "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society", and that capital punishment could serve the legitimate social purposes of retribution and deterrence. The Court found Georgia's guided-discretion procedures — bifurcated trials, specified aggravating circumstances, and automatic appellate review — sufficiently guarded against the arbitrary and capricious infliction of death sentences that had doomed the earlier statutes in Furman.

Aftermath

Executions resumed in the United States in January 1977 with the execution of Gary Gilmore in Utah. Gregg himself escaped from prison in 1979 and was killed in a bar fight the night before his scheduled execution. Gregg remains the foundational modern case on the constitutionality of capital punishment in the United States.
