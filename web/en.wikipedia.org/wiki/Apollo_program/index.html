Apollo program

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An Apollo astronaut standing beside the Lunar Module on the Moon's grey surface]
Apollo 11 on the lunar surface.

The Apollo program was NASA\'s human spaceflight programme that landed the first humans on the Moon. Announced by President John F. Kennedy in 1961, it achieved its goal with Apollo 11 on 20 July 1969, when Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed while Michael Collins orbited above. Six landings (1969–1972) brought twelve astronauts to the lunar surface, returned 382 kg of Moon rock, and cost about $25 billion — before the programme ended with Apollo 17.

Origins and development

Apollo answered the Soviet lead in the Space Race (Gagarin, April 1961). NASA chose lunar-orbit rendezvous: a Saturn V rocket launched the Command/Service Module and Lunar Module together. The programme nearly ended with the Apollo 1 fire (1967) that killed Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee; Apollo 8 first orbited the Moon in December 1968.

Landings

Apollo 11\'s Eagle landed in the Sea of Tranquility; five more landings explored highlands, rilles and mountains, with the last three missions using the Lunar Roving Vehicle. Apollo 13 (1970) survived an oxygen-tank explosion in a famous rescue. Skylab and the Apollo–Soyuz mission (1975) used Apollo hardware afterwards.

Legacy

Apollo remains the only programme to send humans beyond low Earth orbit. Its samples transformed lunar science, and its computers and management methods influenced technology industries. NASA\'s Artemis programme aims to return astronauts to the Moon in the later 2020s using the SLS rocket and Orion spacecraft.
