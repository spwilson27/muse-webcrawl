Foreign aid

Foreign aid is the voluntary transfer of resources from one country to another, typically for economic, humanitarian, or strategic purposes. Aid may be provided bilaterally (government to government), multilaterally through international organisations such as the World Bank and United Nations agencies, or by private charities and foundations. Official development assistance (ODA), as defined by the OECD Development Assistance Committee, is government aid designed to promote the economic development and welfare of developing countries, provided on concessional terms.

Types of aid

Aid takes many forms: grants and concessional loans; food aid and emergency humanitarian relief; technical assistance and capacity building; debt relief; military aid and security assistance; and project or budget support. "Tied aid" requires the recipient to spend the funds on goods and services from the donor country, a practice widely criticised for reducing value for money.

History

Modern foreign aid is usually dated to the post-Second World War period, with the United States' Marshall Plan (1948–1952) transferring over $13 billion to rebuild Western Europe — often considered the most successful aid programme in history. During the Cold War, aid from both superpower blocs was heavily influenced by geopolitical competition. The United Nations set a target for developed countries to give 0.7% of gross national income as ODA, a goal met by only a handful of donors. In the 21st century, major donors include the United States (the largest in absolute terms), Germany, the United Kingdom, Japan, and France, alongside growing South–South cooperation from China and others.

Effectiveness debate

The effectiveness of aid is vigorously debated. Supporters cite successes in public health (such as smallpox eradication, antiretroviral treatment, and vaccination programmes), famine relief, and infrastructure, while economists such as Jeffrey Sachs argue aid can help end extreme poverty. Critics such as William Easterly and Dambisa Moyo argue that much aid has failed to produce growth, can entrench corruption and dependency, and that trade and domestic reform matter more. Empirical research suggests aid works better in good policy environments and for specific, measurable interventions.
