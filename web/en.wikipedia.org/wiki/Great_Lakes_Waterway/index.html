Great Lakes Waterway

The Great Lakes Waterway (French: Voies navigables des Grands Lacs) is a system of natural channels and artificial canals in North America that enables large vessels to travel between all of the Great Lakes and into the Atlantic Ocean via the Saint Lawrence River. Together with the Saint Lawrence Seaway, it forms part of a single navigable system connecting the interior of the continent with the ocean.

Components

The waterway includes the locks and canals that overcome elevation differences between the lakes. From the Atlantic, vessels pass through the Saint Lawrence Seaway to Lake Ontario, then ascend the Welland Canal in Ontario, which bypasses Niagara Falls with eight locks and raises ships about 99.5 metres (326 feet) to Lake Erie. From Lake Erie, ships travel through Lake St. Clair and the Detroit and St. Clair rivers to Lake Huron, then through the St. Marys River and the Soo Locks at Sault Ste. Marie to Lake Superior. Lake Michigan is reached directly from Lake Huron through the Straits of Mackinac, being at the same elevation.

History and use

Successive canals have crossed the Welland route since 1829; the present fourth Welland Canal opened in 1932. The Soo Locks, first built in 1855, were enlarged repeatedly and are among the busiest lock systems in the world by tonnage. The modern system was completed with the opening of the Saint Lawrence Seaway in 1959.

The waterway carries bulk cargoes such as iron ore from Minnesota and the Labrador region, coal, grain, limestone, and cement, as well as some general cargo and cruise traffic. Navigation is seasonal, generally closing during the winter months when ice forms. Vessel size is limited by lock dimensions—the "Seawaymax" standard on the St. Lawrence and Welland, and larger "lakers" confined to the upper lakes by the Poe Lock at the Soo.
