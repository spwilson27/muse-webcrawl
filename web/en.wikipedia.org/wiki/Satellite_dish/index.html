Satellite dish

A satellite dish is a dish-shaped parabolic antenna designed to receive (or transmit) electromagnetic signals from a communications satellite. The parabolic reflector focuses the extremely weak incoming signal onto a feedhorn at its focal point, where a low-noise block downconverter (LNB) amplifies and down-converts it for a receiver. Domestic dishes used for satellite television are typically 45–90 cm across in temperate regions; large dishes of many metres are used for satellite communications gateways and radio astronomy.

Principle

The reflector's paraboloid shape reflects parallel rays arriving along its axis to a single focal point. Antenna gain rises with dish diameter and with frequency, so higher-frequency Ku-band and Ka-band services can use smaller dishes, while older C-band services need large "big ugly dishes." Because geostationary satellites appear fixed in the sky, most domestic dishes are stationary; motorised mounts can track multiple satellites along the geostationary arc.

History

Parabolic reflectors were used in radar in the Second World War. Hobbyists built C-band TVRO ("television receive-only") systems in the late 1970s, spawning a grey industry receiving unencrypted channel feeds. The launch of high-power direct broadcast satellites in the 1990s shrank dishes to under a metre and enabled mass-market services such as DirecTV and Sky. Modern phased-array flat antennas compete with dishes for satellite internet services.

Uses

Beyond television, dishes serve VSAT data links, satellite internet uplinks, weather-satellite reception and deep-space communications; the largest steerable dishes, such as the 100 m Green Bank Telescope, operate as radio telescopes.
