Ban Chiang

LocationNong Han District, Udon Thani Province, Thailand
TypeArchaeological site
PeriodsNeolithic, Bronze Age, Iron Age
DesignatedUNESCO World Heritage Site (1992)

Ban Chiang (Thai: บ้านเชียง) is an archaeological site in Nong Han District, Udon Thani Province, in northeastern Thailand. It is regarded as the most important prehistoric settlement yet discovered in Southeast Asia, famous for its distinctive red-on-buff painted pottery and for evidence of early bronze metallurgy. The site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1992.

Discovery

The site came to wider attention in 1966, when Harvard anthropology student Stephen Young noticed pottery fragments while conducting fieldwork in the village. Excavations by the Fine Arts Department of Thailand and the University of Pennsylvania in 1974–75, led by Chester Gorman and Pisit Charoenwongsa, uncovered burials and artefacts in stratified layers beneath the modern village.

Chronology

Ban Chiang was occupied from roughly the Neolithic period into the Iron Age. Early thermoluminescence dates suggested bronze working as early as about 2000 BCE or earlier, making it briefly a candidate for the world's earliest Bronze Age; later radiocarbon work placed bronze casting at the site around 1000 BCE. The associated culture practised wet-rice agriculture, raised domestic animals, and buried its dead with pottery, bronze tools and weapons, and later iron objects and glass beads.

Pottery

The site's most celebrated products are large earthenware vessels painted with red curvilinear and spiral designs on a buff slip, produced mainly in the late period of occupation. The pottery is displayed at the Ban Chiang National Museum at the site and in museums abroad.
