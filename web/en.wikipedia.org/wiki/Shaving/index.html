Shaving is the removal of hair, typically from the face, head, legs or underarms, by cutting it close to the skin with a razor. It has been practised since prehistoric times—Stone Age implements of flint, shell and shark teeth served as early razors—and remains one of the most common grooming routines worldwide. Men most often shave facial hair (beards and moustaches); many women shave the legs and underarms.

Traditional wet shaving uses a brush to work soap or cream into a lather before a single-blade razor.

History

Alexander the Great promoted shaving among his soldiers, partly so enemies could not grab their beards in combat, and smooth faces became fashionable in the Roman world, where the first shave (depositio barbae) marked a boy's coming of age. Barbers' shops became social institutions. Fashion swung repeatedly between bearded and clean-shaven norms across the centuries. The modern era began with the straight razor, followed by King Camp Gillette's disposable-blade safety razor (patented 1904), which made home shaving cheap and safe; cartridge systems followed from the 1970s, and Jacob Schick's electric razor (patented 1928, marketed 1931) offered a dry alternative.

Methods

Wet shaving uses water and a lubricant—soap, cream, gel or oil—to soften hair and reduce friction, with a straight razor, safety razor or multi-blade cartridge razor, usually followed by aftershave. Hair is ideally cut after softening with warm water, often "with the grain" to reduce irritation. Electric shaving uses foil or rotary shavers that trap and shear hairs without water. Common side effects include nicks, razor burn and ingrown hairs (pseudofolliculitis barbae, particularly common in men with curly hair, for which military and workplace shaving rules have sometimes been adapted). Depilatory creams and waxing are alternatives but are not, strictly, shaving.

Culture and rules

Shaving carries religious and social meanings: Orthodox Jewish law restricts shaving of parts of the beard; Sikh men do not cut their hair; many monks practise tonsure; and most armed forces require soldiers to be clean-shaven, partly to ensure a gas-mask seal. Clean-shaven norms dominated much Western professional life through the 20th century, though beards returned to mainstream fashion in the 2010s.
