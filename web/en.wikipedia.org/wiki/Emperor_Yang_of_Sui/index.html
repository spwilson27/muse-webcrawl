Emperor Yang of Sui

Emperor Yang of Sui (569 – 11 April 618), personal name Yang Guang, was the second emperor of the Sui dynasty of China. His reign completed some of the most ambitious public works in Chinese history, including the Grand Canal, but his extravagant projects and disastrous wars against Goguryeo exhausted the empire and provoked the rebellions that destroyed the dynasty.

Accession

Yang Guang was the second son of Emperor Wen, the dynasty's founder, and had commanded the armies that conquered the Chen dynasty in 589, reunifying China. After intriguing against his elder brother, the crown prince Yang Yong, he was made heir in 600, and on Emperor Wen's death in 604 — which some historians suspect Yang Guang hastened by murder — he took the throne.

Projects

Emperor Yang rebuilt Luoyang as a grand eastern capital, constructed palaces and granaries across the empire, restored sections of the Great Wall, and above all completed the Grand Canal: a network of waterways, dug by millions of conscripted labourers, linking the Yellow River and the capital region with the Yangtze delta and the south. The canal integrated north and south economically for over a thousand years, but at enormous human cost.

Wars and collapse

Between 612 and 614 Emperor Yang launched three massive invasions of the Korean kingdom of Goguryeo, each reportedly mobilising over a million soldiers; all failed with catastrophic losses. Rebellions spread across the empire from 611 onward. Abandoning the north, the emperor withdrew to Jiangdu (Yangzhou) in 616, where in April 618 he was strangled in a coup led by the general Yuwen Huaji. The Sui dynasty collapsed, and within a few years the Tang dynasty reunified China — inheriting, and benefiting from, many of Yang's creations.

Reputation

Traditional historiography, written under the Tang, portrays Emperor Yang as a archetypal tyrant — extravagant, cruel, and lustful — and gave him the unusually derogatory posthumous name "Yang" (煬, "slothful/cruel"). Modern historians offer a more mixed assessment, crediting his canal and administrative works while condemning the scale of his exactions.
