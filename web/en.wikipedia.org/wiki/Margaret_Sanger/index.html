Margaret Sanger

Sanger in 1922

Margaret Sanger (born Margaret Louise Higgins; September 14, 1879 – September 6, 1966) was an American birth control activist, sex educator, writer, and nurse. She popularized the term "birth control", opened the first birth control clinic in the United States, and established organizations that evolved into the Planned Parenthood Federation of America.

Early activism

Working as a nurse among poor immigrant families on New York's Lower East Side, Sanger saw frequent pregnancies, miscarriages and deaths from self-induced abortion. In 1914 she launched the newsletter The Woman Rebel, challenging the Comstock laws that classified contraceptive information as obscene, and fled briefly to Europe to avoid prosecution. On 16 October 1916 she opened a clinic in Brownsville, Brooklyn, which police raided after nine days, but the ensuing court case led to a ruling allowing doctors to prescribe contraception for medical reasons.

Organizations and later work

Sanger founded the American Birth Control League in 1921, which merged with other groups and was renamed Planned Parenthood in 1942. She founded the International Planned Parenthood Federation in 1952 and in the 1950s helped raise funds for Gregory Pincus's research that produced the first oral contraceptive pill, approved in 1960.

Controversy

Sanger's legacy is debated: her advocacy is credited with advancing women's health and reproductive freedom, while some of her writings and alliances, particularly her support for aspects of eugenics that were mainstream in her era, have been widely criticised, including by Planned Parenthood itself, which in 2020 removed her name from its Manhattan clinic.
