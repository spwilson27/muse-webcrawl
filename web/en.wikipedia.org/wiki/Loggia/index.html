Loggia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A loggia (Italian: [ˈlɔddʒa]) is an architectural feature: a roofed gallery or corridor that is open to the air on one or more sides, typically supported by columns or arches and forming part of a building rather than standing apart from it. Loggias are characteristic of Italian architecture, where they provide shaded outdoor space protected from sun and rain, and they have been copied in palaces, villas, churches, and public buildings across Europe and beyond.

Characteristics and history

Unlike a portico, which projects from a building like a porch, a loggia is recessed within the building's volume, often on an upper floor and sometimes stacked in tiers. Unlike a veranda it is an integral, load-bearing part of the structure, usually of masonry, with an architectural treatment matching the façade. Famous examples include the Loggia dei Lanzi in Florence (built 1376–1382), an open-air sculpture gallery beside the Palazzo Vecchio; the loggia of the Ospedale degli Innocenti by Brunelleschi, a landmark of early Renaissance architecture; and Raphael's Loggias in the Apostolic Palace in the Vatican, decorated with frescoes by Raphael's workshop in 1518–19 and later imitated at the Hermitage in St Petersburg. Municipal loggias served as covered market and meeting spaces in Italian towns, and the form persisted through the Baroque and into 19th- and 20th-century revival styles such as Italianate and Mediterranean Revival houses, many of which feature a recessed arched loggia as their principal architectural ornament.
