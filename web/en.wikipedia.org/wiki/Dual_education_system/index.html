Dual education system

Workplace-based training is one of the two pillars of the dual system

The dual education system (German: duales Ausbildungssystem) is a model of vocational education and training in which apprentices split their learning between two venues: a company, where they receive practical, on-the-job training, and a vocational school (Berufsschule), where they study the theoretical and general-education components of their occupation. The system is most fully developed in Germany, and versions of it operate in Austria, Switzerland, South Tyrol, Denmark and several other countries.

Structure

A typical dual programme lasts between two and three and a half years. Trainees sign a contract with an employer, are paid a training wage, and typically spend three to four days per week in the company and one to two days in the vocational school. Training occupations (Ausbildungsberufe) — around 320 recognised occupations in Germany — are defined by national training regulations agreed with employers' associations and trade unions, and final examinations are administered by the chambers of industry and commerce or crafts.

History

The system's roots lie in the medieval guilds and in nineteenth-century German continuation schools. Its modern form was codified by the Vocational Training Act (Berufsbildungsgesetz) of 1969, which established the shared responsibility of the federal government, the Länder, employers and unions for the system.

Assessment

The dual system is credited with Germany's comparatively low youth unemployment and smooth school-to-work transitions, and it is frequently promoted internationally as a model for export. Critics point to declining numbers of firms offering training places, mismatches between applicants and vacancies, low training wages in some occupations, and the difficulty of reproducing the system in countries without strong chambers and collective institutions.
