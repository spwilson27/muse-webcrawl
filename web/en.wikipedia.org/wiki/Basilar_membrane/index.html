Basilar membrane

The basilar membrane is a stiff structural element within the cochlea of the inner ear that separates two fluid-filled tubes, the scala media and the scala tympani, and supports the organ of Corti, where the sensory hair cells sit. It is central to the sense of hearing in mammals.

Frequency analysis

The membrane's mechanical properties vary along its length: it is narrow and stiff at the base of the cochlea and wider and more flexible at the apex. When sound vibrates the stapes, a travelling wave runs along the membrane, peaking at a place determined by frequency — high frequencies near the base, low frequencies near the apex. This tonotopic arrangement, described by Georg von Békésy (Nobel Prize, 1961), performs a mechanical frequency analysis that underlies pitch perception.

Hair cells and amplification

Inner hair cells on the membrane transduce its motion into nerve signals, while outer hair cells actively amplify the travelling wave (the cochlear amplifier), sharpening tuning and giving the ear its sensitivity and frequency selectivity.
