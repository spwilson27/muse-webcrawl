Linear free-energy relationship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physical organic chemistry, a linear free-energy relationship (LFER) describes the relationship between the logarithm of a reaction rate constant or equilibrium constant for one series of chemical reactions and the logarithm of the rate or equilibrium constant for a related series of reactions. Because the logarithm of a rate or equilibrium constant is proportional to a free energy, such correlations are linear relations between free energies.

Examples

The earliest widely used example is the Brønsted catalysis equation (1924), relating acid strength to catalytic activity. The best known is the Hammett equation, developed by Louis Plack Hammett in the 1930s: log(k/k₀) = σρ, where σ is a substituent constant derived from the ionisation of substituted benzoic acids and ρ (rho) is a reaction constant characterising the reaction's sensitivity to substituent effects. Related treatments include the Taft equation for aliphatic systems, the Grunwald–Winstein equation for solvent effects on solvolysis, and Swain–Scott and Edwards equations for nucleophilicity.

Interpretation

A successful linear correlation within a reaction series implies that substituents affect the transition state and the reference equilibrium in a proportional way, i.e. that the reaction mechanism is constant across the series. Deviations from linearity — breaks in a Hammett plot — are therefore taken as evidence of a change in mechanism or rate-determining step.
