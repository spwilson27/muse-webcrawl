The 1952 steel strike was a United Steelworkers of America (USWA) strike against the major American steel producers that lasted 53 days, from June 2 to July 24, 1952. The strike is best remembered for the constitutional crisis that preceded it, when President Harry S. Truman seized the nation's steel mills to avert a strike during the Korean War, and the U.S. Supreme Court held the seizure unlawful in Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer.

Background

The USWA's contract with the steel companies expired at the end of 1951. The union sought a wage increase and a union shop. Because steel was essential to the Korean War effort, the dispute went to the Wage Stabilization Board, which recommended a settlement of roughly 26 cents per hour in wages and benefits. The steel companies refused unless the government allowed compensating price increases, which the Office of Price Stabilization would not grant.

The seizure and Youngstown

On April 8, 1952, hours before a scheduled strike, Truman issued Executive Order 10340 seizing the steel mills under government control. The companies sued, and on June 2, 1952, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that the President lacked authority to seize the mills: Justice Hugo Black's majority opinion found no constitutional or statutory basis, and Justice Robert H. Jackson's concurring framework for evaluating presidential power became the case's most influential legacy.

The strike and settlement

The steelworkers walked out the day of the decision. The 53-day strike idled most of the industry during the war. A settlement was reached on July 24, 1952: workers received about 21.5 cents per hour in combined increases, and the companies received a price increase of about $5.20 per ton. Philip Murray, who had led the USWA since 1942, died a few months later.
