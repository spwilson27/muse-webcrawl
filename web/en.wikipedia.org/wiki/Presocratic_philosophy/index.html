Pre-Socratic philosophy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Pre-Socratic philosophy flourished in the Greek cities of Ionia and Magna Graecia from the 6th century BC.

Pre-Socratic philosophy is ancient Greek philosophy before Socrates (c. 470–399 BC). The pre-Socratic philosophers, who were active from the early 6th century BC onwards, were the first thinkers in the Western tradition to attempt to explain the world through reason and observation rather than through myth and religion. Their central questions concerned the origin and nature of the universe (physis), the fundamental substance or principle (archē) of all things, and the possibility of change and permanence.

Schools and figures

The earliest school, the Milesians of Miletus in Ionia, proposed single material principles: Thales held that the archē was water, Anaximander proposed the boundless apeiron, and Anaximenes identified air. Heraclitus of Ephesus taught that all things are in flux, unified by the logos. In reaction, Parmenides of Elea and his followers the Eleatics argued that being is one, changeless, and indivisible, and that motion and plurality are illusions—a position defended by Zeno's famous paradoxes.

Later pluralists tried to reconcile change with Parmenidean logic. Empedocles posited four roots (earth, air, fire, water) mixed and separated by Love and Strife; Anaxagoras proposed an infinite mixture ordered by Mind (nous); and the atomists Leucippus and Democritus explained all phenomena through indivisible atoms moving in the void. The Pythagoreans, followers of Pythagoras, held that numbers and mathematical relations constitute the true nature of reality.

Sources

No complete works of the pre-Socratics survive. Their thought is known from fragments quoted by later authors and from summaries (doxography) in writers such as Aristotle, Theophrastus, and Diogenes Laertius. The standard collection of the texts is the Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker of Hermann Diels and Walther Kranz, whose "DK numbers" remain the standard way of citing fragments.

Legacy

The pre-Socratics mark the transition from mythological to rational explanation in Greek thought and laid the foundations for natural science, metaphysics, and logic. Their works were read and reinterpreted by Plato and Aristotle, and in the modern era by philosophers such as Friedrich Nietzsche and Martin Heidegger.
