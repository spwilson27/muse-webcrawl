Schiehallion experiment

Schiehallion experiment
Date1774
LocationSchiehallion, Perthshire, Scotland
Lead astronomerNevil Maskelyne
PurposeMeasure Earth's mean density

The Schiehallion experiment was an 18th-century scientific experiment to determine the mean density of the Earth. Funded by the Royal Society, it was conducted in the summer of 1774 around the isolated Scottish mountain Schiehallion in Perthshire.

IMAGE: The symmetrical cone of Schiehallion mountain in Perthshire, Scotland, photographed from across Loch Rannoch

Method

The experiment measured the tiny deflection of a plumb line caused by the gravitational attraction of the mountain. Astronomer Royal Nevil Maskelyne observed star positions from observatories on the north and south sides of the mountain — drawing on star catalogue positions — while surveyor Charles Hutton surveyed the mountain's volume. To compute its volume, Hutton invented contour lines, joining points of equal elevation — a lasting contribution to cartography. Comparing the deflection with the mountain's modelled mass yielded an estimate of Earth's density, roughly 4.5 times that of water in Hutton's final analysis, close to the modern value of about 5.5.

Legacy

The experiment was a landmark in geodesy — the study of Earth's shape and gravity field, closely related to the geometry of the terrestrial spheroid — and paved the way for Cavendish's later laboratory determination of the gravitational constant.
