Arsphenamine, marketed as Salvarsan, was the first effective treatment for syphilis and the first modern chemotherapeutic agent. Developed in the laboratory of Paul Ehrlich and first marketed in 1910, it was an organoarsenic compound famously known as compound 606.


Salvarsan, Ehrlich's "magic bullet"

Discovery

Ehrlich's programme sought a "magic bullet" — a compound toxic to a pathogen but not its host. His assistant Sahachiro Hata showed in 1909 that preparation 606, synthesised by Alfred Bertheim, cured syphilis-infected rabbits. Clinical success followed, and by 1910 Salvarsan was the most widely prescribed drug in the world.

Use and difficulties

Arsphenamine had to be freshly dissolved and injected intravenously with great care; the solution oxidised rapidly and the drug caused significant side effects, including rashes, liver damage and the Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction. A more soluble derivative, Neosalvarsan (compound 914), followed in 1912. Arsenical therapy remained the standard for syphilis — often combined with mercury or bismuth — until penicillin superseded it in the 1940s.

Structure

For nearly a century arsphenamine was drawn as a simple arsenic–arsenic double-bonded dimer by analogy with azobenzene; mass-spectrometric work in 2005 showed it is actually a mixture of cyclic trimers and pentamers of the arsenobenzene unit.
