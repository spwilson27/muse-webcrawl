C-command

In generative linguistics, c-command (short for constituent command) is a fundamental structural relation between nodes in a syntactic tree. It plays a central role in the theory of binding, in the characterization of anaphora and pronoun coreference, and in the explanation of scope phenomena such as those involving quantifiers and negation.

Definition

The modern definition, due to Tanya Reinhart (1976), states that a node X c-commands a node Y if and only if:

  • X does not dominate Y and Y does not dominate X;
  • the lowest branching node that dominates X also dominates Y.

Informally, a node c-commands its sister node and everything its sister dominates. An earlier version of the notion, called simply "command", was introduced by Klima (1964) in work on negation, and the term c-command was introduced by Lasnik (1976).

Uses

C-command is crucial to the binding conditions of Government and Binding theory. Conditions A and B of the binding theory, for example, state that an anaphor must be bound — that is, coindexed with a c-commanding antecedent — within a certain local domain, while a pronoun must be free (not bound) within the same domain. The relation also underlies the standard account of why himself can be an antecedent in some sentences but not others, and why coreference is blocked in so-called crossover configurations.

Beyond binding, c-command constrains quantifier scope: in many configurations a quantifier can only take scope over material that it c-commands. Theories of reconstruction, weak crossover, and negative polarity item licensing all appeal to c-command or closely related notions.

Related notions

Related structural relations include m-command (Aoun and Sportiche), in which the dominating node relevant to the definition is the first maximal projection, and the looser notion of precedence combined with c-command, which together define the asymmetric relations used in the Linear Correspondence Axiom of Kayne's antisymmetry theory.
