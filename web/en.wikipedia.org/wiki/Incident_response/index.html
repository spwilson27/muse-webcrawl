Incident response is the organised approach an organisation takes to prepare for, detect, contain and recover from a disruptive event — in information security, typically a cyberattack, data breach or system compromise; more broadly the term also covers operational incidents such as outages. Effective incident response aims to limit damage, reduce recovery time and cost, and preserve evidence, while learning from the event to prevent recurrence.

Lifecycle

The widely used NIST SP 800-61 model describes four phases:

  1. Preparation — establishing an incident response team (often a CSIRT), policies, communication plans, tooling and training before any incident occurs.
  2. Detection and analysis — identifying that an incident has occurred from alerts, logs, user reports or threat intelligence; triaging severity and scope, and preserving forensic evidence.
  3. Containment, eradication and recovery — short-term containment to stop the incident spreading (e.g. isolating hosts), long-term containment while systems are rebuilt, removal of the root cause such as malware or compromised accounts, and restoring systems to normal operation with heightened monitoring.
  4. Post-incident activity — a "lessons learned" review (sometimes called a postmortem) documenting what happened, how well the response worked, and what should change; reports may also be required by regulators or breach-notification laws.

The SANS Institute's PICERL model expands the cycle into six steps: Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery and Lessons Learned.

Practice

Organisations codify their approach in an incident response plan and supporting playbooks for common scenarios such as ransomware, phishing or insider misuse. Tabletop exercises and simulated incidents test readiness. Digital forensics and incident response (DFIR) combines evidence collection — memory captures, disk images and log analysis, handled under chain-of-custody procedures — with the operational work of containment and recovery. Severity classification schemes prioritise response effort, and external parties such as law enforcement, insurers and retained incident-response firms may be engaged for major events.
