Semantic dementia (SD), also known as the semantic variant of primary progressive aphasia (svPPA), is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a profound loss of semantic memory in both verbal and non-verbal domains. It is one of the three canonical clinical syndromes associated with frontotemporal lobar degeneration, alongside behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia and nonfluent/agrammatic primary progressive aphasia.

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark of semantic dementia is a fluent but progressively empty speech: patients lose the meanings of words, asking questions such as "What is a zebra?" while retaining fluent articulation, grammar, and phonology. Naming of objects and comprehension of single words are severely impaired, while repetition, reading regular words, day-to-day episodic memory, and visuospatial skills are relatively preserved early on. Surface dyslexia — reading irregular words as if they were regular (e.g., pronouncing "pint" to rhyme with "mint") — is characteristic.

Pathology and neuroimaging

Semantic dementia is associated with focal, often asymmetric atrophy of the anterior temporal lobes, typically worse on the left. The underlying pathology most often involves TDP-43 protein inclusions (FTLD-TDP type C). The anterior temporal lobes are thought to act as a transmodal "hub" for semantic knowledge, explaining why conceptual loss cuts across modalities.

Treatment

There is no disease-modifying treatment. Management focuses on speech and language therapy, including word-relearning approaches that can temporarily restore vocabulary for trained items, and on supportive care as the disease progresses to involve behaviour and broader cognition.
