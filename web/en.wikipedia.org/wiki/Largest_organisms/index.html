Largest organisms

This article lists the largest organisms on Earth, measured by mass, volume, area, length, height, or other criteria. The answer depends on how an "organism" is defined: the largest single animals and trees are individual bodies, while the very largest living things are clonal colonies — genetically identical organisms spread over huge areas — such as aspen groves, seagrass meadows, and fungal mycelia. The blue whale is the largest animal ever known to have existed, while certain fungi and clonal plants exceed every animal in area and mass.

Clonal giants

In Oregon's Malheur National Forest, a single genetic individual of the honey fungus Armillaria ostoyae extends over about 9.6 km² (roughly 3.7 square miles) and is estimated to be thousands of years old; it is widely cited as the largest organism by area. A related claim comes from Michigan, where an Armillaria gallica individual covers about 0.75 km². Among plants, Pando, a clonal grove of quaking aspen in Utah, consists of some 47,000 stems arising from one root system over 43 hectares, with an estimated mass of about 6,000 tonnes — among the heaviest known organisms. In 2022 researchers reported that a meadow of the seagrass Posidonia australis in Shark Bay, Western Australia, is a single clone spanning some 200 km², making it arguably the largest known plant — and possibly the largest organism — by area.

Animals

The blue whale (Balaenoptera musculus), at lengths approaching 30 m and masses up to about 180 tonnes, is the largest animal known, larger than any dinosaur. The longest animals include the lion's mane jellyfish and bootlace worm. On land, the African bush elephant is the largest living land animal; the extinct Paraceratherium and the great sauropod dinosaurs were far larger. The largest land carnivore is the polar bear (with its close relative the Kodiak brown bear).

Trees

By volume, giant sequoias of California's Sierra Nevada dominate: General Sherman holds about 1,487 m³ of wood and masses well over 1,000 tonnes. By height, coast redwoods exceed 115 m, with Hyperion the tallest known living tree; historical Australian mountain ash may have been taller. The broadest trunks belong to baobabs and the Árbol del Tule, a Montezuma cypress in Mexico.
