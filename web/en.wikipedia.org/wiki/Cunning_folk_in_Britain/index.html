Cunning folk in Britain

[Image: A nineteenth-century cunning woman consulting a client in a cottage interior]

Cunning folk, also known as wise men and wise women, were professional practitioners of folk medicine, traditional healing, and divination in Britain from the medieval period until the early twentieth century. They were a common feature of rural and urban communities, consulted by people from all social levels for help with illness, lost or stolen property, and suspected bewitchment.

Services

Clients sought out cunning folk to heal people and livestock, find lost or stolen goods, predict the future, remove curses, and counteract witchcraft — a service known as "unwitching". Their methods combined herbal remedies, charms and incantations (often Christian in form), astrology, and the use of magical objects such as witch bottles. Notable practitioners included figures such as James Murrell of Hadleigh in Essex and Tamsin Blight of Cornwall.

Distinction from witches

Although they practised magic, cunning folk generally distinguished themselves from the witches they were hired to fight: they were seen as useful and morally good members of the community, whereas witches were believed to work harm. Nevertheless, the Witchcraft Acts theoretically criminalised their activities, and some cunning folk were prosecuted, particularly under the Witchcraft Act 1735, which treated claims of magical power as fraud.

Decline

The profession declined during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries with the spread of professional medicine, police forces, insurance, and changing religious attitudes, though folk healers survived in some areas into the 1900s. Historians such as Owen Davies and Emma Wilby have extensively documented their role in British popular culture.
