Sallen–Key topology

The Sallen–Key topology is an electronic filter topology used to implement second-order active filters, including low-pass, high-pass and band-pass designs. It was introduced by R. P. Sallen and E. L. Key of MIT Lincoln Laboratory in 1955. The circuit uses a single operational amplifier configured as a non-inverting amplifier, together with two resistors and two capacitors in the frequency-selective network.

Design

In the low-pass version, the input passes through two series resistors with capacitors shunting to the amplifier output and to ground, respectively; swapping the roles of resistors and capacitors yields the high-pass version. The cutoff frequency is set by the RC products, while the quality factor (Q) is determined by the component ratios and the amplifier gain. A common special case uses equal resistors and equal capacitors with a gain of about 1.586 to obtain a Butterworth (maximally flat) response. With unity gain (amplifier as a voltage follower), the circuit is simple and needs few components, though its Q then depends heavily on component ratios and is sensitive to tolerances.

Applications

Sallen–Key stages are widely used in audio equipment, instrumentation, data-acquisition front ends and anti-aliasing filters, and are cascaded to build higher-order Butterworth, Bessel and Chebyshev responses. Higher-order filters built from cascaded Sallen–Key sections are a staple of analogue signal processing because of the topology's low component count and its non-inverting, high-input-impedance buffer.
