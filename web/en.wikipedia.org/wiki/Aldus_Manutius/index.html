Aldus Pius Manutius (c. 1449/1452 – 1515) was an Italian humanist scholar and printer who founded the Aldine Press in Venice in 1494. He revolutionised publishing by producing accurate, affordable editions of Greek and Latin classics in a revolutionary small format, and his innovations — above all italic type — shaped the printed book for centuries.

The Aldine Press

Manutius set up his press in Venice, then Europe's printing capital, with the aim of rescuing Greek literature from decay. Working with scholars such as Erasmus (who lived at the press in 1508), he published major editions of Aristotle, Aristophanes, Thucydides and other Greek authors, many of them editiones principes (first printed editions). His printer's device — a dolphin entwined with an anchor, symbolising the motto festina lente ("make haste slowly") — became one of the most famous marks in publishing.

Innovations

In 1501 Manutius issued a Virgil in a small octavo format — a portable "pocket book" intended for reading rather than library display — set in the new slanted type cut by Francesco Griffo that became known as italic, originally designed as a compact book hand that could fit more text per page. He also standardised typographic conventions including the modern comma and semicolon. His books were so prized that counterfeiters in Lyon imitated them, prompting Manutius to publish warnings to readers.

Legacy

The press continued under his son Paulus and grandson Aldus the Younger until 1597. Aldine editions are today treasures of rare-book collections, and Aldus is commemorated in projects from the Aldine typefaces to the name of the Aldus Corporation, maker of PageMaker.
