The horizontal branch (HB) is the region of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram occupied by low-mass stars immediately after the red-giant branch. Horizontal-branch stars burn helium in their cores (the triple-alpha process) and hydrogen in a surrounding shell; their name comes from their roughly horizontal distribution—nearly constant luminosity with a wide range of surface temperature—in the colour–magnitude diagrams of globular clusters.

Evolution

A low-mass star ascends the red-giant branch with an inert, degenerate helium core. When the core reaches about 100 million kelvin, helium ignites explosively in the helium flash; the star's structure then rearranges, its envelope contracts, and it settles on the horizontal branch as a smaller, hotter star of roughly similar luminosity. The star's position along the branch depends chiefly on the mass of its remaining hydrogen envelope: stars that lost more mass on the giant branch sit hotter and bluer. After core helium exhaustion (about 108 years), the star ascends the asymptotic giant branch.

Morphology and the second-parameter problem

Metal-poor globular clusters typically have blue horizontal branches and metal-rich ones red clumps of HB stars near the giant branch. However, clusters of similar metallicity can have quite different HB morphologies—the "second-parameter problem"—attributed to differences in age, helium abundance, rotation or mass loss. Parts of the branch cross the instability strip, producing the pulsating RR Lyrae variables, which serve as standard candles for measuring distances to old stellar populations.

Importance

Horizontal-branch stars dominate the ultraviolet output of old elliptical galaxies (contributing to the "UV upturn"), and their numbers and distribution are used to calibrate models of mass loss, cluster ages and chemical enrichment. Extreme horizontal-branch (subdwarf B) stars, stripped almost entirely of hydrogen, are the likely progenitors of some white dwarfs that skip the second giant ascent.
