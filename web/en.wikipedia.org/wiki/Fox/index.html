Foxes are small-to-medium-sized omnivorous mammals belonging to several genera of the family Canidae. They have a flattened skull; upright, triangular ears; a pointed, slightly upturned snout; and a long, bushy tail. Twelve species belong to the monophyletic "true fox" group of genus Vulpes. Approximately another 25 current or extinct species are referred to as foxes; these foxes are either part of the paraphyletic group of the South American foxes, or of the outlying group consisting of the bat-eared fox, grey foxes, and island fox.

Distribution and habitat

Foxes live on every continent except Antarctica. The most common and widespread species of fox is the red fox (Vulpes vulpes), with about 47 recognised subspecies. Foxes inhabit habitats from Arctic tundra (the Arctic fox) to deserts (the fennec fox, the world's smallest fox species). The global distribution of foxes, together with their widespread reputation for cunning, has contributed to their prominence in popular culture and folklore in many societies around the world.

Behaviour and diet

Foxes are generally solitary hunters, unlike pack-hunting canids such as wolves. They feed on rodents, rabbits, birds, insects, eggs, fruit and other small prey, caching surplus food. Foxes hear low-frequency sounds exceptionally well and use a characteristic pounce, guided partly by the Earth's magnetic field, to catch prey hidden under vegetation or snow. A male fox is called a tod or dog, a female a vixen, and the young are cubs, pups or kits; a group is a skulk or leash.

Relationship with humans

Foxes have long been hunted for fur and sport, farmed for fur, and celebrated as trickster figures in folklore — from Aesop's fables and Reynard the Fox to the fox spirits of East Asia. In the famous Russian farm-fox experiment begun in 1959, silver foxes bred for tameness developed dog-like traits, a celebrated case study in domestication.
