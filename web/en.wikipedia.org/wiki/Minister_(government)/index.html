Minister (government)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A minister is a politician who heads a ministry, making and implementing decisions on policies in conjunction with other ministers; the term derives from Latin minister, a servant or attendant — the minister as the sovereign's (later the public's) servant. Ministers form the political executive in parliamentary systems, are collectively organised in the cabinet, and are individually responsible to the legislature for their departments (ministerial responsibility), a convention enforced by resignation in cases of serious failure or scandal, unevenly across countries and eras.

Ranks and roles

Hierarchies run from cabinet ministers (secretaries of state in the UK and US senses; federal ministers elsewhere) through ministers of state and junior ministers to parliamentary private secretaries, with titles varying (secretary, minister, chancellor for finance in some states, attorney general for law officers). Ministers direct civil-service departments without (in principle) administering them personally, answer questions and steer legislation, and observe collective responsibility — public unity or resignation. US usage differs: "ministers" are diplomatic or religious figures, and department heads are "secretaries" confirmed by the Senate; translations of foreign systems routinely sand off these differences.
