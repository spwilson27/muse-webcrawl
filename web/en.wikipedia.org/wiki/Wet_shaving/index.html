Wet shaving

Wet shaving

Razors usedSafety razor · Straight razor · Cartridge razor

Wet shaving is a method of shaving in which the face is prepared and lubricated with water and a lather generated from shaving soap or cream, typically applied with a shaving brush, before the hair is cut with a razor. It contrasts with dry shaving using an electric razor. Traditional wet shaving usually refers to the use of a single-blade safety razor or a straight razor with brush-made lather, rather than canned foam or gel.

Method

The beard is first softened with warm water or a hot towel. A brush — traditionally badger, boar, horse hair, or modern synthetics — is loaded with soap and worked in a bowl or directly on the face to build a dense, hydrating lather that lifts the hairs and lubricates the skin. The razor is drawn with the grain, then, after relathering, optionally across or against it. The shave finishes with a cold rinse and, commonly, an alum block or an alcohol-based or balm aftershave.

History and revival

Wet shaving with a brush and soap was the standard method from the 19th century until the mid-20th, when aerosol foams and multi-blade cartridge razors, and later electric razors, displaced it. King Camp Gillette's disposable-blade safety razor (patented 1904) had itself been a wet-shaving innovation. Since the 2000s, a hobbyist revival — driven by cost savings on blades, reduced skin irritation for some users, and the ritual appeal of the process — has supported a market of artisan soaps, restored vintage razors, and online communities devoted to the practice.
