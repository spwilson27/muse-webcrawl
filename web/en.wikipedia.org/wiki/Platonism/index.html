Platonism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Platonism is the philosophy of Plato and the philosophical systems derived from it, centred on the theory of Forms: the view that abstract objects and universals exist in a realm distinct from, and more real than, the changing material world perceived by the senses. The term also describes the continuation of Plato's thought through the Old Academy, Middle Platonism, Neoplatonism, and later traditions in Christian, Islamic and Renaissance philosophy.

Central ideas

Platonism holds that for each kind of thing there is a perfect exemplar — the Form — in which sensible particulars "participate". Knowledge (episteme) is directed at the Forms, whereas the senses yield only opinion (doxa) about the world of becoming. Plato's ethics and politics are built on this metaphysics: the soul is immortal and tripartite, the just city mirrors the just soul, and the philosopher who has apprehended the Form of the Good is fit to rule.

Historical development

After Plato's death the Academy passed through sceptical phases under Arcesilaus and Carneades. In the Roman era, Middle Platonists such as Plutarch systematised Plato's doctrines, and in the third century AD Plotinus founded Neoplatonism, organising reality into a hierarchy emanating from the One through Intellect and Soul. Neoplatonism deeply influenced Augustine and medieval theology; Plato's works, preserved in Byzantium and the Islamic world, returned to Western prominence in the Renaissance through Marsilio Ficino's translations.

Modern usage

In contemporary philosophy, "Platonism" most often refers to the view in metaphysics and the philosophy of mathematics — also called Platonic realism — that abstract objects such as numbers and sets exist independently of minds and language. Figures such as Gottlob Frege and Kurt Gödel defended versions of this view.
