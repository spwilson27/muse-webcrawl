German gold mark

Unit: Mark (ℳ)
Introduced: 1871/1873
Subunit: 1⁄100 Pfennig
Standard: Gold (0.358 g fine gold per mark)
Superseded by: Papiermark (1914–1923)

The German gold mark (German: Goldmark) was the currency of the German Empire from 1873 until 1914. It was backed by gold at a rate of 2,790 marks per kilogram of fine gold, so each mark contained about 0.358 grams of gold. One mark was divided into 100 Pfennig.

History

Before unification, the German states used a variety of currencies based on the Vereinsthaler and the South German gulden. After the founding of the Empire in 1871, currency legislation of 1871 and 1873 introduced the mark as a single imperial currency on the gold standard, and the older state currencies were withdrawn by 1876. The name "Goldmark" is retrospective: it was applied after 1914 to distinguish the gold-backed currency from the inconvertible paper mark that followed.

Coins and notes

Gold coins of 5, 10, and 20 marks were struck bearing the portraits of the various state rulers, alongside silver subsidiary coins and Reichsbank notes. The imperial mint system retained mints in Berlin, Munich, Dresden, Stuttgart, Karlsruhe, Darmstadt, and Hamburg.

End

With the outbreak of World War I in August 1914, gold convertibility was suspended and the Reichsbank began issuing unbacked paper money. The resulting Papiermark eventually collapsed in the hyperinflation of 1923, after which the Rentenmark and then the Reichsmark restored stability.
