Ice beer

Ice beer is a style of beer that is partially frozen during production, in a process derived from the German eisbock tradition. By chilling the beer until ice crystals form and then removing the ice, brewers concentrate the remaining liquid, slightly raising its alcohol content and producing a smooth flavor. Ice beers typically have an alcohol by volume of about 5.5 to 5.9 percent, higher than regular lagers.

Process

In ice brewing, a finished lager is cooled to below freezing, around −4 °C (25 °F), so that a portion of its water freezes into crystals. Because alcohol freezes at a much lower temperature than water, the ice that forms is mostly water; removing it leaves a beer that is proportionally stronger and, according to brewers, smoother, since some harsh-tasting proteins and polyphenols are removed with the ice or precipitated by the cold.

History

The technique descends from eisbock, a strong beer from Kulmbach, Germany, traditionally made by freezing bock beer and removing the ice. Ice beer as a mass-market category emerged in the early 1990s. Molson introduced Molson Ice in Canada and the United States in 1993 using a trademarked "Ice Brewing" process; Labatt followed with Labatt Ice, and major American brewers launched competing brands such as Bud Ice (1994) and Milwaukee's Best Ice. The style became a significant segment of the North American beer market in the mid-1990s, marketed with imagery of cold and smoothness.

Regulation

In the United States, labeling rules historically restricted how brewers could describe ice beers, since removing water to concentrate alcohol raised regulatory questions about whether the product was still beer. In Canada, ice beer is a standard category of strong lager. Despite the name, ice beer is not served with ice and is not necessarily served colder than other beers.
