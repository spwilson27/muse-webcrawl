The Kara-Khanid Khanate (also spelled Qarakhanid or Karakhanid) was a Turkic khanate that ruled Central Asia from 840 to 1212. Formed by Karluks and other Turkic peoples after the fall of the Uyghur Khaganate, it conquered Transoxiana from the Samanids in 999 and became the first Turkic dynasty to adopt Islam on a large scale. Its lands, stretching from the Chu valley to Kashgar and Khwarazm's borders, are associated with the flowering of early Turkic Islamic literature, including Yusuf Khass Hajib's Kutadgu Bilig and Mahmud al-Kashgari's dictionary of Turkic languages. Origins and conversion to Islam

The khanate arose among the Karluk Turks and allied tribes (Chigils, Yagmas, Tuhsis) of Semirechye and the Chu valley after 840. Its rulers traced descent to the legendary Afrasiyab, hence the Persian historians' name for the dynasty, Al-i Afrasiyab. Around the mid-10th century the khan Satuq Bughra Khan converted to Islam — traditionally through contact with Samanid merchants and exiles — and his descendants made Islam the state religion, the first major conversion of a Turkic ruling house.

Conquest of Transoxiana

In the 990s the Kara-Khanids, led by Nasr ibn Ali (Ilig Khan), occupied Bukhara and Samarkand, ending the Iranian Samanid dynasty in 999. By agreement with Mahmud of Ghazna the Oxus (Amu Darya) became the frontier between the two powers. The khanate was governed as a family confederation with multiple khans and appanage capitals — Balasagun in the east, Kashgar, Uzgend, Samarkand, and Bukhara — a structure that fostered internal rivalry. In the 1040s the state formally split into Eastern and Western khanates.

Division and decline

The Western Khanate, centred on Samarkand and Bukhara, and the Eastern Khanate, centred on Balasagun and Kashgar, both fell under the suzerainty of stronger neighbours: the Seljuks defeated the western khans in the late 11th century, and the Kara-Khitai (Western Liao) imposed overlordship after their victory at Qatwan in 1141. The western khanate was extinguished by the Khwarazmshahs in 1212, and the eastern remnant in Kashgar was destroyed by the Mongol-backed usurper Küchlüg shortly after.

Culture

The Kara-Khanid realm produced foundational works of Turkic letters: Yusuf Khass Hajib composed the mirror-for-princes Kutadgu Bilig ("Wisdom of Royal Glory", 1070) at Kashgar, and Mahmud al-Kashgari compiled the Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk (1070s), the first comparative dictionary of Turkic dialects. Monumental building included the Kalyan minaret in Bukhara (1127) and caravanserais such as Rabati Malik on the Samarkand–Bukhara road, while Kara-Khanid coinage spread Islamic epigraphic norms across the steppe frontier.
