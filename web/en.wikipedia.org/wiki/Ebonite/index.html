Ebonite
Also calledHard rubber, vulcanite
CompositionNatural rubber vulcanised with 25–80% sulfur
PropertiesHard, black, polishable, excellent electrical insulator, chemically resistant
UsesBowling balls, mouthpieces, fountain pens, electrical insulation, combs

Ebonite is a brand name for a material generically known as hard rubber or vulcanite: a very hard, durable form of rubber produced by vulcanising natural rubber with a high proportion of sulfur (typically 25–80 percent) for prolonged periods. It takes its name from its resemblance to ebony wood, for which it was originally used as a substitute. Ebonite is usually black, though coloured variants exist, and it can be machined and polished to a high gloss.

Production and properties

The material was developed in the mid-nineteenth century following Charles Goodyear's work on vulcanisation; Goodyear exhibited ebonite furniture at the Great Exhibition of 1851, and his brother Nelson Goodyear patented hard rubber in the United States. Prolonged heating with sulfur creates extensive cross-linking in the rubber, producing a rigid thermoset solid rather than an elastic material. Ebonite is an excellent electrical insulator, resists most chemicals and water, and does not melt, though it softens slightly with heat and can oxidise and discolour on long exposure to light and air.

Applications

Historically ebonite was used for early electrical insulation, battery cases, dentures, combs, buttons, and photographic and scientific equipment. It remains valued for musical instrument mouthpieces (clarinets, saxophones), where its density and machinability suit it to fine tolerances and its feel in the mouth is preferred by many players; for high-quality fountain pen bodies and feeds; and for bowling balls — Ebonite International was long a leading bowling ball manufacturer, and the brand gave its name to a major line of balls. Ebonite is also used in tobacco pipes, laboratory ware, and linings for chemical tanks.
