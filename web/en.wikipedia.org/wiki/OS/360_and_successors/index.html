OS/360 and successors

OS/360 was the primary operating system developed by IBM for its System/360 mainframe computer family, announced in 1964 and first released in 1966. It was one of the first operating systems designed to run across an entire family of machines, from small to very large, and its descendants — through OS/VS, MVS, OS/390 and today's z/OS — have run IBM mainframes ever since, making the line one of the longest-lived in computing.

Development

OS/360 was notoriously ambitious: a single operating system was to serve all models and all modes of use. The project grew far behind schedule and over budget, employed over a thousand programmers, and produced millions of lines of code. The experience prompted project manager Frederick P. Brooks Jr. to write The Mythical Man-Month (1975), whose lessons — including Brooks's law, that adding manpower to a late software project makes it later — became classics of software engineering. OS/360 was released in three configurations:

  • PCP — Primary Control Program, running one job at a time
  • MFT — Multiprogramming with a Fixed number of Tasks
  • MVT — Multiprogramming with a Variable number of Tasks, the most capable version

Successors

Despite its troubled birth, OS/360 established concepts still in use, including job control language (JCL) and standard access methods. Its line continued with OS/VS1 and OS/VS2 (SVS and then MVS) in the 1970s, adding virtual storage; MVS/ESA in the 1980s; OS/390 in the 1990s; and z/OS from 2000 onward for IBM Z mainframes. Unusually, OS/360 itself was placed in the public domain by IBM and can still be run today on emulators such as Hercules.
