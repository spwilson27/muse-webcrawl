Sexual harassment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sexual harassment is prohibited in workplaces in many jurisdictions.

Sexual harassment is a type of harassment involving the use of explicit or implicit sexual overtones, including the unwelcome and inappropriate promise of rewards in exchange for sexual favors. Sexual harassment can be physical, verbal, or non-verbal in nature and can range from relatively mild transgressions to sexual abuse or assault. It can occur in a range of social settings, including the workplace, the home, school, and religious institutions, and the harasser or victim may be of any sex or gender.

In modern legal contexts, sexual harassment is generally unlawful where it creates a hostile work environment or where a supervisor conditions employment benefits on sexual submission, a pattern known as quid pro quo harassment. Major legal frameworks prohibiting it include Title VII of the United States Civil Rights Act of 1964 and similar legislation in the European Union, the United Kingdom, and many other countries.

Terminology and history

The modern concept of sexual harassment emerged in the 1970s. The American feminist writer Lin Farley is credited with bringing the term into wide use through her 1978 book Sexual Shakedown, and law professor Catharine MacKinnon argued in Sexual Harassment of Working Women (1979) that it constituted sex discrimination under Title VII. The United States Equal Employment Opportunity Commission issued guidelines in 1980, and the Supreme Court recognized the doctrine in Meridian v. Vinson (1986), holding that a hostile work environment created by sexual harassment is itself actionable discrimination.

Categories

Legal and academic literature commonly distinguishes two core categories:

  • Quid pro quo harassment — submission to sexual conduct is made a term or condition of employment, or is used as the basis for employment decisions.
  • Hostile work environment harassment — unwelcome conduct of a sexual nature that is severe or pervasive enough to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment.

Effects

Victims of sexual harassment may experience psychological effects including depression, anxiety, loss of self-esteem, and symptoms consistent with post-traumatic stress, as well as professional consequences such as reduced productivity, absenteeism, and departure from a job or field. Organizations may face legal liability, damages, and reputational harm.

Prevention and response

Employers commonly adopt written policies, complaint procedures, and training programs. Critics debate the effectiveness of training alone and emphasize the role of organizational culture, bystander intervention, and prompt, impartial investigation of complaints. The Me Too movement, which spread widely from 2017, drew global attention to the prevalence of sexual harassment and assault and led to legal and corporate reforms in numerous countries.
