Spider angioma

A spider angioma, also known as a spider naevus or spider telangiectasia, is a type of telangiectasis (swollen blood vessels) found slightly beneath the skin surface, consisting of a central red spot with reddish extensions that radiate outwards like a spider's web. Pressing on the central vessel blanches the lesion, which refills from the centre when released.

Causes

Spider angiomas occur when a central arteriole dilates and feeds a network of small capillaries. They are associated with increased estrogen states and are common in pregnancy and in people taking hormonal contraception; a few lesions are also frequently seen in healthy children and adults. Numerous spider angiomas are a classic cutaneous sign of chronic liver disease, particularly cirrhosis, and they also occur in thyrotoxicosis and malnutrition.

Distribution and significance

Lesions associated with liver disease typically appear in the territory drained by the superior vena cava — the face, neck, upper trunk and arms. One or two isolated lesions are usually of no concern, but many lesions in an adult warrant evaluation for hepatic dysfunction.

Treatment

Treatment is usually unnecessary; lesions appearing in pregnancy often resolve after delivery. For cosmetically troublesome lesions, electrocautery, laser treatment or fine-needle diathermy of the central vessel can be effective, though recurrence is possible.
