Random-dot stereogram

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A random-dot stereogram is a pair of images (or a single image designed for free-fusion) consisting of apparently random dots that conceal a three-dimensional shape. When the two images are viewed with the eyes converged or diverged appropriately, the visual system matches corresponding dots between the eyes and the hidden shape emerges in depth, even though neither image alone contains any recognisable form.

History

Random-dot stereograms were invented around 1959–1960 by the vision scientist Béla Julesz at Bell Laboratories. Julesz used computer-generated dot patterns to demonstrate that depth perception (stereopsis) can occur without monocular cues such as contours or familiar shapes, showing that binocular disparity alone is sufficient for the brain to perceive surfaces in depth. His work reshaped theories of vision, suggesting that stereo matching is a low-level process that precedes object recognition — a phenomenon he called "cyclopean perception", after the mythical one-eyed Cyclops.

Autostereograms

A popular descendant is the random-dot autostereogram, in which a single repeating pattern encodes the depth map, so the shape can be seen without a stereoscope. Such images were popularised in the 1990s by the Magic Eye books. Random-dot stereograms remain standard tools in vision research and clinical testing of binocular vision, including screening for amblyopia and strabismus in children.
