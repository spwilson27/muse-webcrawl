Duchenne muscular dystrophy

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a severe type of muscular dystrophy predominantly affecting boys. It is caused by mutations in the gene encoding the protein dystrophin, located on the X chromosome. Muscle weakness usually begins around the age of four, and worsens rapidly. Most affected individuals are unable to walk by age 12 and eventually require a ventilator; cardiomyopathy is a major cause of death. It is named after the French neurologist Guillaume-Benjamin Duchenne, who described it in the 1860s.

Signs and symptoms

Early signs include delayed motor milestones, frequent falls, difficulty climbing stairs, and a waddling gait. The classic Gower's sign describes a child using his hands to "walk up" his own thighs to rise from the floor. The calves often appear enlarged (pseudohypertrophy) because muscle is replaced by fat and connective tissue. Progressive scoliosis, respiratory muscle weakness, and dilated cardiomyopathy develop in adolescence.

Cause and diagnosis

DMD results from mutations—often large deletions—in the DMD gene, the largest known human gene, which encodes dystrophin, a structural protein linking the muscle fibre cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix. Without dystrophin, muscle membranes tear during contraction, causing progressive fibre death. Diagnosis is by genetic testing, supported by very high serum creatine kinase levels; muscle biopsy shows absent dystrophin staining. Becker muscular dystrophy is a milder allelic condition in which some functional dystrophin is produced.

Treatment

There is no cure; corticosteroids such as prednisone and deflazacort slow progression and prolong walking. Exon-skipping drugs (such as eteplirsen) and the read-through agent ataluren address specific mutations, and a micro-dystrophin gene therapy (delandistrogene moxeparvovec) received approval in the United States in 2023–2024. Supportive care includes physiotherapy, cardiac and respiratory management, and spinal monitoring. Life expectancy has improved into the thirties and beyond with modern care.
