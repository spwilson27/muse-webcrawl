Sound reinforcement system

A sound reinforcement system is the combination of microphones, signal processors, amplifiers and loudspeakers that makes live or prerecorded sounds louder and may also distribute those sounds to a larger or more distant audience. In many situations such a system is also used to enhance or alter the sound of the sources, as opposed to simply amplifying them unchanged.

Components

A typical system begins with transducers — microphones and direct-input boxes — feeding a mixing console, where a front-of-house engineer balances levels and applies equalization and processing such as compression and reverberation. The mixed signal passes through system processors (crossovers, limiters, delay and alignment tools) to power amplifiers and loudspeaker arrays. Performers hear themselves through a separate monitor system of wedge loudspeakers or in-ear monitors.

Applications and scale

Systems range from a single portable powered speaker for a speech, through public address systems in schools, houses of worship and transport hubs, to the line-array rigs flown at arena and stadium concerts, which can deliver sound pressure levels above 100 dB to tens of thousands of listeners. Design uses acoustic modelling software to predict coverage and intelligibility.

Challenges

Engineers must control acoustic feedback, manage the interaction of loudspeakers with room acoustics, align delayed loudspeaker fills with the main system, and comply with noise regulations and hearing-safety limits. The history of the field runs from 1920s public address horns through the large concert systems pioneered in the 1960s and 1970s to modern digitally networked audio (e.g. Dante) and beam-steered arrays.
