Valentine's Day

Valentine's Day, also called Saint Valentine's Day or the Feast of Saint Valentine, is celebrated annually on 14 February. Originating as a Christian feast day honouring one or more early martyrs named Valentinus, it developed through later folk traditions into a significant cultural and commercial celebration of romance and love in many parts of the world.

Origins

The historical Saint Valentine is obscure: the Roman Martyrology records a Valentine martyred on the Via Flaminia on 14 February, and later legends credit a priest Valentine with secretly marrying couples in defiance of the Emperor Claudius II, or with healing his jailer's daughter and signing a farewell letter "Your Valentine". The feast was established by Pope Gelasius I in 496, possibly supplanting the Roman festival of Lupercalia. The association with romantic love is first documented in the circle of Geoffrey Chaucer in the 14th century, when the day was linked with the mating season of birds.

Customs

By the 18th century in England, lovers exchanged flowers, confectionery and handwritten "valentines"; printed cards became mass-produced in the 19th century, and in the United States Esther Howland pioneered their commercial production from the 1840s. Today the celebration centres on cards, red roses, chocolates and romantic dinners, generating billions in retail spending. The day is observed, with local variations, in many countries: in Japan and South Korea women traditionally give chocolate to men, reciprocated on White Day a month later, while in some countries the holiday has attracted religious or political opposition.

Symbols

The heart shape, doves, roses and the winged figure of Cupid are the day's enduring symbols, drawn from courtly-love and classical traditions.
