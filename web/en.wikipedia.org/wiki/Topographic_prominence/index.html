Topographic prominence


Vertical arrows show the topographic prominence of three peaks on an island. The dashed horizontal lines link to the highest points separated by the low points or cols.

Topographic prominence measures the height of a mountain or hill's summit relative to the lowest contour line encircling it but containing no higher summit within it. It is a measure of the independence of a summit. The key col ("saddle") around the peak is a unique point on this contour line and the parent peak is some higher mountain, selected according to various criteria.

Definitions

The prominence of a peak is the minimum of vertical distances one would need to descend from the peak in order to reach a higher summit. Equivalently, imagine the sea level rising to flood the land up to the elevation of the key col; the peak would then be the highest point of an island, and its prominence is the height of that island — the difference between the peak's elevation and the elevation of the surrounding sea at the flooding level.

For Mount Everest, the highest point on Earth, there is no higher summit anywhere, so its prominence is conventionally defined as equal to its elevation above sea level (8,848.86 metres). In general, the summit of the highest point of an island or landmass has a prominence equal to its elevation, because the "sea" for its key col is the actual sea.

Parent peak

The parent peak of a summit is a higher peak resulting from a particular definition connecting the summit's contour lines. Several conventions exist: the prominence parent is the highest peak reached by following the contour line from the key col; the encirclement parent is the highest point of the island that would result if the sea rose to the key col level; and the line parent is identified by a recursive procedure following ridge lines. Different choices can give strikingly different parents; for many Alpine peaks the line parent is Mont Blanc, while encirclement parents can lie far away across low saddles.

Prominence and peak lists

Prominence is used as a criterion in many lists of mountains, because it measures how independently a summit stands from its neighbours. Peaks with high prominence are likely to be the highest points around and tend to have sweeping views. Lists based on prominence include:

  • Ultras (ultra-prominent peaks): peaks with prominence of 1,500 metres or more; there are about 1,524 such peaks on land worldwide.
  • Majors in Britain and Ireland: peaks with prominence of 150 metres or more (the Marilyn criterion).

History

Prominence as a formal concept developed in the twentieth century as peak baggers sought objective criteria for which summits "count" as separate mountains. It was applied widely in the British Isles, where J. Rooke Corbett used a 500-foot prominence criterion for his list of Scottish hills (the Corbetts), and later lists such as the Marilyns extended the idea. In the United States, analogous concepts appear in the rules for the Adirondack Forty-Sixers and the "fourteeners" of Colorado, where a rise of 300 feet is commonly required for a summit to count separately.

Computing prominence

Prominence values are computed from elevation data, traditionally derived from topographic maps by examining contour lines, and increasingly from digital elevation models such as those produced by the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission. Computing prominence for all summits of a region can be done with algorithms that process the terrain as a graph of peaks and saddles.
