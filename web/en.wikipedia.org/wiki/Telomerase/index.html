Telomerase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein enzyme—a reverse transcriptase—that adds repetitive DNA sequences (TTAGGG in vertebrates) to the 3' ends of chromosomes at regions called telomeres, thereby counteracting the progressive shortening that occurs with each round of DNA replication (the "end replication problem").

Composition

Human telomerase consists of a catalytic protein subunit, TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase), and an RNA component, TERC, whose template sequence directs the synthesis of telomeric repeats. Associated proteins, including dyskerin, regulate assembly and stability of the complex.

Discovery

Telomerase activity was discovered in the ciliate Tetrahymena in 1985 by Carol Greider working with Elizabeth Blackburn. Blackburn, Greider, and Jack Szostak received the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering how chromosomes are protected by telomeres and telomerase.

Biological roles

Telomerase is active in germline cells, stem cells, and activated lymphocytes, but largely absent from most somatic cells, linking telomere shortening to cellular senescence and ageing. Conversely, about 85–90% of human cancers reactivate telomerase, making it a target of oncology research. Defects in telomerase components cause diseases such as dyskeratosis congenita and some forms of aplastic anemia and pulmonary fibrosis.
