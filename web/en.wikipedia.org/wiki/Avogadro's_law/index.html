Avogadro's law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Avogadro's law (sometimes referred to as Avogadro's hypothesis or Avogadro's principle) is an experimental gas law relating the volume of a gas to the amount of substance of gas present. A modern statement of Avogadro's law states: "Equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, have the same number of molecules."

Equal volumes of gases at equal temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

Mathematical form

For a given mass of an ideal gas, the volume and amount (in moles) of the gas are directly proportional if the temperature and pressure are constant:

V ∝ n   or   V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂

where V is the volume and n the amount of substance. Combined with Boyle's law, Charles's law and Gay-Lussac's law, it yields the ideal gas law, PV = nRT.

History

The hypothesis was formulated by Amedeo Avogadro in 1811. It was initially neglected but was revived by Stanislao Cannizzaro in 1860, providing a consistent way to determine atomic and molecular masses. A mole of gas at standard temperature and pressure occupies about 22.4 litres; the number of molecules per mole is given by the Avogadro constant, approximately 6.022×1023 mol−1.
