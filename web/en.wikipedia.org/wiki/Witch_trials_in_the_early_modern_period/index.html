Witch trials in the early modern period

In the early modern period, roughly between 1400 and 1775, there were a series of witch trials across Europe and in the European colonies in the Americas. The peak of the persecutions occurred during the decades around 1580–1630, in the context of the Reformation, religious wars, social instability and climatic deterioration during the Little Ice Age. Modern scholarly estimates suggest that tens of thousands of people — commonly estimated at 35,000 to 50,000 executions — were put to death, with women making up roughly three-quarters of the victims in most regions.

Beliefs and legal framework

The cumulative concept of witchcraft combined folk beliefs in harmful magic (maleficium) with learned demonology: a pact with the Devil, attendance at a nocturnal sabbath, and flight. Treatises such as the Malleus Maleficarum (1486) systematised these beliefs. Trials took place in both ecclesiastical and secular courts; inquisitorial procedure and judicial torture produced confessions and denunciations that fuelled chain-reaction panics, most notoriously in the German prince-bishoprics of Würzburg, Bamberg and Trier.

Regional variation

Persecution was most intense in the Holy Roman Empire, France, Scotland and Switzerland, while the Spanish and Italian Inquisitions were comparatively restrained in witchcraft cases. In England, accused witches were hanged under the Witchcraft Acts, and the Salem witch trials of 1692–93 in colonial Massachusetts remain the best-known American episode.

Decline

From the later 17th century, judicial scepticism grew, torture was restricted, and central courts increasingly overturned local convictions. The last executions for witchcraft in most European countries took place in the 18th century, and witchcraft statutes were progressively repealed.
