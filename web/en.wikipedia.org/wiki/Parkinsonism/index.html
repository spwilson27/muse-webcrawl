Parkinsonism

SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsTremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability
CausesParkinson's disease, drugs, toxins, other neurodegenerative disorders

Parkinsonism is a neurological syndrome characterised by tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia (slowness of movement) and postural instability. It results from dysfunction of the basal ganglia, most often through loss of dopaminergic neurons or blockade of dopamine receptors. The most common cause is Parkinson's disease, in which parkinsonism is idiopathic; the term is also used for similar presentations caused by other conditions.

Causes

  • Parkinson's disease — the commonest cause, typically asymmetric onset with resting tremor and good response to levodopa.
  • Atypical parkinsonism ("Parkinson-plus" syndromes) — multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, and dementia with Lewy bodies.
  • Drug-induced parkinsonism — caused by dopamine-receptor blocking drugs such as antipsychotics and metoclopramide.
  • Vascular parkinsonism — from multiple small strokes in the basal ganglia, usually gait-predominant.
  • Toxic and metabolic causes — MPTP, manganese, carbon monoxide, Wilson's disease, normal pressure hydrocephalus.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by the response to levodopa, brain imaging to exclude structural causes, and sometimes dopamine transporter (DaT) scans. Distinguishing features — early falls and gaze palsy in progressive supranuclear palsy, early autonomic failure in multiple system atrophy, early dementia and hallucinations in Lewy body dementia — help identify atypical causes, which generally respond poorly to levodopa and carry a worse prognosis.

Management

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause where possible: withdrawal of offending drugs, treatment of hydrocephalus, or chelation in Wilson's disease. Symptomatic therapy centres on dopaminergic drugs, physiotherapy, speech and occupational therapy.
