Senator for life

A senator for life is a member of a senate who holds the office for life, usually by appointment rather than election. Life senatorships exist or have existed in several countries, often as a means of honouring former heads of state or distinguished citizens and of adding experienced, non-partisan voices to the upper house.

Italy

In Italy, former Presidents of the Republic become senators for life by right, unless they renounce the office. The President may also appoint up to five citizens who have "honoured the country through their outstanding achievements in the social, scientific, artistic and literary fields". A 2020 constitutional reform clarified that the total number of appointed life senators may not exceed five. Notable appointees have included the scientist Rita Levi-Montalcini, the conductor Claudio Abbado, and the architect Renzo Piano; former prime minister Mario Monti was appointed in 2011 before forming a government.

France

Under the French Third Republic, the Senate established in 1875 included 75 life senators (sénateurs inamovibles), initially elected by the National Assembly. The life seats were phased out by an 1884 law and the last life senator died in 1918.

Other countries

In Canada, senators historically served for life until 1965, when a mandatory retirement age of 75 was introduced. The constitution of Chile under Pinochet included designated senators, among them former presidents as senators for life; the institution was abolished by the 2005 reforms. Paraguay's constitution provides for former democratically elected presidents to be senators for life with voice but no vote. Similar ex officio life seats for former heads of state have existed in Burundi and elsewhere.
