Cagan model

The Cagan model is a model of money demand and inflation introduced by economist Phillip Cagan in his classic 1956 study "The Monetary Dynamics of Hyperinflation", published in Milton Friedman's edited volume Studies in the Quantity Theory of Money. It remains the standard framework for analysing hyperinflation and the behaviour of real money balances under expected inflation.

The model

Cagan specified demand for real money balances as a function of expected inflation: m − p = −α πe (in logarithms, ignoring other influences), where α measures the semi-elasticity of money demand with respect to expected inflation. As expected inflation rises, people economise on cash balances, so real balances shrink. Under adaptive expectations, Cagan derived stability conditions: if expectations adjust too fast relative to α, the price level can become unstable.

Empirical findings

Studying seven hyperinflations (including post–World War I Germany, Austria, Hungary and Poland, and Hungary's extreme 1945–46 episode), Cagan found the simple log-linear money demand fit remarkably well. He also showed that governments face a seigniorage "Laffer curve": beyond a maximum inflation rate, faster money growth actually reduces real revenue because real balances collapse — implying hyperinflations are self-limiting or end in monetary reform.

Influence

The model underpins later work by Sargent and Wallace on ending hyperinflations and rational-expectations versions of money demand, and it remains a standard teaching model in graduate macroeconomics.
