Regenerative circuit

A regenerative circuit is an amplifier circuit that employs positive feedback (also known as regeneration or "tickling"). The circuit exploits the fact that the gain of an amplifying device can be greatly increased by feeding a portion of the output signal back into the input in phase, so that the effective gain rises as the feedback approaches the threshold of oscillation. The circuit was invented in 1912 and patented in 1914 by American electrical engineer Edwin Armstrong when he was an undergraduate at Columbia University.

Regenerative receiver

The regenerative receiver was widely used in radio from the 1920s to the Second World War. A single vacuum tube (or later transistor) served simultaneously as RF amplifier and detector, with feedback controlled by a "tickler coil" or variable capacitor. Adjusted just below oscillation it gave high gain and sharp selectivity from very few components, making home construction cheap; at or above oscillation it could also demodulate CW (Morse) and, later, single-sideband signals. Drawbacks included radiation of interference when oscillating, critical adjustment, and inferior selectivity compared with the superheterodyne receiver, which Armstrong also invented and which eventually displaced it.

Superregeneration

The related superregenerative circuit, also Armstrong's, periodically quenches the oscillation, yielding very high gain from one stage. Superregenerative receivers survive in simple low-cost applications such as garage-door openers, radio-control links and short-range remote controls.
