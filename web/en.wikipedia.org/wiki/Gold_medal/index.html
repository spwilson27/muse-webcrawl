Gold medal

A gold medal is a medal awarded for highest achievement in a non-military field. Its name derives from the use of at least a fraction of gold in form of plating or alloying in its manufacture.

Since the eighteenth century, gold medals have been awarded in the arts, for example, by the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts, usually as a symbol of an award to give an outstanding student some financial freedom. Others offer prestige. Many organizations now award gold medals either annually or extraordinarily, including various academic societies.

Olympic Games

While some gold medals are solid gold, those used at the Olympic Games are usually gold-plated. At the modern Olympic Games, winners of a sporting discipline receive a gold medal in recognition of their achievement; the second-place finisher receives a silver medal and the third a bronze medal. At the ancient Olympic Games only one winner was crowned, with an olive wreath. Solid gold medals were awarded at the 1904, 1908 and 1912 Games; since then Olympic gold medals have been made mostly of silver with gold plating — since 1912 the standard has been gilt silver containing at least six grams of pure gold.

Military use

Before the establishment of standard military awards, e.g. the Medal of Honor, it was common practice for a nation to make a bespoke gold medal in recognition of a victorious military or naval commander's services. The United States Congress issued gold medals, the most senior American expression of national appreciation for distinguished achievements and contributions.

Other uses

The Nobel Prize medal is made of 18-carat gold plated with 24-carat gold. Academic institutions award gold medals to top graduating students, and the phrase "gold medal" is used figuratively for any supreme achievement.
