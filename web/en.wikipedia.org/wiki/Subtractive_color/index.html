Subtractive color

A subtractive color model explains the mixing of a limited set of pigments, dyes, inks, and natural colorants to create a wider range of colors, each the result of partially or completely subtracting (that is, absorbing) some wavelengths of light and not others. The color that a surface displays depends on which parts of the visible spectrum are not absorbed and therefore remain visible to the observer. Subtractive mixing is the opposite of additive color mixing, in which colored lights are combined.

CMY and CMYK

The theoretical primary colors of subtractive mixing are cyan, magenta, and yellow (CMY): cyan absorbs red light, magenta absorbs green, and yellow absorbs blue. Layering all three in sufficient density should in principle absorb all visible light and produce black. Because real printing inks are imperfect, commercial printing adds a black ink ("K", for "key") to form the CMYK color model, improving density and reducing ink use. The traditional painter's model of red, yellow, and blue (RYB) primaries predates modern color science and is still taught in art education, though it has a smaller gamut than CMY.

Applications

Subtractive color mixing governs printing (including color photography on film and paper), painting, and the coloring of textiles, plastics, and other materials viewed in reflected light. The appearance of subtractive mixtures depends on the illumination spectrum; under different lighting, mixtures may match or differ (metamerism).
