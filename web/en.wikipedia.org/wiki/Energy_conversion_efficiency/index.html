Energy conversion efficiency

Energy conversion efficiency (η) is the ratio between the useful output of energy and the input, in energy terms, of an energy conversion machine. The output may be chemical, electric power, mechanical work, light (radiation) or heat. Energy conversion efficiency is not defined uniquely; it depends on how the "useful" output is defined.

A Sankey diagram illustrating conversion losses in electricity generation.

Examples

Typical efficiencies include: large electric motors, above 90%; combined-cycle gas turbines, around 60%; steam turbines in thermal power stations, roughly 33–48%; internal combustion engines in cars, about 20–35%; incandescent light bulbs, around 2–5% (most input becomes heat); and modern LED lighting, substantially higher. Photosynthesis converts sunlight to chemical energy at only a few percent efficiency under field conditions.

Limits

For heat engines, efficiency is bounded by the Carnot limit, determined by the temperatures of the hot and cold reservoirs. Fuel cells and other electrochemical converters are not heat engines and are limited instead by the ratio of Gibbs free energy to enthalpy of the reaction.
