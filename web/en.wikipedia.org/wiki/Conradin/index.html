Conrad III (25 March 1252 – 29 October 1268), called Conradin (diminutive of Conrad, Italian: Corradino), was the last direct heir of the Hohenstaufen dynasty. He was Duke of Swabia (1254–1268) and nominal King of Jerusalem (1254–1268) and Sicily (1254–1258). After his attempt to reconquer the Kingdom of Sicily from Charles of Anjou failed, he was captured at the Battle of Tagliacozzo and beheaded at Naples at the age of sixteen, ending the legitimate male line of the Hohenstaufen.

Early life

Conradin was born at Wolfstein Castle near Landshut in Bavaria, the only son of King Conrad IV of Germany and Elisabeth of Bavaria. His grandfather was the Emperor Frederick II. When Conrad IV died of malaria in Italy in 1254, the two-year-old Conradin, raised in Bavaria under the guardianship of his uncles, the Wittelsbach dukes Louis II and Henry XIII of Bavaria, inherited the Hohenstaufen claims but not effective power in Germany, where the Great Interregnum began. In Sicily, his uncle Manfred ruled as regent and then took the crown himself in 1258 amid false reports of Conradin's death; Manfred was killed fighting Charles of Anjou at the Battle of Benevento in 1266.

Expedition to Italy and death

Urged on by the Ghibelline cities of Italy and supported by his stepfather Meinhard of Tyrol and his friend Frederick of Baden, the fifteen-year-old Conradin crossed the Alps in 1267 to claim Sicily. He was excommunicated by Pope Clement IV, received enthusiastically in Rome by the senator Henry of Castile, and marched south. On 23 August 1268 his army was defeated by Charles of Anjou at the Battle of Tagliacozzo in the Abruzzi. Conradin fled but was captured at Torre Astura, handed over to Charles, and tried at Naples for treason. On 29 October 1268 he and Frederick of Baden were beheaded in the market place of Naples — an execution, unusual for a royal prisoner, that shocked contemporaries and fixed Conradin's image in European memory as the tragic last Staufer.

Legacy

Conradin's death extinguished the Hohenstaufen dynasty in the male line and secured Angevin rule in Sicily until the Sicilian Vespers of 1282. His rights in Swabia and Jerusalem passed to relatives; the kingdom of Jerusalem claim went to the Lusignan kings of Cyprus. In German culture he became a romantic symbol of doomed youth, commemorated in poetry, drama and a well-known statue by Bertel Thorvaldsen in Naples, where his remains rest in the church of Santa Maria del Carmine.
