Tarot

Tarot is a pack of playing cards, first devised in mid-15th-century Italy (as trionfi) for playing trick-taking games, and still used for games in parts of Europe. Since the late 18th century, tarot decks — particularly the Tarot de Marseille pattern and later designs such as the Rider–Waite deck — have also been widely used for divination and cartomancy, and in occult and esoteric traditions.

Structure of the deck

A standard tarot pack has 78 cards:

  • 56 suit cards in four suits (in Italian decks, Swords, Batons, Cups, and Coins), each with ten pip cards and four court cards (King, Queen, Knight, and Jack/Knave).
  • 22 special cards: 21 trump cards, in later esoteric usage called the Major Arcana — the Magician, the High Priestess, the Empress, the Emperor, the Hierophant, the Lovers, the Chariot, Strength, the Hermit, the Wheel of Fortune, Justice, the Hanged Man, Death, Temperance, the Devil, the Tower, the Star, the Moon, the Sun, Judgement, and the World — plus the unnumbered Fool.

History

Tarot appeared in northern Italy in the 1440s as a permanent fifth suit of trumps added to the ordinary pack, an innovation probably emerging from the courts of Milan and Ferrara. Sumptuous hand-painted decks such as the Visconti-Sforza cards survive from this era. The game spread through Europe; tarot games (French jeu de tarot, Austrian Königrufen, Italian tarocchini) remain popular in several countries, and jeu de tarot is among France's most-played card games.

Divinatory use

Although there is no evidence of divinatory use before the 18th century, French occultists from Court de Gébelin (1781) onward claimed an ancient Egyptian origin for the cards and developed elaborate correspondences with Kabbalah, astrology, and alchemy. Éliphas Lévi, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, and Arthur Edward Waite — whose Rider–Waite deck (1909), illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith, gave fully pictorial scenes to the suit cards — shaped the modern fortune-telling deck. Tarot reading remains popular today for divination, meditation, and psychological self-reflection; historians of playing cards, notably Michael Dummett, emphasise that the occult interpretation is a late invention layered onto a game pack.
