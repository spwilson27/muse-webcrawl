Bob Marley

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Bob Marley performing in 1980, dreadlocks flying, guitar slung low, in the final year of his touring life]

Robert Nesta Marley (6 February 1945 – 11 May 1981) was a Jamaican singer, songwriter and guitarist, the global ambassador of reggae and of the Rastafari movement. With the Wailers he carried Jamaican music from Kingston's studios to the world: Catch a Fire and Burnin' (both 1973), Natty Dread (1974), Rastaman Vibration (1976), Exodus (1977 — named album of the century by Time magazine) and Uprising (1980).

Marley's songs — 'No Woman, No Cry', 'One Love', 'Redemption Song', 'Get Up, Stand Up', 'Three Little Birds' among them, cited here by title — became universal anthems of peace and resistance, sung in languages he never spoke. The compilation Legend (1984) is the best-selling reggae album of all time.

From Trenchtown to Island Records

Born in Nine Mile, Jamaica, to a white British officer father and a Jamaican mother, Marley grew up in Trenchtown, Kingston, forming the Wailers with Peter Tosh and Bunny Wailer under producer Leslie Kong and then the formidable Coxsone Dodd, singing ska and rocksteady before the slower, bass-heavy reggae emerged around 1969. Chris Blackwell's Island Records marketed the Wailers as a rock act — Eric Clapton's cover of 'I Shot the Sheriff' (1974) opened America.

Politics, exile and death

An assassination attempt in December 1976, two days before the Smile Jamaica peace concert, failed to silence him; he performed, then exiled himself to London, writing Exodus there. The 1978 One Love Peace Concert in Kingston brought Jamaica's rival political leaders onto his stage, hands joined — for which the UN awarded him its Peace Medal of the Third World. Melanoma, found under a toenail in 1977, spread when treatment was limited by his faith; he died in Miami aged 36, and Jamaica gave him a state funeral.
