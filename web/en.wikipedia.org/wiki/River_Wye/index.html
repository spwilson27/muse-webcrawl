The River Wye (Welsh: Afon Gwy) is the fifth-longest river in the United Kingdom, flowing 215 km (134 mi) from its source on Plynlimon in the Cambrian Mountains of mid-Wales to the Severn Estuary below Chepstow. For much of its lower course it forms the boundary between England and Wales. Its valley, the Wye Valley, is a designated National Landscape famous for its wooded limestone gorge.

Course

Rising a short distance from the source of the Severn, the Wye flows east through Rhayader and Builth Wells — where it is joined by the Irfon — then south past Glasbury to the book town of Hay-on-Wye, and on to Hereford, its only city. Below Hereford it meanders through a wide pastoral valley, joined by the Lugg near Mordiford, before cutting through the limestone gorge at Ross-on-Wye, Symonds Yat and Monmouth, where the Monnow joins it. The final reach from Monmouth to Chepstow passes Tintern Abbey in a steep, wooded defile, and is tidal; the Wye enters the Severn Estuary beneath the Wye Bridge of the M48.

History and tourism

The Wye tour of the 18th century — boat trips from Ross to Chepstow popularised by William Gilpin's Observations on the River Wye (1782) — helped define the idea of the Picturesque in British landscape taste, and the gorge's scenery around Tintern Abbey inspired Wordsworth's famous lines of 1798. Historically the river was navigable to Hereford and above, carrying barges of coal, iron, timber and cider; weirs and locks regulated it from the 17th century.

Ecology

The Wye is designated a Special Area of Conservation for its Atlantic salmon run — historically one of the most productive in England and Wales — along with otters, twaite shad and white-clawed crayfish. In recent years the river has been at the centre of controversy over phosphate pollution from agriculture in its catchment, which has damaged its ecology and triggered planning restrictions in Herefordshire and Powys.
