Pyrophyte

Pyrophytes are plants which have adapted to tolerate fire. Fire acts favourably for some species — a phenomenon known as pyrophily — because it clears competing vegetation, enriches the soil with ash, and can trigger seed release or germination.

Passive pyrophytes

Passive pyrophytes resist fire through thick insulating bark, moist tissues, or rapid resprouting from protected buds and lignotubers. Examples include many eucalyptus species and the longleaf pine.

Active pyrophytes

Active pyrophytes produce flammable oils that encourage fires which eliminate competitors; some Australian and South African species behave this way. Others grow rapidly after fire to exploit the temporary absence of competition.

Pyrophytic reproduction

Some plants practise serotiny: their cones or fruits — for example those of lodgepole pine and Banksia — are sealed with resin that melts only in fire, releasing seeds onto the ash bed. Smoke-derived chemicals called karrikins stimulate germination in many fire-following species.
