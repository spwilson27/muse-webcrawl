The Mi'kmaq language (spelled and pronounced as Mí'gmaq in some orthographies, and Mi'kmaw in singular form) is an Eastern Algonquian language spoken by nearly 11,000 Mi'kmaq people in Canada and the United States, primarily in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, the Gaspé Peninsula of Quebec, and Maine.

Features

Mi'kmaq is a polysynthetic language with complex verb morphology and an animate–inanimate gender distinction. Unusually for an Algonquian language, its word order is relatively flexible, and it lacks the direct–inverse system characteristic of its relatives. It is written in several competing orthographies, of which the Francis–Smith system is the most widely used in Nova Scotia.

Writing systems

Before European contact and into the colonial period, Mi'kmaq used a distinctive system of logographic–mnemonic marks, the Mi'kmaq hieroglyphic writing (gomgwejui'gasit), which missionaries such as Chrétien Le Clercq and Pierre Maillard adapted to record prayers and catechisms. Roman-letter orthographies were developed in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Status and revitalization

Mi'kmaq is the official language of the Mi'kmaq Nation and is taught in schools and immersion programs in several communities. In 2022 Nova Scotia passed legislation recognizing Mi'kmaq as the province's first language.
