Moshava

A moshava (Hebrew: מוֹשָׁבָה, plural moshavot) is a form of rural Jewish settlement in Ottoman Palestine and later Israel, established by Jews of the First Aliyah in the late 19th century. Unlike the collective kibbutz and the cooperative moshav that came later, the moshava was based on private land ownership and private farming, modeled loosely on European agricultural villages.

History

The first moshavot were founded in the 1870s and 1880s by members of the Hovevei Zion movement and other early Zionist settlers. Petah Tikva, founded in 1878, is often called the "mother of the moshavot." Other early moshavot included Rishon LeZion (1882), Zikhron Ya'akov (1882), Rosh Pinna (1882), Yesod HaMa'ala (1883), Gedera (1884), Rehovot (1890), and Hadera (1891).

Many of the early settlements struggled economically and received support from Baron Edmond de Rothschild, whose administration helped sustain them but also imposed a paternalistic system that some settlers resented. Under Rothschild's sponsorship, vineyards and wineries became a prominent feature of several moshavot, particularly Rishon LeZion and Zikhron Ya'akov.

Characteristics

In a moshava, each family owned its own plot of land and house, arranged typically along a main street. Decisions about communal matters were made by a local committee. Because land was privately held and hired labor was common, the moshava differed from the later labor-Zionist settlement forms, which emphasized self-labor and collective or cooperative ownership.

Legacy

Several moshavot grew into modern Israeli cities, including Petah Tikva, Rishon LeZion, Rehovot, and Hadera. Others retained a smaller, village character. The historic cores of some moshavot, such as Zikhron Ya'akov, have been preserved as heritage and tourist sites.
