Khiva

Khiva (Uzbek: Xiva; Persian: خیوه) is a city in the Khorezm Region of Uzbekistan, with a population of roughly 90,000. For centuries it was the capital of the Khanate of Khiva and one of the great slave-trading and caravan cities of Central Asia, on routes between the Caspian Sea and Bukhara. Its walled inner town, the Itchan Kala, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, preserved as an open-air museum of mosques, madrasas and minarets.

History

Legend attributes Khiva's founding to Shem, son of Noah; archaeology shows settlement in the oasis for well over a millennium. Khiva was a minor town of Khwarezm until the early 16th century, when Uzbek Shaybanid rulers made it capital of a new khanate after the Amu Darya shifted course away from the old capital, Konye-Urgench. The khanate grew rich on the caravan trade and on raids that supplied its notorious slave market with Persian and Russian captives.

Russian expansion into Central Asia ended Khiva's independence: the expedition of General von Kaufman forced a treaty in 1873 making the khanate a Russian protectorate, and in 1920 the last khan was deposed and the Khorezm People's Soviet Republic proclaimed. The city then became part of the Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic and, from 1991, independent Uzbekistan.

Sights

The Itchan Kala contains more than 50 monuments, including the Juma Mosque with its forest of carved wooden columns, the turquoise-tiled Kalta Minor minaret, the Tash Khauli palace of the khans, and the mausoleum of the saint and wrestler Pahlavan Mahmud, patron of the city.
