The New York State Senate is the upper house of the New York State Legislature, the state legislature of the U.S. state of New York. It comprises 63 senators elected from single-member districts to two-year terms with no term limits. The Senate meets in the New York State Capitol in Albany.

Composition and leadership

The Lieutenant Governor of New York is constitutionally the President of the Senate and may cast a tie-breaking vote, though in practice the Senate is led by its Temporary President, who since 2009 has also served as Majority Leader. Day-to-day power — control of the legislative calendar, committee assignments, and the flow of bills — rests with the majority leader. Senators represent roughly equal populations under decennial redistricting; the number of seats has been fixed at 63 since the 2012 cycle.

History

The Senate was created by the first New York Constitution of 1777, originally with 24 members elected from multi-county districts serving four-year terms; members initially included a property qualification. Successive constitutions expanded the body as the state grew, and an 1894 amendment established the formula that produced a chamber of about fifty members, growing with census apportionments. For most of the 20th century the Senate was controlled by Republicans, frequently producing divided government with the Democratic-led Assembly; Democrats won a durable majority in 2018 for the first time in decades and subsequently held a two-thirds supermajority.

Powers

Along with the Assembly, the Senate passes bills that require the Governor's signature to become law. The Senate alone confirms gubernatorial appointments to state offices, judgeships, and boards, and it conducts impeachment trials after the Assembly votes articles of impeachment.
