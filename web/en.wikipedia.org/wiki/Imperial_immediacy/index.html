Imperial immediacy

The fragmented map of Imperial Estates enjoying immediacy

Imperial immediacy (German: Reichsunmittelbarkeit) was a privileged constitutional status in the Holy Roman Empire. It granted a person or a territory the privilege of being subject directly to the Holy Roman Emperor and the Empire, and not to any intermediate ruler such as a duke, count, or prince. Holders of immediacy were known as Imperial Estates (Reichsstände) and were represented in the Imperial Diet.

Holders

Imperial immediacy was enjoyed by a wide range of entities: the prince-electors, secular and ecclesiastical princes, many counts and prelates, the Imperial Free Cities, the Imperial Knights, and even some Imperial Villages. Immediate estates possessed rights of high justice, taxation, and coinage within their territories, and in return owed the Emperor taxes and military contingents assessed by the Imperial Register.

Decline and abolition

The number of immediate territories shrank dramatically with the German mediatisation of 1803, in which most ecclesiastical states, free cities, and small principalities were absorbed into larger states. The remaining structure disappeared with the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806, when the mediatised houses lost their immediate status and became subject to the new sovereign states.
