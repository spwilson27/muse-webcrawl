Publish or perish


Pressure to publish in academic journals

"Publish or perish" is an aphorism describing the pressure to publish academic work in order to succeed in an academic career. Such institutional pressure is generally strongest at research universities. Successful publications bring attention to scholars and their institutions, help secure funding and positions, and making progress in an academic career requires substantial publication. Academics who publish infrequently, or who focus on activities that do not result in publications, may find themselves out-competed for available positions.

Origin

The phrase is often attributed to sociologist Logan Wilson in his 1942 work The Academic Man, though earlier uses in academic contexts date to the 1920s and 1930s. The expression became a standard summary of the incentive system of modern research universities.

Criticism

The pressure to publish has been criticised for encouraging quantity over quality, "salami slicing" of results into the least publishable unit, citation gaming, and questionable research practices. It is blamed for contributing to the replication crisis in some fields and for feeding the growth of predatory journals and paper mills. Bibliometric indicators such as the impact factor and h-index, though intended as measures of quality, can become targets in themselves — an instance of Goodhart's law.

Responses

Initiatives such as the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA, 2013) urge institutions not to use journal impact factors in hiring and promotion decisions, and to value diverse outputs such as data sets, software and replication studies.
