Sino-Vietnamese War

Date17 February – 16 March 1979
LocationNorthern Vietnam (China–Vietnam border)
BelligerentsChina; Vietnam
ResultBoth sides claim victory; China withdraws after occupying border towns

The Sino-Vietnamese War (also known in China as the "Counterattack in Self-Defence against Vietnam" and in Vietnam as the "War against Chinese expansionism") was a brief border war fought between China and Vietnam in early 1979. China launched an offensive in response to Vietnam's invasion and occupation of Cambodia in 1978, which had overthrown the Chinese-backed Khmer Rouge regime of Pol Pot.

Background

Relations between the two communist neighbours had deteriorated sharply after Vietnam's reunification in 1975. Vietnam signed a treaty of friendship with the Soviet Union in November 1978 — China's principal rival after the Sino-Soviet split — and persecuted its ethnic Chinese (Hoa) minority, many of whom fled the country. Border clashes multiplied. Deng Xiaoping, seeking to punish Vietnam, expose the limits of Soviet support, and consolidate his own position, secured diplomatic cover during a visit to the United States in January 1979 before ordering the attack.

The war

On 17 February 1979, some 200,000 troops of the People's Liberation Army crossed the border along a broad front, advancing into Vietnam's northern provinces. Vietnamese resistance relied mainly on border militia and regional forces while Hanoi kept its battle-hardened main-force divisions — and the bulk of its army in Cambodia — in reserve. The PLA captured the provincial capitals of Lào Cai, Cao Bằng, and Lạng Sơn, the last on 5 March after heavy fighting. On the same day Beijing announced that its objectives had been achieved and began withdrawing, systematically destroying infrastructure in the occupied zone. The withdrawal was completed by 16 March 1979.

Aftermath

Casualty estimates vary widely, with each side suffering on the order of tens of thousands of killed and wounded in one month. The war exposed serious deficiencies in the PLA, accelerating Chinese military modernisation. Border clashes continued through the 1980s, including at Vị Xuyên, until relations were normalised in 1991. Vietnam remained in Cambodia until 1989, so the war failed in its immediate aim of forcing a withdrawal, though China claimed a strategic success in demonstrating that the Soviet Union would not intervene on Vietnam's behalf.
