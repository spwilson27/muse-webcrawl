James Ramsay MacDonald (12 October 1866 – 9 November 1937) was a British statesman and politician who was Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, the first who belonged to the Labour Party, leading minority Labour governments in 1924 and from 1929 to 1931, and then heading a majority National Government from 1931 to 1935.

Labour leader

Born illegitimate in Lossiemouth, Scotland, MacDonald rose through socialist journalism and organising to become secretary of the Labour Representation Committee (1900), the body that became the Labour Party, and entered Parliament in 1906. His opposition to British entry into the First World War cost him his seat in 1918 but, with hindsight, burnished his reputation for principle. In January 1924 he formed the first Labour government, a minority administration that lasted nine months and achieved modest domestic reforms but fell over the Campbell Case; his greatest success in office was in foreign policy, as his own Foreign Secretary, driving the Dawes Plan settlement and the Geneva Protocol.

The crisis of 1931 and the National Government

Returned to power in 1929, MacDonald's second government was overwhelmed by the Great Depression and the sterling crisis of 1931. When his cabinet split irreconcilably over cuts to unemployment benefit, MacDonald resigned — and then immediately re-emerged at the head of a cross-party National Government dominated by Conservatives. Labour expelled him, and he was branded a traitor by his former party for a generation. The National Government won a landslide in 1931 and introduced protection and managed devaluation, but MacDonald, ageing and in failing health, ceded real power to Stanley Baldwin and Neville Chamberlain before resigning in 1935. He died in 1937 aboard a cruise liner on a recuperative voyage to South America.
