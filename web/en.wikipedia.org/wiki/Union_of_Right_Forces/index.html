The Union of Right Forces (Russian: Союз правых сил, Soyuz pravykh sil; abbreviated SPS) was a Russian liberal political party. It was formed in 1999 as an electoral coalition of several liberal and market-reform-oriented groups and was registered as a political party in 2001. The party advocated free-market economic policies, democratic reforms, and closer integration of Russia with the West.

History

The SPS emerged during the 1999 parliamentary election campaign, bringing together figures associated with the economic reforms of the early 1990s, including Anatoly Chubais, Yegor Gaidar, Boris Nemtsov, Sergei Kiriyenko, and Irina Khakamada. In the December 1999 State Duma election the bloc won 8.5 percent of the party-list vote and 29 seats, a comparatively strong result for Russia's fragmented liberal camp.

In 2001 the bloc transformed itself into a full political party. Boris Nemtsov served as its first leader. The party supported President Vladimir Putin on some economic measures while criticizing democratic backsliding, pressure on independent media, and the war in Chechnya.

In the 2003 Duma election the SPS received about 4 percent of the vote, falling short of the 5 percent threshold required for party-list representation, though several of its candidates won single-member district seats. Nemtsov resigned as leader, and in 2005 Nikita Belykh was elected chairman. In the 2007 election the party won only about 1 percent of the vote amid an increasingly restrictive political environment.

Dissolution

In October 2008 the party's political council voted to dissolve the SPS. Many of its members joined the creation of a new liberal party project, Right Cause (Pravoe Delo), formed in November 2008 from the merger of the SPS, the Democratic Party of Russia, and Civilian Power. Other figures, including Nemtsov, refused to join and became involved in opposition movements outside the official party system.

Ideology

The SPS identified itself with the liberal reform tradition of the Yeltsin era. Its programme emphasized private property, low taxes, deregulation, judicial independence, freedom of the press, and Russia's integration into European and global institutions. Critics associated the party with the unpopular privatization policies of the 1990s, which limited its appeal among voters who had suffered economically during the transition period.
