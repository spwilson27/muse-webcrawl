Genius (mythology)

In Roman religion, the genius (plural genii) was the individual instance of a general divine nature present in every individual person, place, or thing. Like a guardian angel, the genius followed a person from birth until death; for women, the equivalent accompanying spirit was called a Juno. Each individual place likewise had its own spirit, the genius loci.

Nature and worship

The genius was associated with a person's rational powers and character, and with the head of the household (paterfamilias), whose genius was honoured by the family on his birthday and at festivals. Offerings of wine, flowers, and incense were made to one's genius, and the birthday cake and birthday candles of modern custom are sometimes traced to these rites. In Roman art the genius was usually depicted as a young man with wings, or as a serpent for a genius loci, or as a paterfamilias holding a patera (libation bowl) and cornucopia.

Because every entity had a genius, the concept extended far beyond individuals: there were genii of the Roman people (Genius Populi Romani), of the city of Rome, of military units, guilds, theatres, and districts. Under the Empire, the genius of the reigning emperor (Genius Augusti) became an important focus of loyalty; oaths were sworn by the emperor's genius, and refusing to sacrifice to it was treated as treason — a point of conflict with early Christians.

Origins of the modern word

The Latin word is related to gignere, "to beget", and the genius was originally connected with generation and the marriage bed (lectus genialis). Through the idea of an individual's innate guiding spirit, the word developed into the modern sense of exceptional intellectual or creative power — a "genius".
