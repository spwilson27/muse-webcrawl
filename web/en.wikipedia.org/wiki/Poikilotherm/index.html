Poikilotherm

A poikilotherm (from Greek poikilos, "varied," and therme, "heat") is an organism whose internal temperature varies considerably, usually matching or tracking the ambient environmental temperature. Poikilotherms are often, though loosely, called "cold-blooded"; the term is approximate because a poikilotherm in a warm environment may have a high body temperature. Poikilothermy is closely associated with ectothermy, in which an organism relies principally on external heat sources to regulate its temperature.

Characteristics

Because their body temperature follows that of the environment, poikilotherms generally have metabolic rates that change with temperature, roughly doubling or tripling for each 10 °C increase within their tolerated range. Their enzymes and physiological processes may be adapted to function over a broad temperature span, and some species possess antifreeze proteins or accumulate cryoprotectants to survive sub-zero conditions.

Behavioural regulation

Many poikilotherms regulate their temperature behaviourally with great precision: lizards shuttle between sun and shade, fish move between water layers of different temperature, and insects bask or seek shelter. This behavioural thermoregulation allows some species to maintain remarkably stable body temperatures during activity despite having no significant metabolic heat production.

Examples

Most fish, amphibians, reptiles, and invertebrates are poikilotherms. Exceptions and intermediate strategies exist: some large fish such as tuna maintain elevated temperatures in certain tissues (regional endothermy), and brooding female pythons can raise their temperature metabolically while incubating eggs.

Advantages and disadvantages

Poikilotherms require far less food than endotherms of similar size, since they do not burn energy to maintain a constant temperature — an advantage in food-poor environments. However, their activity levels are constrained by environmental temperature, and they may be inactive or sluggish in cold conditions.
