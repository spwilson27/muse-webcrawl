Anaerobic organism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An anaerobic organism or anaerobe is any organism that does not require molecular oxygen for growth. It may react negatively or even die if free oxygen is present. In contrast, an organism that can and does use oxygen as a terminal electron acceptor is an aerobic organism (aerobe). Anaerobes may be unicellular (e.g. protozoans, bacteria) or multicellular.

Types

Obligate anaerobes

Obligate anaerobes are harmed or killed by oxygen, which damages their enzymes or generates toxic reactive oxygen species they cannot detoxify because they lack enzymes such as catalase and superoxide dismutase. Examples include the bacteria Clostridium and Bacteroides.

Facultative anaerobes

Facultative anaerobes can grow either with or without oxygen. They grow faster with oxygen because aerobic respiration generates more ATP, but can switch to fermentation or anaerobic respiration in its absence. Examples include Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus, and yeasts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

Aerotolerant anaerobes

Aerotolerant organisms do not use oxygen but are not harmed by it, fermenting regardless of its presence; lactic acid bacteria are typical examples.

Energy metabolism

Anaerobes obtain energy by fermentation — passing electrons from nutrients to organic products such as lactate or ethanol — or by anaerobic respiration, in which electron acceptors other than oxygen, such as nitrate, sulfate, or carbon dioxide, are used. Methanogens, archaea that produce methane, are strict anaerobes of great ecological importance.

Significance

Anaerobes dominate habitats such as deep sediments, marshes, and the gastrointestinal tracts of animals, including the human gut. Some are important pathogens: Clostridium tetani causes tetanus, Clostridium botulinum produces botulinum toxin, and anaerobic infections often arise in deep wounds and abscesses. Anaerobic digestion is also harnessed to treat waste and produce biogas.
