Rheometer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rheometer is a laboratory instrument used to measure the way in which a viscous fluid, paste, or suspension flows in response to applied forces — that is, its rheological properties. Unlike a viscometer, which measures viscosity over a limited range of conditions, a rheometer can impose controlled stress or strain (including oscillatory deformation) and measure viscosity, elasticity, yield stress, and viscoelastic moduli over a wide range of shear rates and frequencies.

Types

Rotational rheometers

The most common design drives a measuring geometry — cone-and-plate, parallel plates, or concentric cylinders (Couette) — in steady rotation or oscillation while recording torque and displacement. Modern instruments are controlled-stress or controlled-strain machines mounted on air bearings, often with temperature control from below −50 °C to several hundred degrees using Peltier elements or convection ovens.

Capillary rheometers

A piston forces material through a die of known dimensions at a set volumetric flow rate; the pressure drop yields the shear viscosity, a method suited to very high shear rates relevant to polymer extrusion and injection moulding.

Extensional rheometers

These stretch a filament or sheet to measure extensional viscosity, for example by monitoring the thinning of a liquid bridge between two plates (capillary breakup extensional rheometry).

Measurements

  • Flow curves — viscosity versus shear rate, revealing shear thinning, shear thickening, and yield stress.
  • Oscillatory tests — storage modulus G′ and loss modulus G″ versus frequency or amplitude, characterising viscoelastic solids and gels.
  • Creep and recovery — deformation under constant stress over time.

Applications

Rheometers are used across the polymer, food, cosmetics, pharmaceutical, paint, and petroleum industries for formulation development, processing design, and quality control.
