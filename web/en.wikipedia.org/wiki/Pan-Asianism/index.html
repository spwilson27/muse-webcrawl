Pan-Asianism (also known as Asianism or Greater Asianism) is an ideology aimed at creating a political and economic unity among the peoples of Asia. It holds that Asian nations share common values and interests and should cooperate — and in stronger versions unite — against Western imperialism and colonial domination.

Origins

Ideas of Asian solidarity emerged in the late nineteenth century in reaction to European colonial expansion. The Japanese art historian Okakura Kakuzō opened his The Ideals of the East (1903) with the declaration "Asia is one." Japan's victory over Russia in the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05 electrified nationalists across Asia, and Tokyo became a hub for exiled revolutionaries from India, China, Vietnam and elsewhere.

Variants

Pan-Asianism took many forms: Sun Yat-sen's 1924 Kobe speech on "Greater Asianism" urged a humane, anti-colonial unity led jointly by China and Japan, while Indian figures such as Rash Behari Bose promoted Asian liberation movements. In the 1930s and 1940s, however, the Japanese Empire appropriated pan-Asian rhetoric to legitimise its expansion under the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, discrediting the ideal for many after 1945.

Post-war echoes

After the Second World War, Asian regionalism resurfaced in more institutional and less ideological forms: the Bandung Conference of 1955, the Non-Aligned Movement, ASEAN, and various East Asian summit frameworks all drew, in diluted form, on the older pan-Asian impulse toward regional cooperation free of outside domination.
