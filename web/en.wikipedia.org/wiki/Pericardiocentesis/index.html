Pericardiocentesis

Pericardiocentesis is a medical procedure in which fluid is aspirated from the pericardial cavity, the sac surrounding the heart, using a needle and syringe or catheter. It is performed to diagnose the cause of a pericardial effusion and, most urgently, to treat cardiac tamponade, in which accumulated fluid compresses the heart and prevents it from filling.

Indications

The principal emergency indication is cardiac tamponade with haemodynamic compromise. Elective pericardiocentesis may be performed to obtain fluid for analysis when a specific diagnosis (such as bacterial infection or malignancy) is suspected, or to relieve symptoms from a large effusion. Small, asymptomatic effusions are usually observed rather than drained.

Procedure

The procedure is usually carried out under echocardiographic guidance, which identifies the largest and most accessible collection of fluid and monitors the needle position. After sterile preparation and local anaesthesia, the operator advances a needle — commonly from below the xiphoid process (subxiphoid approach) or through an intercostal space near the apex — into the pericardial space. Fluid is aspirated, and a soft pigtail catheter is often left in place over a guidewire for continued drainage. In a patient with tamponade, removal of even a modest volume of fluid can produce a dramatic improvement in blood pressure and cardiac output.

The aspirated fluid is sent for laboratory analysis, which may include cell counts, chemistry, microbiology, and cytology.

Risks

Complications include puncture or laceration of the heart muscle or a coronary artery, injury to the liver or lung (with pneumothorax), arrhythmias, infection, and bleeding. Image guidance and modern technique have greatly reduced the complication rate compared with the historical blind approach. When pericardiocentesis is impossible or effusions recur, surgical drainage through a pericardial window may be required.
