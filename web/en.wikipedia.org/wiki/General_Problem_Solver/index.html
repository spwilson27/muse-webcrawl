The General Problem Solver (GPS) was a computer program created in 1957 by Herbert A. Simon, J. C. Shaw, and Allen Newell, intended to work as a universal problem solver. It was the first program to separate its knowledge of problems (represented as input data) from its strategy for solving them, and it introduced means-ends analysis, a problem-solving technique that became central to early artificial intelligence research.

Overview

GPS represented a problem as a set of objects, operators that could transform one object into another, and differences between objects. Given an initial state and a goal state, the program compared the two, identified the most important difference, and selected an operator relevant to reducing that difference. If the operator could not be applied immediately, the preconditions for applying it became a new subgoal, attacked recursively. In this way GPS built a hierarchy of goals and subgoals, mimicking the planning behaviour observed in the thinking-aloud protocols of human subjects solving logic and puzzle problems.

Domains

GPS was demonstrated on a range of tasks including symbolic logic proofs, trigonometric identities, the Tower of Hanoi puzzle, the missionaries and cannibals problem, and formal integration. Problem-specific knowledge was supplied in a "table of connections" linking differences to operators, keeping the solver itself domain-independent.

Limitations

The program struggled with problems requiring the interleaving of subgoals (such as the two-block stacking anomaly later formalized by other researchers), with ordering interacting goals, and with combinationally explosive search spaces. Its performance depended heavily on the quality of the hand-supplied difference ordering, leading critics to observe that much of the "intelligence" lay in the problem formulation rather than the solver.

Influence

GPS founded the research programme of heuristic search and problem-solving as symbolic manipulation, influenced later systems such as STRIPS and SOAR, and, together with the Logic Theorist, helped establish the physical symbol system hypothesis in cognitive science and AI.
