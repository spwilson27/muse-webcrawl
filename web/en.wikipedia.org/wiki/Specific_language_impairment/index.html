Specific language impairment

Specific language impairment (SLI), now more commonly termed developmental language disorder (DLD), is a communication disorder that interferes with the development of language skills in children who have no hearing loss or intellectual disability. Affected children are late to talk and may struggle with vocabulary, grammar, sentence structure and understanding language.

Characteristics

Children with SLI typically have particular difficulty with grammatical morphology, such as tense marking in English ("he walk" for "he walked"), and may produce shorter, simpler sentences than peers. Receptive language (understanding) can be affected as well as expressive language. Intelligence in nonverbal domains is within the normal range, which distinguishes the condition from global developmental delay.

Causes

The causes are not fully understood but involve genetic and neurobiological factors; the disorder runs in families, and twin studies indicate substantial heritability. No single gene explains most cases, though mutations in FOXP2 cause a severe related speech and language disorder in one well-studied family.

Prevalence and treatment

Estimates suggest around 7 percent of young children are affected. Intervention by speech-language pathologists focuses on building vocabulary, grammar and narrative skills, and early identification improves educational outcomes.
