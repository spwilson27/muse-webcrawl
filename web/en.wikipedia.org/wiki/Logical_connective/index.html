Logical connective

In logic, a logical connective (also called a logical operator, sentential connective, or sentential operator) is a logical constant or symbol used to connect two or more logical formulas, forming a compound statement whose truth value depends on the truth values of its components. Logical connectives are central to propositional logic, Boolean algebra, set theory and computer science, where they appear as logic gates and Boolean operators.

Truth functions

In classical logic, connectives are truth-functional: the truth value of the compound is a function of the truth values of the parts, specifiable by a truth table. There are 16 possible binary truth functions of two variables. Some connectives are functionally complete on their own: NAND and NOR can each be used to define all the others, a fact exploited in digital electronics, where NAND gates are a universal building block.

Natural language

Connectives correspond only roughly to natural-language words. "And" can express temporal sequence ("they married and had a child"), and the natural-language "or" is often exclusive where logical disjunction is inclusive. Material implication also gives rise to the so-called paradoxes of material implication: a false proposition implies anything. Other logics introduce non-truth-functional operators, such as the modal connectives □ (necessarily) and ◇ (possibly), or counterfactual conditionals.
