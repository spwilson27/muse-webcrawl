Ichneumonidae

The Ichneumonidae, also known as ichneumon wasps, ichneumonid wasps, or Darwin wasps, are a family of parasitoid wasps in the order Hymenoptera. With more than 25,000 described species and an estimated total possibly exceeding 100,000, they form one of the largest families in the entire animal kingdom, distributed across some 40 or more subfamilies worldwide.

Biology

Ichneumonids are parasitoids of the larvae and pupae of other insects, especially Lepidoptera (moths and butterflies), Coleoptera, and other Hymenoptera. The female uses her ovipositor — which in some species, such as Megarhyssa, is several times the length of her body — to drill through wood or other substrates and lay an egg in or on a concealed host. The wasp larva consumes the host from within, typically killing it only when the host has finished growing or pupated. Many ichneumonids introduce polydnaviruses that suppress the host's immune system.

Appearance

Adults are typically slender wasps with long antennae of many segments, a narrow "wasp waist," and characteristic wing venation. They range from a few millimetres to over 10 cm in total length including the ovipositor, and many are brightly coloured with banded patterns.

Darwin and the ichneumons

The family is famous through Charles Darwin's 1860 letter to Asa Gray, in which he wrote that he could not persuade himself that a beneficent and omnipotent God would have designedly created the Ichneumonidae with the express intention of their feeding within the living bodies of caterpillars. The passage is frequently cited in discussions of natural theology and the problem of suffering in nature. Ichneumonids are also economically important as natural enemies of forest and agricultural pests, and several species are used in biological control.
