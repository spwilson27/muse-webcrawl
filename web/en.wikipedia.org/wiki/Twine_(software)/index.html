Twine (software)

Original authorChris Klimas
Initial release2009
Written inJavaScript
Operating systemWindows, macOS, Linux, web
TypeInteractive fiction authoring tool
LicenseGPL (free and open-source)

Twine is a free and open-source tool for telling interactive, nonlinear stories. Created by Chris Klimas in 2009, it allows users to construct interactive fiction and hypertext narratives in a visual editor, then publish them as HTML files that can be played in any web browser. Twine requires no programming knowledge for basic stories, though it supports variables, conditional logic, and scripting for more complex works.

The Twine editor displays a story as a map of passages connected by links.

Design

In Twine, a story is composed of passages — chunks of text shown to the reader one at a time. Passages are connected by links written in a wiki-like syntax: text surrounded by double square brackets becomes a link to the passage of that name. The editor presents the story as a zoomable map, making the branching structure of the narrative visible at a glance.

Twine stories are built using story formats, which control how a story is displayed and what scripting features are available. The default and bundled formats include Harlowe, SugarCube, Snowman, and the proofing format Paperthin. Harlowe is the default format in Twine 2 and emphasises a gentle learning curve with a macro-based syntax; SugarCube offers extensive features popular with experienced authors.

History

The original Twine (now called Twine 1) was released in 2009 as a desktop application derived from the TiddlyWiki codebase. Twine 2, released in 2015, was rewritten as an HTML5 application that runs both in the browser and as a downloadable app, and became the standard version of the tool. Twine is developed as an open-source project, with the Interactive Fiction Technology Foundation (IFTF) supporting its continued development.

Use and influence

Twine became a central tool of the early-2010s "Twine revolution" in independent games, lowering the barrier to making personal, experimental, and autobiographical games. Notable works made with or prototyped in it include Depression Quest by Zoë Quinn, Howling Dogs by Porpentine, and Anna Anthropy's works and her book Rise of the Videogame Zinesters, which championed the tool. Twine has also been widely adopted in education, game jams, and digital literature, and commercial games such as Black Closet and episodes of larger productions have used it for prototyping and narrative design.
