The Railroad Commission of Texas (RRC; also sometimes called the Texas Railroad Commission) is the state agency that regulates the oil and natural gas industry, gas utilities, pipeline safety, safety in the liquefied petroleum gas industry, and surface coal and uranium mining. Despite its name, it ceased regulating railroads in 2005.

Established by the Legislature in 1891, it is the oldest regulatory agency in the state and one of the oldest of its kind in the United States. It is headed by three commissioners elected statewide for staggered six-year terms.

History

The Commission was created in 1891 under Governor Jim Hogg, a leading progressive reformer, to regulate railroads, terminals, wharves and express companies, in an era when railroads wielded enormous political and economic power in Texas. John H. Reagan, the former United States Senator and Confederate postmaster general, served as its first chairman.

With the discovery of oil at Spindletop in 1901 and the growth of the Texas petroleum industry, the Commission's authority expanded. A 1917 law declared pipelines common carriers and gave the Commission jurisdiction over them; in 1919 it received authority over oil and gas conservation, and in 1920 over gas utilities. During the East Texas oil boom of the 1930s, the Commission's system of prorationing — setting production allowances for wells and fields — became central to stabilising world oil prices, a role in which the RRC was widely imitated, notably by OPEC.

Current functions

The Commission's responsibilities today include:

  • Issuing drilling permits and administering oil and gas conservation rules, including well spacing, density and production allowables;
  • Pipeline safety and the regulation of intrastate pipelines;
  • Regulation of gas utilities and liquefied petroleum gas;
  • Surface mining and reclamation for coal and uranium;
  • Plugging of orphaned wells and remediation of abandoned oilfield sites.

Its regulation of railroads was transferred to the Texas Department of Transportation in 2005, so the agency no longer has any railway responsibilities despite its name. Periodic "sunset" reviews by the Texas Legislature have repeatedly recommended renaming the agency, but the name has been retained.

Criticism

The Commission has been criticised for close ties to the industry it regulates, since commissioners are elected and campaigns are often financed by oil and gas interests. Its response to induced seismicity linked to wastewater injection wells, and to natural gas flaring, has also drawn scrutiny.
