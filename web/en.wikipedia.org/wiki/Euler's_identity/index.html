Euler's identity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Euler's identity written in elegant mathematical notation]

In mathematical analysis, Euler's identity is the equality e^(iπ) + 1 = 0, where e is Euler's number (the base of natural logarithms), i is the imaginary unit, and π is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter.

It is a special case of Euler's formula e^(ix) = cos x + i sin x, at x = π. The identity is celebrated as an example of mathematical beauty: it links five fundamental constants (0, 1, e, i, π) with the three basic arithmetic operations, each exactly once. Richard Feynman called it 'the most remarkable formula in mathematics'.

Understanding

Geometrically, e^(iπ) is the point reached by rotating halfway around the unit circle in the complex plane, arriving at −1; adding 1 returns to the origin. The identity underlies Fourier analysis and the complex-exponential notation used throughout electrical engineering and quantum mechanics.

Unlike Euler's formula, the identity itself has few direct applications — its fame is aesthetic and pedagogical, a fingerprint of the deep unity of analysis.
