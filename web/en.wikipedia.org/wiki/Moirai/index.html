Moirai

In Greek mythology, the Moirai (Ancient Greek: Μοῖραι, "parts" or "shares") — often known in English as the Fates — were the personifications of destiny. They were three sisters: Clotho (the spinner), who spun the thread of life; Lachesis (the allotter), who measured it; and Atropos (the unturnable), who cut it with her shears, ending a mortal's life.

The Moirai were said to ensure that every being, mortal and divine, lived out the destiny assigned to them by the laws of the universe. Even Zeus was portrayed as subject to, or at least respectful of, their decrees. Their parentage varies between sources: Hesiod's Theogony calls them daughters of Nyx (Night) in one passage and daughters of Zeus and Themis in another.

Role and worship

The Moirai controlled the metaphorical thread of life of every mortal from birth to death. They were usually depicted as old women, ugly and lame, reflecting the inexorability of fate. They had cults in several Greek cities and were sometimes associated with the Erinyes and with goddesses of birth.

Equivalents in other traditions

The Roman counterparts of the Moirai were the Parcae (Nona, Decima and Morta). Norse mythology has the Norns, who weave the fates of gods and men at the well of Urd, and similar triads of fate-weaving women appear in Baltic and Slavic folklore.
