Consumption function

In economics, the consumption function describes the relationship between consumption and disposable income. The concept is believed to have been introduced into macroeconomics by John Maynard Keynes in 1936, who used it to develop the notion of a government spending multiplier.

In its simplest form the consumption function is:

C = a + b·Yd

where a is autonomous consumption (consumption that occurs even when disposable income is zero), b is the marginal propensity to consume — the fraction of each additional unit of disposable income that is spent — and Yd is disposable income.

Keynes's hypotheses

Keynes proposed that consumption depends mainly on current income, that the marginal propensity to consume lies between zero and one, and that the average propensity to consume falls as income rises. These claims generated well-known puzzles: Kuznets's long-run data showed a roughly constant average propensity to consume despite rising incomes.

Later theories

Subsequent theories reconciled the short-run and long-run evidence. Duesenberry's relative income hypothesis made consumption depend on income relative to others and to one's own past peak. Milton Friedman's permanent income hypothesis (1957) argued that consumption depends on long-run expected ("permanent") income, while the life-cycle hypothesis of Franco Modigliani held that households smooth consumption over their lifetimes, saving during working years and dissaving in retirement.
