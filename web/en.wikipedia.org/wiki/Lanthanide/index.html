The lanthanide or lanthanoid series of chemical elements comprises at least the 14 metallic elements with atomic numbers 58 through 71, from cerium through lutetium, in which the 4f orbitals are progressively filled; the series is usually taken to include lanthanum (57) itself, giving 15 elements from lanthanum to lutetium. All are f-block elements, found in nature in minerals such as monazite and bastnäsite, and most are counted among the rare-earth elements.

Because the 4f electrons are shielded and little involved in bonding, the lanthanides are chemically very similar: all are silvery, fairly soft, reactive metals that most characteristically form +3 ions. The gradual decrease in ionic radius across the series — the lanthanide contraction — has wide consequences for the chemistry of the elements that follow them.

Properties

The elements are: lanthanum, cerium, praseodymium, neodymium, promethium (the only lanthanide with no stable isotopes), samarium, europium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, holmium, erbium, thulium, ytterbium and lutetium. Their magnetic and optical properties — arising from unpaired 4f electrons and sharp f–f transitions — underpin many applications: neodymium and samarium in powerful permanent magnets, europium and terbium as phosphors, gadolinium in MRI contrast agents, erbium in fibre-optic amplifiers, and lanthanum in camera lenses and nickel–metal hydride batteries.

Occurrence and production

Despite the name "rare earths," several lanthanides are as abundant in the crust as common metals; their difficulty lies in separating such similar elements from one another, historically by fractional crystallisation and now by solvent extraction and ion exchange. China has dominated modern production, with significant deposits also in the United States, Australia and elsewhere.

History

The series was disentangled over more than a century from minerals found near Ytterby in Sweden and at Bastnäs; lanthanum itself, isolated in 1839 by Carl Gustaf Mosander, takes its name from the Greek lanthanein, "to lie hidden," reflecting how long its oxide had been concealed within ceria.
