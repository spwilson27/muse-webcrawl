Byzantine literature

Byzantine literature is the Greek literature of the Middle Ages, produced in the Byzantine Empire from the 4th/5th century until the fall of Constantinople in 1453. It combines the inherited traditions of ancient Greek literature and Christian theology with influences from Roman, Syrian, and later Western and Slavic cultures. Byzantine writers preserved and transmitted much of classical Greek literature through their copying, commentary, and scholarship.

An illuminated manuscript, typical of Byzantine book production.

Characteristics

Byzantine literature is typically divided between a learned, classicising written language — an archaizing form of Attic Greek — and a vernacular language closer to spoken Greek. Most literary production was strongly influenced by rhetoric and by the educational curriculum (the enkyklios paideia), which emphasised imitation (mimesis) of classical models. Originality was often expressed through compilation, paraphrase, and commentary rather than the invention of new genres.

Genres

Historiography

History writing was a central Byzantine genre, divided between classicising histories (such as those of Procopius, Michael Psellos, Anna Komnene, and Niketas Choniates) and world chronicles written in a simpler register (such as those of John Malalas and Theophanes the Confessor).

Hagiography and theology

Saints' lives (vitae) were among the most widely read texts; the collection of Symeon the Metaphrast standardised the genre in the 10th century. Theological writing included the works of Maximus the Confessor, John of Damascus, and Gregory Palamas.

Poetry and romance

Religious hymnography flourished with Romanos the Melodist's kontakia in the 6th century. Secular poetry includes the epigrams of the Greek Anthology's later books and the satirical and begging poems of the 12th century. Vernacular romances, such as Digenes Akritas and the Palaeologan romances, appeared from the 12th century onward.

Scholarship

Byzantine scholars produced lexica (the Suda), encyclopaedias (the works compiled under Constantine VII), commentaries (Eustathius of Thessalonica on Homer), and the anthologies of Photios (Bibliotheca) and Constantine Cephalas. The 9th-century minuscule reform of bookhand and the scholarly circles of the Macedonian and Palaeologan "renaissances" preserved classical texts that would later reach Renaissance Italy.

Legacy

After 1453, Byzantine scholars such as Bessarion and John Argyropoulos carried manuscripts and Greek learning to the West, contributing to the Renaissance revival of Greek studies, while Byzantine literary forms continued to influence Orthodox and Slavic literatures.
