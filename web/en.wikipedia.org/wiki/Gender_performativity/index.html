Gender performativity

Gender performativity is a term created by American philosopher and gender theorist Judith Butler in their 1990 book Gender Trouble. Butler's theory argues that gender is not an innate essence but is constituted through the repeated performance of acts, gestures, and speech that conform to social expectations. In this view, gender is something one does rather than something one is.

Background

Butler drew on J. L. Austin's theory of performative speech acts, in which certain utterances do not merely describe reality but bring it into being (as in "I now pronounce you married"). Applying this idea to gender, Butler argued that the repeated stylisation of the body within a rigid social framework produces the appearance of a natural gendered self.

Performance versus performativity

Butler distinguishes performativity from theatrical performance. Performativity is not a voluntary act in which a subject chooses a gender as one might choose a costume; rather, it is a forced repetition of norms that precedes and constrains the subject. The "performance" is compelled by social sanctions and taboos, and the subject is itself produced through this repetition.

Criticism and influence

The theory has been highly influential in feminist theory, queer theory, and the study of gender and sexuality. Critics have questioned whether it adequately accounts for the materiality of the body, the role of biology, and individual agency. Butler addressed many of these concerns in the later work Bodies That Matter (1993), clarifying that performativity concerns the process through which bodies come to matter socially.
