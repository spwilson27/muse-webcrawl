Mongolian wrestling

Mongolian wrestling, known as bökh (Mongolian: бөх, "durability"), is the traditional folk wrestling style of the Mongols in Mongolia, Inner Mongolia and neighbouring regions, practised for many centuries. It is one of the "Three Games of Men" at the Naadam festival, alongside horse racing and archery, and is inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity as part of Naadam.

Rules

The object is to force the opponent to touch the ground with any part of the body other than the soles of the feet, using throws, trips and lifts; there are hundreds of named techniques (mets). Bouts have no weight classes in traditional competition and, historically, no time limit. Wrestlers are paired by rank in a single-elimination tournament at Naadam, where hundreds compete over nine rounds.

Attire and ritual

Wrestlers wear a distinctive costume: an open-chested jacket (zodog), briefs (shuudag), traditional boots, and a pointed hat. A legend explains the open chest — that a champion was once revealed to be a woman, after which the jacket was worn open to prove wrestlers are men. Before and after bouts, wrestlers perform the "eagle dance" (devekh), imitating a bird of prey, guided by their second and praise-singer (zasuul), who also holds the wrestler's hat during the bout and sings his praises.

Titles

Ranks are animal-themed: winning five rounds earns hartsaga (falcon), seven rounds zaan (elephant), winning a tournament once arslan (lion), and repeated tournament wins avarga (titan/giant).

History

Wrestling appears in Mongolian culture from the era of the Xiongnu and is depicted in rock paintings; it was a means of keeping warriors fit in peacetime. Under the Mongol Empire it was a fixture of court and military life, and it survived as a national symbol through the socialist period to today. Related styles include Buryat and Inner Mongolian variants and Tuvan khuresh.
