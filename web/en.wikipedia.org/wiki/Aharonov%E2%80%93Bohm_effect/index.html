Aharonov–Bohm effect

The Aharonov–Bohm effect, sometimes called the Ehrenberg–Siday–Aharonov–Bohm effect, is a quantum mechanical phenomenon in which an electrically charged particle is affected by an electromagnetic potential (scalar or vector) even in regions where both the magnetic and electric fields are zero. It demonstrates that in quantum mechanics the electromagnetic potentials, rather than only the fields, have direct physical significance.

Description

In the best-known version, electrons passing on either side of a long solenoid — whose magnetic field is confined inside — produce an interference pattern that shifts when the current through the solenoid changes, even though the electrons travel only through field-free space. The phase shift is proportional to the magnetic flux enclosed and to the line integral of the vector potential A around the path.

History

The effect was first derived by Werner Ehrenberg and Raymond Siday in 1949 and independently rediscovered by Yakir Aharonov and David Bohm in 1959. Early experiments were disputed because of stray fields, but definitive confirmation came from Akira Tonomura's electron holography experiments in the 1980s using toroidal magnets shielded by superconductors.

Significance

The effect foreshadowed the central role of gauge potentials in modern physics and is related to Berry phase phenomena. It shows that quantum mechanics is nonlocal in the sense that the phase of a wavefunction depends on potentials along paths through regions the particle may never enter as a field.
