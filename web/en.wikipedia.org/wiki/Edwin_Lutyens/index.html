Sir Edwin Landseer Lutyens (29 March 1869 – 1 January 1944) was an English architect known for imaginatively adapting traditional architectural styles to the requirements of his era. He designed many English country houses, war memorials, and public buildings. In his biography, the writer Christopher Hussey called him 'the greatest British architect of the twentieth (and of any other) century'.

Country houses

Lutyens made his name with Surrey country houses in the Arts and Crafts manner — Munstead Wood for the garden designer Gertrude Jekyll, with whom he collaborated on over a hundred projects — developing a style that combined vernacular materials with formal classical planning. Later houses such as Castle Drogo in Devon (1911–1930) showed a sterner, monumental classicism.

New Delhi

From 1912 Lutyens, with Herbert Baker, planned the new imperial capital of British India at New Delhi, designing the Viceroy's House (now Rashtrapati Bhavan), the Secretariat buildings around it, and the ceremonial axis of Rajpath, blending European classicism with Mughal and Buddhist motifs in what is called the 'Delhi Order'.

War memorials

As an architect to the Imperial (now Commonwealth) War Graves Commission, Lutyens designed many cemeteries and memorials of the First World War, including the Cenotaph in Whitehall, London, the Thiepval Memorial to the Missing of the Somme, and the Stone of Remembrance found in larger war cemeteries.

Legacy

His unbuilt design for Liverpool's Roman Catholic cathedral would have been among the largest churches in the world; only its crypt was completed. His daughter was the composer Elisabeth Lutyens.
