Modularity of mind

The modularity of mind is the notion that a mind may, at least in part, be composed of innate neural structures or mental modules which have distinct, established, and evolutionarily developed functions. However, different definitions of "module" have been proposed by different authors. According to Jerry Fodor, the author of Modularity of Mind (1983), a system may be considered 'modular' if its functions are made of multiple dimensions or units to some degree. An example of a modular system is the cognitive process underlying perception, which Fodor argues is modular.

Fodor's modularity hypothesis

In Modularity of Mind (1983), Jerry Fodor defined the characteristics of a module, including domain specificity, informational encapsulation, mandatory operation, shallow outputs, fixed neural architecture, and characteristic ontogeny. Fodor focused on low-level perceptual systems such as vision and language processing, which he argued are modular, while higher-level "central systems" such as belief fixation are not, because they are isotropic and quinean — that is, information from anywhere in the system can bear on them.

Massive modularity

Evolutionary psychologists such as Leda Cosmides, John Tooby, and Steven Pinker have proposed a stronger thesis of massive modularity, according to which the mind is composed almost entirely of special-purpose computational modules shaped by natural selection to solve problems faced by our ancestors. On this view, even central cognition is modular to a substantial degree. Critics argue that the evidence does not support such extensive modularity and that many cognitive processes are domain-general.
