Neodymium

Neodymium is a chemical element with the symbol Nd and atomic number 60. It is the fourth member of the lanthanide series and is considered one of the rare-earth metals. It is a hard, slightly malleable, silvery metal that quickly tarnishes in air and moisture.

History

Neodymium was discovered by the Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach in 1885, who separated it — along with praseodymium — from the supposed element didymium. Pure metallic neodymium was not isolated until 1925. The name comes from the Greek neos (new) and didymos (twin).

Applications

Neodymium's most important use is in neodymium magnets (Nd2Fe14B), the strongest type of permanent magnet available, used in hard disk drives, headphones, electric vehicle motors, and wind turbine generators. Neodymium compounds also colour glass in delicate shades of violet and grey, serve in neodymium-doped laser crystals such as Nd:YAG, and are used in welder's and glassblower's goggles ("didymium glass").

Occurrence

Neodymium occurs mainly in the minerals monazite and bastnäsite. Despite the "rare earth" label it is fairly abundant in the Earth's crust, comparable to copper, and China dominates world production.
