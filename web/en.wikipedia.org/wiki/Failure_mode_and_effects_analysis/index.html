Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA; often written with "failure modes" in plural) is the process of reviewing as many components, assemblies, and subsystems as possible to identify potential failure modes in a system and their causes and effects. For each component, the failure modes and their resulting effects on the rest of the system are recorded in a specific FMEA worksheet. There are numerous variations of such worksheets.

A FMEA is primarily a qualitative analysis tool, although it can be extended with semi-quantitative risk ranking. The related technique FMECA (failure mode, effects, and criticality analysis) adds a criticality analysis that charts the probability of failure modes against the severity of their consequences.

History

Procedures for conducting FMECA were described in US Armed Forces Military Procedures document MIL-P-1629 (1949). During the 1960s the technique was adopted by NASA for the Apollo programme, where it was used to identify single-point failures in spacecraft systems. In the 1970s the Ford Motor Company introduced FMEA to the automotive industry following the Pinto affair, and it subsequently became a core tool of quality management in manufacturing, codified in standards such as SAE J1739, AIAG manuals, and the German VDA handbook; AIAG and VDA published a harmonised FMEA handbook in 2019.

Methodology

A typical FMEA proceeds in steps:

  1. Assemble a cross-functional team and define the system, product, or process under analysis.
  2. Identify the functions of each item and the ways it could fail (failure modes).
  3. Describe the effects of each failure on the customer or end user.
  4. Assign rankings for severity (S), occurrence (O), and detection (D), usually on 1–10 scales.
  5. Calculate the risk priority number, RPN = S × O × D, and prioritise the highest risks.
  6. Recommend corrective actions, assign responsibility, and re-score the rankings after action.

Common variants include Design FMEA (DFMEA), focused on product design, and Process FMEA (PFMEA), focused on manufacturing and assembly processes. In healthcare, HFMEA adapts the method to clinical processes.

Criticism

The RPN has been criticised because different (S, O, D) combinations can yield the same RPN while representing quite different risks, and because multiplication of ordinal scales is mathematically dubious. The AIAG–VDA handbook replaced sole reliance on RPN with an "Action Priority" table. FMEA is also resource-intensive and its quality depends heavily on the team's knowledge of failure modes; unknown failure modes cannot be listed.
