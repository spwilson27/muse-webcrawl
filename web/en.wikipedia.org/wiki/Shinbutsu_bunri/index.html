Shinbutsu bunri

The shinbutsu bunri (神仏分離, "separation of kami and buddhas") was the policy of the Meiji government of Japan in the late 1860s and early 1870s to separate Shinto from Buddhism, ending the more than thousand-year-old syncretism of the two religions known as shinbutsu-shūgō.

Background

For centuries, Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines in Japan had been intertwined: shrines were administered by Buddhist priests, Buddhist statues stood in shrine precincts, and kami were regarded as manifestations of buddhas. Nativist Kokugaku scholars of the Edo period criticized this amalgamation and called for a pure, independent Shinto.

The separation orders

In 1868 the new Meiji government issued a series of decrees ordering the removal of Buddhist objects, priests, and terminology from Shinto shrines, and the establishment of Shinto as a distinct institution aligned with the imperial cult. Implementation varied by prefecture, but in many places the policy sparked the haibutsu kishaku movement, an often violent wave of anti-Buddhist destruction in which temples were closed and statues, bells, and scriptures were destroyed or sold.

Effects

The separation forced temples and shrines to define themselves institutionally for the first time, led to the loss of many religious treasures, and left lasting traces in Japanese religious life, even though syncretic practices persisted at the popular level.
