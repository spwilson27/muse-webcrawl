The Battle of Qatwan (Chinese: 卡特万之战) was fought on 9 September 1141 on the steppe of Qatwan north of Samarkand, between the forces of the Qara Khitai (Western Liao) under Yelü Dashi and the army of the Great Seljuk sultan Ahmad Sanjar, together with his Kara-Khanid vassals. The Qara Khitai won an overwhelming victory that ended Seljuk power east of the Oxus and established Qara Khitai supremacy over Transoxiana and Eastern Turkestan.

Background

Yelü Dashi, a prince of the Khitan Liao dynasty that had been destroyed by the Jurchen Jin in 1125, led his followers west across Central Asia and by 1134 had founded a new empire — the Qara Khitai — in the region of Balasagun. As he expanded into the Kara-Khanid khanates, the Kara-Khanid ruler of Samarkand appealed to his overlord Sanjar, the most powerful Seljuk sultan, who marched east in 1141 to restore the situation.

The battle

Accounts of the battle are brief and partly legendary. Sanjar's army — which included the Kara-Khanids of Samarkand — was drawn onto the Qatwan steppe and enveloped by the Qara Khitai horse-archers. Yelü Dashi is said to have divided his army into three parts and attacked from multiple directions; the Seljuk army collapsed with enormous losses. Sanjar himself barely escaped to Termez, leaving his camp, his baggage, and even his wife to be captured. The chronicler Ibn al-Athir described the disaster as one of the greatest defeats suffered by Islam in the region.

Consequences

After Qatwan the Qara Khitai annexed Transoxiana, reducing the Kara-Khanids to tributaries, and Seljuk authority never recovered east of Khurasan; Sanjar's prestige, and with it the Great Seljuk Empire, entered terminal decline (Sanjar was later captured by Oghuz rebels in 1153). In the West, news of a mighty non-Muslim king defeating the Seljuks in the East fed the European legend of Prester John, the mythical Christian priest-king — Yelü Dashi's victory is one of the historical events most often proposed as the kernel of the legend, first recorded by Bishop Otto of Freising a few years later.
