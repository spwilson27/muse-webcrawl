Bluetooth is a short-range wireless technology standard for exchanging data between fixed and mobile devices over short distances using radio waves in the 2.4 GHz ISM band, and for building personal area networks (PANs). It is managed by the Bluetooth Special Interest Group and implemented in billions of devices, from phones and headphones to medical sensors.

History and name

Work began at Ericsson in Sweden in 1994, led by engineers including Jaap Haartsen, as a wireless replacement for RS-232 cables. The name honours Harald "Bluetooth" Gormsson, the 10th-century king who united Danish tribes — the technology was meant to unite devices in the same way — and the Bluetooth logo combines the Hagall and Bjarkan runes of his initials. Intel's Jim Kardach proposed the name in 1997, and the SIG was founded in 1998 by Ericsson, Nokia, IBM, Toshiba and Intel.

Technology

Bluetooth uses frequency-hopping spread spectrum, dividing the band into channels hopped many times per second to resist interference. Classic Bluetooth supports data rates up to about 3 Mbit/s; Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), introduced with version 4.0 in 2010, slashes power consumption for wearables, beacons and IoT devices. Later versions added higher speeds, longer range, direction finding, and LE Audio with Auracast broadcast audio. Devices form small networks called piconets, and pairing — increasingly via secure simple pairing — establishes encrypted links.

Uses

Typical applications include wireless headsets and speakers, keyboards and mice, file transfer, tethering, game controllers, fitness trackers, hearing aids, and indoor positioning beacons.
