Protein structure prediction

Protein structure prediction is the inference of the three-dimensional structure of a protein from its amino acid sequence — that is, the prediction of its folding and its secondary and tertiary structure from its primary structure. Structure prediction is different from the inverse problem of protein design. It has been one of the most important goals pursued by bioinformatics and theoretical chemistry, because a protein's function is determined by its shape, and knowing the structure aids drug design and the understanding of disease.

Background

Experimentally determining a protein structure by X-ray crystallography, NMR spectroscopy or cryo-electron microscopy is slow and expensive, while genome projects produce sequences far faster than structures can be solved. Every two years, the performance of current methods is assessed in the CASP experiment (Critical Assessment of Techniques for Protein Structure Prediction), founded in 1994.

Methods

Homology modelling

If a target sequence resembles a protein of known structure, comparative (homology) modelling copies the fold from the template, on the principle that structure is more conserved than sequence. Related is threading (fold recognition), which fits a sequence onto a library of known folds when no close homologue is detectable by sequence alone.

Ab initio and physics-based methods

Ab initio methods attempt to fold a chain from physical principles alone, sampling conformations guided by energy functions. The Rosetta software suite pioneered fragment-assembly approaches that dominated CASP for many years. Molecular dynamics simulations, and distributed projects such as Folding@home, attack the same problem by simulating the folding process.

Deep learning

At CASP13 in 2018, DeepMind's AlphaFold won decisively using deep learning over evolutionary and geometric information. In November 2020, AlphaFold 2 achieved median backbone accuracy rivalling experimental structures at CASP14, and was widely reported as having effectively solved the single-chain prediction problem. In July 2021 DeepMind and EMBL-EBI released the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database, eventually covering predicted structures for over 200 million proteins. Deep-learning methods such as RoseTTAFold and ESMFold followed. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognised Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for protein structure prediction (shared with David Baker for protein design).

Secondary structure prediction

A simpler sub-problem is predicting local secondary structure — alpha helices, beta strands and coils — from sequence. Early statistical methods (Chou–Fasman, GOR) reached roughly 60–65% three-state accuracy; modern methods using profiles and neural networks exceed 80%.
