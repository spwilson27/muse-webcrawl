Cravats (Croatia)

The Cravats (Croatian: Hrvati; German: Krawatten) were light cavalry units recruited from Croatia that served in the armies of the Habsburg monarchy and other European powers during the seventeenth century, most famously in the Thirty Years' War. They are best remembered today for giving their name to the cravat, the neckcloth that became a forerunner of the modern necktie.

Origins and service

Croatia formed part of the Military Frontier of the Habsburg lands against the Ottoman Empire, and its soldiers developed a tradition of irregular light cavalry warfare — rapid raids, scouting, and skirmishing. During the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), regiments of Croatian horsemen served with the Imperial armies and gained a fearsome reputation across Germany and France, both for their effectiveness and for their plundering. Croatian units were also hired by other powers; a regiment of Croats entered French service under Louis XIII and later Louis XIV, and a Royal-Cravates cavalry regiment existed in the French army into the eighteenth century.

The neckcloth

The Croatian troopers wore distinctive linen or muslin scarves knotted around the neck, part practical protection and part regimental badge. When the Croat regiments appeared in Paris in the 1630s and 1640s, the fashion was adopted at the French court; the French word cravate is a corruption of Croate ("Croat"). From this military neckwear developed the cravat, and ultimately the modern long necktie and bow tie. Croatia commemorates this heritage with events such as the annual celebration of the cravat in Zagreb, and the changing of the guard by a ceremonial Cravat Regiment.

Legacy

The Cravats exemplify the importance of Military Frontier soldiers in early modern European warfare and remain a prominent element of Croatian national symbolism, linking the country's military history to an item of dress worn worldwide.
