Louis Jean-Baptiste Alphonse Bachelier (11 March 1870 – 28 April 1946) was a French mathematician at the turn of the 20th century credited with being the first person to model the stochastic process now called Brownian motion, in his PhD thesis The Theory of Speculation (Théorie de la spéculation), published in 1900.

The Theory of Speculation

Working under Henri Poincaré at the Sorbonne, Bachelier applied the mathematics of random processes to prices on the Paris Bourse. He assumed that price changes follow what is now called a Wiener process, derived the diffusion (heat) equation for the probability distribution of prices, developed an early theory of option pricing, and argued that the mathematical expectation of a speculator is zero — a precursor of the martingale concept and of the efficient-market hypothesis. His work anticipated by five years Einstein's 1905 paper on Brownian motion and by decades the Black–Scholes model.

Career and recognition

Despite the brilliance of his thesis, Bachelier's work was little recognised during his lifetime, partly because of academic setbacks — notably a damaging 1927 evaluation by Paul Lévy, who later retracted his criticism. Bachelier held teaching posts at Besançon, Dijon, Lille and Rennes. His reputation was revived in the 1950s–60s by Leonard Savage and Paul Samuelson, and he is now honoured as the founder of mathematical finance; the Bachelier Finance Society and the Louis Bachelier Prize bear his name.
