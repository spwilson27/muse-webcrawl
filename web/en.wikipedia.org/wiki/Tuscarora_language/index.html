Tuscarora (Skaurure, "hemp gatherers" or "shirt-wearing people") is the Iroquoian language of the Tuscarora people, spoken in western New York and Ontario, and originally in North Carolina before the Tuscarora migration north after 1713. It is the sole surviving member of the southern branch of Northern Iroquoian historically spoken in the southeast.

Status

Tuscarora is critically endangered. By the early 21st century only a few fully fluent elderly speakers remained, and the last native speakers in Canada had died. Revitalization efforts include language classes at the Tuscarora Indian School, adult immersion programmes, dictionaries, and digital learning materials developed with linguists such as Blair Rudes, whose Tuscarora dictionary and grammar remain standard references.

Features

Like other Iroquoian languages, Tuscarora is polysynthetic, building complex words from many morphemes. It has a small consonant inventory, nasal vowels, and distinctive phonological processes including the historical loss of labial consonants shared with its relatives. Its orthography uses a Latin-based alphabet with diacritics marking nasalization and glottal stops.
