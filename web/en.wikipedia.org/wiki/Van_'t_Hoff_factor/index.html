Van 't Hoff factor


Dissociation of a solute into ions increases the particle count

The van 't Hoff factor i (named after Dutch physical chemist Jacobus Henricus van 't Hoff) is a measure of the effect of a solute on colligative properties such as osmotic pressure, relative lowering in vapor pressure, boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression. The van 't Hoff factor is the ratio between the actual concentration of particles produced when the substance is dissolved and the concentration of a substance as calculated from its mass.

For most non-electrolytes dissolved in water, the van 't Hoff factor is essentially 1. For most ionic compounds dissolved in water, the van 't Hoff factor is equal to the number of discrete ions in a formula unit of the substance. This is true for ideal solutions only, as occasionally ion pairing occurs in solution. At a given instant a small percentage of the ions are paired and count as a single particle. Ion pairing occurs to some extent in all electrolyte solutions. This causes the measured van 't Hoff factor to be less than that predicted in an ideal solution.

Relation to degree of dissociation

For a solute that dissociates into n ions with degree of dissociation α, the factor is given by i = 1 + α(n − 1). For association of molecules into aggregates (for example dimerization), the factor is less than 1 and can be expressed in terms of the degree of association.

Deviation from ideal behavior

The deviation for the van 't Hoff factor tends to be greatest where the ions have multiple charges. Ionic compounds often do not fully dissociate in concentrated solutions because of ion pairing, so experimental values fall below the ideal integer values. For example, magnesium sulfate, with divalent ions, shows substantial pairing, so its measured factor is much closer to 1.4 than to the ideal value of 2.

Applications

The factor appears in the standard equations for colligative properties: osmotic pressure Π = nRT/V, boiling-point elevation ΔTb = Kbm, and freezing-point depression ΔTf = Kfm. It is therefore essential for determining molar masses from freezing-point depression and for predicting the freezing points of solutions such as road de-icing brines.
