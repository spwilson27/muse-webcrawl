Originalism

Originalism holds that the Constitution should be interpreted according to its original meaning.

In the context of United States law, originalism is a theory of constitutional interpretation that holds that all statements in the Constitution must be interpreted based on the original understanding "at the time it was adopted." This concept, which views the Constitution as stable in the sense that it can only be changed by the amendment process, is rooted in the notion that the meaning of the text is fixed by the facts at the time it was ratified. Adherents argue that constitutional change by judges is undemocratic and that a fixed meaning constrains judicial discretion.

Varieties

Original intent

The earliest form of originalism sought the subjective intentions of the framers and ratifiers of a provision. Critics objected that collective intent is difficult to identify and that the framers themselves disclaimed that their private intentions should control; this led most originalists to abandon intent in favour of meaning.

Original public meaning

The dominant modern form, original public meaning originalism, asks how a reasonable, informed reader at the time of ratification would have understood the text's public meaning. It is associated with Justice Antonin Scalia, who insisted that the Constitution "says what it says and doesn't say what it doesn't say," and with scholars such as Randy Barnett and Lawrence Solum. Within this framework, a distinction is drawn between interpretation (recovering communicative meaning) and construction (giving legal effect where meaning runs out). "New originalists" accordingly concede a role for judgment in cases of genuine ambiguity.

Criticism

Critics, including proponents of the living Constitution, argue that original meaning is often indeterminate, that the theory privileges the political compromises of a less democratic era (in which women and enslaved people were excluded), and that practices such as the incorporation of the Bill of Rights against the states are hard to justify on originalist grounds. Originalists respond that the theory does not require moral approval of the founding era and that the amendment process remains available for change.

Influence

Originalism rose to prominence in American legal conservatism from the 1970s onward, championed by figures such as Robert Bork, Edwin Meese, and Antonin Scalia, and became the stated methodology of a majority of the Supreme Court by the 2020s, shaping decisions on the Second Amendment (District of Columbia v. Heller, 2008) and other subjects.
