The Somerset Levels are a coastal plain and wetland area in Somerset, England, running south from the Mendips to the Blackdown Hills. Together with the adjacent Moors they form one of the largest lowland wetland landscapes in Britain, drained by the rivers Parrett, Brue, Axe and Tone and criss-crossed by artificial drainage channels known as rhynes. Large parts lie only a few metres above mean sea level and have flooded repeatedly through history.

History

The Levels have been occupied since the Neolithic: the Sweet Track, a wooden causeway dated to about 3807 BCE, is among the oldest engineered roads in Britain. Iron Age lake villages at Glastonbury and Meare stood amid the marshes. In the 9th century King Alfred the Great took refuge at Athelney in the Levels before his victory over the Danes. From the medieval period onward, monasteries and later drainage boards reclaimed the moors by embanking rivers and cutting rhynes, converting marsh into rich summer grazing land.

Ecology

The wetlands support nationally important populations of breeding waders, wintering wildfowl and invertebrates, and form part of the Severn Estuary Ramsar site network. Traditional management includes willow (withy) growing for basket-making and grazing by cattle. In recent decades, conservation bodies have created large wetland reserves, such as those on the Avalon Marshes, where bitterns, great egrets and cranes now breed.

Flooding

Exceptional floods struck the Levels in the winter of 2013–14, inundating villages such as Muchelney and Burrowbridge and prompting a long-term flood action plan involving river dredging, pumping and raised defences. Managing the tension between farming, flood risk and wildlife remains a central policy question for the area.
