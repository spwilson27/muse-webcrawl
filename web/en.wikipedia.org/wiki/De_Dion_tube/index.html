De Dion tube

A De Dion tube suspension: the wheels are joined by a rigid tube while the differential is mounted to the chassis

A De Dion tube is a type of non-independent automobile suspension, one of the simplest of its kind. It is a refinement of the swing axle and live axle designs, used at the driven end of a vehicle to keep the driven wheels upright and parallel to one another while removing the differential and half of the weight of the final drive from the unsprung mass. The system was named after its inventor, Count Albert de Dion, co-founder of the French automobile manufacturer De Dion-Bouton.

Design

In a De Dion layout the two driven wheels are connected by a rigid but lightweight tube (often behind the axle line), which locates the wheels and maintains their camber. Unlike a live axle, the differential and final drive are bolted directly to the vehicle chassis, and drive is transmitted to the wheels by half-shafts with universal joints (or constant-velocity joints) at both ends, which plunge to accommodate suspension movement. Because the heavy differential is sprung weight, ride quality and road holding improve compared with a live axle, while the wheels remain parallel in roll, unlike in a fully independent suspension, which can suffer camber changes.

Because the tube itself does not transmit drive torque, it can be made light. Lateral location of the axle is typically provided by a Watt's linkage, a Panhard rod, or the geometry of the tube's mounting; leaf springs or coil springs support the vehicle, and in the classic De Dion-Bouton implementation a sliding trunnion arrangement accommodated changes in wheelbase length as the tube pivoted.

History and use

De Dion suspensions were used by many manufacturers where a compromise between a live axle's simplicity and independent suspension's ride comfort was desired. Notable applications include various Alfa Romeo models (the Alfetta, GTV and 75/Milano, where the De Dion tube was combined with inboard rear brakes and a Watt's linkage), the Rover P6, the Aston Martin DBS and Lagonda, Opel's Rekord and Commodore models, and some kit and racing cars. The layout has largely been superseded by fully independent multilink suspensions in modern production cars.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages: reduced unsprung mass compared with a live axle; constant wheel camber; no camber change or track change in bump; strong and simple construction.
  • Disadvantages: more expensive and complex than a live axle; the wheels remain linked, so a bump at one wheel affects the other; requires joints and lateral location linkages that a live axle does not.
