Gravity anomaly

A gravity anomaly is the difference between the observed acceleration of gravity at a point on a planet's surface and the value predicted by a theoretical model of the planet's gravity field. Positive and negative anomalies reveal variations in the density of the subsurface and are a basic tool of geophysics and geodesy.

Types and corrections

The free-air anomaly corrects the theoretical gravity only for the latitude and elevation of the observation point, treating the intervening material as if it were air. The Bouguer anomaly additionally corrects for the gravitational attraction of the rock between the point and sea level, and for terrain; it is widely used in mineral and oil exploration, where buried structures of contrasting density produce characteristic anomaly patterns. Isostatic anomalies account for the compensation of surface loads by the crust.

Causes

Anomalies arise from lateral variations in density: mountain roots, ocean trenches, sedimentary basins, ore bodies, and mantle convection all leave signatures. On a planetary scale, the geoid — the equipotential surface approximated by mean sea level — undulates by roughly ±100 m, most notably in the Indian Ocean geoid low.

Measurement

Anomalies are measured with relative and absolute gravimeters on land, at sea, and from aircraft, and globally from satellites such as GRACE and GOCE, which map temporal changes in the field due to groundwater, ice sheets, and ocean currents.
