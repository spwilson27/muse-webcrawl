Uranium enrichment is the process of increasing the proportion of the fissile isotope uranium-235 in uranium. Natural uranium contains only about 0.72 percent U-235, the remainder being mostly uranium-238. Most nuclear reactors and all uranium-based nuclear weapons require a higher concentration, so the isotope separation process of enrichment is a critical step in the nuclear fuel cycle and a central concern of nuclear non-proliferation policy.

Grades

Low-enriched uranium (LEU), below 20 percent U-235, fuels most power reactors at about 3–5 percent. Research reactors and naval propulsion reactors may use higher assays. Highly enriched uranium (HEU) above 20 percent is considered weapons-usable; weapons-grade material is typically enriched above 90 percent. The depleted uranium left over from enrichment ("tails") is used in armour, ballast and some munitions.

Methods

Because the isotopes are chemically identical, separation exploits their slight mass difference. The Manhattan Project industrialised gaseous diffusion and electromagnetic separation (calutrons) at Oak Ridge. Diffusion plants were later built in several countries but are extremely energy-hungry. The gas centrifuge, using uranium hexafluoride gas spun at high speed in cascaded rotors, uses far less energy and is now the dominant technology; it is also compact and difficult to detect, which heightens proliferation concerns. Laser isotope separation has been researched for decades.

Effort and safeguards

Enrichment effort is measured in separative work units (SWU). Most of the work needed to make weapons-grade uranium is expended in reaching the first few percent of enrichment, so a civilian enrichment plant can in principle be reconfigured to produce HEU — the "breakout" scenario that international safeguards administered by the IAEA are designed to detect. Major commercial enrichers include Urenco, Orano, Rosatom's Tenex and CNNC.
