Thomas Hughes

Thomas Hughes
Born: 20 October 1822, Uffington, Berkshire, England
Died: 22 January 1896, Brighton, England
Occupation: Lawyer, judge, author, politician

Thomas Hughes (20 October 1822 – 22 January 1896) was an English lawyer, judge, politician and author. He is most famous for his semi-autobiographical novel Tom Brown's School Days (1857), set at Rugby School under headmaster Thomas Arnold. Hughes was a leading figure in the Christian socialist movement and later became a Liberal Member of Parliament and county court judge.

Early life and education

Hughes was born at Uffington in Berkshire (now Oxfordshire), the son of the squire John Hughes. He attended Rugby School under Dr Thomas Arnold, whose influence shaped his values, and then Oriel College, Oxford. He was a keen sportsman, playing first-class cricket for Oxford University. He was called to the bar in 1848.

Christian socialism and reform

Under the influence of F. D. Maurice, Hughes became a prominent Christian socialist, working alongside Charles Kingsley and J. M. Ludlow. He helped found the Working Men's College in London in 1854 and served as its principal. He supported trade unions, co-operative societies, and legal reform of union rights, and he helped found some of the earliest English trade unions' legal defences. He sat as Liberal MP for Lambeth (1865–68) and Frome (1868–74).

Tom Brown's School Days

Published in 1857 under the pen name "an Old Boy", Tom Brown's School Days drew on Hughes's memories of Rugby and became one of the most famous school stories ever written, defining the genre and popularising the image of the Arnoldian public school. Its sequel, Tom Brown at Oxford (1861), was less successful. In 1880 Hughes helped establish the colony of Rugby, Tennessee, in the United States, intended as an experiment in classless settlement, named for his school.

Later life

Hughes was appointed a Queen's Counsel and in 1882 became a county court judge in Chester, a post he held until his death at Brighton in 1896.
