Eyespot (mimicry)

An eyespot (or ocellus) is an eye-like marking found in many animals, including butterflies, moths, other insects, fish, birds, and reptiles. Eyespots may be a form of mimicry in which a spot on the body of an animal resembles the eye of a different animal, deterring predators; alternatively they may function to deflect attacks away from vital body parts, or in sexual selection and intraspecific signalling.

Functions

Large, suddenly displayed eyespots — as on the wings of the peacock butterfly or the eyed hawk-moth — can startle or intimidate predators such as birds, giving the prey time to escape. Small eyespots near the edges of wings or on the tails of fish are thought to deflect strikes toward expendable tissue. In some fish, eyespots near the tail also confuse predators about which end is the head.

Distribution

Famous examples include the train of the Indian peafowl (used in courtship), the wings of atlas moths (whose wingtips resemble snake heads), the butterflyfish family, and the larvae of some hawk-moths that inflate their thorax to reveal large eye-like spots. The peacock flounder and many cichlids also bear ocelli.
