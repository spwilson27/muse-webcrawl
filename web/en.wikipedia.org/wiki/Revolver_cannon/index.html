Revolver cannon

A revolver cannon is a type of autocannon, commonly used as an aircraft gun, that has a single barrel and uses a cylinder with multiple chambers, like those of a revolver handgun, to speed up the loading, firing and ejecting cycle. Some examples are also power-driven, using electric or hydraulic power, rather than gas pressure from the propellant, to rotate the cylinder.

Mechanism

In a revolver cannon, a rotating cylinder typically contains five chambers. As the cylinder turns, each chamber is loaded, moved into alignment with the barrel and fired, then the spent case is ejected, so that several stages of the cycle happen simultaneously at different chambers. This gives a much higher rate of fire than a conventional single-chamber gun of the same calibre, though generally lower than a multi-barrel rotary cannon such as the M61 Vulcan.

History

The concept descends from weapons such as the nineteenth-century Puckle gun and was first made practical during the Second World War by the German Mauser MG 213, developed for the Luftwaffe. Although the MG 213 never entered service, captured examples strongly influenced post-war designs in Britain, France, Switzerland and the United States.

Examples

Well-known revolver cannon include the British ADEN cannon and its French counterpart the DEFA cannon, both in 30 mm; the Swiss Oerlikon KCA; the German Mauser BK-27, fitted to the Tornado, Alpha Jet, Gripen and Eurofighter Typhoon; and the GIAT 30 used on the Dassault Rafale. In the United States, the Pontiac M39 equipped the F-86, F-100, F-101 and F-5 fighters.

Comparison with rotary cannon

Compared with Gatling-style rotary cannon, a revolver cannon is lighter and more compact for a given calibre, accelerates to full rate of fire almost instantly, and is well suited to air-to-air engagements requiring short, accurate bursts. Rotary cannon achieve still higher maximum rates of fire but are heavier, more complex, and take a fraction of a second to spin up.
