Gin Act 1751

The Gin Act 1751 (the Sale of Spirits Act 1751) was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain intended to reduce the consumption of gin and other distilled spirits, which was widely blamed for crime, poverty, and social disorder during the so-called "Gin Craze" of early 18th-century London.

Background

Cheap gin, imported in large quantities and distilled domestically after restrictions on French brandy, flooded London in the early 1700s. By the 1740s consumption had reached extraordinary levels, especially among the urban poor. Earlier Gin Acts (notably those of 1736 and 1743) had attempted high licence fees and duties but were widely evaded and proved unenforceable. The artist William Hogarth published his prints Beer Street and Gin Lane in 1751 in support of reform.

Provisions

The 1751 Act prohibited gin distillers from selling to unlicensed merchants, restricted retail licences to substantial property holders, raised duties, and barred small retailers and pawnbrokers from the trade. Unlike its predecessors, it worked through the existing licensing system of magistrates rather than relying on informers.

Effect

The Act is generally regarded as successful: combined with rising grain prices and changing social attitudes, it sharply reduced gin consumption and is credited with ending the Gin Craze.
