NASA's X-43A reached Mach 9.6 in 2004, the fastest air-breathing flight on record.

In aerodynamics, a hypersonic speed is one that exceeds five times the speed of sound, often stated as starting at Mach 5 and above. The hypersonic regime is a subset of the supersonic regime, but its flows differ qualitatively: shock layers become thin and close to the body, the air behind the shock wave becomes hot enough to dissociate and ionise, and viscous interaction and entropy-layer effects dominate design.

Regimes of flow

Speeds are conventionally divided into subsonic (below Mach 0.8), transonic (0.8–1.2), supersonic (1.2–5), hypersonic (5–10 in some schemes) and high-hypersonic (10–25), with atmospheric re-entry above that. The Mach 5 boundary is not sharp; individual hypersonic phenomena — thin shock layers, high-temperature chemistry, low-density effects — appear progressively as speed increases.

Vehicles and weapons

Ballistic missile warheads and returning spacecraft routinely travel at hypersonic speeds. Purpose-built vehicles include the rocket-powered North American X-15, which reached Mach 6.7 in 1967 (still the record for a crewed powered aircraft), and the scramjet-powered X-43A and X-51 Waverider demonstrators. Since the 2010s, major powers have developed hypersonic weapons in two classes: boost-glide vehicles that manoeuvre in the upper atmosphere, and hypersonic cruise missiles powered by scramjets; examples include Russia's Avangard and Kinzhal systems, China's DF-ZF, and US programmes such as ARRW and HACM.

Challenges

Surface temperatures can exceed 1,000 °C, requiring refractory materials or ablative heat shields. Ionised air around the vehicle can black out radio communications, as during spacecraft re-entry. Maintaining controlled, stable flight and efficient air-breathing propulsion at these speeds remains at the frontier of aerospace engineering.
