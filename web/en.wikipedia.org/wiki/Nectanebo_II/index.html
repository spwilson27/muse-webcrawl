Nectanebo II

Nectanebo II (Egyptian: Nḫt-Ḥr-ḥbt, "Strong is Horus of Hebit") reigned from 360 to 343 BC as the third and last pharaoh of the Thirtieth Dynasty of Egypt, and the last native Egyptian ruler of ancient Egypt before the Persian reconquest.

Reign

Nectanebo came to the throne after deposing his uncle Teos, whom he had accompanied on a campaign against Persia. With Spartan and Athenian mercenary support — including the aged Spartan king Agesilaus II, whose intervention decided a rival claim to the throne — he secured his rule. His reign saw an impressive programme of temple building across Egypt, including work at Philae, Edfu and Behbeit el-Hagara, and the first phase of the great temple of Isis at Philae dates to his reign.

Fall

In 343 BC the Persian king Artaxerxes III invaded Egypt with a large army, having failed in an earlier attempt a decade before. Nectanebo's forces were defeated at Pelusium and in the Delta; Memphis fell, and Nectanebo fled south to Nubia, disappearing from the historical record. Egypt remained under Persian rule until Alexander the Great's conquest in 332 BC.

Legend

In later legend, elaborated in the Hellenistic Alexander Romance, Nectanebo was transformed into a magician-king who, fleeing to Macedonia, used sorcery to father Alexander the Great upon Olympias — a myth linking Egypt's last native pharaoh to its conqueror. A prophecy of a returning native king, the so-called "Oracle of the Potter", also attached itself to his memory.
