Age of Pisces

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The constellation Pisces and the vernal equinox point (image placeholder)

The Age of Pisces is an astrological age — a period of roughly 2,160 years arising from the precession of the equinoxes — during which the March (vernal) equinox point lies in the constellation Pisces. Astrological ages are a concept of astrology rather than astronomy; depending on the boundaries used, the Piscean age is generally reckoned to have begun around the first century BCE or CE and to be followed, at a date variously calculated between the present era and the 27th century, by the Age of Aquarius.

Astronomical basis

Earth's axis precesses with a period of about 25,772 years, so the equinox point drifts westward through the twelve zodiacal constellations at roughly one constellation per 2,150 years. The equinox has been within the modern boundaries of Pisces since about the first century BCE and will move into Aquarius around the year 2600 by constellation boundaries, though astrologers using equal 30-degree signs compute different dates, some as early as the fifteenth century and others well into the future.

Astrological interpretations

Astrologers associate the Piscean age with themes attributed to the sign Pisces: faith, compassion, sacrifice, and organized religion. The age's beginning roughly coincided with the rise of Christianity, and writers in this tradition note the fish as an early Christian symbol (the ichthys) and the prominence of fishermen among Jesus's disciples. Jung discussed the Piscean symbolism of the fish in his work Aion (1951). Popular culture, notably the musical Hair (1967) with its song "Aquarius", spread the idea that the Piscean age of faith and hierarchy is yielding to an Aquarian age of individualism and technology.

Successive ages

In the astrological sequence the Age of Pisces was preceded by the Age of Aries (associated with the Ram and Mosaic law) and the Age of Taurus before that, and is to be followed by the Age of Aquarius. There is no scientific consensus for any of these periodizations; astronomers treat the boundaries as conventions of constellation definition.
