Bear market

A bear market is a condition in financial markets in which prices fall over a sustained period, encouraging selling and pessimism among investors. The most common working definition is a decline of 20 per cent or more from a recent peak in a broad market index, such as the S&P 500, sustained over at least two months; smaller declines of around 10 per cent are usually termed corrections. The opposite condition is a bull market. An investor who expects prices to fall is described as "bearish."

Terminology

The terms "bear" and "bull" are traditionally explained by the animals' fighting styles — a bear swipes its paws downward, a bull thrusts its horns upward. "Bear" for a pessimistic speculator appears in early eighteenth-century London, in the phrase "to sell the bearskin" — selling securities one did not yet own, in the manner of the proverb about selling the bear's skin before catching the bear.

Causes and characteristics

Bear markets typically accompany economic contraction, rising unemployment, tightening monetary policy, or the collapse of an asset bubble; they may also follow financial crises or shocks such as pandemics. They are often marked by high volatility, sharp short-lived rallies ("bear market rallies") that fail, and a general flight to cash and defensive assets. A prolonged, flat-to-falling bear phase is sometimes called a secular bear market, in contrast to shorter cyclical declines within a longer uptrend.

Examples

Famous bear markets include the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the ensuing decline of nearly 90 per cent in the Dow Jones Industrial Average by 1932; the 1973–74 slump; the bursting of the dot-com bubble in 2000–2002; the fall of roughly 57 per cent in the S&P 500 during the 2007–09 financial crisis; and the rapid COVID-19 crash of early 2020, one of the fastest entries into bear territory on record.
