Memphis (Egyptian: Ineb-hedj, "White Walls"; later Men-nefer, whence the Greek "Memphis") was the ancient capital of Inebu-hedj, the first nome of Lower Egypt, and of the Old Kingdom of Egypt from its foundation until around 2200 BC and later for shorter periods. Its ruins are located near the modern town of Mit Rahina, about 20 km south of Cairo, on the west bank of the Nile.

History

According to tradition recorded by Manetho and Herodotus, Memphis was founded by King Menes (Narmer), who united Upper and Lower Egypt around 3100 BC and built a dam to protect the city from Nile floods. As Egypt's capital during the Old Kingdom, Memphis was the royal residence associated with the pyramid necropolises of Giza, Saqqara, Abusir and Dahshur — its vast necropolis. The city remained a great religious, administrative and commercial centre even after Thebes rose to prominence in the New Kingdom; Ramesses II adorned it with colossal statues, and its great temple of Ptah (Hut-ka-Ptah) was one of Egypt's most important sanctuaries.

Decline

Memphis persisted through the Persian, Ptolemaic and Roman periods as Egypt's second city after Alexandria. It gradually declined after the Arab conquest and the founding of Fustat and later Cairo nearby, whose builders quarried its stone; by the medieval period the city was largely in ruins.

Archaeology

Excavations — notably by Flinders Petrie in the early 20th century — have uncovered the temple precinct of Ptah, palaces, and workshops. Today the site features an open-air museum whose centrepiece is a colossal limestone statue of Ramesses II. In 1979 Memphis and its necropolis were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
