Earl Marshal

Earl Marshal (alternatively marschal or marischal) is a hereditary royal officeholder and chivalric title in the United Kingdom used nowadays in connection with the coronation ceremony and the organisation of state funerals. The current Earl Marshal is Edward Fitzalan-Howard, 18th Duke of Norfolk, who inherited the position in June 2002. There was formerly an Earl Marshal of Ireland and an Earl Marischal of Scotland.


The insignia of the Earl Marshal

The Earl Marshal is one of the Great Officers of State. As such, the post is hereditary. The office was originally held by the Earls of Norfolk; when the title of Duke of Norfolk was created, the earl marshalship went with it. The office of Earl Marshal was for many years hereditary in the Howard family as Dukes of Norfolk. As the King's representative, the Earl Marshal is the chief executive in England of the law of chivalry, and he presides over the Court of Chivalry, which last sat in 1954.

Duties

The Earl Marshal is responsible for organising state funerals and the proclamation of a new monarch in the United Kingdom. He is also a leading officer of arms and oversees the College of Arms, the heraldic authority for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. The Earl Marshal also organises the coronation of a new sovereign and great state ceremonies such as the State Opening of Parliament.

History

The office of marshal dates back to Anglo-Saxon times, when the marshal was responsible for the king's horses. William Marshal, 1st Earl of Pembroke, was one of the most famous holders of the marshalship in the medieval period. The title Earl Marshal came into hereditary use through the Bigod Earls of Norfolk and passed, via the Mowbray family, to the Howards in the late fifteenth century. Despite the Howard family's Roman Catholic faith after the Reformation, they retained the office, with the duties performed by Protestant deputies during periods of religious tension.
