Korean shamanism

A mudang performing a gut (image placeholder)

Korean shamanism (Korean: 무속, musok; the practitioners are called mudang 무당, traditionally mostly women, and male shamans baksu) is the indigenous folk religion of Korea, centred on the worship of gods and ancestors and on rituals conducted by a shaman who mediates between the human and spirit worlds. Its core ceremony is the gut (굿), an elaborate, musical and theatrical rite of song, dance, costume changes, offerings and oracle, performed to heal illness, exorcise misfortune, guide the dead, or bring prosperity and fertility. Shamanism predates the arrival of Buddhism, Confucianism and Christianity in Korea and persists today, especially in the South, despite centuries of official suppression.

History

Shamanic practice goes back to Korea's earliest kingdoms; founding myths such as that of Dangun combine shamanic motifs with state cult, and early kings acted as priest-rulers. Buddhism, arriving in the 4th century, absorbed many shamanic deities as temple guardians, while the Confucian Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) officially disparaged shamanism as superstition and barred mudang from the capital — yet court and commoners alike continued to consult shamans. Under Japanese rule and South Korea's modernisation campaigns of the 1970s ("anti-superstition" drives) rituals were suppressed again; since the 1980s there has been a revival, with gut recognised as intangible cultural heritage and shamans such as the late Kim Keum-hwa honoured as holders of Important Intangible Cultural Properties.

The shaman

Two broad types exist. Charismatic (inspired) shamans, dominant in Seoul and the north's diaspora tradition, are called by the gods through spirit sickness (sinbyŏng): an affliction of illness, dreams and misfortune cured only by accepting the call through an initiation rite (naerimgut), after which the shaman is possessed by and speaks for her gods. Hereditary shamans, found in the south and on Jeju, inherit the office and perform rituals as a learned repertoire without possession. Shamans maintain shrines (sindang) with paintings of their deities and are engaged by clients for private and community rites.

The gut ritual

A gut consists of a sequence of segments (geori), each dedicated to a different god or purpose, in which the shaman changes costume, dances on straw choppers or blades, sings the god's mythology and delivers oracles and entertainment. Famous forms include the jinogwicut (guiding the dead to the afterlife), the chaesugut (for wealth), fishing-village rites such as the byeolsingut for abundant catch and safety at sea, and the rites for the goddess Princess Bari, ancestress of shamans in myth. Performances can last from a few hours to several days and are accompanied by drums and reed instruments.

Beliefs and deities

The musok pantheon is vast and fluid, organised loosely around three levels: a supreme Heavenly god (Haneullim / Sangje), nature and village deities (mountain gods sansin, dragon kings, the Seven Stars), and a host of honoured dead — generals, princes, officials and ancestors — merged with figures borrowed from Buddhism, Taoism and folklore. House gods govern parts of the home, and the shaman works within a cosmos of heavens, earth and underworld.

Modern era

Estimates of the number of mudang in South Korea run into the hundreds of thousands, and shamanic fortune-telling and ritual services form a significant informal economy, consulted even by some Christians and politicians. In North Korea shamanism is officially suppressed as superstition. In the South, scholarly and public interest has reframed musok as Korea's oldest living religious tradition and a reservoir of music, dance and myth.
