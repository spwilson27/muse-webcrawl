The Battle of Mylae was a naval battle fought in 260 BC during the First Punic War. It was the first real naval victory of the Roman Republic and demonstrated the effectiveness of the corvus, the boarding device with which Rome neutralised Carthaginian superiority in seamanship.

Background

Rome, traditionally a land power, built its first major war fleet for the struggle over Sicily, copying a wrecked Carthaginian quinquereme. To offset their crews' inexperience, the Romans fitted their ships with the corvus — a spiked boarding bridge that could be dropped onto an enemy deck, turning a sea fight into the infantry combat at which the legions excelled. The consul Gaius Duilius took command of the fleet after its first admiral was captured at the Lipari Islands.

The battle

Off Mylae (modern Milazzo) the Roman fleet met the Carthaginians under Hannibal Gisco, who advanced contemptuously without proper formation. As the ships closed, the Romans dropped their corvi and boarded; the Carthaginians, unable to manoeuvre, lost some 30–50 ships captured or sunk, including Hannibal's flagship. Hannibal escaped in a skiff and was later crucified by his own men after a subsequent defeat in Sardinia.

Aftermath

Duilius celebrated the first naval triumph in Roman history, and a rostral column decorated with the beaks of captured ships was erected in the Forum in his honour. Mylae proved Rome could contest the sea, though the advantage of the corvus faded as its weight made Roman fleets dangerously unseaworthy in storms.
