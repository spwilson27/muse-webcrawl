Maid

A housemaid in traditional uniform.

A maid, or housemaid or maidservant, is a female domestic worker. In the Victorian era and into the early twentieth century, maids formed the largest category of domestic servants in wealthy and middle-class households in Britain and other industrialised countries, performing cleaning, cooking support, laundry, and personal attendance. The term derives from "maiden", originally indicating a young unmarried woman, and its use for a servant reflects the historical youth and marital status of most women in the role. Today the word survives in terms such as "maid service" (a commercial cleaning company) and in hotel housekeeping roles.

Types of maid

In large households maids occupied a detailed hierarchy. The lady's maid was a senior personal attendant to the mistress of the house, responsible for her wardrobe, hairdressing, and jewellery, and often travelled with her. Housemaids cleaned rooms, made beds, carried water and coal, and answered bells; senior housemaids might supervise others. Parlour maids served at table and received guests, and were expected to be presentable in uniform. Kitchen maids and scullery maids assisted the cook, the scullery maid — usually the youngest and lowest-ranking — washing dishes and vegetables. Nursemaids helped care for children under the nanny. A solitary domestic in a modest household, doing all tasks, was often called a "maid of all work".

History

Domestic service was the largest single occupation for women in nineteenth-century Britain; the 1851 census recorded over a million female domestic servants. Conditions involved very long hours, low wages, one half-day or one day off per week or month, strict discipline under the housekeeper and butler, and living in attics or basements in the employer's house. The work declined sharply after the First World War as women found alternatives in factories, shops, and offices, and as labour-saving appliances spread. In many wealthier households today, cleaning is provided by hourly cleaners or agencies rather than live-in servants, while in parts of Asia, the Middle East, and elsewhere, live-in domestic workers — frequently migrants — remain common, and their legal protection is a continuing labour-rights issue addressed by the ILO's Domestic Workers Convention of 2011.

In popular culture

Maids are stock figures in literature and drama, from the clever serving-women of classical comedy to the staff "below stairs" in Upstairs, Downstairs and Downton Abbey. The stereotyped "French maid" costume derives from theatrical farce. The word also persists metaphorically in compounds such as "bridesmaid" and "milkmaid".
