Boeotia (Greek: Βοιωτία, Voiotía) is a region of central Greece, lying north of the eastern part of the Gulf of Corinth and bordered by Attica to the southeast, Phocis to the west, and Euboea across the Euripus Strait. In antiquity it was a federation of city-states dominated by Thebes; today it forms a regional unit of the Central Greece region, with its capital at Livadeia.

Geography

Boeotia consists largely of two great plains—the Theban plain in the south and the basin of the former Lake Copais in the north, drained in the 19th century—ringed by mountains including Mount Parnassus, Helicon, and Cithaeron. Its fertile lowlands made it rich agricultural country and a natural battleground between northern invaders and the states of southern Greece, notably at Chaeronea (338 BCE), where Philip II of Macedon defeated the allied Greeks.

History

Boeotia was inhabited by Mycenaean times, with a major palace center at Thebes and the fortress of Gla in Lake Copais. In the Archaic and Classical periods its cities formed the Boeotian League, usually under Theban leadership. Boeotia fought against Athens in the Peloponnesian War, and under the generals Epaminondas and Pelopidas Thebes briefly became the leading power in Greece after defeating Sparta at Leuctra (371 BCE) and Mantinea (362 BCE). Alexander the Great destroyed Thebes in 335 BCE. The region produced the poets Hesiod and Pindar and the biographer Plutarch of Chaeronea.

Athenians of the classical era proverbially derided Boeotians as dull ("Boeotian swine"), a reputation modern scholars attribute largely to Athenian prejudice.
