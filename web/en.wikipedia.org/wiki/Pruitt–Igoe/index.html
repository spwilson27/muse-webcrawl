Pruitt–Igoe

The Pruitt–Igoe housing project was a large urban public housing complex in St. Louis, Missouri, United States. Opened in 1954, it consisted of 33 eleven-storey apartment buildings designed by architect Minoru Yamasaki on a 57-acre (23 ha) site. It became notorious for poverty, crime, and racial segregation, and all of its buildings were demolished by 1976 — a failure widely taken to symbolise the end of modernist high-rise public housing.

History

The complex was named for Wendell O. Pruitt, an African-American fighter pilot from St. Louis, and William L. Igoe, a former US congressman. Under the segregation laws of the time, the Pruitt blocks were designated for Black residents and the Igoe blocks for white residents; the project was desegregated after the 1954 Brown v. Board of Education decision, but white residents soon left. Yamasaki's design included modernist features such as skip-stop elevators, communal galleries and ground-floor facilities intended to foster community.

Decline and demolition

Occupancy peaked around 1957 and then fell steadily as St. Louis's population and economy shrank. Federal funding covered construction but not maintenance, so the housing authority depended on rents from impoverished tenants; by the late 1960s buildings were vandalised, services failed, and crime was rampant. Efforts at rehabilitation failed, and demolition began on 16 March 1972 — an implosion televised nationally. The critic Charles Jencks famously claimed that "Modern Architecture died in St. Louis, Missouri on July 15, 1972". The last building came down in 1976.

Legacy

Later scholarship attributes Pruitt–Igoe's failure less to architectural design than to underfunding, racism, declining population, and federal housing policy. The 2011 documentary The Pruitt-Igoe Myth presented residents' perspectives. The site has remained largely vacant, with partial redevelopment proposed.
