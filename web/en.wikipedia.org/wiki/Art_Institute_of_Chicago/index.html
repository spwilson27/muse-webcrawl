Art Institute of Chicago

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Art Institute's Michigan Avenue entrance, the two bronze lions guarding the Beaux-Arts steps]

The Art Institute of Chicago, founded in 1879, is one of the oldest and largest art museums in the United States, housed principally in its Beaux-Arts building (1893) on Michigan Avenue in Grant Park, Chicago, flanked by Edward Kemeys's two bronze lions. Its encyclopaedic collection of about 300,000 works spans five millennia; its Impressionist holdings are among the world's greatest outside Paris.

The museum's icons are American art's pilgrimage sites: Edward Hopper's Nighthawks, Grant Wood's American Gothic, Seurat's A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, Caillebotte's Paris Street; Rainy Day, and the Thorne miniature rooms. The Modern Wing (Renzo Piano, 2009) added a third of a million square feet for modern and contemporary art.

Building the collection

Chicago's merchants built the institute into a rival of the East within two generations — the Ryerson and Buckingham gifts, the acquisition of the Demidoff and Chester D. Tripp-era collections, and the purchase of the Grande Jatte in 1924 (a steel magnate's widow, Helen Birch Bartlett, and the museum's visionary director Daniel Catton Rich in later decades) made it the Midwest's Louvre. Its school, the School of the Art Institute of Chicago, is among America's leading art colleges (Georgia O'Keeffe, Grant Wood and Ivan Albright among its alumni).

Nighthawks and America

Hopper's all-night diner (1942, bought within months of completion for $3,000) became the museum's emblem and American loneliness's portrait; American Gothic (1930), submitted to the museum's own exhibition and purchased for $300, is reproduced beyond counting. Together they explain the institute's place in the national imagination — the museum that holds the country's self-portraits.
