Primer (firearms)

A centre-fire primer seated in the base of a cartridge case.

A primer is the component of a firearm cartridge responsible for igniting the propellant. In modern self-contained ammunition, the primer consists of a small quantity of shock-sensitive explosive compound, sealed in a metal cup, which is struck by the firing pin or hammer and produces a jet of hot gas and particles that ignites the main powder charge.

TypesRimfire, Boxer (centre-fire), Berdan (centre-fire)
Typical compoundsLead styphnate, tetracene, barium nitrate; historically mercury fulminate
FunctionIgnition of propellant

History

Early firearms used external priming — loose powder in a pan ignited by a match, wheel, or flint. The percussion cap (patented in the early 19th century, following Alexander Forsyth's 1807 "scent-bottle" lock using fulminates) placed the priming compound in a small copper cap placed over a nipple. Metallic cartridges of the mid-19th century integrated the primer into the cartridge: rimfire designs (Flobert, 1845) spun the compound into the case rim, while centre-fire systems used a replaceable cap in the case head.

Types

Rimfire

The priming compound is distributed around the hollow rim of the case; the firing pin crushes the rim to fire. Rimfire cases are generally not reloadable. Common in small calibres such as .22 Long Rifle.

Berdan

Patented by Hiram Berdan in 1866; the primer is a simple cup, and the anvil is part of the cartridge case, which has two small flash holes. Dominant in European military ammunition.

Boxer

Patented by Edward Boxer in 1866; the primer contains its own anvil and the case has a single central flash hole, making spent primers easy to remove and the case convenient to reload. Dominant in the United States.

Chemistry

Mixtures historically used mercury fulminate and potassium chlorate, which were corrosive; modern non-corrosive primers typically use lead styphnate as the initiator with tetracene as a sensitiser and barium nitrate as an oxidiser. Lead-free "green" primers substitute compounds such as DDNP (diazodinitrophenol).
