Führer

Führer (German: [ˈfyːʁɐ], spelled Fuehrer when the umlaut is unavailable) is a German word meaning "leader" or "guide". As a political title, it is strongly associated with Adolf Hitler, the dictator of Nazi Germany from 1933 to 1945, who officially styled himself Führer und Reichskanzler ("Leader and Chancellor of the Reich").

Etymology and usage

The word derives from the verb führen, "to lead". In ordinary German it remains a common component of compound words such as Reiseführer (travel guide), Führerschein (driving licence) and Geschäftsführer (managing director), though its use as a standalone political title is avoided because of its Nazi associations.

History

Hitler adopted the title within the Nazi Party in 1921, when he became its chairman and insisted on the Führerprinzip, the "leader principle" demanding absolute obedience. After President Paul von Hindenburg's death on 2 August 1934, Hitler merged the offices of Chancellor and President, taking the title Führer und Reichskanzler, confirmed by a plebiscite later that month. Members of the armed forces swore a personal oath of loyalty to Hitler as Führer.

Führerprinzip

The Führerprinzip held that the Führer's will stood above written law, and that authority flowed downward while responsibility flowed upward. The principle structured the Nazi state and party at every level.

Related titles

Other fascist dictators adopted analogous titles: Benito Mussolini was il Duce in Italy, and Francisco Franco was el Caudillo in Spain. Within the Nazi hierarchy, subordinate leaders bore titles such as Gauleiter and Reichsführer-SS, the latter held by Heinrich Himmler.
