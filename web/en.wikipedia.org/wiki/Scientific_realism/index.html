Scientific realism

Scientific realism

Key figuresHilary Putnam, Richard Boyd, Stathis Psillos, Ian Hacking

Core claimSuccessful scientific theories are (approximately) true, and their theoretical entities exist

Scientific realism is the view that the universe described by science is real regardless of how it may be interpreted. It holds that entities, states and processes described by scientific theories — including unobservable entities such as electrons, genes and curved spacetime — exist in the world, and that our best scientific theories are at least approximately true descriptions of that world, both its observable and unobservable aspects.

Within the philosophy of science, scientific realism is often characterized as a combination of three commitments: a metaphysical thesis that there is a mind-independent world; a semantic thesis that theoretical statements should be taken literally, as having truth values; and an epistemic thesis that mature scientific theories give us justified belief in their approximate truth.

The no-miracles argument

The most influential argument for scientific realism is the no-miracles argument, associated with Hilary Putnam and Richard Boyd. It holds that realism "is the only philosophy that doesn't make the success of science a miracle": the predictive and technological success of mature science would be miraculous if its theories were not approximately true and its central theoretical terms did not refer to real entities. Realism, on this view, best explains why theories enjoy novel predictive success.

Challenges

The main historical challenge is the pessimistic meta-induction, formulated by Larry Laudan: past successful theories (the ether, phlogiston, caloric) were later rejected and their central terms found non-referring, so by induction our current theories are likely false too. A second challenge is the underdetermination of theory by evidence: incompatible theories can fit all available data, so evidence alone cannot decide which is true. Duhem and Quine developed influential versions of this worry.

Variants

  • Entity realism (Ian Hacking, Nancy Cartwright): we are justified in believing in unobservable entities we can manipulate experimentally — "if you can spray them, then they are real" — without committing to the truth of whole theories.
  • Structural realism (John Worrall): what survives theory change is mathematical structure, not ontology; realism should attach to structure rather than objects.
  • Semirealism (Anjan Chakravartty): realism should be directed at specific detection properties rather than theories as wholes.

Rival positions

Scientific realism is opposed by constructive empiricism (Bas van Fraassen), according to which science aims at empirical adequacy rather than truth, and acceptance of a theory involves belief only about its observable consequences; by instrumentalism, which treats theories as useful calculating devices; and by various forms of social constructivism.
