Desmosome

A desmosome (from Greek desmos, "binding", and soma, "body"), also known as a macula adherens, is a cell structure specialised for cell-to-cell adhesion. A type of junctional complex, it is a localised spot-like adhesion where two cells are stoutly attached to one another through intermediate filaments.

Structure

Desmosomes are composed of the desmosome-intermediate filament complex (DIFC), a network of cadherin proteins, linker proteins and keratin intermediate filaments. The transmembrane cadherins — desmogleins and desmocollins — extend into the intercellular space and bind their counterparts on the neighbouring cell. Inside the cell, armadillo-family proteins such as plakoglobin and plakophilins, and the plakin desmoplakin, link the cadherins to the cytoskeleton.

Function and distribution

Desmosomes provide strong mechanical adhesion and are especially abundant in tissues subjected to intense mechanical stress, such as cardiac muscle, the epidermis of the skin, and the lining of the bladder. They act like rivets, keeping cells together while distributing shearing forces through the tissue via the intermediate filament network.

Clinical significance

Defects in desmosomal proteins cause blistering skin diseases and cardiomyopathies. In the autoimmune disease pemphigus vulgaris, autoantibodies against desmoglein cause loss of cell adhesion and blistering. Mutations in desmosomal genes underlie arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy and several inherited skin and hair disorders.
