The Norwegian resistance movement (Motstandsbevegelsen) was the organized opposition to the German occupation of Norway during the Second World War, lasting from the invasion of April 1940 until liberation in May 1945. It combined military resistance, intelligence gathering, civil disobedience, an illegal press and the escape of refugees and downed airmen to safety abroad.

Occupation and early resistance

Germany invaded Norway on 9 April 1940. King Haakon VII and the government refused to surrender, fought alongside Allied forces for two months, and then evacuated to London, where they formed a government-in-exile. Inside Norway, the collaborationist regime of Vidkun Quisling sought to Nazify Norwegian institutions, provoking broad civic resistance from teachers, clergy, athletes and trade unions.

Military and intelligence work

The military organization Milorg grew into a clandestine army of some 40,000 by 1945, preparing to assist liberation and prevent German demolitions. The Norwegian Independent Company 1 — Kompani Linge — trained in Britain and carried out raids and sabotage, most famously the heavy water sabotage at Vemork that crippled the German nuclear program's access to deuterium oxide. The secret intelligence network XU gathered information on German forces for the Allies, while the "Shetland bus" flotilla ferried agents and refugees between Norway and Shetland.

Civil resistance and reprisals

Norwegians circulated hundreds of illegal newspapers, boycotted Nazi organizations, and engaged in symbolic defiance — wearing paperclips or red caps — that the occupiers punished with arrests. Roughly 40,000 Norwegians were imprisoned during the war; more than 700 of Norway's small Jewish community were deported and murdered, though many others escaped to Sweden with resistance help. The village of Telavåg was razed in reprisal in 1942.

Liberation

When Germany capitulated in May 1945, Milorg forces surfaced to take control of key installations and disarm German troops in cooperation with Allied missions, and the government-in-exile returned. Post-war Norway prosecuted Quisling and other collaborators, and resistance veterans played prominent roles in the rebuilt nation.
