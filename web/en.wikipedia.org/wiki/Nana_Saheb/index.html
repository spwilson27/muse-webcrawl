Nana Saheb (born Dhondu Pant, 19 May 1824 – disappeared 1859) was an Indian aristocrat and fighter who led the rebellion at Kanpur (Cawnpore) during the Indian Rebellion of 1857. He was the adopted son of Baji Rao II, the last Maratha Peshwa, and inherited his father's estate at Bithoor after the British East India Company refused to continue the Peshwa's pension to him.

Background

Baji Rao II had been deposed and exiled to Bithoor near Kanpur with an annual pension of £80,000. When he died in 1851, the Company declined to extend the pension and title to his adopted son, Nana Saheb, a decision that embittered him. He sent an envoy, Azimullah Khan, to London to plead his case, without success.

Role in 1857

When the rebellion reached Kanpur in June 1857, Nana Saheb joined the mutineers and besieged the British entrenchment under Major-General Hugh Wheeler. After Wheeler surrendered under terms of safe passage, the evacuees were attacked at the Satti Chaura Ghat on the Ganges on 27 June; most of the men were killed. Surviving women and children were later murdered at the Bibighar on 15 July, events that shocked Britain and hardened its resolve. Nana Saheb's precise responsibility for the massacres remains debated by historians.

Disappearance

After British forces retook Kanpur and defeated his armies under his general Tantia Tope, Nana Saheb fled toward Nepal in 1859 and vanished. Reports of sightings persisted for decades, and his fate was never established. In India he is remembered as one of the principal leaders of the 1857 uprising against British rule.
