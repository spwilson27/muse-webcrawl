The Theosophical Society is an organisation formed in New York City in 1875 to advance the spiritual principles of Theosophy. Its principal founders were Helena Petrovna Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott and William Quan Judge.

Objects and history

The Society's three declared objects are to form a nucleus of universal brotherhood without distinction, to encourage the comparative study of religion, philosophy and science, and to investigate unexplained laws of nature and latent human powers. Blavatsky claimed her teachings derived from hidden Masters or Mahatmas. In 1882 the headquarters moved to Adyar, near Madras (Chennai), India, where Blavatsky and Olcott promoted Hindu and Buddhist revival; Olcott was instrumental in the Buddhist revival in Ceylon (Sri Lanka).

Schisms and influence

After Blavatsky's death in 1891, disputes led to splits, notably the American section under Judge in 1895 and later movements such as Anthroposophy under Rudolf Steiner. Under Annie Besant's presidency the Society promoted the young Jiddu Krishnamurti as a world teacher, a role he renounced in 1929. Theosophy significantly influenced modern Western esotericism, Indian independence politics and Western understanding of Eastern religions.
