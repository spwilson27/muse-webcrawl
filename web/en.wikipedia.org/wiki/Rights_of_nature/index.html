Rights of nature

Rights of nature
ConceptLegal personhood and inherent rights for ecosystems and species
Key textChristopher Stone, "Should Trees Have Standing?" (1972)
First constitutionEcuador, 2008 (rights of Pachamama)
Landmark casesWhanganui River (New Zealand, 2017); Atrato River (Colombia, 2016)

Rights of nature is a legal and jurisprudential theory that describes inherent rights as associated with ecosystems and species, akin to the concept of fundamental human rights. The theory holds that natural entities — rivers, forests, mountains, or nature as a whole — should be recognised as legal subjects with rights of their own, which human guardians may enforce on their behalf, rather than being treated solely as property.

History

The idea entered modern legal scholarship with Christopher D. Stone's 1972 article "Should Trees Have Standing?", published as the United States Supreme Court considered Sierra Club v. Morton. It drew, and draws, on Indigenous worldviews in which nature is a community of subjects rather than a collection of resources, and on earlier thinkers who argued for extending legal rights beyond humans.

Legal developments

In 2008 Ecuador became the first country to recognise rights of nature in its constitution, granting Pachamama (Mother Earth) the right to integral respect, maintenance, and regeneration. Bolivia followed with its 2010 Law of the Rights of Mother Earth. Courts and legislatures elsewhere have recognised particular entities: Colombia's Constitutional Court granted rights to the Atrato River in 2016; New Zealand's Te Awa Tupua Act 2017 made the Whanganui River a legal person represented by Māori and Crown guardians; India's Uttarakhand High Court declared the Ganges and Yamuna legal persons in 2017 (a ruling later stayed); and in Spain the Mar Menor lagoon received legal personhood in 2022. Some municipalities in the United States have passed local rights-of-nature ordinances, with mixed results in court.

Criticism

Critics question how nature's interests are to be identified, who may legitimately speak for it, and whether such rights conflict with human rights to development and property. Supporters respond that legal personhood is a familiar device (used for corporations and ships) and that rights of nature reframe environmental protection from discretionary regulation to enforceable duty.
