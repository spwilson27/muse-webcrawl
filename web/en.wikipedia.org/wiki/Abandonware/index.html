Abandonware

Discontinued software on obsolete media

Abandonware is software — most commonly video games and applications for older computer platforms — that is no longer sold, distributed, or supported by its copyright holder. Although the copyright in such software usually remains in force, abandonware sites distribute copies on the assumption that enforcement is unlikely and that the works would otherwise be lost. The term, a blend of "abandoned" and "software", emerged in the late 1990s, when volunteer-run websites began cataloguing and offering downloads of classic PC games from the 1980s and early 1990s.

History

The abandonware movement grew out of the "oldwarez" scene of early web and Usenet communities. Peter Ringering is credited with coining the term around 1996–97, and sites such as the Home of the Underdogs (founded 1998) became central archives, typically pairing downloads with reviews and technical help for running old software on newer machines using emulators or compatibility layers such as DOSBox. Rights holders' attitudes varied: some publishers formally released their back catalogues as freeware (as id Software and others did with source code releases), while others periodically issued takedown demands — particularly once digital storefronts such as GOG.com demonstrated a commercial market for re-released classics.

Legal status

Abandonware has no special legal status: copyright persists for decades after a work leaves commerce, so unauthorized distribution remains infringement in most jurisdictions. However, preservation efforts have won limited exemptions. The United States Copyright Office has periodically granted exemptions to the anti-circumvention rules of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act allowing libraries, archives, and museums to circumvent copy protection on software no longer reasonably available in order to preserve it, and the Internet Archive maintains a large collection of in-browser playable software under such exemptions. Projects such as GOG's Preservation Program and the Video Game History Foundation advocate for stronger legal protection of game preservation.

Arguments

Supporters argue that abandonware distribution is an act of cultural preservation: much early software survives only on degrading magnetic media, original companies have dissolved or lost the source code, and without copying the works would vanish. Critics note that re-releases, remasters, and licensing deals can revive supposedly abandoned titles at any time, that determining "abandonment" is unreliable, and that unauthorized downloads can undercut legitimate reissue efforts.
