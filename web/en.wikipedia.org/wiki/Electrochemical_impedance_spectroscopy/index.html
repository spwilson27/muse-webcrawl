Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is a technique for studying the electrical properties of electrochemical systems. A small alternating-current signal is applied to an electrochemical cell over a range of frequencies, and the system's impedance response is measured, revealing processes such as charge transfer, diffusion and interfacial capacitance.

Method

In a typical measurement a potentiostat applies a sinusoidal voltage perturbation (often 5–10 mV) around a steady potential, sweeping frequencies from megahertz down to millihertz. Results are displayed as Nyquist plots (imaginary versus real impedance) or Bode plots (magnitude and phase versus frequency). The data are interpreted by fitting them to an equivalent electrical circuit — for example the Randles circuit, which models solution resistance, double-layer capacitance, charge-transfer resistance and a Warburg element representing diffusion.

Applications

EIS is widely used to evaluate corrosion and protective coatings, to characterise batteries and fuel cells (including state-of-health monitoring), in biosensors, and to study electrode kinetics and semiconductor-electrolyte interfaces. Because the perturbation is small, the technique is essentially non-destructive and can monitor a system over time.
