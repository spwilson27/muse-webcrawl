Intransitive verb


Field: Linguistics, grammar
Opposite: Transitive verb

In grammar, an intransitive verb is a verb whose context does not entail a direct object. That is, there is no object that the action is done to. Examples in English include "to sleep", "to die", and "to arrive": in "She slept", the verb takes no object. By contrast, a transitive verb such as "kick" requires an object in its basic use ("She kicked the ball").

Properties

Many verbs can be used both transitively and intransitively (ambitransitive verbs), as in "He reads" versus "He reads a book". Because intransitive verbs have no direct object, they generally cannot be passivised in English, though some languages allow impersonal passives of intransitives. Linguists distinguish two subclasses: unergative verbs, whose subject is agent-like ("run", "talk"), and unaccusative verbs, whose subject is theme-like ("fall", "arrive") — a distinction known as the unaccusative hypothesis.

In other languages

Transitivity is marked differently across languages: case marking, verb morphology, and word order may all signal whether a verb takes an object. In ergative–absolutive languages, the subject of an intransitive verb is treated grammatically like the object of a transitive verb.
