Ketone bodies

Ketone bodies are water-soluble molecules produced by the liver from fatty acids during periods of low food intake (fasting), carbohydrate restriction, prolonged exercise, or untreated type 1 diabetes. The three ketone bodies are acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone (the last formed spontaneously from acetoacetate). They are transported in the blood to extrahepatic tissues, where they are converted back into acetyl-CoA and oxidised for energy.

Production (ketogenesis)

Ketogenesis occurs mainly in the mitochondria of liver cells when fatty acid oxidation generates more acetyl-CoA than can enter the citric acid cycle, typically when oxaloacetate is diverted to glucose production and insulin levels are low. Two acetyl-CoA molecules condense toward acetoacetyl-CoA, then HMG-CoA (pathway shared with cholesterol synthesis), from which acetoacetate is released. Acetoacetate is reduced to β-hydroxybutyrate or decarboxylates to acetone, which gives the breath of people in deep ketosis a characteristic sweet, fruity smell.

Use as fuel

During prolonged starvation, ketone bodies can supply a large share of the brain's energy needs — up to roughly two-thirds — sparing glucose and reducing the breakdown of muscle protein. The heart and skeletal muscle also readily oxidise ketones. The liver itself cannot use ketone bodies because it lacks the enzyme thiophorase (succinyl-CoA:acetoacetate CoA transferase).

Ketosis and ketoacidosis

Elevated ketone levels define ketosis, a normal physiological state during fasting or ketogenic diets. In uncontrolled type 1 diabetes, runaway ketogenesis produces diabetic ketoacidosis, a dangerous metabolic acidosis requiring emergency treatment; alcoholic ketoacidosis is a related condition. SGLT2 inhibitor drugs can rarely trigger euglycemic ketoacidosis.
