Euler method

TypeNumerical method for ODEs

In mathematics and computational science, the Euler method (also called the forward Euler method) is a first-order numerical procedure for solving ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with a given initial value. It is the most basic explicit method for numerical integration of ordinary differential equations and is the simplest Runge–Kutta method. The method is named after Leonhard Euler, who first proposed it in his book Institutiones calculi integralis, published in 1768–1770.

The Euler method approximates the solution curve by stepping along tangent line segments.

The method

Consider the initial value problem y′ = f(t, y), with y(t0) = y0. Choose a step size h > 0 and set tn = t0 + nh. The Euler method advances the approximation by following the tangent line at each step:

yn+1 = yn + h f(tn, yn)

The value of yn is an approximation of the true solution y(tn). Geometrically, the method replaces the unknown solution curve over each short interval with its tangent line at the left-hand endpoint.

Error and stability

The local truncation error of one step is proportional to h2, so the accumulated (global) error is proportional to h: the method is first order. Reducing the step size improves accuracy but increases computation and round-off accumulation. The Euler method is only conditionally stable; for stiff equations the step size needed for stability can be impractically small, which motivates implicit methods such as the backward Euler method, in which the derivative is evaluated at the new point.

Variants

  • Backward Euler: uses yn+1 = yn + h f(tn+1, yn+1), requiring solution of an equation at each step; it is A-stable and suited to stiff problems.
  • Semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler: updates position and momentum in a staggered fashion; widely used in physics simulations and video game engines because of its good energy behaviour.
  • Improved Euler / Heun's method: averages the derivative at both endpoints, giving a second-order method.

Example

For the equation y′ = y with y(0) = 1, whose solution is y = et, the Euler recursion is yn+1 = (1 + h) yn, so after N steps of size h = 1/N the approximation at t = 1 is (1 + 1/N)N, which converges to e as N grows — an illustration both of the method and of its slow, first-order convergence.
