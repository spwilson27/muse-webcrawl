Politics of Germany

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The politics of Germany are those of a federal parliamentary republic organised under the Basic Law of 1949. The head of government is the Chancellor (Bundeskanzler), elected by the Bundestag (the federal parliament, elected by mixed-member proportional representation) and removable only by a constructive vote of no confidence; the head of state is the Federal President, a largely ceremonial office elected by the Federal Convention. Sixteen Länder (states) with their own constitutions, parliaments and governments share power through the Bundesrat, the chamber of state governments that co-legislates on much federal law.

The post-war party system was dominated by the Christian Democrats (CDU/CSU), the Social Democrats (SPD) and the liberal FDP, later joined by the Greens (1980/83), the post-communist Left (Die Linke) and, from 2013, the right-wing populist Alternative for Germany (AfD), whose rise fragmented the old two-and-a-half-party pattern and complicated coalition-building. Grand coalitions (CDU/CSU–SPD) governed for long stretches of the 2000s–2010s; coalition government of two or three parties is the norm at both levels. The Federal Constitutional Court in Karlsruhe, strong federalism and a social-market economy give German politics its characteristic consensual, legalistic style, and its “militant democracy” instruments (party bans, domestic intelligence monitoring of extremists) reflect the Nazi catastrophe that the Basic Law was written to answer.
