Intellectual property

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Intellectual property (IP) is a category of property that includes intangible creations of the human intellect. There are many types of intellectual property, and some countries recognise more than others. The best-known types are patents, copyrights, trademarks and trade secrets. The modern concept originated in late-18th and 19th-century Europe — the Statute of Anne (1710) for copyright and earlier Venetian patent law (1474) are standard landmarks — and is now administered globally through the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO, a UN agency) and the WTO's TRIPS Agreement (1994).

Rights and rationale

IP rights are typically territorial, time-limited (except trademarks and secrets) exclusionary rights: they let owners prevent others from using an invention, work, sign or information, and to license or sell that use. Standard justifications are incentive-based (without exclusivity, creations would be under-produced because copyable at near-zero cost) and, especially in European theory, personality-based (works as extensions of their creators). Critics question empirical incentive claims in some fields, and object to access costs for medicines and educational materials, enclosure of traditional knowledge, and the extension of terms — debates visible in battles over pharmaceutical patents, software and AI training data.
