Fatimid Caliphate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The mosque of al-Hakim in Cairo, with its distinctive minarets]

The Fatimid Caliphate (909–1171) was an Ismaili Shi'a caliphate that rose in North Africa and came to rule an empire from the Atlantic coast of Morocco to Syria and the Hejaz, rivalling the Sunni Abbasids of Baghdad and the Umayyads of Córdoba. Its caliphs claimed descent from Fatima, daughter of Muhammad — hence the name. In 969 the Fatimids conquered Egypt and founded Cairo ('the Victorious') as their capital, building al-Azhar mosque (970), which grew into one of the world's oldest universities, and making Egypt the centre of a wealthy trading empire linking the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean.

Rise and empire

Fatimid missionaries (da'is) won the Kutama Berbers of Ifriqiya, who overthrew the Aghlabids in 909 and proclaimed Ubayd Allah al-Mahdi caliph. After campaigns from their capital Mahdia, the general Jawhar took Egypt from the collapsing Ikhshidids and founded Cairo; under al-Mu'izz and al-Aziz the empire reached its greatest extent, and al-Azhar — founded as an Ismaili mission centre — taught scholars from three continents. The reign of al-Hakim (996–1021), brilliant and erratic, ended in his mysterious disappearance and the Druze schism.

Decline

From the 1060s, famine, military factionalism between Turkish, Sudanese and Armenian troops, and the loss of Syria to Seljuks and Crusaders reduced the caliphs to figureheads of their viziers — increasingly Armenian and military strongmen. The last caliph, al-Adid, died in 1171 days after his vizier, Saladin, abolished the Fatimid caliphate and restored the Abbasid name in the Friday prayer, founding the Ayyubid dynasty.
