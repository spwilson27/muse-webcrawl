St Mark's Basilica

St Mark's Basilica (Italian: Basilica di San Marco) is the cathedral church of the Patriarchate of Venice, Italy, and one of the most famous churches of the city of Venice. It stands at the eastern end of the Piazza San Marco, adjacent to the Doge's Palace. Originally the chapel of the Doge, it has been the city's cathedral since 1807. Nicknamed the Chiesa d'Oro ("Church of Gold") for its opulent design and lavish Byzantine mosaics, it is a symbol of Venetian wealth and power.

History

The first church on the site was begun in 832 to house the reputed relics of Saint Mark the Evangelist, which Venetian merchants had stolen from Alexandria in 828, famously concealing them under a layer of pork to deter Muslim inspectors. That church burned in a rebellion in 976. The present basilica was begun in 1063 under Doge Domenico Contarini and consecrated in 1094; its decoration continued for centuries thereafter.

Architecture

The plan is a Greek cross inscribed in a square, crowned by five great domes — one over the crossing and one over each arm — modelled on the Church of the Holy Apostles in Constantinople. The interior surfaces are covered with more than 8,000 square metres of gold-ground mosaics, mostly executed between the 11th and 13th centuries, depicting stories from the Old and New Testaments.

Above the main portal stand the four bronze Horses of St Mark, looted from Constantinople during the Fourth Crusade in 1204 (the originals are now preserved inside; copies stand outside). The treasury holds the Pala d'Oro, a sumptuous gold altarpiece studded with gems and enamels, behind the high altar beneath which lie the relics of Saint Mark.

Significance

For centuries the basilica was the ceremonial heart of the Venetian Republic, the place where doges were presented to the people. It remains a central monument of Italo-Byzantine architecture and a defining image of Venice.
