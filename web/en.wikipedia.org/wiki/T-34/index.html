The T-34 is a Soviet medium tank introduced in 1940. Its 76.2 mm (3 in) tank gun was more powerful than its contemporaries, while its 60-degree sloped armour provided good protection against anti-tank weapons. The T-34 had a profound effect on the conflict on the Eastern Front in the Second World War, and had a lasting impact on tank design. The tank was praised by multiple German generals, and although its armour and armament were surpassed later in the war, it is often considered the most effective and influential tank design of the conflict.

Development

The T-34 was developed under chief designer Mikhail Koshkin at Kharkov as a replacement for the BT fast tanks and the T-28 medium tank. It combined sloped armour, wide tracks, a powerful diesel engine, and the Christie-derived suspension into a balanced package of firepower, protection and mobility, produced cheaply in enormous numbers after Soviet industry evacuated east of the Urals. Total wartime production exceeded 80,000 vehicles including the upgunned T-34-85 of 1944, making it the second most produced tank of all time.

Combat history

The T-34's appearance in 1941 shocked German forces whose standard anti-tank guns could not penetrate its sloped armour frontally. Early shortcomings — cramped two-man turret, poor optics, few radios, and hastily trained crews — reduced its initial effectiveness, but improvements and overwhelming numbers made it the backbone of the Red Army's armoured forces at Stalingrad, Kursk and the drive to Berlin. Exported widely after the war, T-34s fought in Korea, Vietnam, the Middle East and Africa, and some remained in service or reserve into the 21st century.

Legacy

Historians credit the T-34 with pushing Germany toward heavier designs such as the Panther and Tiger. Despite rough finish and poor crew comfort, its strategic combination of adequate performance and mass-producibility made it an enduring symbol of the Soviet war effort.
