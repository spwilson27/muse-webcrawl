The River Mole is a tributary of the River Thames in southern England. It rises in the Weald near Rusper in West Sussex, flows north through Surrey — passing Horley, Dorking, Leatherhead, Cobham and Esher — and joins the Thames at Molesey, opposite Hampton Court Palace. It is about 80 kilometres long. The Mole is famous for the stretch near Box Hill where it passes through the North Downs chalk in swallow holes, disappearing underground in dry weather — behaviour that gave the river its name, from the burrowing animal.

The swallow holes

Between Dorking and Leatherhead the Mole crosses from impermeable Weald Clay onto chalk and greensand, and in the Burford Bridge–Mickleham reach the riverbed is pierced by swallow holes (swallets) through which much of the flow sinks in summer, sometimes leaving the channel dry. Early descriptions by Daniel Defoe and John Aubrey marvelled at the vanishing river; the novelist George Meredith lived at Box Hill beside it. The Mole Gap, cut through the Downs, is a protected landscape with chalk grassland on Box Hill and Norbury Park, and the river here supports the Mole Gap to Reigate Escarpment conservation area.

History and ecology

The Mole powered numerous mills — including leather, corn and snuff mills — reflected in names such as Fetcham Mill and Leatherhead itself. It is a valued coarse and game fishery with barbel, chub, dace and brown trout, and its banks support kingfishers and, increasingly, otters. Flooding from the Mole affected Horley, Leatherhead and Molesey in the storms of 1968 and again in 2013–14, prompting flood alleviation work. Whether the Roman road Stane Street crossed the river at Mickleham and how the river's name relates to the town of Molesey — "Mul's island" in the Thames — remain points of local antiquarian interest.
