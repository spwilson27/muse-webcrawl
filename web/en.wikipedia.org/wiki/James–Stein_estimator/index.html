James–Stein estimator

The James–Stein estimator is a biased estimator of the mean of a multivariate random vector. It is famous for a surprising result, first discovered by Charles Stein in 1955 and developed with Willard James in 1961, showing that when estimating three or more means simultaneously, the ordinary maximum likelihood estimator (the sample mean vector) is inadmissible under squared-error loss: the James–Stein estimator always has lower total mean squared error.

Statement

Suppose X is normally distributed with unknown mean vector θ of dimension p ≥ 3 and known identity covariance. The James–Stein estimator shrinks each component of X toward zero (or, in variants, toward any chosen point or subspace) by a data-dependent factor of the form 1 − (p − 2)/‖X‖². Because the shrinkage factor is the same for every component, information is "borrowed" across coordinates, reducing total risk even though each coordinate is estimated with bias.

Stein's paradox

The result is often called Stein's paradox: even when the components are entirely unrelated — for example, batting averages of baseball players or unrelated physical measurements — shrinking all estimates toward a common value improves the expected accuracy of the whole set. The estimator is a cornerstone of shrinkage estimation and influenced later developments such as ridge regression, empirical Bayes methods, and Stein's unbiased risk estimate (SURE).

Positive-part estimator

A common refinement, the positive-part James–Stein estimator, replaces the shrinkage factor with its positive part, preventing overshrinkage past the target; it dominates the original estimator. Extensions handle unknown and unequal variances and general loss functions.
