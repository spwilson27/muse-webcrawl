A bonfire of the vanities (Italian: falò delle vanità) is a burning of objects condemned by religious authorities as occasions of sin. The phrase is most associated with the climax of the campaign of the Dominican friar Girolamo Savonarola in Renaissance Florence. The best-known bonfire took place on 7 February 1497, when Savonarola's supporters collected and publicly burned thousands of objects in the Piazza della Signoria.

Background

Savonarola gained influence in Florence after the expulsion of the Medici in 1494. Preaching against corruption, luxury, and secular art and literature, he organized bands of youths who went door to door collecting "vanities" — items associated with moral laxity — for destruction.

The events

Large burnings occurred during the carnival seasons of 1497 and 1498. Objects destroyed included secular books (among them works by Boccaccio and Petrarch), manuscripts, paintings — legend holds that the artist Sandro Botticelli threw some of his own works into the flames — mirrors, cosmetics, fine clothing, musical instruments, playing cards, dice, and chess pieces. Estimates of the value of destroyed goods vary widely, and many stories about specific artworks lost are considered embellishments.

Aftermath

Savonarola's regime collapsed the following year: he was excommunicated by Pope Alexander VI, arrested, tortured, and hanged and burned in the same piazza on 23 May 1498. The phrase "bonfire of the vanities" entered wider usage, and was adopted by Tom Wolfe as the title of his 1987 novel The Bonfire of the Vanities, about ambition and excess in 1980s New York.
