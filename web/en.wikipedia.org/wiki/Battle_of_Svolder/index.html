Battle of Svolder

Date9 September 999 or 1000
LocationIn the western Baltic, near Rügen or near Øresund (disputed)
ResultAllied victory; death of Olaf Tryggvason; partition of Norway
BelligerentsNorway vs. Denmark, Sweden, and the earls of Lade
CommandersOlaf Tryggvason †; Sweyn Forkbeard; Olof of Sweden; Eiríkr Hákonarson

The Battle of Svolder (Norwegian: Slaget ved Svolder) was a major naval battle fought in September 999 or 1000 in the western Baltic Sea between King Olaf Tryggvason of Norway and an alliance of the kings of Denmark and Sweden and the Norwegian earls of Lade. Olaf was ambushed on his return voyage from Wendland, outnumbered, and killed; Norway was then divided among the victors. The battle is one of the most famous of the Viking Age and is described in several Norse sagas, above all Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla.

Background

Olaf Tryggvason had seized the Norwegian throne in 995 and pursued a forceful campaign of Christianization, often by coercion. His power threatened his neighbors: Sweyn Forkbeard of Denmark had old claims to overlordship in Norway and resented Olaf's independence, while Olof Skötkonung of Sweden and the exiled earls of Lade, Eiríkr and Sveinn Hákonarson, each had their own grievances. In the summer of the battle year, Olaf sailed south to Wendland (the Slavic coast of the southern Baltic), in the sagas' telling to seek the hand or the property of a Wendish princess. The allies assembled a great fleet and waited in ambush behind the island called Svolder, whose exact location is unknown.

The battle

According to the saga account, the allied fleet lay hidden and allowed most of Olaf's ships to pass before attacking, so that Olaf's force was reduced to eleven ships, including his great flagship the Long Serpent (Ormr inn langi), the largest longship yet built in the North. Olaf lashed his ships together side by side and fought a defensive action from them as a floating fortress. The allies attacked in turn — first the Swedes, then the Danes, both repulsed with loss — before Eiríkr Hákonarson, attacking the flanks, began clearing Olaf's outer ships one by one. Einarr Þambarskelfir, Olaf's famous bowman, features in a celebrated episode: his bow was broken by a shot, and when Olaf asked what had broken, Einarr replied, "Norway, from your hands, king."

At last the Long Serpent itself was boarded and overwhelmed. Olaf, seeing the battle lost, leaped into the sea in full armor with his shield over his head and was never found. Later legends held that he had survived, escaped to Wendland or to the Holy Land, and lived on as a hermit, but no contemporary evidence supports this.

Aftermath

After the victory, Norway was partitioned. Sweyn Forkbeard took direct control of the districts around the Oslofjord, Olof of Sweden received a coastal strip, and the remainder of the country was ruled by Eiríkr and Sveinn Hákonarson as earls under Danish (and in part Swedish) overlordship. Pagan reaction followed in some areas, and the systematic Christianization of Norway was left to Olaf's successor of a generation later, Saint Olaf Haraldsson.

Sources

The battle is described in Oddr Snorrason's saga of Olaf Tryggvason, in Heimskringla, in Fagrskinna, and in skaldic verse by Hallfreðr Óttarsson and others that anchors the story in near-contemporary tradition. Adam of Bremen also mentions Olaf's death in a sea battle against the Danes. Details such as the speeches, the bow-breaking episode, and precise ship numbers are saga embellishments and should be treated with caution.
