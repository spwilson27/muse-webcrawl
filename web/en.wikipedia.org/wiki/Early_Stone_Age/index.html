Early Stone Age

The Early Stone Age (ESA) is the earliest division of the Stone Age in African archaeology, broadly equivalent to the Lower Paleolithic elsewhere. It spans the period from the first stone tools, about 3.3 million years ago, to roughly 300,000–200,000 years ago, when it gives way to the Middle Stone Age. The concept was developed to describe the African sequence, where the three-age system was adapted by archaeologists such as John Desmond Clark.

Oldowan choppers (left) and a later Acheulean handaxe typify Early Stone Age technology.

Industries

The ESA encompasses two great technological traditions. The Oldowan, first recognised at Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania, consists of simple pebble tools, choppers and flakes, and appears by about 2.6 million years ago (with the still earlier Lomekwian at about 3.3 million years). The Acheulean, emerging around 1.76 million years ago, is characterised by large bifacial tools—handaxes and cleavers—knapped into symmetrical shapes, a tradition that persisted for well over a million years across Africa and into Eurasia.

Hominins

Early Stone Age tools were made by a succession of hominins, including Australopithecus, early Homo such as Homo habilis, and later Homo erectus (or Homo ergaster), whose association with the Acheulean and with the controlled use of fire marks a major step in human evolution. ESA sites document butchery of animals, the occupation of varied environments, and the first dispersals of hominins out of Africa.

Transition

By about 300,000 years ago, prepared-core techniques such as Levallois flaking and the appearance of early Homo sapiens fossils, for example at Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, signal the transition to the Middle Stone Age.
