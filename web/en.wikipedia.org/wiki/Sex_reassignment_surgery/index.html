Sex reassignment surgery

Sex reassignment surgery (SRS), also known as gender-affirming surgery or gender confirmation surgery, is a group of surgical procedures by which a person's sexual characteristics are altered to resemble those socially associated with their identified gender. It forms one part of medical transition for some transgender and transsexual people, alongside hormone therapy, though not all transgender people seek or have access to surgery.

Terminology and standards of care

The procedures are typically performed in accordance with the Standards of Care published by the World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH), which set out assessment and referral criteria. Major medical associations in many countries recognise the procedures as medically necessary treatments for appropriately assessed patients with gender dysphoria.

Procedures

Feminising procedures

  • Vaginoplasty (construction of a vagina, e.g. by penile inversion or intestinal techniques)
  • Orchiectomy and labiaplasty
  • Breast augmentation
  • Facial feminisation surgery and chondrolaryngoplasty (tracheal shave)

Masculinising procedures

  • Mastectomy ("top surgery") and chest reconstruction
  • Hysterectomy and oophorectomy
  • Metoidioplasty and phalloplasty (construction of a penis)
  • Scrotoplasty and testicular implants

History

Some of the earliest documented procedures were carried out in Berlin in the 1920s and early 1930s under Magnus Hirschfeld's Institute for Sexual Science; patients included Dora Richter and Lili Elbe. The field gained wide public attention in 1952 with Christine Jorgensen's transition in Denmark. Harry Benjamin's work in the United States helped establish clinical programmes, and university-based gender clinics spread through the 1960s and 1970s.

Legal and social aspects

Access to, and insurance coverage for, these surgeries varies widely by jurisdiction, and in many legal systems they have historically been linked to legal gender recognition; some jurisdictions formerly required sterilisation, requirements increasingly held to violate human rights.
