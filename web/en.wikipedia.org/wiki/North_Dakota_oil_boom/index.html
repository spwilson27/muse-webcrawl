North Dakota oil boom

[Image: Oil wells in western North Dakota]

The North Dakota oil boom was a period of rapidly expanding oil extraction from the Bakken formation in the Williston Basin of western North Dakota, beginning with the discovery of the Parshall Oil Field in 2006 and the widespread application of hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling. By 2014 North Dakota was producing over one million barrels of oil per day, making it the second-largest oil-producing U.S. state after Texas.

Growth and effects

The boom transformed towns such as Williston, Watford City, Dickinson, and Minot, drawing tens of thousands of workers, straining housing and infrastructure, and producing some of the lowest unemployment rates in the United States. State revenues surged, funding the North Dakota Legacy Fund. Development also brought environmental and social problems, including gas flaring, wastewater spills, increased traffic accidents, and a crime wave documented around the "man camps" housing oil workers.

Slowdown

The collapse of oil prices from 2014 to 2016 cut drilling rig counts by more than half and slowed growth, though production recovered to new highs by 2018–2019 before the COVID-19 demand shock of 2020. The boom also drove pipeline controversies, most prominently the Dakota Access Pipeline protests of 2016–17 near the Standing Rock Sioux Reservation.
