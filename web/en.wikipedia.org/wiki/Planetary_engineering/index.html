Planetary engineering

Terraforming Mars is the best-known hypothetical example of planetary engineering.

Planetary engineering is the application of technology for the purpose of influencing the global properties of a planet. The goal may be to make the planet habitable to life, to remove an existing hazard, or to adjust its climate or rotation. The most studied form of planetary engineering is terraforming, the hypothetical process of deliberately modifying the atmosphere, temperature, surface topography or ecology of a planet or moon to be similar to the environment of Earth to make it habitable by Earth-like life.

Types

Terraforming

Proposals for Mars involve thickening its thin carbon dioxide atmosphere, warming the surface (by orbital mirrors, imported greenhouse gases, or darkening the polar caps), and releasing water locked in polar ice and regolith. Proposals for Venus are generally considered far more difficult, requiring removal or sequestration of most of its dense CO₂ atmosphere and reduction of its surface temperature from about 460 °C.

Geoengineering

On Earth, proposed climate engineering (geoengineering) methods include solar radiation management, such as stratospheric aerosol injection, and carbon dioxide removal. These are sometimes regarded as applied planetary engineering on humanity's home planet, and are controversial because of governance problems, termination shock, and uneven regional effects.

Other forms

Other concepts include planetary protection against impacts (asteroid deflection), adjusting a world's rotation or orbit, building artificial magnetospheres to shield a planet such as Mars from solar wind, and "paraterraforming" — enclosing a world in a permanent pressurised shell ("worldhouse") rather than altering the whole atmosphere.

Challenges and ethics

Planetary engineering projects would operate on timescales of centuries to millennia and require energy resources far beyond present capability. Ethical questions include obligations to any indigenous microbial life, the irreversibility of altering another world, and who would have the authority to authorise such changes. The Outer Space Treaty provides only a partial legal framework.
