Enryaku-ji (延暦寺) is a Tendai monastery located on Mount Hiei in Ōtsu, overlooking Kyoto, Japan. Founded in 788 by the monk Saichō, it is the headquarters of the Tendai school of Japanese Buddhism and one of the most significant monasteries in Japanese history. Many influential monks studied here, including the founders of other Japanese Buddhist schools such as Hōnen (Pure Land), Shinran (Jōdo Shinshū), Eisai (Zen), Dōgen (Sōtō Zen) and Nichiren.

History

Saichō established a small hermitage on Mount Hiei in 788; the temple received the name Enryaku-ji from Emperor Kanmu in 823, shortly after Saichō's death. Positioned northeast of the capital — the direction from which demons were believed to come in Chinese geomancy — the temple served as guardian of Kyoto. Over the centuries Enryaku-ji grew into a vast complex of some 3,000 sub-temples and acquired great political and military power, fielding armies of sōhei (warrior monks) who periodically descended on the capital to press demands on the court.

Destruction and revival

In 1571 the warlord Oda Nobunaga, seeking to break the temple's power, besieged Mount Hiei and burned Enryaku-ji to the ground, massacring thousands of monks and residents — an event remembered as one of the notorious acts of his unification campaigns. The temple was rebuilt under the Tokugawa shogunate, though never regaining its former military influence. Today the complex is divided into three areas — Tōtō (east), Saitō (west) and Yokawa — and is famed for its "marathon monks" (kaihōgyō), who undertake extreme walking pilgrimages of up to 1,000 days. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994 as part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto. See also Mount Hiei and Buddhism in Japan.
