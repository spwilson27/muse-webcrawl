The African elephant is the largest land animal on Earth. Two living species are now recognized: the African savanna elephant (Loxodonta africana) and the smaller African forest elephant (Loxodonta cyclotis), distinguished by genetic, morphological, and behavioural differences. African elephants are distinguished from the Asian elephant by their larger, Africa-shaped ears, concave backs, and the presence of tusks in both sexes.

Description

Bulls may stand 3–4 m at the shoulder and weigh up to 6 tonnes; cows are smaller. The trunk, containing around 40,000 muscles, serves as nose, upper lip, and grasping organ. Tusks are elongated incisors that grow throughout life and are used for digging, stripping bark, and defence. Elephants communicate through low-frequency rumbles, some below the range of human hearing, that travel kilometres through the ground.

Behaviour and ecology

Elephants live in matriarchal family groups led by the oldest female, with males dispersing at adolescence. They are keystone species: by felling trees, digging waterholes, and dispersing seeds in dung, they engineer savanna and forest ecosystems. Elephants display remarkable memory, tool use, mourning behaviour at the bodies of dead kin, and self-recognition.

Conservation

African elephants were listed as Vulnerable until 2021, when the IUCN split the assessment: savanna elephants are Endangered and forest elephants Critically Endangered. Poaching for ivory has historically been the main driver of decline; the 1989 CITES ivory trade ban helped some populations recover, though poaching persists. Human–wildlife conflict, habitat loss, and climate change are ongoing threats. Botswana holds the largest remaining population.

In culture

Elephants are among the most iconic animals in African culture and appear on currency, flags, and national emblems. War elephants from North Africa famously carried Hannibal's army across the Alps in 218 BC.
