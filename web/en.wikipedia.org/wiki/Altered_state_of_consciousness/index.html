Altered state of consciousness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An altered state of consciousness (ASC), also called an altered state of mind or mind alteration, is any condition which is significantly different from a normal waking beta-wave state. The term was popularized by the psychologist Charles Tart in 1969. Altered states may be induced deliberately — through meditation, hypnosis, sensory deprivation, or psychoactive substances — or may arise spontaneously or pathologically, as in dreaming, fever, epilepsy, or near-death experiences.

Induction methods

  • Contemplative practices: meditation, chanting, rhythmic drumming and dancing, prolonged prayer, and trance in shamanic and religious ritual.
  • Physiological means: fasting, sleep deprivation, hyperventilation, sensory deprivation tanks, and extreme exercise.
  • Psychoactive substances: alcohol, cannabis, psychedelics such as LSD and psilocybin, dissociatives, and anesthetics.
  • Technological means: hypnosis, biofeedback, and binaural stimulation.

Characteristics

Depending on the state, subjects may report changes in perception (synesthesia, hallucination), in the sense of time and self (ego dissolution, unity experiences), in emotion (euphoria, terror), and in cognition. Researchers such as Adolf Dittrich developed standardized questionnaires (the APZ and later 5D-ASC scales) measuring dimensions like oceanic boundlessness, dread of ego dissolution, and visionary restructuralization. Neuroimaging studies associate many altered states with changes in the default mode network and in thalamocortical signaling.

Clinical and cultural aspects

Altered states are central to many religious traditions, where trance, possession, and mystical experience may be cultivated and valued. In psychiatry, by contrast, altered states may signal disorder — delirium, psychosis, and dissociative conditions — though the boundary between pathological and culturally sanctioned states is context-dependent. Recent clinical research investigates psychedelic-induced states as adjuncts to psychotherapy for depression, PTSD, and addiction.
