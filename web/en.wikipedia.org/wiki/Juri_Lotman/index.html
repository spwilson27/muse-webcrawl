Juri Mikhailovich Lotman (Russian: Ю́рий Миха́йлович Ло́тман; 28 February 1922 – 28 October 1993) was a Soviet and Estonian literary scholar, semiotician, and historian of Russian culture. He was the founder of the Tartu–Moscow Semiotic School and one of the most influential theorists of semiotics in the twentieth century.

Life

Lotman was born in Petrograd (Saint Petersburg) into a Jewish intellectual family. He served in the Red Army during the Second World War and studied philology at Leningrad State University, where he was influenced by the Russian formalists, including Boris Eichenbaum and Vladimir Propp. Barred from an academic post in Leningrad during the late-Stalinist anti-cosmopolitan campaign, he moved in 1950 to the University of Tartu in Estonia, where he spent the rest of his career, becoming professor of Russian literature.

Work

From 1964 Lotman organised the summer schools on secondary modelling systems at Kääriku and edited the journal Sign Systems Studies (Trudy po znakovym sistemam), the founding journal of semiotics. In The Structure of the Artistic Text (1970) and Analysis of the Poetic Text he developed a structural-semiotic account of literature. With Boris Uspensky and others he formulated the semiotics of culture, treating cultures as collective sign systems.

His later work introduced the concept of the semiosphere — the semiotic space, analogous to Vernadsky's biosphere, outside which meaning-making is impossible — developed in Universe of the Mind (1990) and Culture and Explosion (1992). Lotman also wrote extensively on Pushkin, on the Russian nobility of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and on cinema (Semiotics of Cinema, 1973).

Legacy

Lotman's works have been translated into many languages, and the Lotman Institute and the Juri Lotman Semiotics Repository at the University of Tartu preserve his archive.
