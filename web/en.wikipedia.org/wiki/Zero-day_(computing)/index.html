Zero-day (computing)

Zero-day vulnerability
TypeSoftware vulnerability / exploit
MeaningDefenders have had "zero days" to fix the flaw
RelatedZero-day exploit; vulnerability disclosure

A zero-day (also known as a 0-day) is a software vulnerability that is unknown to, or unaddressed by, those who should be interested in its mitigation — typically the software vendor. Until the vulnerability is mitigated, hackers can exploit it to adversely affect programs, data, or systems; an exploit directed at such a flaw is called a zero-day exploit. The term derives from the fact that the vendor has had zero days to prepare a patch once the flaw becomes known to attackers.

Exploitation and markets

Zero-day exploits are prized by intelligence agencies, cybercriminals, and brokers, and can command prices from thousands to millions of dollars depending on the target platform and reliability. Well-known deployments include the Stuxnet worm (which combined several zero-days) and commercial spyware such as Pegasus, which has used zero-click zero-day exploits against smartphones.

Defence and disclosure

Because no patch exists, defence relies on defence-in-depth: intrusion detection, behavioural monitoring, sandboxing, and rapid patching once a fix ships. Ethics and policy debates surround "zero-day hoarding" by governments — retaining flaws for offensive use versus disclosing them for patching — formalised in processes such as the United States' Vulnerabilities Equities Process. Once disclosed, a flaw is tracked under a CVE identifier and it ceases to be a true zero-day.
