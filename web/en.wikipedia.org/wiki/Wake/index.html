Wake (ceremony)

A wake is a social gathering associated with death, usually held before or after a funeral. Traditionally, a wake involves friends and family keeping watch or vigil over the body of the deceased, often in the home of the deceased or of a close relative. In modern usage, the term commonly refers to the reception or viewing held at a funeral home, where mourners pay their respects, view the body, and offer condolences to the bereaved family.

Etymology

The word derives from the Old English wacu, meaning a watch or vigil. The practice of "waking" the dead — keeping watch through the night so that the body was never left unattended — has roots in both practical concerns (guarding against premature burial and disturbance by animals) and religious beliefs about the soul's departure and protection against evil spirits.

History

Vigil customs are ancient and widespread. In medieval Europe, wakes were held in the home, with prayers, psalms, and candles burning through the night. In Ireland and parts of Scotland, the wake developed into a distinctive social occasion combining mourning with storytelling, music, games, and hospitality; the Irish wake in particular became known for keening — ritual lamentation by women — and for a festive atmosphere intended to celebrate the life of the dead.

Regional traditions

Ireland

The traditional Irish wake was held in the deceased's home over one or more nights. Neighbours brought food and drink, clocks were stopped, and mirrors covered. Professional keeners (mná caointe) led the lament. The custom persisted strongly into the twentieth century and remains part of Irish cultural memory, though wakes are now more often held in funeral homes.

New Orleans jazz funerals

In Louisiana, particularly among African-American communities, funerals with brass bands developed a wake-like procession in which a sombre march to the cemetery gives way to upbeat jazz on the return, celebrating the deceased's release.

Other cultures

Similar vigils occur worldwide: the Jewish shmira (watching over the body), Filipino lamay (a multi-day wake often with games and gambling to raise money for the family), and wakes in Latin American and African diasporic traditions.

Modern practice

Today, a wake is often a less formal viewing held the evening before the funeral service, giving mourners a chance to gather, share memories, view photographs, and sign a register book. Many families now frame the event as a "celebration of life."
