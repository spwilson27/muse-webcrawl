Zaidiyyah (Arabic: الزَّيْدِيَّة), also known as Zaidi Shia or Fivers, is a branch of Shia Islam named after Zayd ibn Ali, a grandson of Husayn ibn Ali who led an unsuccessful revolt against the Umayyad Caliphate in 740 CE. Zaidis are found almost entirely in northern Yemen, where they formed a majority of the population and ruled imamates for much of the past millennium.

Doctrine

Zaidis hold that the imamate may pass to any descendant of Ali through Hasan or Husayn who is learned, pious, and willing to rise up and claim authority; unlike Twelver Shia, they do not recognise a line of infallible, divinely designated imams, nor the occultation of a hidden imam. They accept Abu Bakr and Umar as legitimate caliphs, though they regard Ali as the most excellent of the Companions. In jurisprudence and theology Zaidism is often considered the Shia school closest to Sunni Islam, with affinities to Mu'tazilite rationalism.

History

Zaidi states were established in Tabaristan (northern Iran) in the 9th century and in Yemen in 897, where al-Hadi ila'l-Haqq Yahya founded an imamate that endured, in varying forms, until the North Yemen civil war of 1962. In the modern era the Houthi movement (Ansar Allah) emerged from the Zaidi heartland of Saada in northern Yemen.
