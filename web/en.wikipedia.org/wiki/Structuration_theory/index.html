Structuration theory

Structuration theory is a sociological theory developed by British sociologist Anthony Giddens, most fully presented in his book The Constitution of Society (1984). The theory attempts to reconcile two opposing approaches to the study of society: structuralist accounts, which emphasize the determining power of social structures over individuals, and interpretive or action-oriented accounts, which emphasize the free agency of individuals.

The duality of structure

The core concept is the duality of structure: social structures are simultaneously the medium and the outcome of the practices they organize. Structures — sets of rules and resources — exist only insofar as knowledgeable agents draw upon them in their conduct, and in doing so reproduce (or transform) those very structures. Structure and agency are thus not separate entities (dualism) but two sides of the same process (duality).

Key concepts

  • Rules and resources: structures consist of rules (semantic and moral) and resources (allocative and authoritative) that agents use in interaction.
  • Routinization: the habitual, repetitive character of social life that gives it stability across time and space.
  • Reflexive monitoring: agents continuously monitor their own conduct and its contexts, though much of this knowledge is practical rather than discursive.
  • Time-space distanciation: the stretching of social relations across time and space in modern societies.

Influence and criticism

Structuration theory has been widely influential in sociology, geography, organization studies, and information systems research. Critics such as Margaret Archer have argued that the duality of structure conflates structure and agency, making it impossible to analyze their interplay over time; Archer's morphogenetic approach instead proposes an "analytical dualism." Others have charged that the theory is too abstract to guide empirical research directly.
