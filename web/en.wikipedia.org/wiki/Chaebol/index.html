Chaebol

A chaebol (Korean: 재벌) is a large industrial conglomerate in South Korea, typically run and controlled by an owner or a single family, characterised by diversified affiliates, cross-shareholdings and close ties to the state. The largest chaebols — including Samsung, Hyundai, SK Group, LG and Lotte — account for a dominant share of South Korea's exports and market capitalisation.

History

The chaebol system took shape under President Park Chung Hee in the 1960s and 1970s, when the state directed cheap credit, licences and protection to favoured firms in pursuit of rapid, export-led industrialisation. Family founders used networks of subsidiaries and circular shareholdings to retain control with modest direct stakes, a structure likened to Japan's pre-war zaibatsu. The model delivered spectacular growth — the "Miracle on the Han River" — but left groups highly leveraged.

1997 crisis and reform

The Asian financial crisis of 1997 exposed the conglomerates' debts: Daewoo collapsed in 1999 in one of the largest corporate failures in history, and dozens of groups failed or were restructured. IMF-backed reforms imposed greater transparency, reduced cross-debt guarantees and strengthened minority shareholder rights, though family control survived.

Criticism and governance

Critics argue that chaebols stifle small and medium businesses, practise a culture of impunity, and pass control hereditarily despite tiny equity shares; founding-family members have repeatedly been convicted of corruption and later pardoned, including Samsung's Lee Jae-yong, who was jailed in the 2016–17 presidential bribery scandal and paroled in 2021. Defenders credit the groups with building globally competitive industries in semiconductors, shipbuilding, automobiles and electronics.
