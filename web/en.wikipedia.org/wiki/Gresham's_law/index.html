Gresham's law

In economics, Gresham's law is a monetary principle stating that "bad money drives out good." It is commonly applied to currency markets: where two forms of commodity money with the same legal face value but different intrinsic values circulate, the one with the higher intrinsic value (the "good" money) will be hoarded or exported, while the overvalued "bad" money remains in circulation.

History

The principle was named by Henry Dunning Macleod in 1860 after Sir Thomas Gresham (1519–1579), a financier of the Tudor dynasty who observed the phenomenon in connection with Henry VIII's debasement of the coinage and advised Elizabeth I on restoring it. The idea had been stated earlier — by Nicolaus Copernicus in his Monetae cudendae ratio (1526), by the Islamic scholar al-Maqrizi, and implicitly as early as Aristophanes' The Frogs.

Mechanism and conditions

Gresham's law operates where a legal-tender law fixes an exchange ratio between two monies that differs from their market ratio, and both must be accepted at face value. People then spend the money that is overvalued in law and save the money that is undervalued. A famous modern example followed the United States Coinage Act of 1965, when silver dimes and quarters rapidly disappeared as copper-nickel clad coins replaced them.

Reverse: Thiers' law

Where there is no enforced legal ratio — as in hyperinflations where people refuse the depreciating currency — the opposite tendency, sometimes called Thiers' law, appears: "good money drives out bad," as sellers insist on stable foreign currency or hard assets.
