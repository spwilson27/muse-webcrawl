Geodesic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geometry, a geodesic is a curve representing, in some sense, the shortest path (an arc) between two points on a surface, or more generally in a Riemannian manifold. The term comes from geodesy, the science of measuring the size and shape of the Earth; in the original sense it meant the shortest route between two points on the Earth's surface, namely an arc of a great circle.

Definition

In a Riemannian manifold with metric tensor g, a geodesic is a curve γ(t) whose tangent vector remains parallel as it is transported along itself; equivalently, it satisfies the geodesic equation, a system of second-order differential equations:

d²xμ/dt² + Γμνρ (dxν/dt)(dxρ/dt) = 0

where the Γ are the Christoffel symbols of the Levi-Civita connection. A geodesic segment is length-minimising among nearby curves, though a long geodesic may cease to be the globally shortest path past a conjugate point — as happens for great circles on a sphere beyond the antipode.

Examples

  • In the Euclidean plane, geodesics are straight lines.
  • On a sphere, geodesics are great circles.
  • On a cylinder, geodesics are helices (including circles and vertical lines as special cases).
  • In general relativity, free-falling test particles and light rays follow geodesics of spacetime — timelike and null geodesics respectively — as formulated in the geodesic equation of the Einstein field equations' geometry.

Properties and applications

Through any point of a complete Riemannian manifold and in any tangent direction there passes a unique maximal geodesic; the Hopf–Rinow theorem relates geodesic completeness to metric completeness. Geodesics are central to differential geometry, to navigation (great-circle routes), cartography, and to physics, where the variational principle that geodesics extremise length (or proper time) generalises Fermat's principle and underlies the motion of bodies in curved spacetime.
