The Congo Free State, also known as the Independent State of the Congo, was a large state and absolute monarchy in Central Africa from 1885 to 1908. It was privately owned by King Leopold II of Belgium, who exploited its natural resources — above all ivory and wild rubber — through a regime of forced labour whose brutality and demographic catastrophe eventually provoked an international outcry.

Establishment

Through the explorer Henry Morton Stanley and front organisations, Leopold secured recognition of his personal sovereignty over the Congo Basin at the Berlin Conference of 1884–85, presenting the venture as philanthropic and free-trading. In practice the state was run for the king's profit, largely through concessionary companies.

Atrocities and reform campaign

The rubber quota system enforced by the Force Publique led to hostage-taking, mutilation — severed hands became notorious evidence of cartridges not "wasted" — killings, famine and disease. Population estimates for the Congo fell by millions; historians debate the toll, with figures up to around 10 million excess deaths often cited. Reports by missionaries, by E. D. Morel's Congo Reform Association, by Roger Casement (1904 report) and literary protests including Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness (1899) and Mark Twain's King Leopold's Soliloquy (1905) forced change: in 1908 Belgium annexed the territory as the Belgian Congo.

Legacy

The Congo Free State remains a byword for colonial exploitation and figured centrally in Belgium's 21st-century reckoning with its colonial past. See Leopold II and Democratic Republic of the Congo.
