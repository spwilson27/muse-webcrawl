Combtooth blenny

Combtooth blennies are fishes of the family Blenniidae, the largest family of blennies, comprising roughly 400 species in about 58 genera. They take their common name from the single row of numerous small, comb-like teeth in their jaws, used for scraping algae and detritus from rocks and coral. Combtooth blennies are small, scaleless, bottom-dwelling fishes found in shallow marine waters worldwide, with a few species entering brackish or fresh water.

Characteristics

Combtooth blennies typically have elongated, scaleless bodies, a single long dorsal fin, jugular pelvic fins reduced to small splints, and blunt heads often bearing cirri (small fleshy appendages) above the eyes and nostrils. Most species are less than 15 cm long. Their teeth are closely set and comb-like in front of the jaws, with fang-like canines in some genera — in the sabre-toothed blennies (such as Aspidontus and Meiacanthus) these canines may be associated with venom glands, a rare trait among fishes.

Ecology

Most species inhabit rocky shores, tide pools, and coral reefs in tropical and temperate seas of the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans. They are typically herbivorous or omnivorous, grazing algae, though some are planktivores or specialist feeders — the leaping blennies of the genus Alticus spend much of their time out of water on wave-splashed rocks. Males guard eggs laid in holes, shells, or burrows, fanning and defending them until hatching.

Genera

Notable genera include Blennius, Salarias (the lawnmower blennies popular in aquaria), Ecsenius, Ophioblennius, Istiblennius, and Meiacanthus (the fang blennies, some of which are mimicked by other fishes).
