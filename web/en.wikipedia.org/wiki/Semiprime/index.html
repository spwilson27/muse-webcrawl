Semiprime

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A semiprime is the product of exactly two prime numbers, counted with multiplicity.

In mathematics, a semiprime is a natural number that is the product of exactly two prime numbers, which need not be distinct. Semiprimes include 4 = 2×2, 6 = 2×3, 9 = 3×3, 10, 14, 15, 21, 22, 25, 26, and so on. By definition, a semiprime has exactly four divisors or fewer, and its number of prime factors, counted with multiplicity (the function Ω(n)), equals 2. The concept is a special case of an almost prime — specifically a 2-almost-prime.

Properties

Almost all semiprimes have a recognizable arithmetic profile: a semiprime p·q with p ≤ q has divisors 1, p, q, and pq (collapsing to three when p = q). The counting function for semiprimes below x grows approximately as x log log x / log x. Every semiprime is deficient except 6, which is perfect.

Applications

Semiprimes are central to public-key cryptography. The RSA cryptosystem uses a modulus that is the product of two very large distinct primes; the security of RSA rests on the practical difficulty of factoring such a number, so the RSA Factoring Challenge offered prizes for factoring successively larger semiprimes. Semiprimes were also used in the 1974 Arecibo message, whose 1,679 bits were arranged in a grid using the fact that 1679 = 23 × 73 is semiprime.
