Ruled surface

In geometry, a ruled surface is a surface that can be generated by moving a straight line through space. Equivalently, through every point of the surface there passes a straight line that lies entirely on the surface; such a line is called a ruling of the surface.

Examples

Familiar examples include the plane, the cylinder, and the cone, each of which can be swept out by a family of straight lines. More interesting examples are the hyperboloid of one sheet, the hyperbolic paraboloid, and the Möbius strip. The hyperboloid of one sheet and the hyperbolic paraboloid are in fact doubly ruled: through each point there are two distinct rulings lying on the surface, a property exploited in the construction of cooling towers and other hyperboloid structures, which can be assembled from straight beams.

Parametric description

A ruled surface can be written parametrically as S(u, v) = c(u) + v·d(u), where c(u) traces a curve called the directrix and d(u) gives the direction of the ruling through each point. If the rulings all meet a fixed curve and remain parallel to a fixed plane (the director plane), the surface is called a conoid; the right helicoid is a well-known example.

Developable surfaces

A ruled surface whose tangent plane is constant along each ruling is called developable: it can be unrolled onto a plane without stretching. Cones and cylinders are developable, as is the tangent developable of a space curve. A classical theorem states that every developable surface in Euclidean 3-space is locally a plane, cylinder, cone, or tangent developable.

Applications

Ruled surfaces are important in architecture and engineering because they can be formed from straight elements — beams, planks, or strings — while producing curved, structurally efficient shapes. They also appear in computer-aided geometric design, descriptive geometry, and the theory of line geometry developed in the 19th century by Julius Plücker and others.
