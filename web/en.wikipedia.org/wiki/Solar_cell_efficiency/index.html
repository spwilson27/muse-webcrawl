Solar-cell efficiency

Solar-cell efficiency is the portion of energy in the form of sunlight that a photovoltaic cell can convert into electricity. It is quoted under standard test conditions (an irradiance of 1000 W/m², a spectrum approximating sunlight through 1.5 air masses, and a cell temperature of 25 °C) and is a key determinant of the cost and area of solar power installations.

Limits

The theoretical maximum for a single-junction cell under unconcentrated sunlight is the Shockley–Queisser limit, about 33.7% for an optimal bandgap near 1.34 eV. Losses arise because photons below the bandgap are not absorbed, excess energy of high-energy photons is lost as heat, and some generated carriers recombine. Multi-junction cells stack materials of different bandgaps to capture more of the spectrum and can exceed 47% under concentrated sunlight in the laboratory.

Record efficiencies

Best research-cell efficiencies (as maintained in the NREL efficiency chart) have reached roughly 26–27% for single-junction silicon, about 29% for gallium arsenide, above 33% for perovskite–silicon tandem cells, and around 39–47% for multi-junction concentrator cells. Commercial silicon modules typically achieve 20–23%.

Factors affecting real-world efficiency

Efficiency falls as cells heat up (a negative temperature coefficient of roughly −0.3 to −0.5% per °C for silicon), and real systems also lose energy to soiling, shading, wiring, mismatch, and inverter conversion — captured in the system-level "performance ratio". Research directions include passivated contacts (PERC, TOPCon, heterojunction), perovskites, anti-reflection and light-trapping textures, and tandem architectures.
