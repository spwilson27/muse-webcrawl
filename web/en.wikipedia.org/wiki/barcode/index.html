Barcode

A barcode or bar code is a method of representing data in a visual, machine-readable form. Initially, barcodes represented data by varying the widths, spacings and sizes of parallel lines. These barcodes, now commonly referred to as linear or one-dimensional (1D), can be scanned by special optical scanners called barcode readers. Later, two-dimensional (2D) variants were developed, using rectangles, dots, hexagons and other patterns, called matrix codes or 2D barcodes; the QR code is the best-known example.

History

The idea grew out of work by Norman Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver in the late 1940s, inspired by Morse code; their patent was granted in 1952. Barcodes became commercially successful when they were adopted to automate supermarket checkout systems. A pivotal moment came on 26 June 1974, when a pack of Wrigley's chewing gum became the first product scanned with a Universal Product Code (UPC) barcode at a supermarket in Troy, Ohio.

Types and symbologies

Common 1D symbologies include UPC, EAN (used worldwide for retail), Code 39, and Code 128 (widely used in logistics). Two-dimensional codes include QR codes, Data Matrix, PDF417, and Aztec Code; these store far more data and incorporate error correction, allowing them to be read even when partially damaged. The EAN/UPC family encodes a Global Trade Item Number (GTIN), coordinated globally by the organization GS1.

Uses

Barcodes are used nearly everywhere items need identification and tracking: retail point of sale, inventory management, warehouse logistics, shipping labels, library books, airline boarding passes and baggage, healthcare (patient wristbands and medication), manufacturing, and ticketing. Barcode readers range from laser scanners and camera-based imagers to ordinary smartphone apps.

Benefits and limitations

Compared with manual data entry, barcodes are fast and accurate, and cheap to print. Their limitations include short data capacity for 1D codes, line-of-sight and orientation requirements, and poor readability when labels are damaged — shortcomings partly addressed by 2D codes and by RFID technology, which complements rather than replaces the barcode.
