Monody is a style of solo song in early Baroque music, developed in Florence in the late 16th century, in which a single melodic line is supported by a basso continuo accompaniment. The term derives from the Greek monōidia, "solitary song". Monody sought to restore the expressive power that Florentine humanists attributed to ancient Greek music, in which text and emotion were carried by a single voice rather than obscured by polyphony.

Origins

The monodic style grew out of discussions in the Florentine Camerata, a circle of musicians, poets and scholars including Vincenzo Galilei, Count Giovanni de' Bardi, Jacopo Peri and Giulio Caccini. Vincenzo Galilei's Dialogo della musica antica et della moderna (1581) attacked contrapuntal practice and advocated a single melody following the natural inflections of speech.

Characteristics

Monodies were written for solo voice with accompaniment typically provided by theorbo, harpsichord or lute realizing a figured bass. The vocal line used the stile recitativo — a flexible, speech-like declamation — alongside more lyrical, song-like passages. Ornamentation was often written out, and Caccini's preface to Le nuove musiche (1602) is a key source for Baroque performance practice.

Significance

Monody was foundational to the birth of opera: Peri's Dafne (1598) and Euridice (1600), and Caccini's Euridice (1600), presented whole dramas in the new style. It also established the accompanied solo song as a major genre and marked the transition from Renaissance polyphony to Baroque texture, in which a single melody over a harmonic bass became the norm.
