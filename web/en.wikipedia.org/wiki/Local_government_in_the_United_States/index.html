Local government in the United States

Local government in the United States refers to governmental jurisdictions below the level of the state. Most states and territories have at least two tiers of local government: counties and municipalities. Some states also use townships. Municipal forms of government include the city, town, borough, and village.

Local governments in the US
General-purpose governments: counties (~3,000), municipalities (~19,500), townships (~16,000)
Special-purpose: school districts (~12,500), special districts (~38,000)
Legal basis: state constitutions and statutes (Dillon's Rule / home rule)

Types

Counties

Counties are administrative divisions of states. County government typically includes an elected board (commissioners or supervisors), a sheriff, a clerk, and other row officers. In Louisiana they are called parishes and in Alaska boroughs.

Municipalities

Municipalities are incorporated places — cities, towns, boroughs, and villages — providing services such as police, fire protection, water and sewer, and zoning. Their government structure may be mayor–council, council–manager, commission, or town meeting.

Special-purpose districts

Special districts, including independent school districts, deliver single functions (education, water supply, transit, fire protection) across boundaries that may not coincide with county or municipal lines.

Legal status

Under Dillon's Rule, local governments possess only those powers expressly granted by the state, necessarily implied, or essential to declared purposes. Many states instead grant home rule, giving municipalities broader authority to legislate on local matters, often through a charter adopted by local voters.

Native American government

Federally recognized tribes possess inherent sovereignty and operate governments distinct from state and local jurisdiction, administering services for their members and lands.
