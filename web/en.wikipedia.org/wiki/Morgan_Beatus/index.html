The Morgan Beatus is an illuminated manuscript of the Commentary on the Apocalypse by Beatus of Liébana, produced in northern Spain around 940–945. It is one of the most celebrated of the roughly two dozen surviving illustrated "Beatus" manuscripts, renowned for its vivid, flat bands of saturated colour and dramatic apocalyptic imagery. It takes its name from its present home, the Morgan Library & Museum in New York.

The manuscript

The manuscript contains some 300 leaves with about 89 miniatures, many of them full-page. Its scribe and illuminator, Maius (also spelled Magius), signed his work in a colophon asking readers to remember him; the book was probably made for the monastery of San Miguel de Escalada near León. The Commentary itself, compiled in the 8th century from earlier authors such as Tyconius and Jerome, interprets the Book of Revelation — a text of pressing relevance in 10th-century Spain, where the approaching year 1000 and conflict with al-Andalus sharpened apocalyptic expectation.

Style

The Morgan Beatus exemplifies the Mozarabic style of pre-Romanesque Spanish illumination: figures are rendered in a bold, abstract manner with intense, unnaturalistic colour fields — burning oranges, reds, and yellows banded across the picture plane — and images such as the Woman clothed with the Sun, the Great Red Dragon, the Adoration of the Lamb, and the Whore of Babylon are among the most reproduced in medieval art.
