Aruba is a constituent country of the Kingdom of the Netherlands located in the southern Caribbean Sea, about 29 kilometres (18 mi) north of the coast of Venezuela. Together with Bonaire and Curaçao it forms the ABC islands of the Leeward Antilles. Aruba measures 32 kilometres (20 mi) long and up to 10 kilometres (6 mi) wide, with an area of about 179 square kilometres (69 sq mi). Its capital is Oranjestad.

Unlike much of the Caribbean, Aruba has a dry climate and an arid, cactus-strewn landscape. Its consistently warm weather, with average temperatures around 28 °C (82 °F) and persistent trade winds, has helped tourism become the mainstay of the island's economy, accounting for the majority of employment and government revenue.

History

Aruba was first inhabited by the Caquetío people, an Arawak group who arrived from the South American mainland. European exploration began in 1499, when a Spanish expedition led by Alonso de Ojeda and Amerigo Vespucci reached the island. Spain administered Aruba until 1636, when the Dutch captured it during the Eighty Years' War. Aside from a brief British occupation during the Napoleonic Wars (1805–1816), Aruba remained Dutch.

In the early 20th century, oil refining became important: the Lago Oil and Transport Company opened a major refinery at San Nicolaas in 1924, processing crude from Venezuela. After the refinery's decline, tourism took over as the island's economic engine. On 1 January 1986, Aruba seceded from the Netherlands Antilles and became a separate constituent country within the Kingdom of the Netherlands (status aparte), with its own constitution, parliament (the Staten), and government, while the Netherlands retained responsibility for defence and foreign affairs.

Geography

Aruba lies just north of the Paraguaná Peninsula of Venezuela. The island is largely flat, with its highest point being Mount Jamanota at 188 metres (617 ft). The interior is covered with cacti, aloe, and the wind-shaped divi-divi (watapana) trees that lean toward the southwest because of the trade winds. The leeward (western and southern) coasts have the famous white-sand beaches, while the windward northeastern coast is rugged and rocky. Arikok National Park covers roughly a fifth of the island.

Demographics and language

Aruba's population is ethnically diverse, with a large share of mixed European and Indigenous ancestry, alongside immigrants from Latin America, the Caribbean, and elsewhere. The official languages are Dutch and Papiamento, a Portuguese-based creole with Spanish, Dutch, and African influences; Papiamento is the native language of most Arubans. English and Spanish are also widely spoken.

Economy

Tourism dominates the economy, with over a million visitors a year, mainly from the United States. The island is also known for aloe production—Aruba Aloe, founded in 1890, is one of the oldest aloe companies in the world. Oil refining at San Nicolaas has operated intermittently, and offshore banking and data services contribute smaller shares.
