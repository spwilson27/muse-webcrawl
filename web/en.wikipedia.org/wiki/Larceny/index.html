Larceny is a crime involving the unlawful taking or theft of the personal property of another person or business. It was an offence under the common law of England and became an offence in jurisdictions which incorporated the common law of England into their own law (also statutory law), where in many cases it remains in force. In those jurisdictions that have reformed the traditional crime of larceny, the conduct is consolidated into the statutory offence of theft.

The elements of the common law crime were classically stated as the trespassory taking and carrying away (asportation) of the tangible personal property of another, in the possession of another, with the intent to permanently deprive the owner of the property.

History

Larceny developed under the English common law as a protection of possession rather than title. Because the medieval courts were reluctant to punish mere breaches of trust as crimes, the requirement of a trespassory taking was strictly enforced; this led to the creation of separate offences such as embezzlement and obtaining property by false pretences to cover takings that fell outside the narrow definition. The famous Carrier's Case (1473) stretched the doctrine through the concept of "breaking bulk," establishing that a bailee who converted goods entrusted to him could commit larceny.

In England and Wales, the common law offence was abolished and replaced by the statutory offence of theft under the Theft Act 1968. Many United States jurisdictions, by contrast, retain larceny (often graded as grand or petit larceny by value) or have consolidated it into a general theft statute.

Elements

Trespassory taking. The taking must be wrongful — without the consent of the person in possession, or by fraud amounting to a trespass (larceny by trick).

Carrying away (asportation). Only the slightest movement of the property is required, but the defendant must acquire control over it, however briefly.

Personal property. Traditionally only tangible personal property could be stolen; land, real property, services, and intangible rights were excluded, though statutes have widened the categories.

Of another. The property must belong to someone other than the defendant, though "belonging" is understood in terms of possession: an owner can commit larceny of his own property from a person lawfully in possession of it.

Intent. The taker must intend to permanently deprive the owner of the property.

Grading

At common law, larceny was divided into grand larceny (property worth more than twelve pence — historically a capital offence) and petit larceny. Modern statutes preserve the distinction in many jurisdictions, setting monetary thresholds that determine whether the offence is a felony or a misdemeanor.
