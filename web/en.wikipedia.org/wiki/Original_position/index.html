Original position

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The original position is a thought experiment in political philosophy introduced by the American philosopher John Rawls in his book A Theory of Justice (1971). It asks us to imagine the principles of justice that would be chosen by rational, self-interested representatives of free and equal citizens, deliberating behind a veil of ignorance that hides from them their own social position, talents, wealth, sex, race, and conception of the good life. Because no one knows whether they will be rich or poor, talented or untalented, the parties cannot tailor principles to their own advantage; the principles chosen are therefore fair. Rawls used the device to develop his theory of "justice as fairness".

The setup

The parties in the original position are mutually disinterested and rational, and they possess general knowledge about economics, psychology, and social organisation, but no knowledge of their particular identities or circumstances. They must choose principles to govern the "basic structure" of society — its constitution and main economic and social institutions — once and for all, knowing they must live under the outcome whatever position they turn out to occupy. The choice is unanimous and binding.

Rawls argued that parties so situated would protect themselves against the worst outcomes, adopting a "maximin" strategy: maximising the position of the least advantaged. He predicted they would agree on two principles of justice:

  1. Liberty principle: each person has an equal right to the most extensive scheme of basic liberties compatible with a similar scheme for others (freedom of speech, conscience, political participation, rule of law, and so on).
  2. Second principle: social and economic inequalities are to be arranged so that they are (a) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the difference principle), and (b) attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity.

The first principle takes priority over the second: basic liberties may not be traded away for economic gains.

Background and purpose

The original position modernises the social contract tradition of Locke, Rousseau, and Kant. Unlike historical contract theories, it does not pretend that any actual agreement took place; it is a "device of representation" that models the conditions under which an agreement would be fair. It is closely connected to Rawls's method of reflective equilibrium, in which considered moral judgements and theoretical principles are adjusted to one another until they cohere.

Criticism and influence

The original position has been one of the most debated devices in modern philosophy. Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) attacked the patterned distribution implied by the difference principle. Communitarian critics such as Michael Sandel questioned whether persons can be conceived as detached from their communities and ends in the way the original position supposes. Feminist critics, notably Susan Moller Okin, noted that the original parties were implicitly heads of households, leaving justice within the family unexamined. John Harsanyi argued that rational parties behind a veil of ignorance would instead maximise average utility, leading to utilitarianism rather than Rawls's two principles. Despite — or because of — these disputes, the original position remains a standard tool in political philosophy, ethics, and even welfare economics and game theory.
