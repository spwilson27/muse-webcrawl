Fireplace

A fireplace or hearth is a structure made of brick, stone, or metal designed to contain a fire. Historically used for heating a dwelling, cooking, and heating water for laundry and domestic uses, fireplaces today are often valued as much for their architectural and social role — as a focal point of a room — as for heating.

Structure

A traditional masonry fireplace consists of a firebox where fuel burns, a hearth at its base, a lintel and mantelpiece framing the opening, and a chimney that vents smoke and combustion gases. A damper controls airflow up the flue, and a smoke shelf and smoke chamber help prevent downdrafts. Count Rumford's late-18th-century redesign — a shallow, angled firebox — greatly improved radiant heating and draft.

Types

Fireplaces may burn wood, coal, gas, or electricity. Prefabricated metal fireplaces and inserts are factory-built units installed within a framed chase. Gas fireplaces and electric fireplaces simulate flames without a real wood fire, and ventless designs dispense with a chimney entirely. Outdoor fireplaces and fire pits serve similar social functions.

Efficiency and environment

Open fireplaces are inefficient heaters, losing most heat up the chimney, and wood smoke contributes to air pollution; many jurisdictions regulate wood-burning appliances. Modern inserts and stoves with catalytic or secondary combustion achieve far higher efficiency and lower emissions.
