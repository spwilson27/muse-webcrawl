The Amesbury Archer is an early Bronze Age (Bell Beaker) man whose grave was discovered in May 2002 during excavations at Boscombe Down near Amesbury, Wiltshire, England, only a few kilometres from Stonehenge. Dated to around 2300 BC, the burial is the richest of its period ever found in Britain, containing more grave goods than any other contemporary burial, and has been nicknamed the "King of Stonehenge".

The burial

The man was aged about 35 to 45 at death and was buried in a rectangular grave, crouched on his left side. Around him were placed some 100 objects, including 15 flint arrowheads (which gave him his nickname), three copper knives, two gold hair ornaments — among the earliest gold objects found in England — wrist guards of stone, boars' tusks, shale belt rings, pottery beakers and a cushion stone used in metalworking. The grave goods suggest he was one of the earliest metalworkers in Britain, associated with the introduction of copper and gold metallurgy from continental Europe.

Origins and health

Oxygen isotope analysis of his tooth enamel indicated that he grew up in the Alpine region of central Europe — possibly in what is now Switzerland, Austria or southern Germany — and migrated to Britain as an adult. He suffered from a serious knee injury or bone infection that would have left him lame, and he had an abscess in his jaw. A second, younger man buried in a nearby grave appears, from shared skeletal traits (a rare bone fusion in the feet), to have been a relative raised in southern England.

Significance

The Archer's grave is central to debates about the spread of the Bell Beaker culture and early metallurgy into Britain. His continental origin supports the view that the Beaker phenomenon involved substantial migration. The finds are displayed at the Salisbury Museum, and the burial has been associated in popular accounts with the later phases of Stonehenge's use.
