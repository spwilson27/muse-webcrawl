The Chaldean Catholic Church is an Eastern Catholic particular church (ecclesia sui iuris) in full communion with the Pope of Rome. It retains the East Syriac liturgical traditions of the historic Church of the East, whose heartland lies in northern Mesopotamia (modern northern Iraq, southeast Turkey, and northwest Iran). Its adherents are mostly ethnic Assyrians who speak dialects of Aramaic (Sureth) and Arabic.

History

The church traces its origins to the schism of 1552, when a faction of the Church of the East, dissatisfied with the hereditary succession of the patriarchate, elected Yohannan Sulaqa as a rival patriarch. Sulaqa travelled to Rome, professed Catholic faith, and was consecrated by Pope Julius III as "Patriarch of Mosul in Eastern Syria," founding the Shimun line of patriarchs in communion with Rome. Over the following centuries relations with Rome fluctuated, with lines of patriarchs entering and leaving communion.

A second line, centred at Diyarbakır (Amid) under Joseph I, entered communion in 1681. The two lines were eventually united in 1830, when Pope Pius VIII recognised Yohannan Hormizd as Patriarch of Babylon of the Chaldeans, establishing the modern Chaldean Catholic patriarchate. The name "Chaldean" had been applied to East Syriac Christians in communion with Rome since the fifteenth century.

Modern era

The church suffered severely during the Assyrian genocide (Sayfo) of 1915, losing a large share of its population in the Ottoman Empire. In Iraq, Chaldean Catholics became the largest Christian community. After the 2003 invasion of Iraq, waves of persecution and emigration drastically reduced the Christian population; many Chaldeans emigrated to the United States (especially metropolitan Detroit), Australia, and Europe. In 2014 the Islamic State's capture of Mosul and the Nineveh Plain displaced nearly the entire remaining population of the region.

Structure and liturgy

The church is governed by the Patriarch of Baghdad of the Chaldeans, elected by its synod of bishops. Its liturgy is the East Syriac Rite, celebrating the Divine Liturgy (Qurbana) of Addai and Mari, historically notable for lacking an explicit institution narrative. Liturgical languages include Classical Syriac and Arabic, with Sureth used in some parishes and diaspora communities.
