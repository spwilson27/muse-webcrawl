Id, ego and super-ego

In psychoanalytic theory, the id, ego and super-ego are the three agents of the personality described in Sigmund Freud's structural model of the psyche, set out most fully in The Ego and the Id (1923). The id is the wholly unconscious reservoir of instinctual drives; the ego is the organized, realistic part that mediates between the id, the super-ego, and external reality; and the super-ego is the internalized moral conscience and ego ideal acquired from parents and society.

The id

The id (German: das Es, "the it") is present from birth and contains the inherited drives, principally the libido and the aggressive drive. It operates on the pleasure principle, seeking immediate discharge of tension without regard to reality, logic, or morality. Its contents are unconscious, and it knows no negation, no time, and no contradiction. Freud described the newborn infant as "nothing but an id."

The ego

The ego (German: das Ich, "the I") develops out of the id as the infant interacts with the external world. It operates on the reality principle, postponing gratification until a suitable object or circumstance is available. The ego performs perception, memory, judgement, and motor control, and it defends against anxiety — arising from conflict among id impulses, super-ego prohibitions, and external dangers — by means of defence mechanisms such as repression, denial, and rationalization. Part of the ego is conscious, part preconscious, and part unconscious.

The super-ego

The super-ego (German: das Über-Ich) forms through the resolution of the Oedipus complex, when the child internalizes parental authority and cultural norms. It comprises the conscience, which punishes misdeeds with guilt, and the ego ideal, an image of how one ought to be, which rewards good behaviour with pride. Much of the super-ego is unconscious, and it can be as irrational and harsh as the id, demanding perfection rather than realistic morality.

Development and criticism

Freud's structural model replaced his earlier topographic model of conscious, preconscious, and unconscious systems, though the two schemes overlap rather than coincide. Later psychoanalysts, including ego psychologists such as Anna Freud and Heinz Hartmann, expanded the theory of the ego's autonomous functions. Critics of psychoanalysis have faulted the model for its lack of empirical testability, while historians note its lasting influence on literary criticism, philosophy, and everyday language.
