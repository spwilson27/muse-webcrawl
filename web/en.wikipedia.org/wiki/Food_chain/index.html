Food chain

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A food chain is a linear sequence of organisms through which nutrients and energy pass as one organism eats another: producers (plants and other photosynthesizers) are eaten by herbivores, which are eaten by small carnivores, which may be eaten by larger ones. Each step is a trophic level, and only about 10% of the energy at one level becomes biomass at the next — which is why big fierce animals are rare and food chains seldom exceed four or five links.

Real ecosystems are food webs, not chains: most consumers eat several species and are eaten by several. The food-chain concept, developed by Charles Elton in the 1920s, remains the entry point to trophic ecology and explains phenomena such as biomagnification — pesticides like DDT concentrating to harmful levels in top predators, the story at the heart of Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962).

Detritus and decomposition

Most plant production is never eaten alive; it enters the detrital chain of dead matter worked on by decomposers — fungi and Bacteria — and detritivores such as earthworms, returning nutrients to producers. In the deep ocean, 'marine snow' sustains whole food webs without sunlight.

Keystone species

Some consumers punch above their numbers: remove sea otters and kelp forests collapse under unchecked urchins (a finding of Robert Paine's keystone work). Trophic cascades like this, and the debated wolf-driven changes in Yellowstone, show food webs can reorganise around single links.
