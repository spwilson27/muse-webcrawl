Haldane's rule

Haldane's rule is an observation in evolutionary biology, formulated by J. B. S. Haldane in 1922, concerning hybridisation and speciation: "When in the offspring of two different animal races one sex is absent, rare, or sterile, that sex is the heterozygous [heterogametic] sex." In other words, when hybrids between two species suffer reduced fitness in only one sex, that sex is usually the one with mismatched sex chromosomes — XY (or ZW) — rather than XX (or ZZ).

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. The dominance theory holds that hybrid problems caused by recessive alleles on the X (or Z) chromosome are fully expressed in the heterogametic sex but masked in the homogametic sex. Other proposed contributors include faster evolution of male reproductive traits ("faster male"), and meiotic drive.

Significance

Haldane's rule is one of the most general patterns in speciation genetics, documented in mammals, birds, insects and other groups, and it implies that postzygotic isolation often evolves through problems concentrated in the heterogametic sex.
