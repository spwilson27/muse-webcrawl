Ketone

In organic chemistry, a ketone is an organic compound with the structure R–C(=O)–R′, where R and R′ are carbon-containing groups. The carbon–oxygen double bond is called a carbonyl group; in a ketone the carbonyl carbon is bonded to two other carbons, distinguishing ketones from aldehydes, in which the carbonyl carbon bears at least one hydrogen. The simplest ketone is acetone (propanone), (CH3)2CO.

Ketone
Functional group: Carbonyl, C=O
General formula: R–C(=O)–R′
Simplest example: Acetone (propanone)
Related: Aldehydes, carboxylic acids, esters

Nomenclature

Under IUPAC nomenclature, ketones are named by changing the suffix of the parent alkane from -e to -one, with a locant where needed: for example propanone, butan-2-one, and pentan-3-one. Older common names list the two alkyl groups followed by the word ketone, as in methyl ethyl ketone. Cyclic ketones take names such as cyclohexanone.

Structure and properties

The carbonyl carbon is sp2-hybridised and trigonal planar, with bond angles near 120°. The C=O bond is polar, making ketones polar molecules that are good solvents and hydrogen-bond acceptors, though they cannot donate hydrogen bonds to one another. Small ketones such as acetone are miscible with water; solubility falls as the carbon chain lengthens. Ketones generally have higher boiling points than comparable alkanes but lower than comparable alcohols. Many have characteristic odours: acetone smells sweet and solvent-like, while others contribute fruity or buttery notes.

Synthesis

Ketones are prepared industrially and in the laboratory by oxidation of secondary alcohols, by ozonolysis of alkenes, by Friedel–Crafts acylation of aromatic rings, and by hydration of alkynes. Acetone is produced on a very large scale as a co-product of the cumene process for phenol. Butanone and cyclohexanone — the latter a precursor to nylon — are other major industrial ketones.

Reactions

The carbonyl group dominates ketone reactivity. Nucleophiles add to the carbonyl carbon: hydride reduces ketones to secondary alcohols, Grignard reagents give tertiary alcohols, and amines form imines and enamines. The hydrogens on the α-carbon are weakly acidic, so ketones form enols and enolates, enabling aldol reactions, α-halogenation (including the haloform reaction), and alkylation.

Biology and medicine

Sugars containing a ketone group are called ketoses; fructose is a familiar example. The so-called ketone bodies — acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone — are produced by the liver during fasting and carbohydrate restriction and serve as fuel for the brain and muscles. In uncontrolled diabetes, overproduction leads to diabetic ketoacidosis, a dangerous fall in blood pH. Steroid hormones and many other natural products also contain ketone groups.
