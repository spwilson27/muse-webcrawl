Alternating current

Alternating current (AC) is an electric current that periodically reverses direction and changes its magnitude continuously with time, in contrast to direct current (DC), which flows only in one direction. Alternating current is the form in which electric power is delivered to businesses and residences, and it is the form of electrical energy that consumers typically use when they plug kitchen appliances, televisions, fans and electric lamps into a wall socket.

The usual waveform of alternating current in most electric power circuits is a sine wave, whose positive half-period corresponds with positive direction of the current and vice versa (the full period being the passage of the alternative current wave). In certain applications, different waveforms are used, such as triangular or square waves. Audio and radio signals carried on electrical wires are also examples of alternating current. These types of alternating current carry information such as sound (audio) or images (video), sometimes carried by modulation of an AC carrier signal. These currents typically alternate at much higher frequencies than are used in power transmission.

Transmission, distribution, and domestic power

Electrical energy is distributed as alternating current because AC voltage may be increased or decreased with a transformer. This allows the power to be transmitted through power lines efficiently at high voltage, which reduces the energy lost as heat due to the resistance of the wire, and converted to a lower, safer voltage for use. Use of a higher voltage leads to significantly more efficient transmission of power. The power losses in a conductor are a product of the square of the current and the resistance of the conductor, described by the formula P = I2R.

AC voltage is usually expressed as a root mean square (RMS) value; in North America the standard household supply is 120 V RMS at 60 Hz, while most of the rest of world uses 220–240 V RMS at 50 Hz. The first alternator to produce alternating current was a dynamo electric generator based on Michael Faraday's principles, constructed by the French instrument maker Hippolyte Pixii in 1832.

History

Alternating current technology was developed in Europe in the 1880s, with key early work by Lucien Gaulard, John Dixon Gibbs and the ZBD team in Hungary. In the United States, George Westinghouse acquired the US patents and hired Nikola Tesla, whose polyphase induction motor and related patents made AC practical for power distribution. The late 1880s saw the so-called war of the currents, a commercial rivalry between the AC system promoted by Westinghouse and the DC system of Thomas Edison, which ended with AC becoming the dominant standard for power transmission.

Mathematics of AC voltages

An AC voltage v can be described mathematically as a function of time by the equation v(t) = Vpeak sin(ωt), where Vpeak is the peak voltage and ω is the angular frequency, equal to 2π times the frequency in hertz. The RMS voltage is Vpeak/√2.

Effects at high frequencies

At high frequencies, current in a conductor tends to flow near its surface — the skin effect — which increases the effective resistance of the conductor. Hollow conductors and litz wire are among the techniques used to mitigate this effect in high-frequency applications.
