Languages of Indonesia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

More than 700 living languages are spoken in Indonesia, making it one of the most linguistically diverse countries on Earth — second only to Papua New Guinea in raw counts. The sole official language is Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia), a standardised variety of Malay, which serves as the national lingua franca of administration, education and media across the archipelago, while most Indonesians grow up speaking a regional language at home.

Indonesian

Malay had been a trade lingua franca of the archipelago for centuries. Nationalists proclaimed it the language of unity in the Youth Pledge of 1928, and it was adopted as the national language at independence in 1945. Because it was the mother tongue of only a small minority, it could unite the republic without privileging its largest ethnic group, the Javanese. Today nearly all Indonesians speak Indonesian, and its use as a first language in cities is growing rapidly.

Regional languages

The largest regional languages are Javanese (about 68 million speakers), Sundanese, Madurese, Minangkabau, Buginese, Banjarese, Acehnese, Balinese and Batak languages; most belong to the Austronesian family. In the east, the provinces of Papua host several hundred Papuan (non-Austronesian) languages, the greatest concentration of linguistic diversity in the country. Many regional languages have traditional scripts — Javanese (Hanacaraka), Balinese, Buginese (Lontara) — though the Latin alphabet now predominates.

Endangerment

Hundreds of Indonesian languages have very small speaker populations and are endangered as younger speakers shift to Indonesian. Government language agencies (Badan Bahasa) and universities document and develop regional languages, and local-content curricula teach major regional languages in schools.

Foreign languages

Dutch, the former colonial language, survives mainly among the oldest generation and in legal terminology. English is the principal foreign language taught in schools; Arabic is widely studied for religious purposes, and Mandarin, Japanese and Korean have growing learner communities.
