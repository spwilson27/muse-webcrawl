Kunqu (Chinese: 崑曲; pinyin: Kūnqǔ; Wade–Giles: K'un-ch'ü), also known as Kunju, Kun opera, or Kunqu opera, is one of the oldest extant forms of Chinese opera. It evolved from the local melody of Kunshan in Jiangsu province and dominated Chinese theatre from the 16th to the 18th century. In 2001, UNESCO proclaimed Kunqu a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity (now on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity), the first Chinese art form so honoured.

History

Kunqu singing developed from the Kunshan qiang melodies of the Yuan dynasty. In the Ming dynasty, the musician Wei Liangfu refined the style into the delicate "water-polished" singing (shuimo qiang) characterised by slow tempo, ornamented melody, and precise tonal articulation of lyrics. The playwright Tang Xianzu's The Peony Pavilion (1598) became Kunqu's signature work. Kunqu spread from Suzhou to the imperial court in Beijing and theatres across China, shaping later genres — elements of its music and staging were absorbed into Peking opera. After the 19th century Kunqu declined under competition from regional operas; by the 1950s only a few professional troupes survived.

Performance

Kunqu singing is accompanied principally by the bamboo flute (dizi), with sheng, pipa, and percussion; the repertoire is written in the refined Zhongzhou rhyme with literary diction. Acting emphasises integrated singing, recitation, dance-like movement, and martial or acrobatic display, with role types including sheng (male), dan (female), jing (painted face), and chou (clown). The fluid "water sleeves" and fan work of the dan are emblematic. The modern revival was galvanised by the 1956 production of Fifteen Strings of Cash, said to have "saved" the genre, and later by productions such as the youth edition of The Peony Pavilion produced by Pai Hsien-yung (2004).

Repertoire

Classic plays include Tang Xianzu's The Peony Pavilion, Hong Sheng's The Palace of Eternal Life (on the love of Emperor Xuanzong and Yang Guifei), Kong Shangren's The Peach Blossom Fan, and Li Yu's comedies. Today eight professional troupes in mainland China, plus companies in Taiwan and Hong Kong, maintain the art, performing excerpts and full-length revivals.
