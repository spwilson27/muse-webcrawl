Tuned mass damper

A tuned mass damper (TMD), also known as a harmonic absorber or seismic damper, is a device mounted in structures to reduce mechanical vibrations. It consists of a mass attached to a structure by springs and a damping element, tuned so that the mass oscillates out of phase with the structure's motion, dissipating energy.

Principle

The damper's natural frequency is tuned close to the resonant frequency of the main structure. When the structure sways, the mass moves in opposition, and the relative motion between them is converted to heat by the damper. The concept was described by Hermann Frahm in the early 1900s for reducing ship rolling; Den Hartog later developed the theory.

Applications

Tuned mass dampers are installed in skyscrapers, towers, bridges, and chimneys to control wind-induced sway and seismic vibration. Famous examples include the 660-tonne steel pendulum in Taipei 101, the damper in New York's Citicorp Center, and installations in the Burj Al Arab and Shanghai Tower. Smaller tuned absorbers are used in automotive engineering, power lines (Stockbridge dampers), and machinery.
