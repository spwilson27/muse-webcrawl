Klaus Barbie (25 October 1913 – 25 September 1991) was a German SS officer and Gestapo official who, as head of the Gestapo in Lyon, France, from 1942 to 1944, directed torture, deportation and murder on a large scale, earning the name "the Butcher of Lyon". After the war he worked for American and West German intelligence, emigrated to Bolivia under the name Klaus Altmann, and was finally extradited to France in 1983, where his 1987 trial for crimes against humanity became a landmark in French legal and public memory.

Wartime crimes

Barbie joined the SS in 1935 and served in the SD and Gestapo in the Netherlands and France. Posted to Lyon in November 1942, he established his headquarters at the Hôtel Terminus, where Resistance prisoners were tortured in the cellars. He is held responsible for the arrest in June 1943 of Jean Moulin, the unifying leader of the French Resistance, who died after torture without revealing his secrets; for the April 1944 raid on the Jewish children's home at Izieu, whose 44 children were deported to Auschwitz and murdered; and for the last deportation convoy from Lyon in August 1944, in which some 300 prisoners were sent to the camps. In total, French courts credited his section with thousands of killings and deportations.

Escape, Bolivia and extradition

In 1947 Barbie was recruited by the U.S. Army Counterintelligence Corps as an informant against communist networks; when France sought his extradition, American agents helped him escape along a "ratline" to Bolivia in 1951. Living openly as Klaus Altmann, he became a businessman and adviser to Bolivian military regimes, and was implicated in arms dealing and in the 1980 "cocaine coup". Identified publicly by Nazi-hunters Beate and Serge Klarsfeld in the early 1970s, he was expelled by Bolivia's new civilian government in 1983 and flown to France.

Trial

Tried in Lyon in 1987 — France having twice sentenced him to death in absentia in the 1950s for war crimes, which had expired under limitation rules — Barbie was convicted of crimes against humanity and sentenced to life imprisonment. The case forced French courts and society to define crimes against humanity and to confront the Vichy past; his defence lawyer, Jacques Vergès, pursued a confrontational "rupture" strategy that itself became notorious. Barbie died of cancer in prison in Lyon in 1991.
