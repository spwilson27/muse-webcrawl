Quicklime, formally calcium oxide (CaO), is a widely used chemical compound: a white, caustic, alkaline, crystalline solid at room temperature. It is produced by heating limestone or other calcium carbonate materials in a lime kiln at temperatures above about 825 °C, a process called calcination, which drives off carbon dioxide: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.

Properties and slaking

Quicklime reacts vigorously with water in a strongly exothermic reaction to form calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂, known as slaked lime; a kilogram of CaO can heat several times its weight of water to boiling. This "slaking" underpins most uses of lime. Quicklime is an inexpensive, strongly basic oxide and a powerful desiccant; it must be handled with care because of its causticity and the heat it generates on contact with moisture.

Uses

Lime is one of the oldest industrial chemicals, used since antiquity in mortar and plaster. Today most quicklime goes to steelmaking (as a flux that removes silica, phosphorus, and sulfur impurities), cement and construction (lime mortar, aerated concrete), soil stabilisation, water and sewage treatment (softening, pH control, flocculation), paper production, sugar refining, and environmental applications such as flue-gas desulfurisation. Historically it was also used in limelights — the brilliant stage lighting of the nineteenth century, from which the expression "in the limelight" derives — and in warfare and burial, owing to mistaken beliefs that it rapidly dissolves corpses.
