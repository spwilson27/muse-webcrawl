Deuteronomistic history

The Deuteronomistic history (or Deuteronomist history) is a modern theoretical construct holding that behind the books of Deuteronomy, Joshua, Judges, Samuel and Kings lies a single literary work, composed or edited by an author or school ("the Deuteronomist") who shared the theology and language of Deuteronomy.

Noth's hypothesis

Martin Noth argued in 1943 that a single exilic author composed the history around 562–540 BCE, using older sources, to explain the destruction of Jerusalem as divine punishment for covenant-breaking. The work is unified by a characteristic style, speeches placed at turning points (e.g. Joshua 23, 1 Samuel 12, 1 Kings 8), and a theological pattern: fidelity to Yahweh alone brings blessing; idolatry brings judgement.

Later developments

Frank Moore Cross proposed two editions: a Josianic edition (Dtr1, 7th century BCE) celebrating King Josiah's reforms, later updated by an exilic editor (Dtr2). Rudolf Smend and the "Göttingen school" instead proposed successive layers of editing. Others, notably John Van Seters, have questioned the model.

Theology

Central themes include centralisation of worship in Jerusalem, the election of Israel, the covenant, and the judgement of every king of Israel and Judah against the standard of exclusive loyalty to Yahweh.
