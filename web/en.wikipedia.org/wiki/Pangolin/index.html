Pangolins, sometimes known as scaly anteaters, are mammals of the order Pholidota. They are the only mammals wholly covered in scales — large, protective keratin plates — and they live in Africa and Asia, feeding almost entirely on ants and termites, which they lap up with extremely long, sticky tongues. When threatened, most species roll into a tight, armoured ball.

Description

Pangolins range from the long-tailed pangolin, at about 30 cm of body length, to the giant pangolin, which can exceed 1.2 m. They have poor eyesight but an excellent sense of smell, strong digging claws, and no teeth, grinding insects in a muscular stomach aided by ingested stones and sand. Their tongues can be longer than their bodies and are rooted deep in the chest cavity.

Species and distribution

Eight species are recognised: four in Asia (Indian, Philippine, Sunda and Chinese pangolins) and four in Africa (giant, Temminck's ground, white-bellied and black-bellied pangolins). Habitats range from tropical forest to savanna; some species climb trees, others dig burrows.

Conservation

All pangolin species are threatened, several critically endangered. They are heavily poached for their scales — used in traditional medicine despite being made of ordinary keratin — and for their meat, making pangolins among the most trafficked mammals in the world. International commercial trade has been banned under CITES Appendix I since 2017, and World Pangolin Day is marked each February to raise awareness.
