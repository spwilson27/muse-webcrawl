Conformity is the act of matching attitudes, beliefs, and behaviours to group norms, politics or being like-minded. Social psychologists define it as a change in behaviour or belief toward a group standard as a result of real or imagined group pressure. Conformity is a fundamental mechanism of social cohesion — it enables coordination and transmits culture — but it can also suppress dissent and perpetuate error.

Types

Herbert Kelman distinguished three processes: compliance, publicly agreeing while privately disagreeing, usually to gain approval or avoid punishment; identification, conforming to maintain a valued relationship or membership; and internalisation, genuinely adopting the group's position as one's own. Morton Deutsch and Harold Gerard (1955) separated the underlying motives into normative influence (fitting in) and informational influence (believing the group knows better).

Classic experiments

Muzafer Sherif's 1935 autokinetic experiments showed that individuals judging an ambiguous moving light converged on a shared group norm that persisted when they later judged alone. Solomon Asch's 1951 line-judgment experiments used an unambiguous task: confederates unanimously gave a wrong answer, and about 75% of participants conformed at least once, with conformity on roughly a third of critical trials — though a single dissenting ally dramatically reduced it. Stanley Milgram's obedience studies, though about authority rather than peers, are often discussed alongside conformity research.

Factors affecting conformity

Conformity increases with group size up to about three to five members, with unanimity, group cohesion and status, public (versus written/private) responses, task difficulty and ambiguity, and low self-esteem. It tends to be higher in collectivist cultures and, in many classic studies, modestly higher among women in public settings — differences now attributed largely to social roles and familiarity with the task. A consistent minority can itself sway the majority (minority influence, Moscovici), especially through conversion rather than compliance.

Responses to social influence

Besides conformity, individuals may show independence, anticonformity (deliberately opposing the group), or reactance when they feel their freedom is threatened. Groups enforce conformity through approval, ridicule, ostracism and norms; deviance can trigger corrective "schismogenesis" or expulsion. Conformity pressures contribute to phenomena such as groupthink, pluralistic ignorance and the spiral of silence.
