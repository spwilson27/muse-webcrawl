Neural fold

The neural fold is a structure that arises during the embryonic development of birds, mammals, and other organisms, and is a precursor of the neural tube. During primary neurulation, the margins of the neural plate thicken and rise to form the paired neural folds, which converge at the dorsal midline and fuse to form the neural tube, the precursor of the brain and spinal cord.

The neural folds rise on either side of the neural groove and will fuse to form the neural tube.

Development

Neural folds form at the border between the surface ectoderm and the neuroectoderm. Cells at this border, the neural crest, are induced by signaling interactions involving BMP, Wnt, and FGF pathways. As the folds elevate and approach one another, the neural groove deepens between them. Fusion begins in the cervical region of the embryo and proceeds rostrally and caudally in a "zipper-like" fashion in many species, though humans have multiple closure initiation sites.

The convergence of the folds is driven by convergent extension and by hinge points: a median hinge point above the notochord and paired dorsolateral hinge points, whose formation depends in part on signals such as Sonic hedgehog from the notochord and BMPs from the surface ectoderm.

Clinical significance

Failure of the neural folds to fuse properly results in neural tube defects: failure of cranial closure produces anencephaly, while failure of spinal closure produces spina bifida. Folate supplementation before conception and in early pregnancy greatly reduces the incidence of these defects.
