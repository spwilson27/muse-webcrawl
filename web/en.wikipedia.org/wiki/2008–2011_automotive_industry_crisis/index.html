2008–2011 automotive industry crisis

Period2008 – 2011
CauseGlobal financial crisis, credit crunch, high fuel prices, falling consumer demand
Key eventsBankruptcies of General Motors and Chrysler (2009); government bailouts in the US, Europe and elsewhere
AffectedAutomakers, suppliers, dealers, manufacturing regions worldwide

The 2008–2011 automotive industry crisis was a severe downturn in the global automobile industry that formed part of the wider Great Recession following the financial crisis of 2007–2008. The crisis began in the United States in 2008, when a combination of record fuel prices, a collapse in consumer credit and falling demand for large vehicles struck an industry already weakened by high legacy costs and overcapacity. Sales in the US fell from over 16 million vehicles a year to about 9 million at the trough in 2009, the lowest level in decades.

The crisis spread rapidly to Europe, Japan and emerging markets, forcing plant closures, mass layoffs and emergency government intervention on several continents. In the United States, General Motors and Chrysler both filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in 2009 and were restructured with the support of tens of billions of dollars in federal loans administered through the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP). Ford avoided bankruptcy but mortgaged most of its assets to secure private credit before the downturn.

Causes

Several factors converged. Oil prices spiked to nearly $150 per barrel in mid-2008, collapsing demand for the trucks and SUVs on which Detroit's profits depended. The credit freeze made it impossible for many consumers to obtain auto loans and for dealers to finance inventory. Automakers in the US also carried heavy pension and healthcare obligations for retirees. In Europe, chronic overcapacity of roughly 20–30 percent left manufacturers exposed when demand fell by about a quarter in some markets.

Government responses

Governments responded with loan guarantees, direct bailouts and "cash for clunkers" scrappage schemes designed to pull forward demand for newer, more efficient vehicles. Germany's scrappage premium, France's support for Renault and PSA, and similar programmes in China — which overtook the United States as the world's largest car market in 2009 — helped stabilise sales. Sweden's Saab and other smaller makers were sold or wound down.

Aftermath

By 2010–2011, restructured manufacturers had returned to profitability, US sales recovered toward 13 million vehicles annually, and the US Treasury began selling down its stakes. The crisis accelerated industry consolidation, the shift toward smaller and more fuel-efficient vehicles, and investment in electrification. It remains one of the defining industrial episodes of the Great Recession.
