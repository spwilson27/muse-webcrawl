Endurance art

Endurance art is a form of performance art involving some form of hardship, pain, solitude, confinement, repeated action, or prolonged duration. It often draws on traditions of religious asceticism, ritual ordeal, and the limits of the human body, placing the artist's physical or psychological endurance at the centre of the work.

History

Endurance-based performance emerged in the 1960s and 1970s alongside body art, as artists began using their own bodies as the medium of the work. Chris Burden subjected himself to physical danger in pieces such as Shoot (1971), in which he was shot in the arm, and Trans-Fixed (1974). Marina Abramović's early works, including the Rhythm series (1973–74), tested the limits of her body through exhaustion, pain and loss of consciousness.

In Rhythm 0 (1974), Abramović stood passively for six hours while the audience was invited to use any of 72 objects on her body, an endurance of psychological exposure as much as physical strain. Tehching Hsieh's One Year Performances (1978–1986) extended the durational scale dramatically: he spent one year locked in a cage, one year punching a time clock every hour, one year outdoors, one year tied to another artist, and one year not making art at all.

Notable works and artists

  • Marina Abramović — Rhythm 5, Rhythm 0, and The Artist Is Present (2010), in which she sat silently opposite museum visitors for a total of 736 hours.
  • Tehching Hsieh — the five One Year Performances and the subsequent thirteen-year plan (1986–1999).
  • Chris Burden — Shoot, Five Day Locker Piece (1971), in which he was confined in a small locker for five days.
  • Matthew Barney and others have incorporated exhausting physical tasks, though endurance is most closely associated with the artists above.
  • Zhang Huan — performances involving prolonged stillness under duress, such as sitting covered in fish oil and flies in a public latrine.

Themes

Endurance art frequently engages questions of time, commitment, vulnerability, and the relationship between artist and audience. Critics have linked it to religious practices such as fasting, flagellation and pilgrimage, and to philosophical questions about willpower and the boundaries of the self. Because works often unfold over hours, days or years, the audience may witness only a fragment, with documentation — photographs, contracts, time-clock records — forming a major part of the work's afterlife.
