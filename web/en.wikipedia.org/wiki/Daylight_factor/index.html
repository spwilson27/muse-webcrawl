Daylight factor

The daylight factor is a measure used in architecture and building engineering to quantify the amount of natural daylight available inside a room. It is defined as the ratio of the illuminance at a point indoors to the simultaneous illuminance outdoors on a horizontal plane under an unobstructed overcast sky, expressed as a percentage:

DF = (Eindoor / Eoutdoor) × 100%

Daylight factor falls with distance from the window wall.

Components

The daylight factor at a point is conventionally split into three components: the sky component (light received directly from the sky), the externally reflected component (light bounced off external surfaces such as neighbouring buildings) and the internally reflected component (light reflected around the room's surfaces). Because it is defined relative to the standard CIE overcast sky, the daylight factor is independent of the absolute brightness outside, making it a stable design metric.

Use in design

Typical guidance suggests average daylight factors of around 2% or more for a room to appear daylit, with values below 1% regarded as poorly lit and values above 5% as strongly daylit, though possibly with glare and overheating risks. Building regulations and green-building rating systems in many countries specify minimum daylight factors for habitable rooms. Designers estimate them with scale models under artificial skies, the BRE split-flux formulae, or computer simulation.
