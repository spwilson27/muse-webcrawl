Dongzhi Festival

Observed byChina, Taiwan, and Chinese diaspora communities
TypeTraditional Chinese festival
DateWinter solstice, usually December 21 or 22
ObservancesFamily gatherings, ancestor worship, eating tangyuan or dumplings

The Dongzhi Festival or Winter Solstice Festival (Chinese: 冬至; pinyin: Dōngzhì, "the arrival of winter") is one of the most important Chinese festivals, celebrated on the winter solstice, when daylight is shortest and the days begin to lengthen again. It falls on or around December 21–22 in the Gregorian calendar. Traditionally, Dongzhi was a time for families to gather, worship ancestors, and mark the turning point of the year, and it is sometimes said that "Dongzhi is as important as the New Year."

Tangyuan, sweet glutinous rice balls, are eaten in southern China during Dongzhi; their round shape symbolises reunion and completeness.

Traditions

In southern China and in overseas Chinese communities, families eat tangyuan — glutinous rice balls, often coloured pink and white and served in sweet soup — which symbolise reunion and prosperity, and mark that one is a year older after the festival. In northern China, the custom is to eat jiaozi (dumplings), a tradition legendarily linked to the Han-dynasty physician Zhang Zhongjing, who is said to have fed dumplings to frostbitten villagers to prevent ear injuries from the cold. Worship of ancestors and of Heaven was historically central: during the Han dynasty Dongzhi was an official holiday, and later emperors performed the winter solstice sacrifice to Heaven at the Temple of Heaven in Beijing.

Philosophical background

The festival reflects the philosophy of balance and harmony in the cosmos: after the longest night, yang energy begins to grow again, a turning point traditionally welcomed with feasting and positive energy.
