Norris–La Guardia Act

The Norris–La Guardia Act (also known as the Anti-Injunction Bill) is a 1932 United States federal law that banned yellow-dog contracts, barred federal courts from issuing injunctions against non-violent labour disputes, and created a positive right of workers to organise and bargain collectively. It was sponsored by Senator George W. Norris of Nebraska and Representative Fiorello H. La Guardia of New York and signed by President Herbert Hoover on 23 March 1932.

Provisions

  • Yellow-dog contracts — agreements in which workers promised not to join a union as a condition of employment were made unenforceable in federal court.
  • Limits on injunctions — federal courts were prohibited from enjoining strikes, picketing, boycotts and other peaceful union activities arising out of labour disputes, except in narrowly defined circumstances with strict procedural protections, including trial by jury for contempt charges.
  • Policy declaration — the Act declared it the public policy of the United States that workers should have "full freedom of association" and be free from employer interference in designating their bargaining representatives.

Significance

The Act is regarded as the first step in the modern era of American labour law. By removing the federal judiciary's most potent anti-union weapon — the labour injunction, used extensively since the Pullman Strike of 1894 — it paved the way for the National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act) of 1935, which established active federal protection for collective bargaining.
