Reichenbach's tense theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reichenbach's tense theory is an account of tense in natural language proposed by the philosopher Hans Reichenbach in his 1947 work Elements of Symbolic Logic. It analyses every tense in terms of three temporal points: the point of speech (S), the point of reference (R), and the point of the event (E).

The three points

Tenses differ in the ordering relations they assert among S, R and E. In the simple past ("John left"), the event and reference points coincide before the speech point (E = R — S). In the present perfect ("John has left"), the event precedes a reference point that coincides with the moment of speech (E — R = S). In the past perfect ("John had left"), the event precedes a reference point that itself precedes the speech point (E — R — S). With three points and the possible relations of precedence and simultaneity, the system generates thirteen configurations, which correspond closely to the tense systems of English and other European languages.

Influence

Reichenbach's analysis remains one of the most influential frameworks in the semantics of tense and aspect. Later work in formal semantics has refined the notion of a reference point into intervals and adopted the S–R–E scheme as a starting point, while disputing details such as whether the theory explains the behaviour of tenses in embedded clauses and narrative discourse.
