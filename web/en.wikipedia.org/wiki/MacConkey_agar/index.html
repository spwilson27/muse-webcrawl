MacConkey agar is a selective and differential culture medium for bacteria. It is designed to selectively isolate Gram-negative and enteric (normally found in the intestinal tract) bacilli and differentiate them based on lactose fermentation. It is one of the most widely used media in clinical microbiology, and was developed by British bacteriologist Alfred Theodore MacConkey while working for the Royal Commission on Sewage Disposal at the turn of the 20th century.

Composition and principle

The medium contains bile salts and crystal violet dye, which inhibit the growth of most Gram-positive bacteria, making it selective for Gram-negative organisms. It also contains lactose as a fermentable carbohydrate and neutral red as a pH indicator. Bacteria that ferment lactose produce acid, lowering the pH and turning their colonies pink to red; strong fermenters may also precipitate bile salts around the colonies. Non-lactose fermenters use peptone instead, raising the pH and producing colourless or pale colonies.

Interpretation

Lactose-fermenting organisms — such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella, and Enterobacter — appear as pink colonies; E. coli characteristically gives bright pink colonies with a surrounding zone of precipitated bile. Non-lactose fermenters such as Salmonella, Shigella, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa remain colourless. A variant, sorbitol-MacConkey agar, substitutes sorbitol for lactose and is used to screen for enterohemorrhagic E. coli O157:H7, which typically does not ferment sorbitol.
