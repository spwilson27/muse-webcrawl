Socinianism is a Christian theological system named after the Italian reformers Laelius Socinus (1525–1562) and his nephew Faustus Socinus (1539–1604). It is a form of Unitarianism that rejects the doctrine of the Trinity, holding that God is one person and that Jesus was a man — a uniquely commissioned prophet and saviour, but not himself God. Socinians also denied original sin, predestination and the satisfaction theory of the atonement, and emphasised reason in interpreting Scripture.

History

Faustus Socinus settled in Poland in 1579 and became the leading theologian of the Minor Reformed Church, known as the Polish Brethren. Protected by Poland's unusual religious toleration, the Brethren built their centre at Raków, where a famous academy and printing press issued the Racovian Catechism (1605), the classic summary of Socinian doctrine. Socinian teachings included adult baptism by immersion, refusal of military service and oaths, and opposition to the death penalty — positions that made the Brethren targets of suspicion. After the Swedish Deluge, Catholic reaction turned on them: in 1638 the Raków academy was closed, and in 1658 the Polish Sejm ordered the Brethren to convert or leave the country. Refugees spread to Transylvania, the Netherlands, East Prussia and England.

Beliefs

Socinians held that the Bible is authoritative but must be read rationally; doctrines contrary to reason cannot be true. They taught that Adam's sin did not corrupt human nature, that humans are mortal by nature and that immortality is a gift granted to the faithful at the resurrection, and that Christ's death saves by revealing God's love and providing a moral example rather than by paying a debt of punishment. Christ, though born of a virgin and raised from the dead, was not to be worshipped as God — though Socinus controversially permitted his invocation in prayer.

Influence

Although the organised Socinian movement was small, its ideas circulated widely in print. The Bibliotheca Fratrum Polonorum, published in Amsterdam, was read by thinkers including John Locke and Isaac Newton, and Socinian arguments shaped the development of English Unitarianism, biblical criticism and Enlightenment religious rationalism. "Socinian" long served as a general, often pejorative, label for anti-Trinitarian and rationalist theology.
