Circulating tumor DNA

Tumour cells shed DNA fragments into the circulation

Circulating tumor DNA (abbreviated ctDNA) is tumor-derived fragmented DNA in the bloodstream that is not associated with cells. ctDNA is a component of the broader pool of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) present in plasma, and it carries the genetic and epigenetic alterations of the cancer from which it originated. Because it can be sampled by a simple blood draw, ctDNA forms the basis of many "liquid biopsy" tests intended for cancer detection, treatment selection, and monitoring.

Origin and characteristics

ctDNA is released from tumor cells through apoptosis, necrosis, and possibly active secretion. Its fragments are typically short — around 130–170 base pairs — and are cleared from the blood rapidly, with a half-life of a few hours at most, so ctDNA levels can track changes in tumor burden in near real time. The fraction of ctDNA within total cfDNA varies enormously, from a large share in advanced metastatic disease to well below 0.1 percent in early-stage cancers, which places demanding limits on assay sensitivity.

Detection methods

Detection methods target recurrent mutations (for example in KRAS, EGFR, or TP53), copy-number changes, or tumor-specific methylation patterns. Techniques include digital PCR (including droplet-based systems), targeted next-generation sequencing with error suppression through molecular barcoding, and genome-wide approaches analysing fragment sizes and methylation. Distinguishing true tumor mutations from mutations arising in blood cells themselves — clonal hematopoiesis — is an important source of false positives.

Clinical applications

Established and investigational uses of ctDNA include selecting targeted therapies when tissue is unavailable (the first regulatory-approved liquid biopsy tests identified EGFR mutations in lung cancer from plasma), detecting acquired resistance mutations, monitoring response to therapy, detecting minimal residual disease after curative-intent treatment to identify patients at high risk of recurrence, and multi-cancer early-detection screening tests that assay methylation signatures in asymptomatic people. Evidence that acting on ctDNA findings improves outcomes is accumulating in some settings — for example, guiding adjuvant chemotherapy in stage II colon cancer — while screening applications remain under evaluation in large prospective trials.
