Charles William Ferdinand, Duke of Brunswick-Lüneburg (German: Karl Wilhelm Ferdinand; 9 October 1735 – 10 November 1806) was the sovereign Duke of Brunswick from 1780 and a Prussian field marshal. He commanded the allied army that invaded France in 1792 and issued the threatening Brunswick Manifesto. He was mortally wounded at the Battle of Jena–Auerstedt in 1806.

Early life and reign

The son of Duke Charles I of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel, he distinguished himself as a young officer in the Seven Years' War, fighting at Hastenbeck, Krefeld and Minden, and gained a reputation as one of the finest soldiers of his generation. In 1764 he married Princess Augusta, sister of George III of Great Britain. Succeeding his father in 1780, he governed Brunswick as an enlightened reformer: he repaired the duchy's finances, reformed schools, and patronised the arts, while keeping his small state closely aligned with Prussia.

Revolutionary wars

In 1787 he commanded the Prussian army that restored the stadtholder in the Dutch Republic. In 1792, as commander of the Austro-Prussian invasion of France, he issued the Brunswick Manifesto (25 July 1792), threatening Paris with destruction if the royal family were harmed; the manifesto backfired, helping provoke the storming of the Tuileries and the abolition of the monarchy. His cautious advance ended after the cannonade of Valmy (20 September 1792) and a controversial retreat. He resigned his command in 1794.

Death

Recalled in 1806 as commander-in-chief of the Prussian army against Napoleon, the 71-year-old duke was shot and blinded at the Battle of Auerstedt on 14 October 1806. Carried before the French advance, he died at Ottensen near Hamburg on 10 November. Brunswick was dissolved into the Kingdom of Westphalia the following year.
