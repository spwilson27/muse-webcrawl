Persian music refers to the musical traditions of Iran and the wider Persian-speaking world. Its classical tradition is a modal, largely improvisatory art organised around the dastgah system and preserved in a canonical repertoire called the radif.

History

Music was cultivated at the courts of ancient Persia; the Sasanian king Khosrow II patronised the legendary musician Barbad, credited with organising an elaborate modal system. After the Arab conquest, Persian theorists such as al-Farabi and Safi al-Din al-Urmawi shaped the music theory of the Islamic world. The modern classical system crystallised in the Qajar era, when master musicians such as Mirza Abdollah assembled the radif — a collection of several hundred short melodic movements (gusheh) grouped into twelve dastgahs.

Characteristics

Persian classical music is monophonic and melodic, centred on a singer or solo instrument accompanied by a small ensemble. Performances unfold within a dastgah, moving through its gushehs with improvised development (avaz) interspersed with composed pieces (tasnif) and rhythmic instrumental works. Microtonal intervals such as the koron (quarter-flat) distinguish its scales from Western tuning. Vocal music traditionally sets classical Persian poetry — Rumi, Hafez, Saadi — and singing is ornamented with the vibrato-like tahrir.

Instruments

Principal instruments include the tar and setar (long-necked lutes), the santur (hammered dulcimer), the kamancheh (spike fiddle), the ney (end-blown flute), and percussion such as the tombak (goblet drum) and daf (frame drum, associated with Sufi ritual).

Modern developments

Twentieth-century figures such as Abolhasan Saba and, later, Mohammad Reza Shajarian, Hossein Alizadeh, Kayhan Kalhor, and Mohammad Reza Lotfi renewed the tradition, while Persian pop, rock, and hip-hop developed both inside Iran and in the diaspora. Regional folk traditions — Kurdish, Azeri, Baluchi, Khorasani — remain vibrant alongside the classical canon.
