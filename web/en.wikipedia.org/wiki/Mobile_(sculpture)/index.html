Mobile (sculpture)

A mobile is a type of kinetic sculpture constructed to take advantage of the principle of equilibrium. It consists of a number of rods, from which weighted objects or further rods hang; the objects hanging from the rods balance each other so that the rods remain roughly horizontal, and the whole assembly moves gently in air currents.

History

The form is most closely associated with the American sculptor Alexander Calder, who began making mobiles in the early 1930s after a visit to Piet Mondrian's studio prompted him to experiment with abstraction in motion. The word "mobile" was coined in 1931 by Marcel Duchamp to describe Calder's work, playing on the French meanings of "motion" and "motive". Calder distinguished his moving works from his static sculptures, which Jean Arp dubbed "stabiles".

Design

A mobile's elements are typically cut from sheet metal and painted in bold primary colours and black. Because the balance of each arm depends on the weights at its ends, constructing a large mobile requires careful calculation; Calder developed intuitive and mathematical methods for balancing his compositions, which range from tabletop pieces to monumental works suspended in airport terminals and museum atriums.
