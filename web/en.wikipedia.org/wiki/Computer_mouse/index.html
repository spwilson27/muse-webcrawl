Computer mouse

A computer mouse (plural mice; also mouses) is a hand-held pointing device that detects two-dimensional motion relative to a surface. This motion is typically translated into the motion of the pointer (called a cursor) on a display, which allows a smooth control of the graphical user interface of a computer. The first public demonstration of a mouse controlling a computer system was in 1968, in Douglas Engelbart's "Mother of All Demos".

History

The mouse was invented at Stanford Research Institute by Douglas Engelbart and built by Bill English in the early 1960s; Engelbart's patent (filed 1967, granted 1970) described an "X-Y position indicator for a display system" in a wooden shell with two perpendicular wheels. Engelbart demonstrated it on 9 December 1968 with his NLS system. At Xerox PARC the mouse was redesigned with a ball (English, 1972) and shipped with the Xerox Alto and later the Star (1981). Apple popularised the mouse with the Lisa (1983) and Macintosh (1984); it became standard with the spread of graphical user interfaces in the late 1980s and 1990s.

Types

  • Mechanical (ball) mice — a rubber ball rolls internal rollers and encoders; largely obsolete and prone to dirt.
  • Optical mice — an LED and sensor image the surface and track movement by correlation; work on most surfaces.
  • Laser mice — use a laser diode for illumination, allowing higher resolution and use on glossier surfaces.
  • Variants — trackballs, vertical/ergonomic mice, gaming mice with high-DPI sensors and extra buttons, 3D/connexion controllers, and touch mice.

Most mice have two buttons and a scroll wheel; clicking, double-clicking, dragging, and right-clicking for context menus form the basic interaction vocabulary. Modern mice connect by USB or wirelessly (Bluetooth or 2.4 GHz receivers), and sensitivity is measured in dots (counts) per inch (DPI/CPI).

Use and ergonomics

The mouse embodies direct manipulation in graphical interfaces. Prolonged use is associated with repetitive strain complaints, addressed by ergonomic shapes, keyboard shortcuts, and alternative pointing devices. Touchpads, touchscreens, and styli have displaced mice on many portable devices, but the mouse remains the precision pointing device of choice for desktop computing, design, and gaming.
