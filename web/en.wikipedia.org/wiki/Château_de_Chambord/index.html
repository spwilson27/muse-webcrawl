The Château de Chambord (French pronunciation: [ʃɑto d(ə) ʃɑ̃bɔʁ]) in Chambord, Centre-Val de Loire, France, is one of the most recognisable châteaux in the world because of its very distinctive French Renaissance architecture, which blends traditional French medieval forms with classical Renaissance structures. It was built by King Francis I beginning in 1519 as a hunting lodge and a monument to royal power, and was never completed in his lifetime; Francis in fact spent only a few weeks there in total.

Architecture

The château has 440 rooms, 282 fireplaces and 84 staircases, within a park enclosed by a 32-kilometre wall — the largest enclosed forest park in Europe. Its roofscape of turrets, cupolas and chimneys is famously ornate. At its heart is the great double-helix staircase, in which two spiral flights wind around a common core so that people ascending and descending never meet; tradition attributes the design to Leonardo da Vinci, Francis's guest at nearby Clos Lucé in the last years of his life, though the attribution is unproven. The plan, a keep with four corner towers around a Greek-cross core, mixes Italian Renaissance symmetry with the silhouette of a medieval fortress — decorative rather than defensive, since the moated château stood in marshland drained for its construction.

Later history

Louis XIV completed aspects of the building and held court there; Molière's troupe performed Le Bourgeois gentilhomme at Chambord in 1670. In the 18th century it housed the exiled Polish king Stanisław Leszczyński and then the Maréchal de Saxe. State-owned since 1930, it served during the Second World War as a refuge for art treasures evacuated from the Louvre, including the Mona Lisa. It is now a major tourist attraction within the UNESCO-listed Loire Valley cultural landscape.
