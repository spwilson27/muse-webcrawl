A thesaurus is a reference work that groups words and expressions by meaning, listing synonyms, antonyms and related terms so a writer or reader can move from an idea to alternative wording. Unlike a dictionary, whose basic unit is the word and its definition, a thesaurus is organised around semantic relations. Some are arranged alphabetically like dictionaries; others follow a conceptual classification that places terms within hierarchies of ideas.

History

The Greek word thēsauros means a treasure or storehouse. Early modern writers used “thesaurus” for treasuries of knowledge and language. Peter Mark Roget, a British physician and natural theologian, gave the modern genre its enduring form with Thesaurus of English Words and Phrases (1852). Roget classified vocabulary under six great classes—Abstract Relations, Space, Matter, Intellect, Volition and Affections—then subdivided them into concepts and cross-references. Later editions kept his architecture while adding new words.

Other traditions developed synonym dictionaries for rhetoric, law, medicine and science. In the twentieth century, information science adopted the thesaurus as a tool for indexing: a controlled vocabulary that selects preferred terms, records variants and shows broader, narrower and related terms so documents can be retrieved consistently.

Structure

A general thesaurus entry may list near-synonyms whose meanings are close but not interchangeable—thin, slim, lean, gaunt and emaciated differ in tone and evaluation. Good editions mark register, region and connotation to prevent false substitutions. Antonyms and contrast sets help define boundaries; example phrases show usage. Conceptual thesauri add pathways: a search under “emotion” can lead to joy, anger, fear and their refinements.

Information-science thesauri

In libraries, archives and databases, a thesaurus is a controlled vocabulary governed by standards such as ISO 25964. Preferred terms (descriptors) are distinguished from non-preferred synonyms; equivalence, hierarchical and associative relationships are recorded. Examples include the Art & Architecture Thesaurus, MeSH in medicine and AGROVOC in agriculture. Such systems support search expansion, consistent metadata and interoperability between collections.

Use and misuse

Writers use thesauri to escape repetition, find the exact register and recover a word on the tip of the tongue. The danger is ornament without understanding: replacing a plain accurate word with a rarer near-synonym can produce malapropism or purple prose. Lexicographers therefore stress that synonyms are rarely perfect; context decides whether terms can be exchanged. In natural-language processing, lexical resources such as WordNet function partly as machine-readable thesauri, encoding synsets and semantic relations for search, translation and language models.
