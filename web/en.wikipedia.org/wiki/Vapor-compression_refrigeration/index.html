Vapor-compression refrigeration

Vapor-compression refrigeration is the most widely used cycle for air conditioning and refrigeration, in domestic fridges, commercial cold stores, large building chillers and heat pumps. A circulating refrigerant absorbs heat at low pressure in an evaporator and rejects it at high pressure in a condenser, driven by a mechanical compressor.

The cycle

  1. Compression — the compressor raises the pressure and temperature of the refrigerant vapour.
  2. Condensation — the hot, high-pressure vapour flows through a condenser, where it rejects heat to the surroundings and condenses into a liquid.
  3. Expansion — the liquid passes through an expansion valve or capillary tube; its pressure and temperature drop sharply, producing a cold liquid–vapour mixture.
  4. Evaporation — in the evaporator the cold refrigerant absorbs heat from the cooled space and boils, returning as vapour to the compressor.

Performance is measured by the coefficient of performance (COP), the ratio of useful cooling to work input.

Refrigerants

Early systems used ammonia, sulfur dioxide or methyl chloride. From the 1930s chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, "Freon") dominated until their role in ozone depletion led to the Montreal Protocol phase-out. Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs such as R-134a) followed, and are themselves being phased down under the Kigali Amendment because of their high global warming potential, in favour of hydrofluoroolefins, hydrocarbons such as propane (R-290), ammonia and carbon dioxide (R-744).

History

Oliver Evans proposed a vapour-compression machine using ether in 1805; Jacob Perkins built a working ice-making machine on the principle in 1834. Ferdinand Carré's absorption systems competed in the 19th century, but electric compressors made vapour-compression dominant in the 20th century.

Applications

Beyond refrigerators and air conditioners, the same cycle run in reverse is a heat pump, moving heat into a building rather than out of it.
