Tropical cyclone intensity

Tropical cyclone intensity is described by two related measures: the maximum sustained wind speed near the centre and the minimum central barometric pressure. Relationships between wind and pressure, and the scales used to classify intensity, vary by ocean basin.

Classification scales

In the Atlantic and eastern Pacific, the Saffir–Simpson hurricane wind scale rates hurricanes from Category 1 (119–153 km/h) to Category 5 (≥252 km/h), based on one-minute sustained winds. Other basins use ten-minute sustained winds and their own terminology — typhoon, severe tropical cyclone, and so on — administered by the World Meteorological Organization's regional centres.

Measurement

Direct measurement is possible only with reconnaissance aircraft (dropping dropsondes) or coastal instruments. Over the open ocean, intensity is usually estimated from satellite imagery using the Dvorak technique, which relates cloud patterns to wind speeds. Remote scatterometers and microwave sounders provide additional data.

Potential intensity and record storms

The maximum potential intensity of a cyclone is set by sea-surface temperature and atmospheric thermodynamics. The most intense tropical cyclone reliably observed was Typhoon Tip (1979), with a central pressure of 870 hPa; Hurricane Patricia (2015) recorded the highest sustained winds, 345 km/h. Rapid intensification — an increase of at least 56 km/h in 24 hours — is a major forecasting challenge.
