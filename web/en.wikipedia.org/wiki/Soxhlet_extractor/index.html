Soxhlet extractor

A Soxhlet extractor is a piece of laboratory glassware invented in 1879 by the German agricultural chemist Franz von Soxhlet. It was originally designed for the extraction of lipids from a solid material, and it remains a standard apparatus for continuously extracting compounds that have limited solubility in a solvent, or for separating soluble components from insoluble matter.

Design and operation

The apparatus consists of three main parts: a round-bottom flask containing solvent, the extraction chamber in the middle, and a condenser on top. A sample is placed in a porous thimble, usually made of cellulose filter paper, inside the chamber. When the solvent in the flask is heated, its vapour travels up a side arm and condenses above the sample. Freshly condensed solvent percolates through the thimble, dissolving the target compounds. When the liquid level in the chamber reaches the top of a siphon tube, the entire contents of the chamber siphon back into the flask. The cycle then repeats.

Because only pure condensed solvent contacts the sample, and the extracted material accumulates in the flask over many cycles, Soxhlet extraction is efficient for compounds of low solubility. A typical extraction runs for hours to days depending on the material. After extraction, the solvent is evaporated, commonly with a rotary evaporator, leaving the extract behind.

Applications

Soxhlet extraction is used in food analysis (for example, determining the fat content of foods — the method for which it was originally devised), in environmental analysis of soils and sediments for pesticides and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, in natural-product chemistry, and in quality control of pharmaceuticals and polymers. Modern automated variants (such as the Soxtec system) speed up the process by immersing the sample in boiling solvent, but the classical apparatus remains widespread because of its simplicity and reliability.
