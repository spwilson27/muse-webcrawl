Rudolf Ludwig Carl Virchow (13 October 1821 – 5 September 1902) was a German physician, pathologist, anthropologist, biologist and politician, known as the founder of cellular pathology and a father of modern pathology and social medicine.

Cellular pathology

In his landmark work Cellular Pathology (1858), based on lectures at the Charité in Berlin, Virchow popularised the doctrine omnis cellula e cellula ("every cell from a cell"), arguing that diseases arise from changes in cells rather than from imbalances of bodily humours — a decisive break with two millennia of medical tradition. He described leukaemia, thrombosis and embolism (he coined several of these terms), and formulated "Virchow's triad" of factors in thrombosis. He founded the journal now known as Virchows Archiv in 1847.

Public health and politics

Sent to investigate a typhus epidemic in Upper Silesia in 1848, Virchow concluded that the remedy was social and political — education, employment and democracy — famously declaring that "medicine is a social science, and politics is nothing else but medicine on a large scale". A liberal politician, he co-founded the German Progressive Party, sat in the Prussian parliament and the Reichstag, and opposed Bismarck, who reportedly once challenged him to a duel. As a Berlin city councillor he drove reforms in sewers, water supply, hospitals and meat inspection.

Anthropology and later life

Virchow founded German anthropological societies and journals and conducted huge schoolchildren surveys of hair and eye colour; he opposed Darwinian evolution and Haeckel's monism, and fiercely rejected claims of "racial" science misused in his field. He died in Berlin in 1902 after fracturing his thigh jumping from a tram.
