Koschei

Koschei the Deathless, painted by Viktor Vasnetsov (1917–1926)

Koschei (Russian: Коще́й, Koshchey), often given the epithet "the Deathless" or "the Immortal", is a stock villain of Russian and Slavic folklore: an ancient, gaunt, immensely wealthy sorcerer who abducts the hero's beloved and cannot be killed by ordinary means, because his death is hidden outside his body. He appears in numerous fairy tales, most famously in The Frog Princess and tales of Ivan Tsarevich, and in the story collections of Alexander Afanasyev.

The hidden death

Koschei's defining feature is his external soul — his "death" is separated from his body and concealed, typically in a needle, which is inside an egg, inside a duck, inside a hare, inside an iron chest, buried beneath an oak on the island of Buyan in the middle of the ocean. So long as the needle is intact Koschei cannot die; only by seizing the egg and breaking the needle (in some versions, breaking it against Koschei's forehead) can the hero destroy him. This "external soul" motif is classified in the Aarne–Thompson–Uther index as tale type 302, "The Ogre's Heart in the Egg".

Character

Koschei is typically depicted as extremely old, skeletal, and hoarding treasure in an underground or distant palace ("in the thrice-ninth kingdom"). Unlike the witch Baba Yaga, who can sometimes be a helper, Koschei is essentially always an antagonist: he kidnaps princesses and tsaritsas, rides a magical horse, and pursues the hero across the earth. His name is sometimes connected with the Slavic root kost' ("bone"), highlighting his emaciated appearance.

In later culture

Koschei inspired Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov's opera Kashchey the Deathless (1902) and appears as a figure menacing the hero in Igor Stravinsky's ballet The Firebird (1910). He remains a popular figure in modern fantasy, where the hidden-death motif surfaces in villains whose souls are kept in objects, vessels, or lich phylacteries.
