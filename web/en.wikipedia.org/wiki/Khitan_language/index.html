The Khitan language (also spelled Kitan or Khita) is an extinct language once spoken by the Khitan people, founders of the Liao dynasty (907–1125) in northern China. It belongs to the Para-Mongolic branch of the proposed Serbi–Awar (Xianbei) group, making it a relative — though not a direct ancestor — of the Mongolic languages.

Scripts

Khitan was written in two scripts created under the Liao emperors. The Khitan large script, devised around 920 under Abaoji, was a logographic system modelled partly on Chinese characters and contained some 3,000 characters. The Khitan small script, created soon afterwards — reportedly inspired by the Uyghur alphabet — was a semi-syllabic system of about 378 blocks, each composed of phonetic elements arranged in clusters. Inscriptions survive mainly on tomb epitaphs, memorial steles, mirrors and coins.

Decipherment

Khitan remains only partially deciphered. Because the Liao dynasty fell before bilingual lexicons could survive, scholars rely on Chinese transcriptions of Khitan words, comparison with Mongolian, and formulaic epitaph texts with known dates. The small script, being largely phonetic, has yielded more progress: many grammatical suffixes and vocabulary items can now be read, and standing epitaphs such as that of Xiao Xiaozhong (1114) have been partially translated. The logographic large script remains considerably less understood.

Characteristics

From the readable material, Khitan appears to have been an agglutinative language with vowel harmony and a subject–object–verb word order, features shared with Mongolic and Turkic languages. Its numerals and many basic words show closer affinity to Mongolic, while other vocabulary suggests an earlier, distinct Para-Mongolic lineage descending from the language of the Xianbei confederation.
