Newton's laws of motion

Formulated1687, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica
FormulatorIsaac Newton
FieldClassical mechanics

Newton's laws of motion are three physical laws that form the foundation of classical mechanics. Published by Isaac Newton in the Principia Mathematica (1687), they describe the relationship between the motion of an object and the forces acting on it, and they remained the basis of dynamics until the development of relativity and quantum mechanics in the 20th century.

The three laws

First law (law of inertia). An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction, unless acted upon by a net external force. The first law defines inertial reference frames and asserts that force is what changes motion, not what maintains it.

Second law. The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass:

F = ma, or more generally F = dp/dt

where p is momentum. The second law is the quantitative engine of classical mechanics: given the forces, it predicts trajectories.

Third law. For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: if one body exerts a force on a second body, the second exerts an equal force in the opposite direction on the first. The pair of forces acts on different bodies and so does not cancel.

Scope and limits

Together with the law of universal gravitation, the laws explain phenomena from falling apples to planetary orbits, and they remain excellent approximations at everyday speeds and scales. They fail at velocities near the speed of light (requiring special relativity), for very massive and dense systems (general relativity), and at atomic scales (quantum mechanics). Later formulations by Lagrange and Hamilton recast the same physics in more general mathematical forms.
