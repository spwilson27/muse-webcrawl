Thalweg

In geography and fluvial geomorphology, a thalweg is the line or curve of lowest elevation within a valley or watercourse. The word comes from the German Thalweg (modern spelling Talweg), meaning "valley path": Thal (valley) plus Weg (way, path).

The thalweg traced along the deepest part of a channel

Hydrology

Under a river, the thalweg is the line of fastest flow, roughly migrating toward the outside of bends where erosion is greatest. Mapping the thalweg by connecting the deepest points along a stream's longitudinal profile is important for navigation channel maintenance, fisheries science and flood modelling, since the deepest, swiftest thread of current usually follows it.

Political geography

Because the thalweg usually follows the deepest and most navigable channel, it has become a legal principle in international law for defining boundaries in navigable rivers: under the thalweg doctrine, the boundary between two states separated by a river follows the thalweg of the principal navigable channel, so that each state has access to navigation. The doctrine has figured in disputes such as the Shatt al-Arab boundary between Iran and Iraq and the Kasikili/Sedudu Island case between Botswana and Namibia decided by the International Court of Justice in 1999.
