Morning Glory cloud

Morning Glory cloud
GenusRoll cloud (a type of arcus cloud)

The Morning Glory cloud is a rare meteorological phenomenon consisting of a low-level, roll-shaped cloud, sometimes accompanied by a series of parallel roll clouds, that can stretch up to 1,000 kilometres (620 mi) long. It occurs most famously and predictably over the Gulf of Carpentaria in northern Australia, particularly near the town of Burketown in Queensland, during the spring months from late September to early November.

Formation

The Morning Glory is a solitary wave, or soliton, that forms in a stable layer of the lower atmosphere. Over the Gulf of Carpentaria, sea breezes from the Gulf and from the Coral Sea converge over the Cape York Peninsula; the colliding air masses generate a disturbance in the atmosphere that travels like a wave on the ocean. Air rising at the front of the wave condenses into the rolling cloud, while air behind it sinks and becomes clear again, so the cloud appears to roll forward as it travels. The cloud typically forms overnight and arrives at the coast around sunrise, which accounts for its name. A sudden pressure jump, a sharp shift in wind, and a brief squall accompany its passage.

Burketown and gliding

Burketown, at the southern edge of the Gulf, is the best-known place in the world to see the phenomenon. During the season, gliding and hang-gliding pilots travel there to "surf" the powerful updraft at the leading edge of the cloud, riding the wave for long distances much as a surfer rides an ocean wave. The cloud is also culturally significant to local Aboriginal people, including the Gangalidda and Garrawa peoples, who associate its arrival with the season and birdlife.

Occurrence elsewhere

Similar roll clouds have been reported in other parts of the world, including the English Channel, the North Sea, the Sable Island region of Canada, the coast of Brazil, Mexico, and the Arabian Sea, but nowhere are they as regular and spectacular as in the Gulf of Carpentaria.
