Anaxagoras (c. 500 – c. 428 BC) was a Pre-Socratic Greek philosopher from Clazomenae in Ionia who brought philosophy to Athens. He held that the universe began as an undifferentiated mixture containing a portion of everything — "everything in everything" — and that cosmic order arose when this mixture was set into rotation by Nous (Mind), an infinite, self-ruled, unmixed intelligence. His introduction of a separate ordering mind influenced Socrates and Plato, though both criticised him for not consistently explaining nature teleologically.

Life

Born into a wealthy family, Anaxagoras gave up his property to pursue philosophy. He moved to Athens around 480 BC, became a friend and adviser of the statesman Pericles, and spent some thirty years there. Late in life he was prosecuted for impiety — reportedly for teaching that the Sun was a fiery mass of metal larger than the Peloponnese and that the Moon shone by reflected light and was made of earth — and, defended by Pericles, he withdrew to Lampsacus, where he died.

Philosophy

Accepting the Eleatic constraint that nothing can come from nothing, Anaxagoras argued that change is only mixture and separation of eternally existing ingredients. Because division never reaches a smallest part that is purely one thing, every substance contains portions of all qualities, with its character determined by what predominates. Nous alone is unmixed and initiates the cosmic vortex that separates the dense from the rare, hot from cold, and bright from dark. He also gave correct accounts of eclipses and argued that the Moon is illuminated by the Sun.

Legacy

Fragments of Anaxagoras's single book survive through Simplicius. Plato's Phaedo records Socrates' initial excitement and subsequent disappointment with Nous; Aristotle praised Anaxagoras as "a sober man among babblers" for positing Mind, yet faulted him for using it only as a deus ex machina. The crater Anaxagoras on the Moon is named after him.
