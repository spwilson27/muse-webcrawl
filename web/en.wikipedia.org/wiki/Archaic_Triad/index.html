The Archaic Triad is a group of three gods of early Roman religion: Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus. According to tradition, they were worshipped together on the Capitoline Hill before being displaced in prominence by the later Capitoline Triad of Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva, which reflected Etruscan and Greek influence.

Structure and priesthood

The Archaic Triad corresponded to the three senior priests of the Roman state cult, the flamines maiores: the Flamen Dialis served Jupiter, the Flamen Martialis served Mars, and the Flamen Quirinalis served Quirinus. The order of these flamens in the ordo sacerdotum preserved the hierarchy of the triad itself. The antiquity of the arrangement is suggested by the archaic ritual obligations imposed on the flamens, some of which survived into the late Republic as fossilized taboos.

Dumézil's theory

The French scholar Georges Dumézil made the Archaic Triad the cornerstone of his trifunctional hypothesis of Indo-European religion. In his reading, Jupiter embodied sovereignty and sacred authority, Mars embodied military force, and Quirinus embodied the producing populace — the three functions of sovereignty, war, and fertility/production that Dumézil traced across Indo-European cultures, with counterparts in the Indian Mitra–Varuna, Indra, and the Aśvins, and in Norse religion's Odin, Thor, and Freyr.

Later development

As Rome came under Etruscan kingship in the sixth century BC, the Capitoline temple was dedicated to Jupiter Optimus Maximus together with Juno and Minerva, and the Archaic Triad receded to a secondary place in public cult, though its flamens and festivals — such as the Quirinalia — remained fixtures of the religious calendar.
