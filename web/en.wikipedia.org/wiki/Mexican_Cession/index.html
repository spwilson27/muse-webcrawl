Mexican Cession

The Mexican Cession (Spanish: Cesión mexicana) is the region in the modern-day southwestern United States that Mexico ceded to the United States in the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848, which ended the Mexican–American War. The area comprised the modern states of California, Nevada and Utah, most of Arizona, and parts of Colorado, New Mexico and Wyoming — about 529,000 square miles (1,370,000 km²), or roughly 14.9% of the total land area of the United States today.

Background

War between the United States and Mexico began in 1846 over the annexation of Texas and the disputed boundary between the Rio Grande and the Nueces River. American forces under Zachary Taylor, Winfield Scott and Stephen Kearny occupied New Mexico and California and captured Mexico City in September 1847. Negotiator Nicholas Trist concluded the treaty at Guadalupe Hidalgo against the wishes of President Polk, who had recalled him.

Terms

Mexico recognised the Rio Grande as the border of Texas and ceded Alta California and Santa Fe de Nuevo México to the United States. In return, the United States paid $15 million and assumed up to $3.25 million in claims of American citizens against Mexico. Articles VIII and IX promised that Mexican nationals in the ceded lands could retain their property and choose US citizenship; the US Senate struck out Article X, which would have explicitly guaranteed Mexican land grants, though most grants were still adjudicated under the protocol of Querétaro.

Aftermath

The Cession was one of the largest territorial additions in US history, comparable to the Louisiana Purchase. Gold was discovered in California days before the treaty was signed, and California entered the Union as a free state in 1850, upsetting the balance between free and slave states and intensifying the sectional crisis that led to the Civil War. The remaining territory was organised through the Compromise of 1850, and the southern boundary was adjusted by the Gadsden Purchase of 1853–54.
