CompStat

[Image: A CompStat meeting at NYPD headquarters]

CompStat — short for COMPuter STATistics or COMParative STATistics — is a police-management and crime-reduction strategy developed by the New York City Police Department (NYPD) in the 1990s. It combines timely, computerised crime data and mapping with regular accountability meetings at which precinct commanders report on conditions in their areas and are questioned by senior executives about their strategies and results.

Development

CompStat was introduced in 1994 under Police Commissioner William Bratton and his deputy for crime control strategies, Jack Maple, drawing on Maple's hand-drawn "Charts of the Future" crime maps and on management ideas adapted from the private sector. Precinct commanders were given greater authority over their commands in exchange for accountability for crime trends; weekly CompStat meetings, held at One Police Plaza, reviewed statistics on the seven major felony offences and interrogated commanders on patterns, deployments and follow-up.

Spread and assessment

The model was widely imitated by police departments in the United States and abroad, including Baltimore's CitiStat (which extended the approach to other city agencies) and departments in Los Angeles, Philadelphia and many other cities. New York's crime rates fell sharply during the 1990s, and CompStat was credited by its proponents and by the 1996 National Institute of Justice assessment as one contributing factor, though criminologists debate how much of the decline it caused relative to other factors such as demographic change, the waning crack epidemic and national trends.

Critics have alleged that pressure to show reductions in reported crime can create incentives to downgrade or discourage complaints, citing surveys of retired NYPD commanders and investigations into statistical manipulation. CompStat has also influenced performance-management systems in government beyond policing.
