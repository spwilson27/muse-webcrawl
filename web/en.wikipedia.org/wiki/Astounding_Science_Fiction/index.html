Astounding Science Fiction, now published as Analog Science Fiction and Fact, is an American science fiction magazine first published in January 1930. The longest continuously published magazine in the genre, it was the central venue of the "Golden Age of Science Fiction" under editor John W. Campbell, who launched or shaped the careers of Isaac Asimov, Robert A. Heinlein, A. E. van Vogt, Theodore Sturgeon and many others.

History

The magazine began as Astounding Stories of Super-Science, a Clayton pulp edited by Harry Bates. Street & Smith bought it in 1933, and under F. Orlin Tremaine published landmark stories such as Charles R. Tanner's and Jack Williamson's work. In late 1937 John W. Campbell took over as editor, retitling it Astounding Science-Fiction, raising literary standards, demanding scientifically plausible, idea-driven stories, and in 1939 publishing the first stories of Asimov, Heinlein and van Vogt in a burst later called the Golden Age. Campbell's editorials and story ideas seeded classics including Asimov's Foundation series and the "Three Laws of Robotics".

Name changes and later years

Campbell gradually changed the title in 1960 to Analog Science Fact & Fiction, partly to shed the pulp connotations of "Astounding". After Campbell's death in 1971, Ben Bova and then Stanley Schmidt (1978–2012) edited the magazine, which remained a leading hard science fiction market and multiple Hugo Award winner. It is currently published by Dell Magazines.

Influence

The magazine's name survives in the Astounding Award for Best New Writer (formerly the John W. Campbell Award, renamed in 2019), presented annually at the World Science Fiction Convention.
