Arbuscular mycorrhiza

An arbuscular mycorrhiza (plural mycorrhizae, adjective mycorrhizal) is a type of mycorrhiza in which the symbiont fungus (arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, or AMF) penetrates the cortical cells of the roots of a vascular plant, forming branching, tree-like structures called arbuscules, and often balloon-like storage organs called vesicles.

Arbuscular mycorrhizae are by far the most widespread type of mycorrhizal symbiosis: roughly 72% of vascular plant species, including most crops and grasses, form them. The fungi belong to the subphylum Glomeromycotina and are obligate symbionts — they cannot complete their life cycle without a living host root.

Exchange of nutrients

The symbiosis is generally mutualistic. The fungal hyphae extend far beyond the root's depletion zone into the soil, scavenging immobile mineral nutrients — above all phosphorus, but also nitrogen, zinc, and copper — and transferring them to the plant at the arbuscule interface. In return, the plant supplies the fungus with photosynthetically fixed carbon, in the form of sugars and lipids; up to a fifth of a plant's photosynthate may pass to its fungal partners. The association also improves plant water relations and resistance to some root pathogens and soil toxins.

Evolution and ecology

The partnership is ancient, dating back some 450 million years; arbuscule-like structures occur in fossils of early land plants such as Rhynia, and molecular evidence suggests the symbiosis helped plants colonize land. Because AMF connect multiple plants through common mycelial networks, they redistribute nutrients across plant communities and contribute substantially to soil carbon storage, including through the production of the persistent glycoprotein glomalin.

Agriculture

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are increasingly used as biofertilizers to reduce phosphate fertilizer inputs. However, heavy phosphorus fertilization and intensive tillage suppress colonization, and modern crop breeding under high-input conditions may have weakened the symbiosis in some varieties.
