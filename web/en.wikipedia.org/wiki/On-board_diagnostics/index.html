On-board diagnostics

On-board diagnostics (OBD) is a term referring to a vehicle's self-diagnostic and reporting capability. OBD systems give the vehicle owner or repair technician access to information about the health of the various vehicle sub-systems, in particular those related to emissions. When a fault is detected, the system stores a diagnostic trouble code (DTC) and typically illuminates the "check engine" light (malfunction indicator lamp) on the dashboard.

History

Early manufacturer-specific systems appeared in the 1980s. California's Air Resources Board required OBD-I on all new cars sold in the state from 1991. The more comprehensive OBD-II standard became mandatory for all cars and light trucks sold in the United States from the 1996 model year, specifying a standard 16-pin data link connector, standardised trouble codes, and a set of monitor tests for emissions-related components. Equivalent standards include EOBD in Europe and JOBD in Japan.

Use

Mechanics and motorists connect scan tools to the OBD-II port to read live sensor data and freeze-frame data, read and clear fault codes, and verify repairs. OBD data is also used in periodic emissions inspections, fleet telematics, and usage-based insurance devices.
