Chen's theorem

Chen's theorem: every large even number is p + P2

Chen's theorem, proved by the Chinese mathematician Chen Jingrun in 1966 (with the full proof published in 1973), states that every sufficiently large even number can be written as the sum of a prime and a number that is either prime or the product of two primes. In the shorthand of additive number theory, every large even number is of the form p + P2 — a "1 + 2" result.

Relation to Goldbach's conjecture

The Goldbach conjecture asserts that every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes ("1 + 1"). Chen's theorem is the closest approach to the conjecture proved to date: it falls one prime factor short. Its proof required major advances in sieve theory, including Chen's new "double sieve" switching technique, which allowed him to handle error terms that had defeated earlier attempts by mathematicians such as Renyi, who had proved "1 + c" for some large constant, and Pan Chengdong and Barban, who reached "1 + 5" and "1 + 4".

Statement and extensions

More precisely, Chen showed that for a large even number N, the number of representations N = p + P2 (with p prime) is bounded below by a positive quantity proportional to N / (log N)2 times the usual Goldbach singular series factor. A companion result concerns twin primes: there are infinitely many primes p such that p + 2 is either prime or a product of two primes.

Later authors simplified Chen's argument and made parts of it explicit, but improvement to "1 + 1" is widely believed to require ideas beyond sieve methods alone, because of the parity problem that limits what sieves can detect. Chen's work, accomplished in China during the Cultural Revolution, made him a national hero and one of the most celebrated mathematicians of the twentieth century.
