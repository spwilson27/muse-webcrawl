Cymbal

A suspended cymbal from a modern drum kit.

A cymbal is a common percussion instrument consisting of a thin, normally round plate of metal, usually a bronze alloy. Cymbals have been played since antiquity in religious ritual, military music, orchestras, and dance. In modern music they are fundamental to the drum kit and to the orchestral percussion section.

Types

Orchestral cymbals include crash cymbals played in pairs (one in each hand, clashed together) and the suspended cymbal, struck with mallets or sticks. The drum kit typically features a hi-hat — two cymbals mounted face-to-face on a pedal-operated stand — plus crash, ride, splash, and China cymbals, each size and weight producing a distinct voice. The ride cymbal articulates patterns; the crash punctuates accents.

Construction and sound

Traditional cymbals are cast from bronze (commonly B20 alloy: 80% copper, 20% tin), then hammered, lathed, and tuned. Their sound is complex and inharmonic, with a bright attack and long shimmer decay. Major makers such as Zildjian, Sabian, Paiste, and Meinl each have signature processes and alloys; the secrets of Turkish cymbal making were carried to America by the Zildjian family in the twentieth century.
