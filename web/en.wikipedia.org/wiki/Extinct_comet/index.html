Extinct comet

An extinct comet is a comet that has expelled most of its volatile ice and has little left to form a tail and coma. In a dormant comet, rather than extinct, volatile components are sealed within an inactive surface layer. Over many passages near the Sun, a comet's volatile compounds are depleted until only rocky or rubble-pile material remains, resembling an asteroid.

Formation

As a comet repeatedly approaches the Sun, sublimation strips away its ices. A crust of dust and rock may form over the nucleus, sealing remaining volatiles inside. Eventually outgassing becomes undetectable and the object appears inactive. Such bodies are thought to make up a significant fraction of near-Earth asteroids; the object 3200 Phaethon, the parent body of the Geminid meteor shower, is considered a candidate extinct or dormant comet nucleus, as is the Damocloid asteroid population.

Examples and study

Comet Encke and other short-period comets show gradually decreasing activity. Spacecraft visits, such as the European Space Agency's Rosetta mission to 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, have revealed crusty, consolidated surfaces consistent with progressive devolatilization. Distinguishing extinct comets from true asteroids remains difficult because old comet nuclei are dark and may reactivate if their surfaces crack.
