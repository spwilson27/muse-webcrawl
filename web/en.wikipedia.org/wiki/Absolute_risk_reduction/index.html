Absolute risk reduction

In epidemiology and evidence-based medicine, absolute risk reduction (ARR) — also called the risk difference — is the difference between the proportion of people experiencing an outcome in a control group and the proportion in a treated (or exposed) group. It is expressed in percentage points and directly reflects the size of an effect in absolute terms, in contrast to relative risk reduction, which can make small absolute effects appear large.

Definition and example

If 10% of untreated patients experience an event and 6% of treated patients do, the absolute risk reduction is 0.10 − 0.06 = 0.04, i.e. 4 percentage points. The same data give a relative risk reduction of 40%, which sounds far more impressive — a standard illustration of why clinicians and journalists are urged to report absolute effects. When the treated group fares worse, the negative ARR is instead reported as an absolute risk increase, whose reciprocal is the number needed to harm.

Number needed to treat

The inverse of the ARR is the number needed to treat (NNT): with an ARR of 0.04, the NNT is 25, meaning 25 patients must be treated for one additional patient to benefit. NNT depends on baseline risk and the time horizon of follow-up, so ARR and NNT from trials apply strictly to populations with comparable baseline risk over comparable periods.

Interpretation

Because ARR scales with baseline risk, a treatment with a constant relative effect yields larger absolute benefits in high-risk patients. Reporting guidelines for clinical trials recommend presenting both absolute and relative measures together with confidence intervals, since relative measures alone systematically exaggerate perceived benefit, especially for rare outcomes and in marketing of drugs and screening programmes.
