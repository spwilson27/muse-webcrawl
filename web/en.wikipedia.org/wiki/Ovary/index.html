Ovary

The ovary is a female reproductive organ found in the female reproductive system of humans and other vertebrates. In humans, the ovaries are a pair of almond-shaped organs, each about 3–5 cm long, situated on either side of the uterus in the pelvic cavity. The ovaries serve two principal functions: they produce the female gametes (ova, or egg cells) through the process of oogenesis, and they secrete the sex hormones oestrogen and progesterone, which regulate the menstrual cycle, pregnancy and secondary sexual characteristics.

Structure

Each ovary is attached to the broad ligament of the uterus by the mesovarium, to the uterus by the ovarian ligament, and to the pelvic wall by the suspensory ligament, which carries the ovarian vessels. Histologically, the ovary consists of an outer cortex and an inner medulla. The cortex contains ovarian follicles at various stages of development, embedded in connective tissue stroma, while the medulla contains blood vessels, lymphatics and nerves. The surface of the ovary is covered by a single layer of cuboidal cells known as the germinal epithelium, beneath which lies a dense connective tissue layer, the tunica albuginea.

Development of follicles and ovulation

A female is born with a finite stock of primordial follicles — roughly one to two million at birth, declining to a few hundred thousand by puberty — each containing an immature oocyte arrested in meiosis. During each menstrual cycle, under the influence of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), a cohort of follicles begins to grow; usually one becomes dominant and matures into a Graafian follicle. In response to a surge of luteinising hormone (LH), the mature follicle ruptures and releases its oocyte in the process of ovulation. The ruptured follicle then transforms into the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone to prepare the uterine lining for possible implantation. Over a reproductive lifetime, only about 400–500 oocytes are ovulated; the remainder degenerate through atresia.

Hormonal function

The ovaries are endocrine glands as well as reproductive organs. Granulosa and theca cells of the developing follicle produce oestrogens, principally oestradiol, which drive the proliferative phase of the menstrual cycle and maintain female secondary sexual characteristics. After ovulation, the corpus luteum secretes progesterone and oestrogen during the secretory (luteal) phase. The ovaries also produce smaller amounts of androgens, inhibin and relaxin. Ovarian activity is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis and ceases at the menopause, typically around the age of 50, when the follicular reserve is exhausted.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the ovary include polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), a common endocrine condition associated with irregular ovulation, hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance; ovarian cysts, most of which are functional and resolve spontaneously; ovarian torsion, a surgical emergency; and ovarian cancer, which is often diagnosed late because early symptoms are vague. Premature ovarian insufficiency describes loss of ovarian function before the age of 40. Surgical removal of the ovaries is termed oophorectomy.

In plants

In flowering plants (angiosperms), the ovary is the enlarged basal part of the carpel (pistil) that contains the ovules. After fertilisation, the ovules develop into seeds and the ovary wall (pericarp) typically develops into the fruit. Depending on its position relative to other floral parts, an ovary is described as superior (hypogynous flower) or inferior (epigynous flower).
