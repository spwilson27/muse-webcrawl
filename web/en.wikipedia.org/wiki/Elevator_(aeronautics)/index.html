Elevator (aeronautics)

Elevators are flight control surfaces, usually at the rear of an aircraft, which control the aircraft's pitch, and therefore the angle of attack and lift of the wing. The elevators are usually hinged to the tailplane or horizontal stabilizer. They may be the only pitch control surface present, and are sometimes located at the front of the aircraft (early airplanes and canards) or integrated into a rear "tailplane" also known as a slab elevator or stabilator.

Function

By deflecting the elevator upward, the pilot increases the downward force on the tail, raising the nose and increasing the wing's angle of attack; deflecting it downward lowers the nose. Elevator inputs are made with the control stick or yoke—pulling back raises the nose. Because pitch control determines speed and climb performance, the elevator is a primary control. Its effectiveness grows with airspeed, and at the point of stall the elevator is used to lower the nose and recover.

Design variations

Many aircraft incorporate an adjustable stabilizer or trim tab so that the continuous force needed in cruise can be trimmed out. On delta-winged aircraft without a separate tailplane, pitch and roll control are combined in elevons. In the V-tail configuration, ruddervators perform the functions of both rudder and elevators. Large airliners often split the elevator into inboard and outboard sections driven by separate hydraulic actuators for redundancy.

History

The Wright brothers' 1903 Flyer used a forward elevator (canard) for pitch control; the rear-elevator arrangement that became conventional was standard by the First World War. A faulty or jammed elevator—such as the stabilizer failures involved in several accidents—remains a critical failure mode addressed by redundant design.
