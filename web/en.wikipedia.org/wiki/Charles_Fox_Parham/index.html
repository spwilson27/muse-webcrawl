Charles Fox Parham

Charles Fox Parham (June 4, 1873 – January 29, 1929) was an American preacher and evangelist. Together with William J. Seymour, he was one of the two central figures in the development and early spread of Pentecostalism. It was Parham who first articulated the theological position that speaking in tongues was the "Bible evidence" of Spirit baptism, the defining doctrine of the emerging movement.

Topeka and the birth of Pentecostalism

Parham, a former Methodist pastor influenced by the Holiness movement, opened the Bethel Bible School in Topeka, Kansas, in October 1900. He set his students to study what constituted the biblical evidence of baptism in the Holy Spirit; they concluded it was speaking in tongues. On January 1, 1901, student Agnes Ozman spoke in tongues after Parham laid hands on her, an event Pentecostals regard as the movement's starting point. Parham promoted the new teaching through his Apostolic Faith movement and newspaper of the same name.

Houston and influence on Seymour

In 1905 Parham moved his base to Houston, Texas, where he ran a second Bible school. William J. Seymour, a Black Holiness preacher, attended the lectures — segregated, listening from the hallway — and carried Parham's teaching to Los Angeles, where in 1906 he led the Azusa Street Revival, the event that launched Pentecostalism worldwide. Relations between the two men later soured, in part over race and the conduct of the revival.

Later life and controversies

Parham's influence waned after scandals and doctrinal disputes, including an arrest in 1907 on morals charges that were later dropped, and his promotion of Anglo-Israelite ideas. He continued evangelistic work from Baxter Springs, Kansas, until his death in 1929. Historians credit Parham as Pentecostalism's principal theologian and founder, while recognising Seymour's greater role in its global spread.
