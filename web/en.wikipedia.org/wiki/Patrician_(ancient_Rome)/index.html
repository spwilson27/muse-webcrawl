Patrician (ancient Rome)

The patricians (Latin: patricii, singular patricius) were originally a group of ruling-class families in ancient Rome. The distinction between patricians and the common people, the plebeians, was one of the central social divisions of the Roman Republic, and the struggle between them — the Conflict of the Orders — shaped Roman politics for two centuries.

Origins

According to Roman tradition, the patricians were the descendants of the patres, the "fathers" who formed the Senate under Romulus and the early kings. Patrician status was hereditary and confined to a fixed set of clans (gentes maiores and gentes minores), such as the Julii, Cornelii, Claudii, and Fabii. Only patricians could initially hold magistracies, priesthoods, and senate seats, and marriage between patricians and plebeians was prohibited by the Twelve Tables.

Conflict of the Orders

From 494 BC, when the plebeians first seceded from the city, plebeian pressure gradually dismantled patrician monopoly: the creation of the tribunate, the publication of the laws, the admission of plebeians to the consulship (Licinio-Sextian laws, 367 BC), and finally the Lex Hortensia (287 BC), which made plebiscites binding on all citizens. Thereafter "patrician" was largely an inherited honour, and political power increasingly belonged to a mixed patricio-plebeian nobility.

Late Republic and Empire

By the late Republic few old patrician families survived, and adoption between classes (famously by Julius Caesar's adoption of Octavian, and by the plebeian Clodius's adoption into a patrician family in reverse) blurred the line. Under the Empire, emperors could create new patricians by adlection, and in late antiquity patricius became a high court title, later borne by rulers such as Theodoric and by the exarchs of Ravenna.
