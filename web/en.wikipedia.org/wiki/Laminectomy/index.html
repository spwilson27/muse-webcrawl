Laminectomy

Laminectomy
SpecialtyNeurosurgery / orthopaedic surgery

A laminectomy is a surgical procedure in which the surgeon removes the posterior arch of a vertebra — the lamina, together with the spinous process — in order to access the spinal canal and relieve pressure on the spinal cord or nerve roots (decompression). It is most commonly performed in the lumbar spine to treat spinal stenosis, but it is also used in the cervical and thoracic spine, and as an approach for removing spinal tumours, treating epidural abscesses or haematomas, or repairing other intraspinal pathology.

Indications

The classic indication is lumbar spinal stenosis causing neurogenic claudication or radiculopathy that has failed conservative treatment: narrowing of the canal by degenerative changes, thickened ligamentum flavum, facet joint hypertrophy and disc bulging compresses the cauda equina. Laminectomy may also be combined with discectomy for a herniated disc that cannot be reached otherwise. In the cervical spine, decompressive laminectomy (sometimes with fusion) is used for cervical spondylotic myelopathy.

Procedure

Through a midline posterior incision, the paraspinal muscles are retracted, and the laminae of one or more levels are removed with rongeurs and high-speed drills. The thickened ligamentum flavum is excised and the lateral recesses and neural foramina may be undercut (foraminotomy) to free the nerve roots, while care is taken to preserve enough of the facet joints to maintain stability. More limited variants — laminotomy (removal of part of one lamina), hemilaminectomy (one side only) and laminoplasty (hinging the lamina open rather than removing it, common in the cervical spine) — aim to reduce tissue disruption. If instability exists or is created, the decompression is supplemented by spinal fusion with instrumentation.

Outcomes and risks

Laminectomy generally gives good relief of leg pain and claudication, though results for back pain are less predictable, and randomised trials have shown benefits over non-operative care for carefully selected patients with stenosis. Risks include dural tear with cerebrospinal fluid leak, infection, epidural haematoma, nerve injury, and late instability or recurrent stenosis, occasionally requiring reoperation.
