Frustule

Frustule
Component ofDiatom cell wall
CompositionOpaline silica (SiO2·nH2O)
PartsEpitheca, hypotheca, girdle bands (cingulum)

A frustule is the hard and porous cell wall or external layer of diatoms. The frustule is composed almost purely of silica, made from silicic acid taken up from the water, and is coated with a layer of organic substance, which was referred to in the early literature on diatoms as pectin. Its two halves fit together like the two parts of a petri dish or pill box: the larger upper half is the epitheca and the smaller lower half the hypotheca, joined by overlapping girdle bands called the cingulum.

Structure

Frustules are intricately patterned with pores, ribs and processes whose arrangement is a principal basis for diatom taxonomy. Centric diatoms show radial symmetry, while pennate diatoms are bilaterally symmetric; pennate forms that move possess a slit-like structure, the raphe, along which mucilage is secreted. Because a daughter cell inherits one half of the parent frustule and builds a new smaller half inside it, one line of descendants progressively diminishes in size until size is restored by sexual reproduction (auxospore formation).

Fossils and uses

Being made of silica, frustules resist decay and accumulate in sediments as diatomaceous earth (diatomite), which is mined for use as a filter aid, mild abrasive, absorbent and filler. Fossil frustules in sediment cores are used to reconstruct past environmental conditions.
