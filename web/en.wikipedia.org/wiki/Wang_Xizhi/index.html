Wang Xizhi (Chinese: 王羲之; Wade–Giles: Wang Hsi-chih; c. 303 – c. 361) was a Chinese calligrapher, writer and official of the Eastern Jin dynasty, generally considered the greatest Chinese calligrapher in history. He is commonly honoured in China as the "Sage of Calligraphy" (書聖).

Life

Wang was born into the eminent Wang clan of Linyi, one of the great aristocratic families of the Jin dynasty, and was taught calligraphy by his relative Wang Yi and, according to tradition, by the celebrated calligrapher Wei Shuo (Lady Wei). He rose to the rank of general and served as administrator of Kuaiji (in modern Zhejiang). In later life he withdrew from office to the mountains around Shaoxing, devoting himself to calligraphy, Daoist pursuits and the raising of geese, whose supple neck movements he is said to have studied for brush technique.

Works and legacy

Wang excelled in every script type, but especially in the semi-cursive or "running" script (xingshu). His most celebrated work is the Lantingji Xu ("Preface to the Poems Composed at the Orchid Pavilion"), written in 353 CE during a famous literary gathering of scholars at the Orchid Pavilion near Shaoxing on the Shangsi Festival. The preface, a meditation on the brevity of life, is regarded as the supreme masterpiece of running-script calligraphy. The original was so treasured by Emperor Taizong of Tang that it was reputedly buried with him; it survives only in traced copies, of which the version attributed to Feng Chengsu is considered the finest.

No autograph original of Wang Xizhi survives, but early copies and engraved model-letter compendia transmitted his style for more than a millennium, defining the classical canon of Chinese calligraphy. His seventh son, Wang Xianzhi, also became a celebrated calligrapher; father and son are known together as the "Two Wangs".
