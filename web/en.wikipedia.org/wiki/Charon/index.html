In Greek mythology, Charon or Kharon (Greek: Χάρων) is the ferryman of Hades who carries the souls of the newly deceased across the river Styx (in other accounts, the Acheron) that divided the world of the living from the world of the dead. A coin to pay Charon for passage — usually an obolus or danake — was placed in or on the mouth of a dead person. Those who could not pay were doomed to wander the banks of the river for a hundred years.

Depiction

Charon is depicted in ancient art and literature as a grim, sullen old man — Virgil describes him in the Aeneid as filthy, with a matted grey beard and eyes of flame, poling his boat of rust-coloured iron. Aristophanes jokes about his fare of two obols in The Frogs, in which Dionysus rows himself across because Charon refuses passengers without the fare.

Literary appearances

Charon appears in Aristophanes' The Frogs, Virgil's Aeneid Book VI, Seneca's Hercules Furens, Lucian's satires, and Dante's Inferno, where he ferries souls across the Acheron and warns Dante, a living man, to turn back. Michelangelo painted Charon striking the damned with his oar in the Last Judgment in the Sistine Chapel. The figure of the ferryman of the dead has close parallels in other traditions, such as the Mesopotamian ferryman Urshanabi.

Namesakes

Charon, the largest moon of the dwarf planet Pluto, discovered in 1978, is named after the ferryman. The name also suited the discoverer, James Christy, whose wife's name was Charlene ("Char").
