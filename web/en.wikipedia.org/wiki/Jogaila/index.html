Jogaila (Lithuanian: Jogaila; Polish: Władysław II Jagiełło; c. 1352/1362 – 1 June 1434) was Grand Duke of Lithuania from 1377 and, from 1386, King of Poland (jure uxoris with Queen Jadwiga until 1399, then in his own right). He founded the Jagiellonian dynasty, which ruled Poland and Lithuania — and at times Bohemia and Hungary — for over two centuries. His reign began the long union of the Polish and Lithuanian states that culminated in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.

Grand Duke of Lithuania

Jogaila was a son of Grand Duke Algirdas and inherited the Lithuanian throne in 1377. He contended with his uncle Kęstutis and cousin Vytautas in civil wars, and faced pressure from the Teutonic Order to the west and the growing power of Moscow to the east. In 1380 he allied with the Golden Horde against Moscow at Kulikovo, and in 1382 he captured and imprisoned Vytautas, who escaped.

Union with Poland and conversion

By the Union of Krewo (1385), Jogaila agreed to convert to Christianity, marry the young Polish queen Jadwiga, and join his Lithuanian and Ruthenian lands to the Polish Crown. Baptised as Władysław in Kraków in February 1386 and married to Jadwiga, he was crowned king on 4 March 1386. He then presided over the Christianisation of Lithuania — the last pagan state in Europe — beginning with mass baptisms in Vilnius in 1387.

War with the Teutonic Order

Jogaila reconciled with Vytautas, who became Grand Duke of Lithuania under Polish suzerainty in 1401. Their alliance crushed the Teutonic Knights at the Battle of Grunwald (Tannenberg) on 15 July 1410, one of the largest battles of medieval Europe, though the subsequent siege of Marienburg failed and the Peace of Thorn (1411) yielded modest territorial gains. The Union of Horodło (1413) deepened Polish–Lithuanian integration.

Later reign and legacy

Jogaila refounded the Kraków Academy (Jagiellonian University) in 1400 using Jadwiga's bequest. After her death in 1399 he ruled in his own right, and by his fourth marriage, to Sophia of Halshany, he fathered sons Władysław III and Casimir IV, securing the succession. He died in 1434 and was buried in Wawel Cathedral. In both Poland and Lithuania he is remembered as a unifier and Christianiser, though Lithuanian historiography also notes his Polonisation of the Lithuanian state.
