Typhoon Haiyan, known in the Philippines as Super Typhoon Yolanda, was one of the most powerful tropical cyclones ever recorded. It devastated portions of Southeast Asia, particularly the Philippines, in early November 2013. At its peak intensity Haiyan had one-minute sustained winds of 315 km/h (195 mph), among the highest ever estimated in a tropical cyclone, and it remains the strongest storm ever recorded to make landfall, striking the Philippine province of Eastern Samar at near-peak intensity on 7 November 2013.

Haiyan near peak intensity, shortly before landfall in the Philippines.

Meteorological history

Haiyan developed from a low-pressure area east-southeast of Pohnpei in the western Pacific on 2–3 November 2013, intensifying rapidly in warm waters under low wind shear. It passed through Palau as a typhoon and underwent explosive deepening on 6–7 November, reaching super typhoon strength with a minimum central pressure estimated at 895 hPa. After landfall at Guiuan, Eastern Samar, it crossed Leyte, northern Cebu, Panay, and the Sulu Sea, weakening gradually before passing Palawan and making final landfalls in northern Vietnam and dissipating over Guangxi, China, on 11 November.

Impact in the Philippines

The catastrophe was caused chiefly by storm surge: walls of seawater up to about 5–7 metres inundated coastal cities, most notoriously Tacloban, Leyte, which was almost entirely destroyed. Officials confirmed more than 6,300 deaths in the Philippines alone (over 7,900 including missing persons and other countries), with more than 16 million people affected, some 4 million displaced, and over a million houses damaged or destroyed — making Haiyan the deadliest Philippine typhoon in modern history. Airports, ports, and power and water supplies were knocked out; relief efforts were hampered by debris and looting.

Response and aftermath

The disaster prompted one of the largest international relief operations in the region's history, with military and humanitarian assets deployed by many countries and agencies. Criticism of the speed of the Philippine government's early response drew political controversy. Because of the devastation, the name Haiyan was retired by the ESCAP/WMO Typhoon Committee and replaced with Bailu; PAGASA likewise retired Yolanda, replacing it with Yasmin. Haiyan became a landmark case in debates over climate change and "loss and damage" for vulnerable states at international climate negotiations.
