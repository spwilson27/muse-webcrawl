Radical (chemistry)

Placeholder image: the hydroxyl radical, with its unpaired electron.

In chemistry, a radical, also known as a free radical, is an atom, molecule, or ion that has at least one unpaired valence electron. With some exceptions, these unpaired electrons make radicals highly chemically reactive. Many radicals spontaneously dimerise. Most organic radicals have very short lifetimes.

A notable example of a radical is the hydroxyl radical (HO•), a molecule that has one unpaired electron on the oxygen atom. Two other examples are triplet oxygen and triplet carbene (•CH2), which have two unpaired electrons. Conversely, radicals that are persistent and even isolable include nitric oxide (NO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and sterically shielded carbon radicals such as trityl.

Formation

Radicals are formed by homolytic bond cleavage, in which a covalent bond breaks so that each fragment keeps one electron (often induced by heat, light, or radiation), by redox reactions involving single-electron transfer, and by photolysis. Chain reactions proceed by initiation, propagation and termination steps; characteristic examples include combustion, atmospheric ozone chemistry, and radical polymerisation used to make many plastics.

Stability and reactivity

Radical stability is influenced by resonance delocalisation (as in benzyl and allyl radicals), by neighbouring heteroatoms (the captodative effect), and by steric shielding. The classic order of C–H bond strength — benzylic and allylic weakest, then tertiary, secondary, primary — reflects the stability of the resulting carbon radicals. Because of their unpaired electron, radicals are paramagnetic and can be studied by electron spin resonance (ESR/EPR) spectroscopy, and they can be intercepted with spin traps or scavengers known as antioxidants.

Biological significance

Reactive oxygen species such as superoxide and hydroxyl radicals are generated in metabolism and immune defence, but excess radicals damage DNA, proteins and lipids, and have been implicated in ageing and many diseases. Organisms defend themselves with enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase, and with dietary antioxidants.

History

The concept of a "radical" began in the nineteenth century as a group of atoms behaving as a unit in reactions. Moses Gomberg's 1900 report of the triphenylmethyl radical was the first characterisation of a persistent organic free radical and a landmark in the field.
