Philip I (23 May 1052 – 29 July 1108), called the Amorous (French: Philippe l'Amoureux), was King of the Franks from 1060 to 1108. His reign of 48 years was one of the longest in French history, though the royal domain he directly controlled remained small, centred on Paris and Orléans.

Early life

Philip was the son of King Henry I and Anne of Kiev. Unusually for the time, he was given the Byzantine name Philip by his mother. He was crowned as co-king at Reims on 23 May 1059, aged seven, and succeeded his father the following year. During his minority the regency was exercised by his mother and by Baldwin V, Count of Flanders.

Reign

Philip's reign saw continued conflict with William the Conqueror, Duke of Normandy and after 1066 King of England. Philip supported Robert Curthose, William's eldest son, against his father, and in 1087 William died from injuries sustained while campaigning against Philip's forces at Mantes. The two kings' rivalry set the pattern for centuries of Anglo-French conflict.

Within the royal domain, Philip worked to extend Capetian authority against unruly castellans, acquiring the Gâtinais in 1068 and Bourges in 1100. He also made early alliances between the crown and the growing towns, granting charters to several communes.

Marriage scandal and excommunication

In 1092 Philip repudiated his wife, Bertha of Holland, and married Bertrade de Montfort, wife of Fulk IV, Count of Anjou, although both spouses were still living. The scandal led to his excommunication by Pope Urban II in 1094, repeatedly renewed. Philip was formally absolved in 1104 after promising to separate from Bertrade, though the couple continued to live together. Because of the excommunication, Philip did not take part in the First Crusade proclaimed at Clermont in 1095, though his brother Hugh of Vermandois did.

Death and succession

Philip associated his son Louis with the throne from about 1100 to secure the succession. He died at Melun on 29 July 1108 and, because of his excommunication, was buried not at Saint-Denis with the other Capetian kings but at the abbey of Fleury at Saint-Benoît-sur-Loire. He was succeeded by Louis VI, "the Fat".
