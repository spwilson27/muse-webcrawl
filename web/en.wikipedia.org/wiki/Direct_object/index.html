Direct object

FieldGrammar / linguistics
Related termsIndirect object, transitive verb, accusative case

In linguistics, a direct object is a noun phrase or pronoun that is directly acted upon by the action expressed by a transitive verb. In the sentence "She kicked the ball", the ball is the direct object because it receives the action of kicking. Direct objects typically answer the question "what?" or "whom?" after the verb.

Properties

Verbs that take direct objects are called transitive verbs; those that do not are intransitive. In many languages the direct object is marked with the accusative case, and in English its position normally follows the verb. A direct object contrasts with an indirect object, which indicates the recipient or beneficiary of the action ("She gave him the ball").

Passivisation

A characteristic test for direct objects in English is that they can become the subject of a passive sentence: "The ball was kicked by her." This distinguishes direct objects from other complements and adjuncts.

Across languages

Languages differ in how they mark direct objects — by case inflection, word order, or object agreement on the verb. In ergative–absolutive languages the corresponding category is organised differently, with the object of a transitive verb grouped with the subject of an intransitive verb.
