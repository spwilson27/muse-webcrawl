The patriarchs (Hebrew: אבות, Avot) of the Bible, when narrowly defined, are Abraham, his son Isaac, and Isaac's son Jacob, also named Israel, the ancestor of the Israelites. These three figures are referred to collectively as the patriarchs, and the period in which they lived is known as the patriarchal age. They play significant roles in Hebrew scripture during and following their lifetimes. They are used as a significant marker by God in revelations and promises, and they continue to play important roles in the Abrahamic religions. Judaism, Christianity and Islam hold that the patriarchs, along with their primary wives — Sarah (wife of Abraham), Rebekah (wife of Isaac) and Leah and Rachel (wives of Jacob) — are entombed at the Cave of Machpelah in Hebron, a site held holy by the three religions.

More broadly, the term patriarchs can be used to describe the twenty male ancestor-figures between Adam and Abraham, or the first ten of these, called the antediluvian patriarchs because they came before the Flood.

Narratives

The patriarchal narratives occupy Genesis chapters 12–50. Abraham, originally named Abram, is called by God to leave Mesopotamia and journey to Canaan, where God promises him descendants as numerous as the stars and possession of the land. His faith is tested in the Binding of Isaac, in which he prepares to sacrifice his son before being stopped. Isaac, the more passive of the three, is father of the twins Esau and Jacob. Jacob, who wrestles with a divine being and is renamed Israel, fathers twelve sons — among them Joseph — who become the ancestors of the Twelve Tribes of Israel. The narrative concludes with Jacob's family settling in Egypt during a famine, setting the stage for the Exodus.

Historicity

Scholars debate the historical reality of the patriarchs and the patriarchal age. Early 20th-century scholars such as William F. Albright argued that customs described in the narratives matched those of the second millennium BCE known from archives at Mari and Nuzi. From the 1970s, works by Thomas L. Thompson and John Van Seters challenged this consensus, arguing that the narratives reflect the concerns of much later periods, particularly the first millennium BCE. The mainstream scholarly position treats the patriarchal stories as largely legendary traditions shaped over centuries, though they may preserve memories of ancient West Semitic pastoral life.
