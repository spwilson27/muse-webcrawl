Oophorectomy

Oophorectomy (from Greek ōophoron, "egg-bearing," plus ektomē, "cutting out") is the surgical removal of one or both ovaries. Removal of one ovary is a unilateral oophorectomy; removal of both is a bilateral oophorectomy. The procedure is often performed together with removal of the fallopian tubes (salpingo-oophorectomy) or with hysterectomy.

Indications

Oophorectomy may be performed to treat ovarian cancer and other ovarian diseases, benign ovarian cysts or tumors, ovarian torsion, endometriosis, ectopic pregnancy in the tube or ovary, and pelvic infection. In people at high inherited risk of ovarian and breast cancer — for example carriers of BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations — prophylactic (risk-reducing) removal of the ovaries and fallopian tubes substantially lowers the risk of ovarian cancer.

Consequences

Removal of both ovaries before natural menopause causes immediate "surgical menopause," with loss of estrogen and progesterone production. This is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, and cognitive effects, which must be weighed against the benefits of surgery; hormone therapy may mitigate some risks in younger patients. Removal of one ovary generally has little effect on fertility or hormone status, as the remaining ovary compensates.

Procedure

The operation can be performed through open abdominal surgery or, more commonly today, by minimally invasive laparoscopy, sometimes robot-assisted. As understanding of the long-term effects of ovary removal has grown, guidelines have shifted toward conserving healthy ovaries when surgery is done for benign indications, and toward considering removal of the fallopian tubes alone (opportunistic salpingectomy) as a cancer-prevention strategy.
