Putinism is the term used by scholars, journalists and politicians for the political system, ideology and style of rule that developed in Russia under Vladimir Putin, president from 2000 to 2008 and again from 2012 (and prime minister 2008–2012). It denotes a form of personalist, centralised rule in which democratic institutions formally exist but are subordinated to the executive — sometimes described, in the Kremlin's own vocabulary, as "managed democracy" or "sovereign democracy" — combined with state nationalism, social conservatism, and a political economy in which loyalty to the leadership structures access to wealth in strategic sectors such as energy.

Features

Political centralisation

Early in Putin's presidency the "power vertical" was constructed: federal districts and presidential envoys were created over the regions, independent national television was brought under state-friendly ownership, the Federation Council was reformed, and gubernatorial elections were abolished (2004, later restored in filtered form). Elections continued but under conditions analysts classify as electoral authoritarianism: opposition parties face administrative barriers, and the dominant party United Russia functions as a party of power. Constitutional amendments in 2020 "zeroed" Putin's previous terms, permitting him to remain president until 2036.

Siloviki and political economy

Putin, a former KGB officer, staffed the state with associates from the security services (the siloviki) and St Petersburg networks. The 2003 arrest of oligarch Mikhail Khodorkovsky and the dismantling of Yukos signalled that large private wealth was conditional on political loyalty; state corporations and loyal businessmen came to dominate oil, gas, banking and media.

Ideology

Analysts debate how far Putinism is an ideology rather than a practice of power. Its proclaimed elements include Russian state civilisation and the "Russian world" (Russkiy mir), Orthodox-inflected traditional values, anti-Westernism and great-power status, and the celebration of the Soviet victory of 1945. Ideologues such as Vladislav Surkov supplied concepts ("sovereign democracy"); after 2012, and sharply after the 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the system's rhetoric and repression intensified, with wartime censorship laws and the elimination of remaining independent media.

Criticism and assessments

Critics describe Putinism as kleptocratic and neo-imperial; supporters credit it with restoring order and growth after the 1990s. Scholars differ on terminology — comparing it variously with Bonapartism, fascism, or patrimonial authoritarianism — debates that sharpened following the annexation of Crimea (2014) and the invasion of Ukraine (2022) and the death in prison of opposition leader Alexei Navalny (2024).
