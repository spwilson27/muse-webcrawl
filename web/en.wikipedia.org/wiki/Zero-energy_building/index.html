Zero-energy building

A zero-energy building (ZEB), also known as a net-zero energy building, is a building which, over the course of a year, consumes on balance no more energy than it produces from renewable sources. The goal is reached by combining greatly reduced energy demand—through insulation, airtight construction, heat-recovery ventilation, passive solar design and efficient systems—with on-site generation, typically rooftop solar photovoltaics, exchanging surplus and deficit energy with the grid. Definitions vary: some count only site energy, others source energy, energy cost, or carbon emissions ("zero-carbon buildings").


A net-zero energy house

Design principles

Designers first minimise heating, cooling and lighting loads using the building fabric: superinsulation, high-performance glazing, thermal-bridge-free construction, orientation for passive solar gain, and shading. Mechanical systems are then downsized—often heat pumps and energy-recovery ventilators—before renewable generation is sized to cover the remaining annual load. The German Passivhaus standard and pilot projects such as the Solar Settlement in Freiburg demonstrated feasibility in the 1990s and 2000s.

Policy

The European Union's Energy Performance of Buildings Directive required all new buildings to be "nearly zero-energy" from 2021, and its 2024 recast moves toward zero-emission buildings. California's Title 24 standards have required net-zero energy for new homes since 2020. The US Department of Energy has published a common definition of zero-energy buildings for federal programmes.

Criticisms and challenges

Critics note that annual netting can mask large seasonal grid demands, that accounting conventions (site vs. source energy, treatment of embodied energy) change results, and that occupant behaviour produces a performance gap between modelled and actual use. Off-site renewable procurement in "zero energy" claims also draws scrutiny. Even so, the concept anchors building codes and voluntary rating systems worldwide.
