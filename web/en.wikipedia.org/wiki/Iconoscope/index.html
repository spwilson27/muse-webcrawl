Iconoscope

The iconoscope was the first practical video camera tube to be used in early television cameras. It was invented by Vladimir K. Zworykin and developed at RCA, which filed a patent application in 1923 (granted in 1938 after prolonged interference proceedings). The iconoscope was used in RCA's experimental and early commercial broadcasting from 1936 until 1946, when it was replaced by the much more sensitive image orthicon.

Operation

Inside the iconoscope, an image is projected onto a photosensitive mosaic of tiny globules of silver treated with caesium on a mica plate. Light causes each globule to emit electrons and store a charge proportional to the brightness at that point. An electron beam then scans the mosaic in a raster; as the beam discharges each globule in turn, it produces a signal current varying with the stored charge, generating the video signal. Because the stored charge accumulates between scans, the iconoscope was far more sensitive than earlier devices based on mechanical scanning, such as the Nipkow disk systems and Farnsworth's image dissector, which did not store charge.

Drawbacks

The iconoscope required very bright studio lighting, produced an unwanted secondary signal (the "iconoscope tilt and bend") that required correction, and had an erratic black level. Its successors — the image iconoscope (Super-Emitron in Europe), the image orthicon and the vidicon — progressively solved these problems.
