Lords of Galloway

The Lords of Galloway were the rulers of Galloway, a region of south-western Scotland, during the 11th to 13th centuries. The lordship, semi-independent of the Scottish crown, was ruled by a Gaelic-speaking dynasty descended from Fergus of Galloway (died 1161), and its lords played a major part in the politics of Scotland, northern England and the Irish Sea world.

Fergus and his sons

Fergus, the first recorded lord, appears in the reign of David I of Scotland and founded abbeys including Dundrennan. After his death the lordship passed to his sons Uhtred and Gille Brigte, whose rivalry ended with Uhtred's brutal murder in 1174. Their descendants — Lochlann (Roland) and later Alan of Galloway (died 1234) — became great magnates: Alan was Constable of Scotland and amassed lands in Ulster and the Isle of Man as well as Galloway.

The succession crisis

Alan left no legitimate son; his heirs were three daughters and an illegitimate son, Thomas. The men of Galloway preferred Thomas, but Alexander II of Scotland enforced partition among the daughters' husbands — Roger de Quincy, John I de Balliol and William de Forz — ending the native lordship in 1234–35 after a rebellion in which the Galwegians were defeated. Through Devorguilla of Galloway, wife of John de Balliol, the Galloway inheritance passed to their son, King John Balliol of Scotland.

Legacy

The lords patronised Cistercian and Premonstratensian houses — Dundrennan, Glenluce and Soulseat abbeys — and their memory persisted in Galwegian identity long after the lordship's extinction.
