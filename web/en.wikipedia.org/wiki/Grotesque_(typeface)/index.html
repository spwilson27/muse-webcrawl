Grotesque (typeface)

A specimen of grotesque letterforms

Grotesque is a classification of sans-serif typefaces that emerged in the nineteenth century, among the first sans-serifs to achieve wide commercial use. Grotesques are characterised by relatively even stroke weight, a somewhat irregular or "awkward" charm compared with later geometric designs, apertures that tend to be closed, and in many designs a spurred capital G and double-storey letterforms. The name is also used more loosely in Britain for sans-serif type in general.

History

The first sans-serif printing type was shown by William Caslon IV in 1816, initially as a novelty for display use. Through the nineteenth century German and British foundries developed the style for jobbing printing and posters; influential releases included the Berthold foundry's Akzidenz-Grotesk (1896), which became a foundation of modern typography, and in Britain the Stephenson Blake grotesques and Edward Johnston's typeface for the London Underground. Monotype's Grotesque series of the 1920s was widely used in British printing.

Characteristics and related styles

In the Vox-ATypI classification, grotesques form one family of the "lineal" sans-serifs, alongside neo-grotesques such as Helvetica (more regularised, with horizontal terminals), geometric sans-serifs such as Futura (built from circles and straight lines), and humanist sans-serifs such as Gill Sans (modelled on classical letter proportions). Grotesques typically have a larger x-height than traditional serif text faces and a slightly condensed feel, suiting them to headlines, signage and editorial design.

Usage

Grotesque typefaces became emblematic of the Swiss Style of graphic design in the mid-twentieth century and remain in constant use in corporate identity, publishing and digital interfaces, both in their historic forms and in modern revivals and reinterpretations.
