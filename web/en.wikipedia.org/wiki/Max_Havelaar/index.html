Max Havelaar; or, The Coffee Auctions of the Dutch Trading Company (Dutch: Max Havelaar, of de koffij-veilingen der Nederlandsche Handel-Maatschappij) is an 1860 novel by Eduard Douwes Dekker, writing under the pen name Multatuli ("I have suffered much"). It is a landmark of Dutch literature and a famous indictment of Dutch colonial rule in the East Indies (now Indonesia).

Content

The novel is framed as a manuscript written by the idealistic colonial official Max Havelaar, edited and commented on by the complacent Amsterdam coffee broker Batavus Droogstoppel. As assistant resident of Lebak in Java, Havelaar confronts the exploitation of the Javanese population by local regents and the indifference of his Dutch superiors; his reports are ignored and his career destroyed. Embedded tales, most famously the story of Saïdjah and Adinda, depict the suffering of Javanese peasants under forced labour and extortion.

Background and impact

Dekker drew on his own experience as a colonial official in Sumatra and Java; dismissed from service and in debt, he wrote the book in Brussels in a few weeks in 1859. The novel shocked Dutch readers, fuelled debate over the infamous Cultivation System, and is often credited with inspiring the more reformist "Ethical Policy" and, in the long term, Indonesian nationalism. It has been translated into many languages and was filmed in 1976. The fair-trade certification label "Max Havelaar", founded in the Netherlands in 1988, takes its name from the novel.
