Whistleblowing (also whistle-blowing) is the act by which a person — usually an employee — exposes information or activity within a private, public, or governmental organisation that is deemed illegal, immoral, illicit, unsafe, or fraudulent. The term derives from the practice of British policemen and sports referees blowing a whistle to alert the public or stop play upon seeing wrongdoing; the modern usage was popularised by consumer advocate Ralph Nader in the early 1970s.

Channels and protection

Disclosures may be internal (to supervisors or hotlines), external (to regulators, law enforcement, or journalists), or public via leaks. Because whistleblowers commonly face retaliation — dismissal, demotion, blacklisting, prosecution — many jurisdictions have enacted protective legislation. In the United States, the False Claims Act (1863, amended 1986) allows qui tam suits and shares of recoveries; the Whistleblower Protection Act (1989) protects federal employees, and the Dodd–Frank Act (2010) created bounty programmes at the SEC and CFTC. The United Kingdom's Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 and the European Union's Whistleblowing Directive of 2019 provide similar frameworks.

Notable cases

Well-known whistleblowers include Daniel Ellsberg (the Pentagon Papers, 1971), Frank Serpico (NYPD corruption), Mark Felt ("Deep Throat", Watergate), Sherron Watkins (Enron), Chelsea Manning and Edward Snowden (2013 surveillance disclosures), and Frances Haugen (Facebook, 2021). Their reception ranges from official honours to criminal prosecution under espionage laws, particularly where classified information is involved.

Ethics and research

Research on whistleblowing finds that most disclosures are made internally first, and that legal protection often lags behind formal policy. Ethicists debate the balance between organisational loyalty and duties to the public, and the concept is distinct from mere leaking in that it is usually framed as an appeal to corrective authority.
