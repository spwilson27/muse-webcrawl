José Vasconcelos Calderón (28 February 1882 – 30 June 1959) was a Mexican writer, philosopher, and politician. A leading intellectual of the Mexican Revolution, he served as the first Secretary of Public Education and sponsored the Mexican muralist movement; his 1925 essay La raza cósmica ("The Cosmic Race") is one of the most influential and debated texts in Latin American thought.

Career

Vasconcelos studied law in Mexico City and opposed the Porfirio Díaz regime, supporting Francisco Madero. After the revolution he became rector of the National University (1920) — he gave it its motto, "Por mi raza hablará el espíritu" — and in 1921 President Álvaro Obregón appointed him head of the newly created Secretariat of Public Education.

As minister he launched an ambitious campaign of rural schools, libraries and popular education, and commissioned artists including Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco and David Alfaro Siqueiros to paint murals in public buildings, igniting Mexican muralism. He resigned in 1924 after political disagreements.

La raza cósmica and later life

In La raza cósmica (1925) Vasconcelos argued that Latin America's mixture of Indigenous, European, African and Asian peoples was forming a "fifth race" that would inaugurate a spiritual age, anticipating later ideas of mestizaje as a positive identity, though the essay also contains hierarchical and racial views criticized by later readers. He ran for president in 1929, lost amid allegations of fraud, and went into exile. His four-volume autobiography, beginning with Ulises criollo (1935), is a classic of Mexican literature. His later writings took controversial far-right turns. He died in Mexico City in 1959.
