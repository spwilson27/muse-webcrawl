Execution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An execution is the carrying out of a sentence of death — capital punishment — by the state. Methods used in the modern era include hanging, firing squad, electrocution, gas chamber, lethal injection and beheading; historically they also included burning, crucifixion, breaking on the wheel and other deliberately painful methods, whose abolition tracks the modern history of penal reform from Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (1764) onward.

Executions are now concentrated in a small number of states: Amnesty International's annual counts (excluding China's unreported figures, believed to be in the thousands) typically record the large majority of known executions in Iran, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Egypt and the United States. Most of the world has abolished the death penalty in law or practice (more than two-thirds of countries as of the mid-2020s, on Amnesty's classification). Debates concern deterrence, wrongful conviction, discrimination in application, the humanity of methods (botched lethal injections; the unavailability of drugs as manufacturers refuse supply), and the rights of victims' families, who are divided on “closure”. Public executions persist in a few jurisdictions and are condemned by UN bodies.
