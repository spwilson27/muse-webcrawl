Humphry Davy

Humphry Davy
Born: 17 December 1778, Penzance, Cornwall
Field: Chemistry

Sir Humphry Davy (1778–1829) was a Cornish chemist who pioneered electrochemistry and isolated more elements than anyone of his era. In 1807 he electrolysed molten potash and soda to discover potassium and sodium; in 1808 he isolated magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium.

Davy proved chlorine was an element (1810), helped establish that diamond is carbon, investigated laughing gas, and invented the miners' safety lamp (1815) that bears his name. As professor at the Royal Institution he was a celebrity lecturer, and his assistant Michael Faraday became his most famous protégé.

[Image: Humphry Davy with his safety lamp and voltaic pile]

Early career

Apprenticed to an apothecary, Davy taught himself chemistry and was hired at 19 by the Pneumatic Institution in Bristol to test gases — inhaling many himself, including nearly fatal doses of carbon monoxide and nitrous oxide (which he suggested might be an anaesthetic).

The Davy lamp

After the Felling colliery disaster (1812), Davy designed a lamp whose flame, enclosed in metal gauze, could not ignite firedamp in mines — though it gave poor light and its invention was contested by George Stephenson.
