Phthalate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


General chemical structure of a phthalate ester.

Phthalates (pronounced THAL-ates) are a group of chemical compounds mainly used as plasticizers — substances added to plastics to increase their flexibility, transparency, durability, and longevity. They are esters of phthalic acid. Most phthalates are used to soften polyvinyl chloride (PVC); others are used as solvents and fixatives in personal-care products and many other applications.

Common examples include DEHP (bis(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate), long the dominant plasticiser for PVC; DINP and DIDP, used in flexible vinyl products; and lower-molecular-weight compounds such as DEP and DBP, used in cosmetics, adhesives, and pharmaceuticals coatings. Because phthalates are not chemically bound to the polymer, they migrate out of products over time and are now found widely in indoor dust, food, and the environment.

Health and regulation

Some phthalates are endocrine disruptors with anti-androgenic effects; epidemiological and animal studies have linked prenatal and childhood exposure to effects on reproductive development and other outcomes. As a result, several phthalates are restricted: the European Union limits DEHP, DBP, BBP and DIBP under REACH and RoHS rules, and the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 restricts several phthalates in children's toys and childcare articles. Alternatives include citrate esters, adipates, and bio-based plasticisers.
