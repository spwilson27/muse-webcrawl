Hypercalcaemia

Hypercalcaemia, also spelled hypercalcemia, is a high calcium (Ca2+) level in the blood serum. The normal range for total serum calcium is approximately 2.1–2.6 mmol/L, and levels above this range define hypercalcaemia. Symptoms classically include abdominal pain, bone pain, confusion and kidney stones, summarized in the mnemonic "stones, bones, groans, moans and psychiatric overtones".

Causes

The most common causes are primary hyperparathyroidism, usually from a single parathyroid adenoma, and malignancy, in which tumours secrete parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP) or destroy bone directly. Other causes include excess vitamin D, granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis, thyrotoxicosis, immobilization, thiazide diuretics and milk-alkali syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

Mild hypercalcaemia is often asymptomatic and found on routine blood tests. Higher levels cause polyuria and dehydration, constipation, muscle weakness, depression and cognitive disturbance; an ECG may show a shortened QT interval. Very high levels can cause arrhythmias and coma.

Treatment

Initial treatment is aggressive intravenous hydration with normal saline to restore volume and increase renal calcium excretion. Bisphosphonates such as zoledronic acid inhibit bone resorption, and calcitonin acts more quickly but for a shorter time. The underlying cause—such as parathyroid surgery for an adenoma—should be addressed.
