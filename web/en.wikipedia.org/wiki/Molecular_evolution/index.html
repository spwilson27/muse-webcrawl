Molecular evolution

Molecular evolution is the study of how hereditary material — DNA, RNA and proteins — changes over evolutionary time through mutation, recombination, gene duplication, natural selection and genetic drift. It bridges genetics, biochemistry and evolutionary biology, and provides the basis for reconstructing phylogenies and estimating divergence times with molecular clocks.

History

The field emerged in the 1960s as protein sequencing became feasible. Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling proposed the molecular clock in 1962 after finding that differences in haemoglobin sequences accumulated roughly in proportion to evolutionary distance. In 1968 Motoo Kimura advanced the neutral theory of molecular evolution, arguing that most evolutionary changes at the molecular level are caused by random drift of selectively neutral mutations rather than by positive selection — a framework later refined as the nearly neutral theory by Tomoko Ohta.

Mechanisms

  • Mutation — point substitutions, insertions, deletions, duplications and rearrangements supply molecular variation. Substitution rates differ among sites, genes and lineages; synonymous substitutions in coding regions are typically freer to accumulate than nonsynonymous ones.
  • Gene duplication — creates redundant gene copies that can evolve new functions (neofunctionalisation) or partition ancestral functions (subfunctionalisation).
  • Gene conversion, transposition and horizontal gene transfer — especially important in prokaryotes and in genome-size evolution.
  • Selection and drift — purifying selection removes deleterious changes in functionally important sequences, conserving them over vast timescales; positive selection accelerates change at adaptive sites.

Applications

Molecular data underpin modern phylogenetics, the dating of evolutionary events, studies of genome evolution (introns, repetitive DNA, pseudogenes), evolutionary developmental biology, and the tracing of pathogen outbreaks. Comparisons of genomes have revealed phenomena such as the relative constancy of protein evolution, the "twofold cost" debates, and punctuated patterns of molecular change.
