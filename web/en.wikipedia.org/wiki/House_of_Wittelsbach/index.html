The House of Wittelsbach is a German dynasty that ruled Bavaria from 1180 until the end of the German monarchies in 1918—one of the longest continuous reigns of any European house—and also held the Electoral Palatinate, provided kings of Sweden and (briefly) of Greece, and produced two Holy Roman Emperors. The family name comes from Wittelsbach Castle near Aichach in Bavaria. Its blue-and-white lozenge arms remain the emblem of Bavaria.

Origins and the Bavarian succession

The family appears in the 11th century as counts of Scheyern; Otto of Wittelsbach was enfeoffed with the duchy of Bavaria by Frederick Barbarossa in 1180 after the fall of Henry the Lion. The partition of 1255 split the inheritance into Upper Bavaria with the Palatinate and Lower Bavaria, and the Treaty of Pavia (1329) divided the house into a Bavarian line and a Palatinate line, whose heads later took turns in the imperial succession dispute. Louis IV of the Bavarian line became Holy Roman Emperor (1314–1347).

Electors and kings

The Palatinate line, elevated as electors of the Holy Roman Empire, converted to Calvinism and played a leading part in the start of the Thirty Years' War: the elector Frederick V's acceptance of the Bohemian crown in 1619 (the "Winter King") triggered the conflict and cost his family the electoral dignity, transferred to Bavaria in 1623. The Palatinate branch later inherited Bavaria itself when the Bavarian line died out in 1777. Wittelsbachs also reigned in Sweden (the Palatinate-Zweibrücken line, 1654–1720, including Charles X Gustav and Charles XII) and provided Otto, the first king of Greece (1832–1862). In 1806 Napoleon raised Bavaria to a kingdom; its kings—Maximilian I Joseph, Ludwig I, Maximilian II, Ludwig II (the builder of Neuschwanstein) and Ludwig III—reigned until the revolution of November 1918 deposed the dynasty.

The house today

The head of the house since 1996 is Franz Bonaventura, Duke of Bavaria, who by Jacobite reckoning is also the senior heir of the Stuart kings of England and Scotland, though he has never asserted the claim. The family's historical properties and collections are administered in part through the Wittelsbach Compensation Fund (Wittelsbacher Ausgleichsfonds), established in 1923, which maintains palaces and museums open to the public.
