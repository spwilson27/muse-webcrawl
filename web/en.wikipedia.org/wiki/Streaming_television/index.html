Streaming television

Streaming television delivers video content over internet protocols on demand.

Streaming television is the digital distribution of television content, such as television shows and films, via the Internet. Streaming TV may be contrasted with traditional terrestrial television, cable television, and satellite television, which deliver their content through dedicated broadcast channels on fixed schedules. In streaming television, media is delivered continuously and played as it arrives, typically on demand, giving viewers the ability to choose what to watch and when to watch it.

Streaming television services generally fall into several categories: subscription video on demand (SVOD) services such as Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, Hulu, and Max; advertising-supported services (AVOD and FAST channels); transactional services renting or selling individual titles; and live TV streaming services that replicate cable bundles over the internet, such as YouTube TV and Sling TV.

History

Early attempts at streaming video date to the 1990s, when companies such as Progressive Networks (RealNetworks) introduced streaming media players. Bandwidth limitations kept quality low. Broadband adoption in the 2000s made practical streaming feasible; YouTube launched in 2005, and Netflix began streaming in 2007, shifting its business from DVD-by-mail rental. Hulu followed in 2008 as a joint venture of broadcast networks.

Through the 2010s, original programming from streaming services — beginning with Netflix's House of Cards (2013) — won major awards and attracted top talent. Traditional media companies launched their own services, including Disney+ and HBO Max (later Max) in 2019–2020, a period sometimes called the "streaming wars." Cord-cutting accelerated: by the early 2020s, streaming accounted for the largest share of television viewing in the United States according to Nielsen measurements.

Technology

Streaming television typically uses adaptive bitrate streaming protocols such as Apple HLS and MPEG-DASH, which split video into short segments encoded at multiple quality levels; the player switches between them based on available bandwidth. Content is distributed through content delivery networks (CDNs) that cache segments close to viewers. Video is compressed with codecs including H.264/AVC, H.265/HEVC, VP9, and AV1. Digital rights management (DRM) systems such as Widevine, FairPlay, and PlayReady protect licensed content.

Viewers access services through smart TVs, streaming sticks and boxes (Roku, Amazon Fire TV, Apple TV, Chromecast), game consoles, computers, tablets, and phones.

Business models

  • SVOD — subscription video on demand, a flat monthly or annual fee.
  • AVOD — advertising-supported video on demand, free or discounted tiers with ads.
  • FAST — free ad-supported streaming television: scheduled linear channels delivered over the internet, e.g. Pluto TV and Tubi.
  • TVOD — transactional rental or purchase of individual titles.
  • vMVPD — virtual multichannel video programming distributors ("skinny bundles") such as YouTube TV.

Impact

Streaming changed viewing habits, normalizing binge-watching, whole-season releases, and personalized recommendation. It disrupted cable television economics, led to the fragmentation of content across many services, and prompted consolidation and bundling strategies in the 2020s. It also reshaped sports rights, windowing of films, and global distribution of non-English-language programming.
