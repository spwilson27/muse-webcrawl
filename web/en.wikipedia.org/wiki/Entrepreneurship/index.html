Entrepreneurship

[Image: a small startup team working around a table covered with laptops and sticky notes]

Entrepreneurship is the process of creating a new business or venture — identifying an opportunity, assembling resources, bearing uncertainty and risk, and organising production — and the entrepreneur is the person who does it. The word comes from the French entreprendre, 'to undertake'; Richard Cantillon gave it economic content in the 18th century.

Theories and role

Joseph Schumpeter cast the entrepreneur as the agent of "creative destruction", whose innovations — new products, methods, markets, sources of supply and forms of organisation — drive economic growth and the business cycle. Frank Knight distinguished uninsurable uncertainty (borne for profit) from calculable risk; Israel Kirzner, in the Austrian tradition, stressed alertness to unnoticed price discrepancies. In practice, new ventures are financed from savings, family, banks and venture capital, most fail within a few years, and a small minority — 'gazelles' and the rare 'unicorns' valued above $1 billion — create a disproportionate share of jobs and returns. Governments promote entrepreneurship through education, incubators, tax treatment and insolvency law that permits a fresh start, since small firms supply most new employment in many economies.
