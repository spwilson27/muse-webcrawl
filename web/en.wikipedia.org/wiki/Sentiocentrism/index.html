Sentiocentrism

Sentiocentrism, or sentientism, is an ethical position that gives moral consideration to all sentient beings — beings capable of subjective experience, in particular of suffering and enjoyment. Sentiocentrism holds that sentience, rather than species membership, rationality or ecological role, is the criterion for moral status: if a being can suffer, its interests count.

Background

The idea has roots in Jeremy Bentham, who wrote of animals: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" It was developed in modern animal ethics from the 1970s, notably by Peter Singer, who argued that the capacity for suffering gives a being interests that must be weighed equally regardless of species — a position he termed opposition to "speciesism".

Scope and debates

Because sentience is a matter of empirical evidence, sentiocentrists debate where its boundaries fall — most accept that vertebrates are sentient, with growing evidence for invertebrates such as octopuses, crabs and insects. The position differs from biocentrism, which values all living things, and from ecocentrism, which values ecosystems and species; sentiocentrists respond that ecosystems and species, not being sentient, cannot themselves be harmed, though harming them affects the sentient individuals within them. Sentiocentrism also figures in debates about wild-animal suffering and about the possible sentience of artificial intelligences.
