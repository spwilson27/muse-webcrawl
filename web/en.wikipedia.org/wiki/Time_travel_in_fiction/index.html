Time travel in fiction


Time machines are a staple of science fiction

Time travel is a common theme and plot device in science fiction and fantasy literature, film, and other media. Stories of travelling through time have appeared for centuries, but the modern concept — deliberate travel by means of a machine or method, with the possibility of changing history — crystallized in the late nineteenth century, most famously in H. G. Wells's The Time Machine (1895).

History

Early tales sent characters through time by sleeping, as in Washington Irving's "Rip Van Winkle", or by supernatural means, as in Charles Dickens's A Christmas Carol, where ghosts show Scrooge his past and future. Mark Twain's A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court (1889) transported its hero to the past, and Enrique Gaspar's 1887 novel El anacronópete featured an actual time machine before Wells popularized the idea. Wells coined the enduring image of the traveller who controls his journey through the fourth dimension.

Themes and paradoxes

Time travel fiction explores the consequences of altering the past. The grandfather paradox asks what happens if a traveller prevents his own existence; stories resolve it through immutable timelines, self-consistency, or branching parallel universes. The bootstrap paradox involves objects or information with no origin, existing in a causal loop. Many works use time travel for social satire, historical reflection, or romance, from Back to the Future and Doctor Who to The Time Traveler's Wife.

Methods

Fictional mechanisms range from machines and vehicles to natural portals, relativistic effects, magic, and dreams. Some stories restrict travel to observation, while others permit intervention, with rules that vary from work to work and define much of the plot's tension.
