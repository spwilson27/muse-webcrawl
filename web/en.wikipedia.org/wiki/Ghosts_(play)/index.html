Ghosts (Danish and Norwegian: Gengangere) is a play by the Norwegian playwright Henrik Ibsen. Written in 1881 and first performed in 1882, it is a scathing commentary on nineteenth-century morality. Because of its controversial themes — which include religious hypocrisy, venereal disease, incest and euthanasia — the play was fiercely attacked upon publication and early performance.

Plot

Helene Alving, a widow, is about to dedicate an orphanage she has built in memory of her late husband, Captain Alving, a respected pillar of the community. In reality, Captain Alving was a dissolute philanderer, and Mrs. Alving concealed his conduct throughout their marriage to preserve appearances, partly under the influence of Pastor Manders, her former love and spiritual adviser.

Her son Oswald has returned from Paris, where he has been living as a painter. Oswald is suffering from an inherited disease — implied to be congenital syphilis passed down from his father — and he has fallen in love with the housemaid Regina, unaware that she is his father's illegitimate daughter and therefore his half-sister. Mrs. Alving is haunted by "ghosts": not only the dead ideas and conventions of the past, as she explains in the play's famous speech, but the literal sins of the father visited upon the children. As the orphanage burns down uninsured (Pastor Manders having advised against insurance as a sign of insufficient faith), Oswald's mind collapses, and the play ends at sunrise with Oswald begging his mother to give him an overdose of morphine, leaving her — and the audience — with the terrible decision unresolved.

Characters

  • Mrs. Helene Alving — widow of Captain Alving
  • Oswald Alving — her son, a painter
  • Pastor Manders — the family's clergyman and adviser
  • Jacob Engstrand — a carpenter
  • Regina Engstrand — the Alvings' maid, secretly Captain Alving's daughter

Reception and legacy

The first performance was given by a Scandinavian company in Chicago on 20 May 1882, because no European theatre would stage it. The 1891 London production provoked outrage, with one critic calling it "an open drain; a loathsome sore unbandaged; a dirty act done publicly." Today Ghosts is regarded as one of Ibsen's greatest works and a landmark of modern drama, central to the development of realism and naturalism in the theatre.
