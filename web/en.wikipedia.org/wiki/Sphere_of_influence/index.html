Sphere of influence

In the field of international relations, a sphere of influence is a spatial region or concept division over which a state or organisation has a level of cultural, economic, military or political exclusivity, accommodating to the interests of powers outside the sphere. The influenced state may formally retain its independence and even its government, while in practice deferring to the dominant power in foreign policy.

Historical examples

The term became prominent in the era of European imperialism, when colonial powers divided regions such as Africa and China into spheres of influence short of outright annexation. The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 divided Persia into Russian and British spheres. During the Cold War, the United States and the Soviet Union maintained spheres in the Americas and Eastern Europe respectively; the Monroe Doctrine of 1823 had earlier claimed the Western Hemisphere as a United States sphere, and the Brezhnev Doctrine asserted a Soviet right to intervene within its sphere.

Contemporary usage

The concept is frequently invoked in debates about great-power competition, for example in discussion of Russian claims in its "near abroad", China's influence in East and Southeast Asia, and the reach of regional powers. Critics argue that spheres of influence conflict with the principle of sovereign equality in the UN Charter; supporters treat them as a pragmatic description of how international politics works.
