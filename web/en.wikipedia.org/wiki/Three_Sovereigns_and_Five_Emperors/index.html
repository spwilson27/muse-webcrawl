Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors

PinyinSān huáng wǔ dì
PeriodMythical, traditionally c. 2852–2070 BC
RegionAncient China

The Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors (Chinese: 三皇五帝; pinyin: Sān huáng wǔ dì) were a group of mythological rulers of ancient China credited in later Chinese historiography with founding Chinese civilization. According to traditional accounts, they reigned in the third millennium BC, before the semi-legendary Xia dynasty, and were revered as cultural heroes who taught the Chinese people essential skills and institutions.

The legendary sage-kings of ancient China

The identities of the figures vary between sources. The Three Sovereigns are most commonly listed as Fuxi, Nüwa, and Shennong, though some lists substitute Suiren, Zhurong, or Gonggong. The Five Emperors are usually given as the Yellow Emperor (Huangdi), Zhuanxu, Emperor Ku, Emperor Yao, and Emperor Shun.

The Three Sovereigns

Fuxi is credited with inventing hunting and fishing tools, domesticating animals, and creating the eight trigrams (bagua) that underlie the I Ching. Nüwa, his sister or wife in various traditions, is said to have created humanity from yellow clay and repaired the sky after a great catastrophe. Shennong, the "Divine Farmer," is credited with inventing agriculture, the plow, and herbal medicine; tradition holds that he tasted hundreds of herbs to determine their properties.

The Five Emperors

The Yellow Emperor is regarded as the ancestor of the Huaxia people and is credited with numerous inventions, including the compass, the calendar, and Chinese characters (through his historian Cangjie). His legendary victory over Chiyou at the Battle of Zhuolu consolidated his rule. Zhuanxu and Emperor Ku were his descendants who organized religious and social institutions. Emperor Yao was praised by Confucius as a model of virtue, and Emperor Shun, famed for his filial piety, was chosen by Yao as successor over Yao's own son — an early statement of the ideal of meritocratic succession. Shun in turn passed the throne to Yu the Great, founder of the Xia dynasty.

Historiography

The scheme of Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors was systematized during the Warring States period and Han dynasty. Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian (c. 91 BC) opens with the Five Emperors, beginning with the Yellow Emperor, treating them as historical figures. Modern historians generally regard them as legendary or mythological, possibly deified tribal leaders or culture heroes, though the era they represent may preserve faint memories of Neolithic developments such as the spread of farming in the Yellow River valley.

Legacy

The sage-kings became ideals of benevolent rule in Confucian political philosophy. Yao and Shun in particular were invoked for millennia as the standard against which emperors were measured, and the Yellow Emperor remains a central symbol of Chinese national identity; the traditional Chinese calendar counts years from his reign.
