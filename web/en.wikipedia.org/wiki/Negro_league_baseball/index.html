The Negro leagues were professional baseball leagues in the United States comprising teams predominantly made up of African Americans, and to a lesser extent Latin Americans, during the era when organised baseball was racially segregated. The leagues operated from the late 19th century until about 1960. The term may be used broadly to include the professional Black teams outside league play, and narrowly for the seven relatively successful leagues that operated from 1920 to 1948, which Major League Baseball recognised in 2020 as major leagues.

Origins

Black players such as Moses Fleetwood Walker played in organised baseball in the 1880s, but a colour line was drawn by the 1890s, excluding Black players from the major and affiliated minor leagues; it stood until 1947. Black professional teams such as the Cuban Giants (formed 1885) and the Chicago American Giants barnstormed and played in informal circuits. In 1920 Andrew "Rube" Foster founded the Negro National League in Kansas City, the first stable Black league, earning him recognition as the "father of Black baseball". The Eastern Colored League followed in 1923, and the first Negro World Series was played in 1924.

Golden age

The first Negro National League folded in 1931 during the Great Depression, but a second Negro National League (1933) and the Negro American League (1937) sustained top-level Black baseball through its golden age. Stars such as pitcher Satchel Paige, catcher Josh Gibson — often likened to Babe Ruth — and outfielder Cool Papa Bell drew enormous crowds, and the East–West All-Star Game regularly outdrew Major League Baseball's own All-Star Game. Teams relied heavily on barnstorming and exhibition games against white major leaguers to make ends meet.

Integration and decline

On 15 April 1947 Jackie Robinson debuted for the Brooklyn Dodgers, breaking the colour line. The ensuing signing of Black stars by major league clubs drained the Negro leagues of talent and fans; the Negro National League folded after 1948 and the Negro American League lingered into the early 1960s as a shadow of itself.

Legacy

The National Baseball Hall of Fame began inducting Negro league figures in 1971, beginning with Satchel Paige, and a special election in 2006 added seventeen more. In December 2020 MLB formally recognised the seven major Negro leagues of 1920–1948 as major leagues, and in 2024 incorporated their statistics into the official record books — briefly making Josh Gibson, among others, the all-time career leader in batting average and slugging percentage. The Negro Leagues Baseball Museum in Kansas City preserves the leagues' history.
