Active optics

Actuators behind a thin meniscus mirror correct its shape in real time.

Active optics is a technology used with reflecting telescopes developed in the 1980s, in which the shape of the telescope's primary mirror is actively adjusted by computer-controlled actuators to compensate for distortions caused by gravity, temperature changes and wind.

How it works

A wavefront sensor (often a Shack–Hartmann sensor) analyses the image of a reference star and measures the mirror's optical aberrations. A control system then commands actuators supporting the mirror to bend it slightly, restoring the correct figure. Corrections are made relatively slowly — on timescales of seconds to minutes — which distinguishes active optics from adaptive optics, which corrects atmospheric turbulence hundreds of times per second using a deformable mirror.

Applications

Active optics allows large mirrors to be made thin and lightweight rather than massively thick and stiff, greatly reducing cost. The technology was pioneered on ESO's New Technology Telescope (1989) and is used on the Very Large Telescope, the Keck telescopes (with segmented mirrors), and most modern large observatory telescopes.
