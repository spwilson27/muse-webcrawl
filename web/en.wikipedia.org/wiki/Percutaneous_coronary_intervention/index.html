Percutaneous coronary intervention

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), commonly known as coronary angioplasty or simply angioplasty, is a non-surgical procedure used to treat the stenotic (narrowed) coronary arteries of the heart found in coronary heart disease. The stenotic segment is dilated using balloon angioplasty, and a stent is very often mounted on the balloon and expanded in place so that it props the vessel open and maintains blood flow.

Procedure

A catheter is inserted through a sheath placed in the femoral or radial artery and advanced to the heart under X-ray guidance. A guide wire is passed across the narrowed segment, a balloon is inflated to compress the plaque against the arterial wall, and a metal mesh stent — in modern practice usually a drug-eluting stent — is left in place. Patients typically receive dual antiplatelet therapy afterwards to reduce the risk of stent thrombosis.

Indications

PCI is a mainstay treatment for acute coronary syndromes, particularly ST-elevation myocardial infarction, where it is preferred over thrombolysis when it can be performed promptly. It is also used for stable angina that does not respond adequately to medical therapy. For multivessel disease, coronary artery bypass grafting may be preferred depending on the pattern of disease and the presence of diabetes.

History

The technique was pioneered by Andreas Gruentzig, who performed the first successful balloon angioplasty of a coronary artery in 1977. Bare-metal stents were introduced in the late 1980s, and drug-eluting stents, which release medication to reduce restenosis, followed in the early 2000s.

Risks

Complications include bleeding at the access site, contrast-induced kidney injury, arterial dissection, restenosis and, rarely, emergency bypass surgery or death. Radial artery access has reduced bleeding complications compared with the femoral route.
