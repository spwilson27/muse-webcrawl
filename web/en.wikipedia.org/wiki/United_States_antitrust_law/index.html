United States antitrust law

United States antitrust law is a body of federal and state statutes and case law that regulates the conduct and organisation of businesses to promote competition and prevent monopolies and restraints of trade. Its core statutes are the Sherman Act of 1890, the Clayton Act of 1914 and the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914. Enforcement is shared between the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice, the Federal Trade Commission, state attorneys general and private plaintiffs, who may recover treble damages.

The statutes

Section 1 of the Sherman Act prohibits contracts and conspiracies in restraint of trade; certain agreements, such as price-fixing and bid-rigging among competitors, are illegal per se, while others are judged under the "rule of reason" from Standard Oil Co. v. United States (1911), the case that broke up the Standard Oil trust. Section 2 prohibits monopolisation and attempts to monopolise. The Clayton Act addresses specific practices — anticompetitive mergers and acquisitions (strengthened by the Celler–Kefauver Act of 1950 and the Hart–Scott–Rodino premerger notification regime of 1976), price discrimination (Robinson–Patman Act), tying and interlocking directorates. The FTC Act bans "unfair methods of competition".

Development

After early landmark cases (Northern Securities, Standard Oil, American Tobacco), enforcement waxed and waned: vigorous under the New Deal and post-war decades — producing the 1956 AT&T consent decree, the 1974–82 AT&T breakup case and United States v. IBM (dismissed 1982) — then narrowed from the 1970s under the influence of the Chicago school and the "consumer welfare standard" associated with Robert Bork's The Antitrust Paradox (1978). Major modern cases include United States v. Microsoft (2001) and, in the 2020s, actions against Google (found liable for monopolising search in 2024) and other technology platforms, amid renewed debate over antitrust's goals.

US antitrust law has profoundly influenced competition regimes worldwide, including European Union competition law, though the systems differ in approach and remedies.
