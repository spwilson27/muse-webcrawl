Burkitt lymphoma

Burkitt lymphoma is a cancer of the lymphatic system, particularly B lymphocytes found in the germinal centre. It is named after Denis Parsons Burkitt, the Irish surgeon who first described the disease in 1958 while working in equatorial Africa. It is one of the fastest-growing human tumours, with a cell doubling time of roughly 24 hours.

Types and causes

Three main variants are recognised: the endemic form, common in African children and strongly associated with Epstein–Barr virus infection and chronic malaria; the sporadic form, seen worldwide; and the immunodeficiency-associated form, linked to HIV infection. Burkitt lymphoma is characterised genetically by a translocation involving the MYC gene on chromosome 8, most commonly t(8;14).

Treatment

Despite its aggressive growth, Burkitt lymphoma is highly sensitive to intensive chemotherapy, and cure rates are high, especially in children. Under a microscope it shows a characteristic "starry sky" appearance, with pale macrophages scattered among dense tumour cells.
