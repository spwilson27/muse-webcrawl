Flagellation

Flagellation (from Latin flagellum, "whip") is the act of beating the human body with special implements such as whips, lashes, rods, switches, the cat o' nine tails, and the sjambok. Typically, flagellation is imposed on an unwilling subject as a punishment; it may also be inflicted on oneself, as in religious mortification of the flesh, or used in consensual contexts.

History

Whipping was a common punishment in the ancient world, in Roman law, and in military and naval discipline into the nineteenth century — the British Royal Navy's cat o' nine tails is a famous example; flogging in the British Army was abolished in 1881. Judicial corporal punishment by whipping persists in some legal systems, including those based on Islamic criminal law and in countries such as Singapore and Malaysia (caning), and it remains controversial on human-rights grounds; the UN Convention Against Torture and other instruments are generally read to prohibit judicial corporal punishment.

Religious flagellation

Self-flagellation has been practised as a devotional discipline in Christianity — notably by medieval flagellant movements that swept Europe during the Black Death (1348–49), when processions of penitents scourged themselves in public — and in some monastic orders using a small whip called a "discipline". In Shia Islam, some mourners practise self-flagellation with chains or blades (matam) during Ashura commemorations of the martyrdom of Husayn ibn Ali, a practice discouraged by many religious authorities. Devotional re-enactments of the Passion, including scourging, take place in parts of the Philippines and Spain during Holy Week.

Effects

Flagellation can cause injuries ranging from welts and lacerations to serious tissue damage, infection and, historically, death from severe floggings; modern regulations where the practice survives generally specify the implement, the number of strokes and medical supervision.
