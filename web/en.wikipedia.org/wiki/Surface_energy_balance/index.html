Surface energy balance

The surface energy balance describes how energy received at the Earth's surface — principally net radiation — is partitioned among the fluxes that heat the air, evaporate water, and warm the ground. It is a central concept in micrometeorology, hydrology, and climatology, governing surface temperature, evapotranspiration, and the development of the atmospheric boundary layer.

The balance equation

The balance is commonly written as Rn = H + LE + G, where Rn is net radiation (incoming shortwave and longwave radiation minus reflected shortwave and emitted longwave), H is the sensible heat flux to the atmosphere, LE is the latent heat flux associated with evaporation and transpiration, and G is the ground heat flux into the soil. During the day over moist land, much of the available energy goes into latent heat; over dry surfaces, sensible heat dominates.

Bowen ratio

The ratio of sensible to latent heat flux, the Bowen ratio, characterises a surface: it is low over oceans and irrigated fields (often below 0.5) and high over deserts (frequently above 5). The Bowen ratio method is also a practical technique for measuring evaporation by combining energy-balance and gradient measurements.

Measurement and closure

Fluxes are measured with net radiometers, soil heat-flux plates, and eddy-covariance systems that directly measure H and LE from turbulent fluctuations. A persistent finding is the "energy balance closure problem": eddy-covariance measurements often account for only 70–90% of the available energy, a discrepancy attributed to instrument limitations, advection, and large-scale turbulent motions.

Applications

Surface energy balance models drive estimates of evapotranspiration for irrigation scheduling, parameterise land-surface schemes in weather and climate models, and explain phenomena such as the urban heat island, where altered partitioning toward sensible heat and storage warms cities relative to their surroundings.
