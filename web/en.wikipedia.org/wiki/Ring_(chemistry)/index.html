Ring (chemistry)

In chemistry, a ring is an ambiguous term referring either to a simple cycle of atoms and bonds in a molecule or to a connected set of atoms and bonds in which every atom and bond is part of a cycle (also called a ring system). A ring system that is a simple cycle is called a monocycle or simple ring, and one that is not a simple cycle is a polycycle or polycyclic ring system.

A ring system is said to be a single ring (also called a monocyclic ring) when the ring system is a single cycle. Molecules containing one or more rings are called cyclic compounds, as opposed to acyclic (open-chain) compounds. Many important compounds in chemistry and biology are ring compounds, including most drugs, fuels such as benzene, and biomolecules such as sugars and many amino acids.

Types of rings

Rings may be homocyclic (all ring atoms are the same element, usually carbon) or heterocyclic (containing at least one atom of a different element, such as nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur). Rings are also classified by size: three-membered rings such as epoxides and cyclopropane are highly strained, while five- and six-membered rings are the most common because their bond angles are close to the ideal tetrahedral angle, minimizing angle strain.

Ring strain

Ring strain arises when bond angles in a ring deviate from their ideal values, when atoms are forced into unfavorable eclipsed conformations (torsional strain), or when non-bonded atoms are forced close together (steric strain). Cyclopropane, with bond angles of 60° compared to the ideal 109.5°, is highly strained and correspondingly reactive. Larger rings can pucker to relieve strain; cyclohexane adopts a chair conformation that is essentially strain-free.

Ring formation and opening

Reactions that form rings are called cyclizations. Intramolecular reactions that close rings are governed by Baldwin's rules, which classify cyclizations by ring size, the geometry of the bond broken (exo or endo), and the hybridization of the electrophilic carbon (tet, trig or dig). Rings can also be opened in ring-opening reactions, driven by release of ring strain or by cleavage of a weak bond.
