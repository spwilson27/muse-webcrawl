Blue carbon

[Hero image: a mangrove forest at low tide, its root systems trapping carbon-rich sediment]

Blue carbon is the term for carbon captured by the world's ocean and coastal ecosystems, and stored in biomass and sediments. The most important blue carbon ecosystems are mangrove forests, salt marshes and seagrass meadows, which sequester carbon at rates several times higher per unit area than terrestrial forests and can bury it in waterlogged sediments for millennia.

Mechanisms and magnitude

Coastal vegetation fixes carbon dioxide through photosynthesis; because the underlying soils are anaerobic, decomposition is slow and organic carbon accumulates in deep sediment layers. Sediment cores from mangroves and marshes show carbon stocks hundreds to thousands of years old. Global estimates suggest these ecosystems, though occupying less than 2% of the ocean's area, account for a disproportionate share of carbon buried in marine sediments — figures of around half are often cited.

Threats and emissions

Mangrove clearing for shrimp aquaculture and development, marsh drainage, and seagrass loss from dredging and poor water quality convert blue carbon stores into emissions, as the exposed sediments oxidise and release carbon dioxide and methane. Of the world's mangroves, roughly a third have been lost since 1980, though deforestation rates have since slowed.

Conservation and carbon markets

Because protection and restoration of coastal wetlands yield both climate and coastal-protection benefits, blue carbon projects have entered voluntary carbon markets — early examples include community mangrove projects such as Mikoko Pamoja in Kenya — and methodologies exist under standards such as Verra's VM0033. Blue carbon is also recognised in some national greenhouse gas inventories under IPCC wetlands guidance.
