Abstraction

Abstraction is a process or result in which general rules and concepts are derived from concrete instances, by omitting or suppressing details not relevant to the purpose at hand. It is a central operation in philosophy, mathematics, art, and computer science: to abstract is to attend to selected features of things while disregarding others, thereby forming concepts such as redness, number, or tree.

In philosophy

Aristotle and the medieval scholastics analysed how the mind forms universal concepts from sense experience, a process they called abstraction. John Locke later described the mind "separating" ideas from their particular circumstances to form general ideas. Debates between nominalists and realists concern whether abstract objects — numbers, properties, propositions — exist independently of minds and language; Platonists affirm that they do, while nominalists explain abstraction without such commitments.

In mathematics and computing

Mathematics proceeds by abstraction: group theory abstracts the common structure of many symmetry systems; category theory abstracts across mathematical theories themselves. In computer science, abstraction layers — from transistors to logic gates, machine code, operating systems, and high-level languages — let engineers work at one level while ignoring the machinery below. Data abstraction and procedural abstraction are core techniques of software design.

In art

Abstract art departs from representational depiction, generalising or discarding natural appearances in favour of form, colour, and structure. The term also names the intellectual operation shared by all these fields: compressing experience into manageable, transferable ideas.
