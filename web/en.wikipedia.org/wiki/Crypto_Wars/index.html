The Crypto Wars is an informal name for the ongoing efforts by the United States and allied governments to limit public and foreign access to strong cryptography, and the countervailing efforts of privacy advocates, cryptographers and industry to make strong encryption widely available. The conflict raises questions about national security, law enforcement, free speech and personal privacy.

Export controls

During the Cold War, cryptographic equipment was classified as a munition under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations. Software implementations were caught by the same rules: until the late 1990s, exported software was generally limited to 40-bit keys, which were trivially breakable. Phil Zimmermann's release of PGP in 1991 led to a criminal investigation (dropped in 1996), and the Bernstein v. United States litigation established that source code is speech protected by the First Amendment. Export rules were substantially liberalized in 1999–2000.

The Clipper chip

In 1993 the Clinton administration proposed the Clipper chip, a telecommunications device using the classified Skipjack cipher with a government-held escrowed key. Research by Matt Blaze demonstrated a flaw in the protocol, and the proposal collapsed by 1996 amid industry and public opposition.

"Crypto War 2.0"

Debate reignited in the 2010s as default end-to-end encryption spread to consumer messaging. The 2016 Apple–FBI encryption dispute, in which Apple refused a court order to help unlock an iPhone used by a mass shooter, became a landmark episode. Law-enforcement calls for "lawful access" or "exceptional access" continue, while most cryptographers argue that any backdoor necessarily weakens security for all users.
