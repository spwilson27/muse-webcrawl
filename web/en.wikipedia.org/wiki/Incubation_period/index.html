Incubation period

The incubation period is the time between exposure to a pathogenic organism, chemical, or radiation and the appearance of the first symptoms or signs of disease. During this interval the pathogen multiplies within the host; the host is usually not yet ill, but in some diseases may already be infectious before symptoms begin.

Typical values

Incubation periods vary widely between diseases. Common examples include about 1–4 days for influenza, roughly 2–14 days for COVID-19 (with a median near 5 days in early variants), 9–90 days for rabies depending on the bite site, and 6 days to 6 months for hepatitis B. Values are usually quoted as a range or as a median with percentiles, since incubation times in a population often follow a log-normal distribution.

Factors affecting incubation

The length of the incubation period depends on the dose of the pathogen, the route of entry, the rate of pathogen growth, and the host's immune status and age. A large inoculum generally shortens incubation, while partial immunity may lengthen it or prevent symptoms altogether, producing subclinical infection.

Public health significance

Knowledge of the incubation period underlies quarantine policy: contacts of cases are monitored for the maximum plausible incubation period, and health authorities use the distribution of incubation times to reconstruct when and where exposures occurred during outbreak investigations. The related concept of the latent period — the time until the host becomes infectious — determines whether pre-symptomatic transmission can occur.
