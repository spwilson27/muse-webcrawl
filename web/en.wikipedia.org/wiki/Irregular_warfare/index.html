Irregular warfare

Irregular warfare is warfare in which one or more combatants are irregular military — fighters who are not part of a state's regular armed forces — or in which regular forces employ unconventional methods. United States doctrine defines it as "a violent struggle among state and non-state actors for legitimacy and influence over the relevant population(s)". Irregular warfare favours indirect and asymmetric approaches, though it may employ the full range of military capabilities to erode an adversary's power, influence and will.

Characteristics

Irregular combatants typically avoid set-piece battle against superior conventional forces, instead relying on ambush, raids, sabotage, assassination, improvised explosive devices and propaganda — the classic repertoire of guerrilla warfare. The population, rather than terrain, is usually the centre of gravity: irregular forces seek to win or coerce popular support, draw legitimacy from grievances, and blend into civilian life. Conflicts are often protracted, since the weaker side can usually avoid decisive defeat by dispersal.

Forms

  • Insurgency — an organised movement to overthrow a government through subversion and armed conflict.
  • Terrorism — violence against civilians intended to coerce political outcomes through fear.
  • Unconventional warfare — operations by special forces to enable resistance movements in denied territory.
  • Foreign internal defence — support by one state to another's counterinsurgency.
  • Counterinsurgency (COIN) — the combined military, political and civic effort to defeat an insurgency, classically expounded by theorists such as David Galula and in the US Army/Marine Corps manual FM 3-24.

History

Irregular methods are as old as war: Scythian harassment of Darius, Iberian guerrillas (from whom the word guerrilla derives) against Napoleonic France, Boer commandos, and Arab irregulars under T. E. Lawrence. In the 20th century Mao Zedong codified protracted people's war, which was adapted by the Viet Minh and Viet Cong, Algerian FLN, Cuban revolutionaries and many others. Since 2001, the "war on terror", the Iraq and Afghanistan insurgencies, and conflicts involving groups such as the Taliban and Islamic State have made irregular warfare the dominant form of armed conflict, blurring into "hybrid" or "grey zone" strategies in which states use proxies, militias, information operations and deniable forces.

Legal status

The laws of armed conflict struggle to accommodate irregular fighters. The Geneva Conventions grant prisoner-of-war status to militias only if they have a responsible command, a fixed distinctive sign, openly carried arms and adherence to the laws of war; fighters failing these tests may be treated as unprivileged belligerents, though Common Article 3 guarantees minimum humane treatment in non-international conflicts.
