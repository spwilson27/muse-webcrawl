Civic republicanism

Civic republicanism is a tradition of political thought, descending from ancient Greece and Rome and revived in Renaissance Italy and early modern England and America, that centres on the republic as a form of government in which citizens actively participate in public life and cultivate civic virtue — the disposition to subordinate private interests to the common good. In contemporary philosophy, republicanism is often defined by freedom as non-domination: a person is unfree not only when actually interfered with, but when subject to another's arbitrary power.

Ancient origins

The tradition draws on Aristotle's Politics, with its mixed constitution and its idea of citizens who "rule and are ruled in turn"; on Polybius's account of Rome's balance of consuls, Senate, and people; and on Cicero's defence of the res publica (the public thing). Machiavelli's Discourses on Livy transmitted republican themes to early modern Europe, arguing that popular tumults and civic arms preserved Roman liberty.

Early modern and American republicanism

In seventeenth-century England, writers such as James Harrington, John Milton, and Algernon Sidney developed republican ideas against absolute monarchy; in the eighteenth century, Country-party critics of corruption and the authors of Cato's Letters carried them to America, where they deeply influenced the Founding generation. Historians of the "republican synthesis" — Bernard Bailyn, Gordon Wood, and J. G. A. Pocock, whose The Machiavellian Moment (1975) traced the tradition from Florence to America — argued that republicanism, not Lockean liberalism alone, shaped American political culture, with its fears of corruption, standing armies, and luxury, and its ideal of the independent, virtuous citizen.

Contemporary republicanism

Since the 1990s, Philip Pettit and Quentin Skinner have revived republicanism as a systematic philosophy distinct from liberalism. On Pettit's account, freedom as non-domination requires laws and institutions that cannot be wielded arbitrarily: the rule of law, separation of powers, and a contestatory citizenry that can challenge government decisions. Republican theory has influenced debates on workplace power, campaign finance, constitutional design, and even the theory of the European Union. Critics object that civic virtue is too demanding or too communitarian for pluralistic societies, and feminists have noted the tradition's historical exclusion of women and the propertyless from citizenship.
