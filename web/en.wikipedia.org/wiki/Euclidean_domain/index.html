Euclidean domain

In mathematics, more specifically in ring theory, a Euclidean domain (also called a Euclidean ring) is an integral domain that can be endowed with a Euclidean function which allows a suitable generalisation of the Euclidean division of integers. This generalised Euclidean algorithm can be put to many of the same uses as Euclid's original algorithm in the ring of integers: in any Euclidean domain, one can apply the Euclidean algorithm to compute the greatest common divisor of any two elements. In particular, the greatest common divisor of any two elements exists and can be written as a linear combination of them (Bézout's identity).

Definition

An integral domain R is a Euclidean domain if there exists a function f from the nonzero elements of R to the nonnegative integers, called a Euclidean function, such that for any two elements a and b in R with b nonzero, there exist elements q and r in R such that

a = bq + r,   with either r = 0 or f(r) < f(b).

Unlike the corresponding property for fields, the quotient and remainder need not be unique, though there may be only one possible choice if additional conditions are imposed.

Examples

The integers, with the Euclidean function given by the absolute value, form a Euclidean domain; this is the setting of ordinary division with remainder. The Gaussian integers, with the norm function, are likewise Euclidean. Any field is trivially a Euclidean domain. The ring of polynomials in one variable over a field, with the degree as Euclidean function, is a Euclidean domain. Certain rings of algebraic integers, such as the Eisenstein integers, are also Euclidean, though not all rings of integers of number fields have this property.

Properties

Every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain (PID): every ideal is generated by a single element, namely any element of the ideal with minimal Euclidean value. Consequently every Euclidean domain is a unique factorisation domain and a GCD domain. The converse does not hold: there exist principal ideal domains that are not Euclidean for any choice of Euclidean function. A standard example is the ring of integers of the number field Q(√−19).

Comparison

Euclidean domains sit within a hierarchy of commutative rings: fields ⊂ Euclidean domains ⊂ principal ideal domains ⊂ unique factorisation domains ⊂ GCD domains ⊂ integral domains. Each inclusion is strict.
