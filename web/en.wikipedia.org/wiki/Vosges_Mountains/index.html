Vosges

The Vosges (French: les Vosges; German: Vogesen) are a range of medium mountains in Eastern France, near its border with Germany. Together with the Palatine Forest to the north on the German side of the border, they form a single geomorphological unit. The range runs roughly north–northeast to south–southwest from the Saverne gap to the Belfort gap, separating the Lorraine plateau to the west from the Alsace plain and the Rhine valley to the east.

Geography

The highest point is the Grand Ballon (1,424 m), in the southern part of the range. The summits of the Higher Vosges are characteristically rounded, dome-shaped peaks known locally as ballons. The range is heavily forested, with fir and beech woods on the slopes and high pastures (chaumes) on the summits. The eastern slopes fall steeply to the Alsace plain, while the western side descends more gently toward Lorraine.

The Vosges were formed in the Paleozoic era and were elevated and shaped by the same tectonic forces that created the Rhine Rift Valley; the Black Forest across the Rhine is their geological counterpart. Much of the range is protected within two regional natural parks, the Ballons des Vosges and the Northern Vosges parks.

History

The Vosges formed part of the frontier between France and Germany after the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, when Alsace was annexed by the German Empire. The range saw heavy fighting during the First World War, notably at Hartmannswillerkopf (Vieil Armand). Cheese production, in particular Munster cheese, and tourism including skiing and hiking, are important to the local economy.
