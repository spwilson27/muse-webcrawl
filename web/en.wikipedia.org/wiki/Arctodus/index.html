Arctodus

Arctodus
Temporal range:Pliocene–Pleistocene (~2.5 million – 12,800 years ago)
Family:Ursidae
Tribe:Tremarctini (short-faced bears)
Species:A. simus, A. pristinus

Arctodus is an extinct genus of short-faced bears that inhabited North America during the Pliocene and Pleistocene epochs, from about 2.5 million years ago until roughly 12,800 years ago. Its two recognized species are the lesser short-faced bear (Arctodus pristinus) and the giant short-faced bear (Arctodus simus), among the largest terrestrial mammalian carnivores ever to have lived on the continent.

Description

Arctodus simus stood about 1.6 metres (5.2 ft) at the shoulder on all fours and could reach over 3.4 metres upright; large males are estimated at 800–1,000 kg. The genus takes its "short-faced" name from its deep, shortened muzzle, an adaptation that increased bite force at the front teeth. Its limbs were long and relatively slender, suggesting a more mobile, wide-ranging animal than modern bears.

Diet and ecology

The diet of Arctodus is debated. Early studies portrayed it as a hypercarnivorous predator or kleptoparasitic scavenger that stole kills from dire wolves and saber-toothed cats. Isotope analyses, however, indicate an omnivorous diet varying by region — highly carnivorous in Alaska and the Yukon (including reindeer and muskox), more mixed farther south. Its enormous size would have let it dominate carcasses and deter competitors.

Extinction

Arctodus ranged from Alaska to Mexico and coast to coast. It vanished at the end of the Pleistocene, about 12,800 years ago, amid the Quaternary extinction event that eliminated most North American megafauna — likely from a combination of climate-driven habitat change, collapsing prey populations, and possibly human hunting pressure. Its only surviving relative is the spectacled bear of South America.
