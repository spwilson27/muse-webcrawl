German General Staff

The German General Staff, originally the Prussian General Staff and officially the Great General Staff (German: Großer Generalstab), was a full-time body at the head of the Prussian Army and later the German Army, responsible for the continuous study of all aspects of war, and for drawing up and reviewing plans for mobilization and campaign. It was the world's first professional general staff and became the model copied by armies around the world.

Origins and development

The staff system emerged from the reforms that followed Prussia's catastrophic defeat by Napoleon at Jena–Auerstedt in 1806. Reformers such as Gerhard von Scharnhorst and August Neidhardt von Gneisenau created a merit-based officer corps, the Kriegsakademie for staff training, and a permanent staff to plan for future wars. The chief of staff system paired each commander with a professionally trained chief of staff who shared responsibility for decisions.

Under Helmuth von Moltke the Elder, chief of staff from 1857 to 1888, the Great General Staff came into its own. Exploiting railways and the telegraph and rigorous staff rides and war games, Moltke planned the victories over Denmark (1864), Austria (1866), and France (1870–71) that unified Germany. His doctrine emphasized mission-type orders (Auftragstaktik) — telling subordinates what to achieve, not how.

World Wars

Alfred von Schlieffen (chief 1891–1906) devised the famous Schlieffen Plan for a two-front war, executed in modified form by Helmuth von Moltke the Younger in 1914. In the First World War, under Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff, the General Staff effectively dominated German strategy and, by 1916–18, much of national policy. The Treaty of Versailles abolished the Great General Staff in 1919; it survived in disguised form as the Truppenamt within the Reichswehr.

In the Third Reich the staff system continued within the OKW and OKH, but Adolf Hitler progressively subordinated and bypassed the professional staff, taking personal command of the army in 1941. The General Staff produced both brilliant operational planning and the strategic overreach that ended in Germany's defeat in 1945, after which the institution was finally dissolved.

Influence

The German staff system — selected, rigorously educated staff officers rotated between staff and line duty, institutionalized planning, and historical study of campaigns — was adopted in some form by nearly every modern army, including those of the United States, Britain, Japan, and the Soviet Union.
