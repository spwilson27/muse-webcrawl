Battle of Horseshoe Bend (1814)

The Battle of Horseshoe Bend, fought on 27 March 1814 in present-day Alabama, was the decisive engagement of the Creek War. United States forces under Major General Andrew Jackson, together with Cherokee and Lower Creek allies, destroyed the Red Stick faction of the Creek (Muscogee) Nation encamped at Tohopeka, a fortified village inside a bend of the Tallapoosa River.

Background

The Creek War began as a civil war within the Creek Nation, inflamed by the Shawnee prophet movement of Tecumseh and by the Red Stick attack on Fort Mims in August 1813. Jackson's army of Tennessee militia, US regulars of the 39th Infantry, and allied Cherokee and Creek warriors advanced down the Tallapoosa in the spring of 1814 to the Red Stick stronghold at Tohopeka, where roughly 1,000 warriors had built a stout log breastwork across the neck of a 100-acre peninsula formed by a sharp bend in the river.

The battle

Jackson opened with a two-hour artillery bombardment that made little impression on the breastwork. Cherokee allies, meanwhile, swam the river, seized the Red Sticks' canoes, and attacked the village from the rear, setting it ablaze. Jackson then ordered a frontal assault; the 39th Infantry, led by Colonel John Williams and with Ensign Sam Houston among the first over the wall, carried the breastwork in fierce hand-to-hand fighting. The battle lasted until dark, and most of the Red Stick warriors were killed; some 557 bodies were counted on the peninsula, and many more died attempting to cross the river.

Aftermath

American losses were about 47 killed and 159 wounded, with additional casualties among their Native allies. The Red Stick movement was broken. In August 1814 Jackson imposed the Treaty of Fort Jackson, by which the Creek Nation — including Jackson's own allies — ceded some 23 million acres, roughly half of present-day Alabama and part of southern Georgia, to the United States. The victory made Jackson a national hero and launched his political career.
