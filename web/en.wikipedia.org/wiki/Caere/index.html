Caere (Etruscan: Caisra or Cisra; Greek: Ἄγυλλα, Agylla) was one of the twelve cities of the Etruscan League and one of the most important and populous centres of ancient Etruria. It was located near the modern town of Cerveteri, about 40 kilometres north-west of Rome, a few kilometres inland from the Tyrrhenian coast, to which it was connected by its ports of Pyrgi, Punicum and Alsium.

History

The site was inhabited from the Iron Age, and by the 7th century BC Caere had grown wealthy through trade with Greece, Phoenicia and the wider Mediterranean. The city is famous for its vast necropolises, particularly the Banditaccia necropolis, whose great rock-cut tumuli imitate the interiors of houses and preserve much of what is known of Etruscan domestic architecture and funerary practice. Gold tablets inscribed in Etruscan and Phoenician, dedicated by the ruler Thefarie Velianas around 500 BC, were found at Caere's port of Pyrgi and provide one of the longest bilingual texts for the partial decipherment of Etruscan.

Caere had a long and complex relationship with Rome. Tradition held that the sacred objects of Rome were sheltered at Caere during the Gallic sack of Rome in 390 BC, in gratitude for which the Caerites received special privileges. The city later became the first municipium sine suffragio, a status named the ius Caeritum. After revolts in the 4th century BC Caere lost territory and autonomy, and it gradually declined into a minor town in the Roman period.

Archaeology

The necropolises of Cerveteri, together with those of Tarquinia, were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2004. Notable tombs include the Tomb of the Reliefs, whose walls are decorated with stucco reliefs of household objects, and the Regolini-Galassi tomb, excavated in 1836, which yielded rich gold jewellery and bronzes.
