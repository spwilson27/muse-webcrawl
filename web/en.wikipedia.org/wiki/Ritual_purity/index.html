Ritual purity

Ritual purity is the state of being free from ritual impurity or pollution required by many religions for participation in worship, sacrifice, or entry into sacred spaces. Ritual impurity is usually distinct from moral wrongdoing: it may be contracted through ordinary natural events such as childbirth, menstruation, contact with a corpse, or certain diseases, and is removed by prescribed rites of purification — washing, bathing, sprinkling, waiting periods, or sacrifice.

In Judaism

The Hebrew Bible's laws of tumah and taharah (impurity and purity), chiefly in Leviticus and Numbers, made purity a precondition for entering the Tabernacle and later the Temple in Jerusalem and for eating consecrated food. Sources of impurity included corpses, childbirth, menstruation (niddah), abnormal discharges, and tzaraath (a skin condition). Purification typically involved immersion in living water — institutionalised in the mikveh (ritual bath) — and, for corpse impurity, sprinkling with water mixed with the ashes of the red heifer. After the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE, most purity laws lapsed in practice, though ritual hand-washing and mikveh observance remain significant in Orthodox Judaism.

In Islam

Islamic law (tahara) treats purity as a condition of valid prayer. Minor impurity (after sleep, urination, and similar events) is removed by wudu, the ritual washing of hands, mouth, nose, face, forearms, head and feet; major impurity (after sexual intercourse, menstruation or childbirth) requires ghusl, a full-body bath. Where water is unavailable, tayammum (dry ablution with clean earth) is permitted. Mosques consequently provide ablution facilities, and states of impurity such as najasa (impure substances, including blood and alcohol) are carefully regulated in jurisprudence.

Other traditions

In Hinduism, purity and pollution structure daily life and temple worship: bathing, especially in sacred rivers such as the Ganges, purifies; birth and death in the family impose periods of pollution; and concepts of purity historically reinforced caste boundaries. Shinto distinguishes kegare (impurity, associated with death and blood), removed by misogi water ablutions and harae rites performed with a wand of paper streamers. Zoroastrianism maintains elaborate purification ceremonies such as the bareshnūm, given its theology of opposing purity to the pollution of death. Similar ideas appear in ancient Greek religion, where miasma barred the polluted from sanctuaries.
