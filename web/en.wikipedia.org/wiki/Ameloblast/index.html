Ameloblast

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ameloblasts are cells present only during tooth development that deposit tooth enamel, which is the hard outermost layer of the tooth crown forming the surface of the crown. Each ameloblast is a columnar cell approximately 4 micrometers in diameter, 40 micrometers in length and hexagonal in cross section. The secretory end of the ameloblast forms a structure known as Tomes's process, a pyramid-like projection that is partly embedded in the forming enamel matrix.

Development

Ameloblasts arise from the inner enamel epithelium of the enamel organ, which develops from oral ectoderm. Their differentiation requires reciprocal signaling with the adjacent odontoblasts, which first deposit dentin; without this signal, ameloblasts do not fully differentiate, and without ameloblasts, dentin does not form properly.

Life cycle

The life cycle of ameloblasts is commonly divided into six stages: morphogenetic, differentiation (presecretory), secretory, transition, maturation, and protective. During the secretory stage the cells lay down enamel matrix proteins including amelogenin, enamelin, and ameloblastin. During maturation the cells modulate between ruffle-ended and smooth-ended forms while removing water and organic material and transporting calcium ions as the enamel crystals grow.

Loss after eruption

Once enamel formation is complete and the tooth erupts, the ameloblasts are lost (they partly become part of the junctional epithelium). Because enamel is therefore acellular, it cannot regenerate after destruction, which is why enamel damage is irreversible.
