Trust account

Funds in a trust account are held for the benefit of another party

A trust account is a bank or financial account in which funds are held by one party (the trustee or account holder) for the benefit of another. Trust accounts are used where a professional or fiduciary receives money belonging to clients or third parties and must keep it separate from their own funds.

Common uses

  • Law firms hold client money — such as settlements and retainers — in client trust accounts. In the United States, accounts for small or short-term sums are often IOLTA accounts (Interest on Lawyers' Trust Accounts), whose interest funds legal aid.
  • Real estate brokers and escrow agents hold earnest money deposits in trust accounts until a transaction closes.
  • Trustees of trusts manage the assets of an estate or family trust through trust accounts for the beneficiaries.
  • Totten trust accounts ("payable on death" accounts) are informal arrangements in which a depositor holds an account in trust for a named beneficiary.

Regulation

Trust accounts are typically subject to strict rules: client funds of different clients may sometimes be pooled but must be fully accounted for, commingling with personal or operating funds is prohibited, and misappropriation is a serious professional and criminal offence. Bar associations, regulators and auditors commonly require regular reconciliations and record-keeping.
