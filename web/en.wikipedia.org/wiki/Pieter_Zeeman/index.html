Pieter Zeeman (25 May 1865 – 9 October 1943) was a Dutch physicist who shared the 1902 Nobel Prize in Physics with Hendrik Lorentz for his discovery of the Zeeman effect: the splitting of spectral lines in the presence of a magnetic field.

Early life and education

Zeeman was born in Zonnemaire, a small town on the island of Schouwen-Duiveland in the Netherlands, the son of a Lutheran minister. He studied at Leiden University under Lorentz and Heike Kamerlingh Onnes, writing his doctoral dissertation on the Kerr effect, the reflection of polarized light from a magnetized surface.

The Zeeman effect

In 1896, working in Leiden, Zeeman observed that the spectral lines of a light source placed between the poles of a strong magnet were broadened and split. Lorentz's electron theory explained the phenomenon as the effect of the magnetic field on the motion of the charged particles — electrons — inside atoms. The discovery provided some of the first direct evidence for the existence of the electron and became a powerful tool in spectroscopy and astrophysics, allowing astronomers to measure magnetic fields in the Sun and stars.

Later career

Zeeman became professor of physics at the University of Amsterdam, where he built a well-equipped laboratory and continued precision measurements of magneto-optical phenomena, including work related to the propagation of light in moving media. His name is commemorated in the Zeeman effect, lunar and Martian craters, and streets throughout the Netherlands.
