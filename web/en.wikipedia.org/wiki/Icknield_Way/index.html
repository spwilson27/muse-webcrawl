Icknield Way

Length: approx. 110 miles (177 km)

Location: Southern England

Designation: Long-distance footpath / ancient trackway

The Icknield Way is an ancient trackway in southern and eastern England, running from Ivinghoe Beacon in Buckinghamshire to Knettishall Heath in Norfolk. It is generally considered to be one of the oldest roads in Great Britain, with sections possibly dating back to the Neolithic period. The name is Celto-British in origin and may derive from the Iceni tribe, whose territory the eastern end of the route traversed.

Route

The way follows the line of chalk hills — the Chilterns and the chalk escarpment of East Anglia — keeping to the spring line where water was available at the foot of the scarp. From Ivinghoe Beacon the route passes through Dunstable, Luton, Baldock, Royston and Newmarket before reaching Knettishall Heath near Thetford. Much of the historic trackway survives as the modern Icknield Way Path, a long-distance footpath of about 110 miles.

History

Antiquarians such as William Camden popularised the idea of the Icknield Way as one of the great prehistoric highways of Britain. Medieval records refer to the "Ikenild Strete" as one of the four royal roads of England mentioned in the Laws of Edward the Confessor, along with Watling Street, Ermine Street and the Fosse Way. Modern scholars caution that the route was probably never a single engineered road, but rather a corridor of parallel tracks used by travellers, herders and traders over many centuries.

Modern path

The Icknield Way Path was established in the late twentieth century and links with the Ridgeway National Trail at Ivinghoe Beacon and with the Peddars Way at Knettishall Heath, forming part of the Greater Ridgeway route to Lyme Regis. It is waymarked and passes sites including the chalk figure at Whipsnade and the Devil's Dyke near Newmarket.
