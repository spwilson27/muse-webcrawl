Head of state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A head of state is the public persona who officially embodies a state and represents its unity and legitimacy. Depending on the country and its form of government, the head of state may be the chief executive (as in presidential systems), or a largely ceremonial figurehead (as in most constitutional monarchies and many parliamentary republics). The head of state is normally contrasted with the head of government, who runs the administration — in parliamentary systems, the Prime Minister.

Roles and powers

Typical functions include representing the state internationally, receiving and accrediting ambassadors, signing legislation into law, appointing the head of government (often following election results), serving as commander-in-chief of the armed forces, granting pardons, and dissolving or summoning the legislature. Many such powers are exercised on ministerial advice in constitutional systems, though reserve powers may matter in crises — as with the dismissal of Australia's Whitlam government by the governor-general in 1975.

Types

Heads of state include hereditary monarchs (kings, queens, emperors), elected presidents, and collective heads such as Switzerland's seven-member Federal Council. The method of selection ranges from hereditary succession and election by parliament or electoral college to direct popular election. Titles and precedence are governed by constitutional law and diplomatic protocol, codified internationally in the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961).
