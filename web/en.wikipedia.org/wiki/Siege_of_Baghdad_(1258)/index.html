The Siege of Baghdad in 1258 was a decisive engagement of the Mongol conquest of the Islamic world. The Mongol army under Hulagu Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, besieged Baghdad, the capital of the Abbasid Caliphate, from 29 January to 10 February 1258. The city fell after a siege of less than two weeks, and the caliph al-Musta'sim was captured and executed, ending five centuries of the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad.

Background

Möngke Khan had charged his brother Hulagu with subjugating the Middle East. After destroying the Nizari Ismaili (Assassin) strongholds in Persia in 1256, Hulagu demanded the caliph's submission. Al-Musta'sim, badly advised by his vizier Ibn al-Alqami, refused and failed to prepare the city's defenses or pay his troops. Hulagu advanced with a huge multinational army, including Chinese siege engineers manning catapults and gunpowder weapons.

The siege

Mongol forces invested both banks of the Tigris, destroyed the caliph's field army on the eastern bank, flooded the Abbasid camp by breaching dikes, and bombarded the walls with catapults and arrows bearing messages. The eastern tower collapsed and the city was stormed on 10 February; al-Musta'sim surrendered. Hulagu allowed the caliph to order the population to lay down arms, after which a general massacre began.

Aftermath

Baghdad was systematically looted and destroyed over about a week. Contemporary sources give civilian death tolls ranging from 200,000 to a million; modern historians regard the true figure as uncertain but very large. The Grand Library of Baghdad, the House of Wisdom, was destroyed — later tradition says the Tigris ran black with ink and red with blood. Al-Musta'sim was executed, reportedly by being rolled in a carpet and trampled by horses, so that his royal blood would not touch the earth. The destruction of irrigation works devastated Mesopotamian agriculture for generations.

Baghdad's fall shocked the Islamic world; the Mongols advanced into Syria in 1260 before being checked by the Mamluks at the Battle of Ain Jalut. The Abbasid line survived only as a shadow caliphate under Mamluk protection in Cairo.
