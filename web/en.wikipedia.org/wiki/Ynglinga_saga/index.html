Ynglinga saga

Ynglinga saga is the first saga in Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla (c. 1230), a history of the kings of Norway. It recounts the origins and early history of the Ynglings, the legendary dynasty from which the Norwegian kings claimed descent, following them from the mythical age of the Norse gods down to the historical king Halfdan the Black in the 9th century.

Sources

Snorri based the saga largely on the earlier genealogical poem Ynglingatal, attributed to the 9th-century skald Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, which lists some thirty generations of Yngling kings and records each one's manner of death and burial place. Snorri quotes the poem extensively and expands its terse verses into prose narrative, supplemented by other poems and traditions.

Content

The saga opens with euhemeristic accounts of Odin, Njord, and Freyr as human rulers who migrated from the east (Ásgarðr placed near the Tanais/Don) to Scandinavia and were worshipped as gods after death. Freyr — also called Yngvi-Freyr — founded the dynasty at Uppsala in Sweden. Subsequent chapters trace the line through figures such as Fjölnir, who drowned in a vat of mead; Sveigðir, who vanished into a stone chasing a dwarf; Vanlandi, trampled by a nightmare (mara); Dómaldi, sacrificed by the Swedes for good harvests; and the feuding kings Alrek and Eirik, and Yngvi and Alf, who killed each other. The dynasty eventually migrates to Norway, ending with the ancestors of Harald Fairhair.

Historical value

Modern scholars treat the early chapters as legend rather than history, but the saga preserves some of the richest surviving material on pre-Christian Scandinavian religion, kingship, sacrifice, and burial customs. It strongly influenced later Scandinavian historiography and national romanticism in Norway and Sweden.
