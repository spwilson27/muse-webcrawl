Statute

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A statute is a formal written enactment of a legislative authority that governs a state, city or country. Typically, statutes command or prohibit something, or declare policy. Statutes are rules made by legislative bodies; they are distinguished from case law (created by courts) and from regulations issued by executives under statutory authority (delegated or secondary legislation).

Enactment and interpretation

A statute begins as a bill, passes through readings and committee stages in the legislature, and enters into force upon assent and promulgation. Over time statutes accumulate into codes in civil-law countries; common-law countries organise them by subject in revised compilations (e.g. the United States Code). Because texts cannot anticipate every case, courts practise statutory interpretation — literal, purposive and other approaches, aided by presumptions, legislative history and, in multilingual systems, parallel texts. In constitutional systems statutes rank below the constitution: courts may invalidate those that conflict with it, or declare incompatibility where sovereignty doctrines forbid invalidation.
