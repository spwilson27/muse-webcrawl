Strict conditional

Possible-worlds semantics: a strict conditional holds when the consequent holds in every accessible world where the antecedent does.

In logic, a strict conditional (also called a strict implication) is a conditional statement governed by a modal operator. It was introduced by C. I. Lewis in his work on modal logic (1912–1918) as an alternative to the material conditional of classical logic, in order to avoid the paradoxes of material implication — for instance, that a false proposition materially implies anything, and that a true proposition is implied by anything.

Definition

Where A ⥽ B is read "A strictly implies B," it is defined using the necessity operator □ and material implication ⊃:

A ⥽ B = □(A ⊃ B)

Equivalently, by modal duality, A ⥽ B means ¬◇(A ∧ ¬B): it is impossible for the antecedent to be true while the consequent is false. In possible-worlds semantics (Kripke semantics), A ⥽ B is true at a world w just in case, at every world accessible from w where A is true, B is also true.

Motivation and properties

Lewis argued that ordinary "if … then" claims often carry this modal force: "if the switch is on, the light is on" asserts a connection that holds across relevant possibilities, not merely that the two are not in fact mismatched. The strict conditional is stronger than the material conditional: strict implication validates modus ponens and transitivity, but it has its own paradoxes — the paradoxes of strict implication: a necessarily false proposition strictly implies everything, and everything strictly implies a necessary truth. It also fails contraposition-like intuitions in some modal systems and does not capture counterfactuals ("if it had rained, the ground would have been wet") well, which led to the later development of variably strict (comparative-similarity) conditionals by Stalnaker and David Lewis.

Related notions

  • The strict conditional underlies Lewis's modal systems S1–S5.
  • It is central to the analysis of entailment and logical consequence in modal terms.
  • Relevance logic was developed partly in response to the paradoxes of both material and strict implication.
