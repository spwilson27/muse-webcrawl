Antoine-Joseph "Adolphe" Sax (6 November 1814 – 7 February 1894) was a Belgian instrument maker and musician who invented the saxophone in the early 1840s, patenting it in Paris in 1846. He also invented the saxotromba, saxhorn, and saxtuba families of brass instruments.

Early life

Sax was born in Dinant, in what is now Belgium, the son of instrument maker Charles-Joseph Sax. He survived an extraordinary series of childhood accidents — falling from a height, swallowing a pin, drinking sulphuric acid, and nearly drowning — prompting his mother to remark that he was "a child condemned to misfortune". He began making instruments as a teenager and studied flute and clarinet at the Royal Conservatory of Brussels.

Career in Paris

Sax moved to Paris in 1842, bringing a new instrument that combined a single reed and clarinet-like mouthpiece with a conical brass body. Hector Berlioz championed his work, and in 1845 Sax's instruments won a celebrated "battle of the bands" against traditional French military ensembles, leading to their adoption by the French army. He patented the saxophone family in 1846, designing fourteen sizes from sopranino to subcontrabass.

Rival makers repeatedly challenged his patents and drove him into bankruptcy three times, but he continued working, taught the first saxophone class at the Paris Conservatoire from 1857, and published a method for his instrument.

Legacy

Although conceived for military and classical music, the saxophone became the defining instrument of jazz in the 20th century. Sax's hometown of Dinant commemorates him with a museum, statues, and saxophone-decorated streetscape. He died in Paris in 1894, poor and largely forgotten, before his invention's greatest fame arrived.
