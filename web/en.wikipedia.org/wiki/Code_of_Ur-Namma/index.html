Code of Ur-Namma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Date: c. 2100–2050 BC
Author: Attributed to Ur-Namma of Ur (possibly his son Shulgi)
Status: Oldest surviving law code

The Code of Ur-Namma is the oldest known surviving law code, written in Sumerian on clay tablets around 2100–2050 BC during the Third Dynasty of Ur. It is attributed to King Ur-Namma of Ur, though some scholars suggest it may have been issued by his son Shulgi. It predates the famous Code of Hammurabi by roughly three centuries.

Discovery and contents

Fragments were first found at Nippur and translated by Samuel Kramer in 1952; further copies come from Ur and Sippar. About 57 laws can be at least partly reconstructed. The text opens with a prologue in which Ur-Namma describes establishing equity in the land, protecting widows and orphans, and standardizing weights and measures.

The laws follow the casuistic "if… then…" pattern typical of ancient Near Eastern codes and cover murder, robbery, false accusation, sexual offences, marriage and divorce, slavery and fugitive slaves, injuries, agriculture, irrigation damage, and river ordeal tests for accusations such as sorcery or adultery.

Notable features

Unlike Hammurabi's later code, which is famous for lex talionis ("an eye for an eye"), Ur-Namma's code generally imposes monetary fines for bodily injuries: for example, severing a foot cost ten shekels of silver and breaking a bone fifteen shekels, with higher fines for injuries to the nose. Capital punishment was retained for murder, robbery, and certain sexual offences.

Legacy

The code influenced later Mesopotamian collections, including the Laws of Eshnunna and the Code of Lipit-Ishtar, and ultimately the Code of Hammurabi. Only about 40 of its provisions are fully legible across the surviving fragments.
