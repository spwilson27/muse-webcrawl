Elis or Elea (Ancient Greek: Ἦλις, Ēlis; in the local dialect Ϝᾶλις, Wālis) is an ancient district in Greece that corresponds to the modern regional unit of Elis. It lies in the western part of the Peloponnese peninsula, bounded by Achaea to the north, Arcadia to the east, Messenia to the south, and the Ionian Sea to the west. Elis is celebrated above all as the home of Olympia, the sanctuary of Zeus where the ancient Olympic Games were held.

Geography

The region consists largely of a fertile coastal plain watered by the rivers Peneus and Alpheus, making it one of the richest agricultural areas of ancient Greece. It was famous for its horse breeding and cattle. The plain was divided into districts including Hollow Elis, Pisatis — the territory around Olympia — and Triphylia in the south.

History

The city of Elis was founded, according to tradition, around 471 BC by synoecism, when the surrounding villages were united into a single polis. The Eleans administered the sanctuary at Olympia and supervised the Olympic festival, a role that gave the small state outsized prestige in the Greek world. Disputes over Olympia with neighbouring Pisa and later with Arcadia punctuated Elis's history. Elis allied variously with Athens, Sparta, and Thebes across the classical period, and after the Battle of Leuctra (371 BC) it lost control of Triphylia and its subject towns. Under Roman rule Elis remained notable chiefly for the continued celebration of the Olympic Games, which endured until the late 4th century AD.

Olympia

The sanctuary of Zeus at Olympia, dominated by the great temple housing Phidias's colossal gold-and-ivory statue of Zeus — one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World — drew competitors and spectators from across the Greek world every four years. The Eleans trained judges (the Hellanodikai), maintained the sacred truce (ekecheiria), and prepared the athletes during a month of training at Elis before the festival.
