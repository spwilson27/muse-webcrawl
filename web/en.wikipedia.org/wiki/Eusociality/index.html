Eusociality

Eusociality
CriteriaCooperative brood care; overlapping generations; reproductive division of labour
Classic examplesAnts, bees, wasps, termites
Other examplesNaked mole-rat, Damaraland mole-rat, some shrimp, aphids, thrips, ambrosia beetles

Eusociality (from Greek eu, "good/true", and social) is the highest level of organization of sociality recognized by sociobiologists. A eusocial group is classically defined by three traits: individuals cooperate in caring for juveniles; generations overlap within the colony; and there is a reproductive division of labour, with largely or wholly sterile members working on behalf of a few reproductive individuals. Colonies function in many respects like a single "superorganism."

Distribution

Eusociality is best known in the Hymenoptera — all ants and some bees and wasps — and in termites, which evolved it independently. It has also evolved in ambrosia beetles, some aphids and thrips, snapping shrimp (Synalpheus), and, uniquely among mammals, in the naked mole-rat and Damaraland mole-rat. Some estimates credit eusocial insects, though a minority of species, with a very large share of total insect biomass.

Evolution

W. D. Hamilton's theory of inclusive fitness (1964) explained the evolution of sterile castes through kin selection: workers can pass on their genes by helping relatives reproduce. The haplodiploidy of Hymenoptera, which makes sisters unusually closely related, was long emphasized but cannot be the whole explanation, since diploid termites are also eusocial; current accounts stress monogamy of the ancestral species, ecology (defensible nests and food), and the lifetime relatedness structure of colonies. A 2010 paper by Nowak, Tarnita, and E. O. Wilson challenging the necessity of inclusive-fitness explanations provoked a major controversy in evolutionary biology.
