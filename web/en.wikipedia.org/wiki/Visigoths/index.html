The Visigoths (Latin: Visigothi) were a Germanic people united under the rule of a king and living within the Roman Empire during late antiquity. Together with the Ostrogoths they formed the two main branches of the Goths. The Visigoths crossed into Roman territory fleeing the Huns, defeated the Romans at Adrianople in 378, and under Alaric I sacked Rome in 410. They later founded a kingdom first in southern Gaul and then in Hispania, which lasted until the Muslim conquest of 711.

Origins and entry into the Empire

The Goths emerged along the lower Danube and Black Sea region. In 376, pressed by the Huns, large Gothic groups under Fritigern were admitted across the Danube into the Eastern Roman Empire. Mistreatment by Roman officials led to revolt, culminating in the Battle of Adrianople (378), where the emperor Valens was killed. Under Alaric, the Visigoths wandered through the Balkans and Italy, sacking Rome in August 410 — an event that shocked the Mediterranean world.

The Visigothic Kingdom

In 418 the Visigoths were settled as foederati in Aquitaine, with their capital at Toulouse. King Euric (466–484) expanded the kingdom across much of Hispania and Gaul and issued an early law code. Defeat by the Franks at Vouillé in 507 cost the Visigoths most of Gaul, and the kingdom refocused on Spain with its capital at Toledo. King Leovigild consolidated royal power, and his son Reccared's conversion from Arianism to Catholic Christianity in 589 at the Third Council of Toledo united Goths and Hispano-Romans religiously. The Liber Iudiciorum (Visigothic Code) of 654 applied a single law to all subjects.

Fall

In 711, amid a disputed succession, Muslim forces under Tariq ibn Ziyad defeated King Roderic at the Battle of Guadalete. Within a few years most of the peninsula was under Umayyad control, ending the Visigothic Kingdom, though its legal and cultural legacy persisted in medieval Spain.
