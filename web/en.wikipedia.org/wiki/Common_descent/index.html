Common descent

Common descent is a concept in evolutionary biology which proposes that groups of organisms share a common ancestor. In modern biology, it is generally accepted that all living organisms on Earth descend from a single common ancestor population known as the last universal common ancestor (LUCA), which lived roughly 3.5 to 3.8 billion years ago. Charles Darwin proposed the theory of universal common descent through an evolutionary process in On the Origin of Species (1859), writing that "all the organic beings which have ever lived on this earth have descended from some one primordial form, into which life was first breathed."

Evidence

Darwin marshaled evidence from comparative anatomy — homologous structures such as the pentadactyl limb of vertebrates — embryology, biogeography, and the fossil record. The 20th century added powerful molecular evidence: all known life uses the same basic genetic code and the same machinery of DNA, RNA, and protein synthesis, and shares core metabolic pathways and molecules such as ATP. Phylogenetic trees built independently from morphology and from molecular sequences agree to a remarkable degree, and the nested hierarchy of species similarities is a strong, testable prediction of common descent. Other evidence includes shared endogenous retroviruses and pseudogenes at corresponding positions in different species' genomes, the fusion pattern visible in human chromosome 2 (matching two ape chromosomes), and observed instances of speciation and transitional fossils.

Concepts

The most recent common ancestor of any two species is the newest ancestral population or individual from which both descend, a notion applied from pairs of species up to the LUCA of all extant life. Carl Woese's three-domain system, based on ribosomal RNA comparisons, places LUCA at the root of the Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. Common descent is the foundation of phylogenetics and of the modern evolutionary synthesis.
