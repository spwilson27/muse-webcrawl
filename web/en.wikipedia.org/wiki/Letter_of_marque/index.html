Letter of marque

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A letter of marque and reprisal is a government license in the Age of Sail that authorized a private person, known as a privateer or corsair, to attack and capture vessels of a nation at war with the issuing state, and to bring them before admiralty courts for condemnation and sale as prizes. The letter distinguished the holder from a pirate: a privateer acted legally under his sovereign's commission, whereas a pirate acted without authority and could be hanged by any state that caught him.

Origins and history

The term "marque" derives from a medieval practice in which a subject who had suffered losses abroad could obtain letters authorizing him to cross the border (the "march" or marque) and seize property of the offending nation by way of reprisal. By the early modern period, letters of marque had become standard instruments of naval warfare, allowing states to multiply their naval power cheaply: private shipowners armed vessels at their own expense in exchange for a share of captured prizes. During the American Revolutionary War, the War of 1812, and other conflicts, American and European privateers captured thousands of merchant vessels.

Legal process

A privateer was required to bring captured vessels ("prizes") into port, where an admiralty court determined whether the capture was lawful. If the prize was condemned, vessel and cargo were sold and the proceeds divided among the ship's owners, captain, and crew according to pre-agreed shares. Privateers were typically required to post bond for good behaviour and to treat prisoners according to the laws of war; exceeding the terms of the commission could forfeit legal protection.

Abolition and survival

The Declaration of Paris of 1856, signed by most major maritime powers at the end of the Crimean War, abolished privateering among its signatories. The United States did not sign but has generally observed the principle, though the U.S. Constitution still grants Congress the power "to grant Letters of Marque and Reprisal" (Article I, Section 8). Occasional proposals to revive the mechanism — including against terrorists and pirates in the 21st century — have been introduced in Congress but never enacted.
