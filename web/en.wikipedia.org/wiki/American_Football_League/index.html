American Football League

The American Football League (AFL) was a major professional American football league that operated from 1960 to 1970, when it merged with the older National Football League (NFL). Founded by Lamar Hunt and other businessmen spurned in efforts to obtain NFL franchises — the self-deprecating "Foolish Club" — the AFL established itself through aggressive player signings, major television contracts, and an entertaining, pass-oriented style. Its ten teams became the core of the NFL's American Football Conference.

History

The AFL debuted in 1960 with eight teams including the Dallas Texans, Houston Oilers, and Boston Patriots. A lucrative NBC television contract from 1965 and the signing of college stars — most famously Joe Namath of the New York Jets, signed for a record $427,000 contract in 1965 — allowed the AFL to challenge the NFL directly. A costly bidding war prompted the 1966 AFL–NFL merger agreement, a common draft from 1967, and an annual championship game that became the Super Bowl.

Super Bowls

NFL champions Green Bay won the first two AFL–NFL championship games, but Namath's Jets stunned the Baltimore Colts in Super Bowl III (January 1969), legitimising the AFL; the Kansas City Chiefs won Super Bowl IV in the league's final game before the merger took full effect in 1970.

Innovations and legacy

The AFL pioneered or popularised the two-point conversion (adopted by the NFL only in 1994), names on jerseys, shared gate and television revenue among teams, and greater openness to Black players recruited from historically Black colleges. All ten AFL franchises survive in the AFC.
