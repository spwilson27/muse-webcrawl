Ring shout

The ring shout is an ecstatic, transcendent religious ritual, first practised by enslaved African Americans in the South, in which worshippers move in a circle while shuffling and stomping their feet and clapping their hands. It is regarded as one of the oldest surviving African American performance traditions.

Practice

Participants — "shouters" — move counterclockwise in a ring to call-and-response singing led by songsters, accompanied by hand clapping, stick beating, and stick-pounding rhythms, without lifting or crossing the feet (which would have been considered dancing, forbidden by some denominations). Sessions could continue for hours, sometimes all night, reaching intense spiritual states.

History and survival

With roots in West and Central African circle dances and ring ceremonies, the shout flourished in praise houses on plantations and after emancipation, especially among Gullah Geechee communities of coastal South Carolina and Georgia. The McIntosh County Shouters of Georgia became its best-known contemporary practitioners, recognised as National Heritage Fellows. Scholars see the ring shout as foundational to later African American music including spirituals, gospel, jazz, and the blues.
