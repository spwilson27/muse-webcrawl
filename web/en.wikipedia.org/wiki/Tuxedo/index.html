Tuxedo

A tuxedo (American English), dinner suit, dinner jacket (British English), or simply DJ, is a suit of evening formal wear that is distinguished primarily by satin or grosgrain facings on the jacket's lapels and buttons, and a matching stripe along the outer seam of the trousers. It is typically black or midnight blue and is traditionally worn with a white dress shirt, a black bow tie, a waistcoat or cummerbund, and black patent leather shoes.

History

The dinner jacket originated in the 1860s–1880s as a less formal alternative to the tailcoat for evenings at country houses. In 1865 the Prince of Wales (later Edward VII) had a short evening jacket made by his Savile Row tailor Henry Poole & Co. The garment reached the United States in the 1880s, where it acquired the name "tuxedo" after Tuxedo Park, an exclusive colony in New York where it was famously worn — according to the best-known account, by the tobacco magnate Griswold Lorillard at the Autumn Ball of October 1886 — quickly spreading among wealthy younger men as semi-formal evening dress.

Etiquette and usage

The tuxedo is the standard outfit for the dress code known as black tie, used for evening weddings, formal dinners, balls, and award ceremonies such as the Academy Awards. It is less formal than white tie but more formal than a lounge suit. Traditional rules hold that the jacket should be single- or double-breasted with peaked or shawl lapels, never a notch lapel; the bow tie should be hand-tied; and accessories such as a cummerbund or low-cut waistcoat cover the waistline.

Variations

Variants include the white (ivory) dinner jacket for warm-weather and tropical events, jackets in midnight blue (considered "blacker than black" under artificial light), and modern slim-cut and coloured versions. Critics of the white dinner jacket have long debated its propriety outside the tropics, but it became iconic through films such as Casablanca and the James Bond series.
