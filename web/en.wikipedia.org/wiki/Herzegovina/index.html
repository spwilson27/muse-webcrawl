Herzegovina (Bosnian, Croatian and Serbian: Hercegovina, Херцеговина) is the southern, predominantly mountainous region of Bosnia and Herzegovina. It has no official status as an administrative division in its own right, but together with Bosnia it forms one of the two historical and geographical regions of the country. Its largest city is Mostar, famous for the Stari Most (Old Bridge) over the Neretva River.

Geography

Herzegovina covers roughly 11,000 square kilometres in the south of the country. The terrain is dominated by the karst limestone of the Dinaric Alps, with deep river canyons carved by the Neretva and its tributaries. The climate ranges from Mediterranean near the Adriatic coast — Herzegovina borders the sea only briefly at Neum — to continental in the highlands. Important towns besides Mostar include Trebinje, Čapljina, Konjic, Široki Brijeg, Ljubuški and Stolac.

History

The name derives from the title herzog (German for "duke") adopted in 1448 by the Bosnian magnate Stjepan Vukčić Kosača, making the region the "duchy" (hercegovina) of St. Stephen. Herzegovina fell to the Ottoman Empire in the late 15th century and remained under Ottoman rule for four centuries. After the Congress of Berlin in 1878 it was occupied, and in 1908 annexed, by Austria-Hungary. Following the First World War it became part of Yugoslavia, and since 1992 it has formed part of independent Bosnia and Herzegovina.

Demographics and culture

The population is a mixture of Bosniaks, Croats and Serbs, with Croats forming a plurality in western Herzegovina and Bosniaks concentrated around Mostar and the upper Neretva valley. The region is known for viticulture (Žilavka and Blatina wines), tobacco, the pilgrimage site of Međugorje, and the medieval stećci tombstones, several necropolises of which are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
