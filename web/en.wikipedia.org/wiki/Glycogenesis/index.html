Glycogenesis


Glycogen, the branched storage polymer synthesised in glycogenesis.

Glycogenesis is the biological process by which glucose molecules are added to chains of glycogen for storage. It occurs principally in the liver and skeletal muscle, is activated in the fed state in response to insulin and high blood glucose, and builds the highly branched glycogen granules that serve as the body's rapidly mobilisable glucose reserve. The reverse process — breakdown of glycogen — is glycogenolysis; glycogenesis should not be confused with gluconeogenesis, the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors.

Steps of the pathway

Glucose entering a cell is phosphorylated by hexokinase (or glucokinase in liver) to glucose 6-phosphate, which is converted to glucose 1-phosphate by phosphoglucomutase. UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase then forms uridine diphosphate glucose (UDP-glucose), the activated donor of glucose units. Because glycogen synthase can only extend an existing chain, initiation requires the primer protein glycogenin, which autoglycosylates itself with the first few glucose residues. Glycogen synthase then extends the chain with α(1→4) glycosidic bonds, and the branching enzyme (amylo-α(1:4)→α(1:6) transglycosylase) transfers short segments to create α(1→6) branch points, producing the characteristic tiered, tree-like granule with many non-reducing ends for rapid synthesis and mobilisation.

Regulation

Glycogen synthase is the rate-limiting, most heavily regulated enzyme of the pathway. It is inactivated by phosphorylation — catalysed by kinases including glycogen synthase kinase 3, protein kinase A and phosphorylase kinase — and reactivated when insulin triggers dephosphorylation by protein phosphatase 1. Glucose 6-phosphate also allosterically activates the enzyme. Glucagon (in liver) and adrenaline suppress glycogenesis while stimulating glycogenolysis, ensuring the two pathways are reciprocally controlled. Defects in the enzymes of glycogen synthesis and breakdown cause the glycogen storage diseases, such as Andersen disease (branching enzyme deficiency) and glycogen storage disease type 0 (glycogen synthase deficiency).
