Tandem chess


A tandem chess (bughouse) game in progress

Tandem chess, more commonly known as bughouse chess (and by many other names, including transfer chess, exchange chess, Siamese chess, and swap chess), is a popular chess variant played on two chessboards by four players in two teams. Normal chess rules apply, except that captured pieces on one board are passed on to teammates on the other board, who then have the option of putting them on their own board.

The short time controls in tandem chess, usually between three and five minutes per player, along with the ability for players to drop pieces, render it a fast-moving, chaotic variant of the game. The game is traditional at scholastic chess clubs and in informal settings, and is played by many grandmasters casually.

Rules

Tandem chess is played by two teams of two players each, sitting side by side. One member of each team plays the white pieces on one board, while their partner plays black on the second board. When a player captures an opponent's piece, they hand it to their partner. The partner may, instead of making a normal move, "drop" the received piece onto any vacant square of their own board, subject to restrictions: pawns may not be dropped on the first or eighth rank, and a dropped pawn may later promote, in which case it reverts to a pawn in the hands of the opposing team when captured. A player may not drop a piece to deliver checkmate in some house rules, though the FIDE-recognised informal rules generally allow it.

Partners may communicate strategy, for example requesting a specific piece ("Knight, please!") or asking the partner to stall or hurry. Because a player may wait for a needed piece, stalling tactics and clock management are central skills. The game ends when either board reaches checkmate or a flag fall; the result on one board decides the whole match.

Names and history

The variant's many names reflect its chaotic character: "bughouse" in the United States, allegedly because the fast play and shouted requests make the room resemble a madhouse; "tandem chess" in the United Kingdom and Germany (Tandemschach); "Siamese chess" and "exchange chess" elsewhere. The game appears to have emerged in the early 1960s and spread through chess clubs and school programs, especially in the United States, where the US Chess Federation has long held national scholastic bughouse side events.

Strategy

Strong tandem play emphasises king safety, because dropped pieces can appear anywhere and open lines are doubly dangerous. Attacks often aim to force the opponent to consume defensive pieces, while material sacrifices on one board are sound if they supply a teammate with a decisive piece. Communication, rapid calculation, and willingness to sacrifice for tempo distinguish expert teams.
