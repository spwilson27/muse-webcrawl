Muism (Korean: 무교, Mugyo, "the religion of the mu") is the indigenous shamanic religion of Korea, also called Musok (무속, "shamanic customs") and, by some modern adherents, Korean shamanism. It is a polytheistic, ritual-centred tradition without scriptures, creeds or central organisation, transmitted through hereditary and charismatic practitioners — the mudang — who mediate between human communities and a crowded spirit world of gods, nature spirits and ancestors.

Beliefs and practice

Muism addresses the practical fortunes of life: health, prosperity, fertility, safe voyages, success in examinations and the peaceful settlement of the dead. Its central rite, the gut, is a festive, theatrical ceremony in which the mudang, wearing the costumes of successive gods, sings, dances, makes offerings and delivers the gods' messages to the sponsors. Deities range from the supreme heavenly god Haneullim and the culture-hero founder Dangun to mountain gods (sansin), dragon kings of the sea, the Seven Stars, and a suite of household gods governing the kitchen, the doorway and childbirth.

History and modern status

Shamanic practice in Korea is ancient, attested in early records of festivals and spirit mediumship, and Muism long coexisted with Buddhism, Confucianism and Daoism, absorbing elements of each. The Confucian Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) officially suppressed it and pushed mudang to the margins of society, and the Japanese colonial period and rapid modernisation further stigmatised it — yet it survived as the private religion of households, especially of women. In contemporary South Korea hundreds of thousands of practitioners serve a large clientele, major gut traditions are protected as Important Intangible Cultural Properties, and Muism is increasingly studied and presented as a core of Korean cultural identity.
