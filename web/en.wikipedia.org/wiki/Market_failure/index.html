Market failure

[Image: a factory chimney emitting smoke over a town, illustrating the negative externality of pollution]

Market failure is the situation in which the free allocation of goods and services by a Market is not efficient — the market produces too much, too little, or the wrong mix. The concept organises most of microeconomic policy analysis: where the assumptions behind the efficiency of competitive markets fail, there is a prima facie case (never a guarantee) that intervention could improve outcomes.

Sources

The standard sources are: market power (Monopoly and Oligopoly pricing above cost); externalities — costs or benefits imposed on third parties, such as pollution, analysed by Pigou and Coase; public goods that are non-excludable and non-rival, like national defence and lighthouses, which invite free-riding; information asymmetry (Akerlof's 'lemons', adverse selection, moral hazard); and incomplete markets, coordination failures and macroeconomic instability. Remedies range from Pigouvian taxes and tradable permits to regulation, public provision and liability rules. The symmetric concept of government failure — capture, ignorance, perverse incentives — warns that intervention may do worse, the central tension in applied welfare economics.
