Bird vocalisation

Bird vocalisation includes both bird calls and bird songs. In non-technical use, bird songs are the bird sounds that are melodious to the human ear. In ornithology and birding, (relatively complex) songs are distinguished by function from (relatively simple) calls. Birds produce sound primarily with the syrinx, a vocal organ unique to birds located at the base of the trachea, which unlike the mammalian larynx can produce two different sounds simultaneously.

Songs and calls

A song is typically a longer, more complex vocalisation associated with courtship, mate attraction, and territorial defense, most often produced by males during the breeding season. A call is usually a shorter, simpler sound used year-round by both sexes for functions such as alarm, flight coordination, and maintaining contact within a flock. The distinction is a continuum, and usage varies between species and authorities.

Production

The syrinx contains vibrating membranes whose tension is controlled by syringeal muscles; songbirds (oscines) have particularly elaborate musculature, enabling their complex songs. Because the syrinx has two sides, each independently controlled, some species can produce two notes at once or rapidly alternate pitch. Air sacs provide the steady pressure required. In a few species the trachea is elongated or coiled, lowering the resonance of vocalisations.

Learning

Oscine songbirds learn their songs during a critical period early in life, through memorization of adult "tutor" songs followed by a practice phase of subsong and plastic song (analogous to human infant babbling) until the song crystallizes. This vocal learning — rare among animals — has made birdsong a major model system for the neuroscience of learning and for the evolution of human language, including study of the FOXP2 gene. Calls, by contrast, are largely innate in most species. Some birds, such as parrots and hummingbirds, also learn vocalisations, and mimics like the superb lyrebird and northern mockingbird incorporate sounds of other species and even human-made noises.

Function and study

Birdsong functions in mate choice — females of many species prefer larger repertoires, which may honestly signal male quality — and in repelling rival males from territories. Alarm calls can encode information about predator type; some species understand the alarm calls of others. Scientists study vocalisations using sonograms (spectrograms), and recordings underpin citizen-science projects and automated identification tools such as Merlin Bird ID.
