Debye

Peter J. W. Debye (1884–1966), the physicist commemorated by the unit.

The debye (symbol: D) is a unit of electric dipole moment used in atomic and molecular physics and in chemistry. It is named after the Dutch-American physicist Peter J. W. Debye. Although it is not an SI unit, it remains the standard unit for reporting the dipole moments of molecules, because typical molecular dipoles are of the order of one debye.

Definition

One debye is defined as 10−18 statcoulomb-centimetre in the Gaussian (cgs) system of units. In SI units, 1 D = 3.33564 × 10−30 coulomb-metre (C·m), approximately. Historically, the debye was defined as the dipole moment produced by two charges of opposite sign of magnitude 10−10 statcoulomb separated by one ångström — a model corresponding roughly to an electronic charge displaced by a fraction of an atomic diameter, which is why dipole moments of polar molecules fall conveniently in the range of a few debye.

Typical values

A water molecule has a dipole moment of about 1.85 D, while hydrogen chloride is about 1.08 D and carbon monoxide only about 0.11 D. The large ionic compound sodium chloride, treated as an ion pair in the gas phase, has a dipole moment near 9 D. Measurement of dipole moments was historically important in establishing molecular geometry, since symmetric molecules such as carbon dioxide have zero dipole moment despite containing polar bonds.
