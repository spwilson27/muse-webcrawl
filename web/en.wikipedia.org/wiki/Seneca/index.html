Lucius Annaeus Seneca (c. 4 BC – AD 65), known as Seneca the Younger, was a Roman Stoic philosopher, statesman and dramatist, and the leading intellectual of Nero's Rome. Son of Seneca the Elder, a famous rhetorician, he rose in the Senate, was exiled to Corsica by Claudius for alleged adultery (AD 41), recalled in 49 to tutor the young Nero, and on Nero's accession (54) became, with the praetorian commander Burrus, the empire's effective co-ruler during the celebrated first five years (the quinquennium Neronis).

Philosophy and death

Seneca's surviving works — the 124 Letters to Lucilius, dialogues and essays such as On the Shortness of Life ("It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a great deal of it"), On Anger, On Clemency (addressed to Nero), and nine blood-soaked tragedies that later shaped Renaissance drama — are practical moral therapy: how to use time, master anger, bear exile and fortune, and prepare for death. The preparation was needed. Enormously wealthy despite preaching frugality (a contradiction his enemies never let go), he lost influence as Nero matured, retired in 62, and in 65 was implicated — perhaps falsely — in the Pisonian conspiracy to kill Nero and ordered to commit suicide. He opened his veins and dictated to the end, in a death Tacitus staged as a conscious imitation of Socrates. His Stoicism, with Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius, anchors the modern revival of Stoicism as practical philosophy.
