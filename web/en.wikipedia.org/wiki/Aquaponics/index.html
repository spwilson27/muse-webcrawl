Aquaponics

Aquaponics is a food production system that couples aquaculture (raising aquatic animals such as fish in tanks) with hydroponics (growing plants in water instead of soil) in a symbiotic, recirculating environment.

How it works

Fish waste, mainly ammonia excreted by the fish, is broken down by nitrifying bacteria in a biofilter — first to nitrite, then to nitrate — which plants absorb as fertiliser. The cleaned water returns to the fish tank, so the system uses far less water than conventional farming, with losses mainly to evaporation and sludge removal. Common fish include tilapia, catfish, trout, and ornamental koi; common crops are leafy greens, herbs, tomatoes, and peppers. System designs include media beds, nutrient film channels, and deep-water culture rafts.

History

Antecedents include Aztec chinampa raised-bed farming on lake shallows and integrated fish-and-rice culture in Asia. Modern recirculating systems were developed in the 1970s and 1980s, notably at the New Alchemy Institute and by James Rakocy's team at the University of the Virgin Islands, whose design became a model for commercial farms. Aquaponics is now used commercially, in schools, and by hobbyists, though economics, energy costs for pumps and lighting, and the need to balance fish and plant needs remain challenges.
