Multiple birth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A multiple birth is the culmination of one multiple pregnancy, where the mother gives birth to two or more babies. The most common form is twins (two babies), followed by triplets (three), quadruplets (four), quintuplets (five) and larger sets, collectively called multiplets.

Prevalence and causes

Twins occur naturally in roughly 1 in 80–90 births, with rates varying by population — highest in parts of West Africa, lowest in East Asia. Fraternal (dizygotic) twins result from the fertilisation of two eggs and become more likely with increasing maternal age, family history, and fertility treatment; identical (monozygotic) twins, from the splitting of one fertilised egg, occur at a fairly constant rate of about 3–4 per 1,000 births worldwide. The use of assisted reproductive technology since the 1980s sharply increased multiple births in developed countries, though single-embryo transfer policies have since reduced the highest-order cases.

Risks and care

Multiple pregnancies carry increased risks for both mother and babies: preterm birth (the majority of twins are born before 37 weeks), low birth weight, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, and, in identical twins sharing a placenta, twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome. Such pregnancies are monitored more intensively, and higher-order multiples are usually delivered by Caesarean section.

Cultural aspects

Twins and other multiples hold special significance in many cultures, in mythology (such as Castor and Pollux or Romulus and Remus), and in scientific research, where twin studies help separate genetic from environmental influences on human traits. Famous sets include the Dionne quintuplets of 1930s Canada and the "Octomom" case of 2009 in the United States, which fuelled debate over fertility practice.
