In mathematics, a topological group is a group that is also a topological space, in such a way that the group operations are continuous: the multiplication map sending a pair of elements to their product, and the inversion map sending each element to its inverse, are both continuous functions. Topological groups unite algebra and topology and form the natural setting for much of harmonic analysis.

Definition and basic properties

Formally, a topological group is a group G equipped with a topology such that multiplication G × G → G and inversion G → G are continuous. A consequence is that the topology is homogeneous: left and right translations are homeomorphisms, so the topology is entirely determined by a neighbourhood base at the identity element. Topological groups are automatically uniform spaces, and many analytic concepts — completeness, uniform convergence, Cauchy sequences — can be formulated in them.

Examples

The real numbers under addition and the circle group under multiplication of complex numbers are basic examples. Every Lie group is a topological group; so is any group given the discrete topology, and any vector space over the real or complex numbers under addition. More exotic examples include the p-adic numbers and profinite groups such as the Galois group of an infinite algebraic extension with the Krull topology.

Haar measure and representation theory

Every locally compact topological group carries a Haar measure — a translation-invariant measure unique up to scale — which makes it possible to integrate functions on the group and to develop Fourier analysis in great generality, from classical Fourier series on the circle to the Peter–Weyl theorem for compact groups. The representation theory of topological groups, especially locally compact groups, is a central organising theme of modern mathematics and mathematical physics.

Hilbert's fifth problem

Hilbert's fifth problem asked whether every locally Euclidean topological group is necessarily a Lie group. The problem was solved affirmatively in the 1950s by Gleason, Montgomery, and Zippin: any locally compact group with no small subgroups is a Lie group, and locally Euclidean groups have no small subgroups.
