Phong reflection model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Phong reflection model (also called Phong illumination or Phong lighting) is an empirical model of the local illumination of points on a surface. In 3D computer graphics, it is sometimes ambiguously referred to as "Phong shading", particularly if the model is used in combination with interpolation of normals and in the context of pixel shaders or where a lighting model can be implemented per-pixel.

It was developed by Vietnamese computer scientist Bùi Tường Phong at the University of Utah, who published it in his 1973 Ph.D. dissertation. Along with the method, Phong published a method for interpolating the calculation for each individual pixel that is rasterised from a polygonal model; the interpolation technique is known as Phong shading, even when it is used with a reflection model other than Phong's.

Description

Phong reflection is an empirical model of local illumination. It describes the way a surface reflects light as a combination of the diffuse reflection of rough surfaces with the specular reflection of shiny surfaces. It is based on Phong's informal observation that shiny surfaces have small intense specular highlights, while dull surfaces have larger highlights that fall off more gradually.

The model sums three components for each light source: an ambient term (light that is present everywhere), a diffuse term proportional to the cosine of the angle between the surface normal and the direction to the light, and a specular term proportional to a power of the cosine of the angle between the reflection direction and the direction to the viewer. The exponent, often called the shininess or specular power, controls the size and sharpness of the highlight.

History

The Phong reflection model was developed by Bùi Tường Phong at the University of Utah, who published it in his 1975 Communications of the ACM paper "Illumination for Computer Generated Pictures". The paper also introduced the interpolation method for normals that made the model practical in scanline renderers. The model dominated real-time graphics for decades and remains a standard teaching example, though it has largely been superseded in production rendering by physically based models such as Blinn–Phong refinements and microfacet BRDFs.
