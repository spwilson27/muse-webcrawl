A tournament, or tourney, was a chivalric competition in medieval and early modern Europe in which knights fought one another in mock combat for prizes, honour and training. Emerging in the 11th century, tournaments developed from chaotic mass battles (the mêlée) into highly regulated spectacles centred on the joust, in which two mounted knights charged each other with lances.

Origins

The earliest tournaments, held in northern France in the late 11th and 12th centuries, were realistic war games: two teams of knights fought across open countryside, capturing opponents for ransom. They were effectively small wars, dangerous and often fatal, and they trained knights in the coordinated cavalry tactics used in real campaigns. The Church repeatedly condemned tournaments — participants killed were denied Christian burial — and several kings restricted or banned them, with limited success.

Development

From the 13th century onwards, tournaments became more theatrical and controlled. Fighting moved into enclosed lists, individual jousts replaced the mêlée as the premier event, and specialised equipment developed: blunted or "coronel"-tipped lances, reinforced jousting armour, and the tilt barrier separating the two riders. Heralds regulated entries, blazoned coats of arms, and recorded results, making tournaments great showcases of heraldry and courtly culture, often framed by feasting, pageantry and romance literature celebrating ideal knighthood.

Decline

Tournament culture peaked in the 15th and early 16th centuries at courts such as Burgundy, the Empire and Tudor England. The death of King Henry II of France in a jousting accident in 1559 — a lance splinter pierced his visor — hastened the sport's decline in France. As gunpowder warfare changed the battlefield role of heavy cavalry, tournaments faded into ceremonial pageantry and survive today in historical re-enactment and modern competitive jousting.

Forms of combat

  • Mêlée – a free-for-all team fight, on horseback or on foot.
  • Joust – a charge by two riders with lances, aiming to break lances or unhorse the opponent.
  • Behourd – a lighter form of combat with padded weapons, often for squires.
  • Pas d'armes – a stylised "passage of arms" in which defenders held a position against all comers.
