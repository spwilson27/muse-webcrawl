Pierre François André Méchain (16 August 1744 – 20 September 1804) was a French astronomer and surveyor who, with Charles Messier, was a major contributor to the early study of deep-sky objects and of comets. With Jean-Baptiste Delambre he measured the meridian arc between Dunkirk and Barcelona, the survey on which the definition of the metre was based.

Astronomical work

Méchain discovered seven comets between 1781 and 1799 and made the observations that helped establish the periodic nature of the comet later known as Encke's. Searching for comets, he found 26 deep-sky objects — nebulae and star clusters — that were incorporated into Messier's catalogue, including the galaxies M94, M95, M96, M97 (the Owl Nebula), M98, M99, M100 and the globular clusters M92, M103 and others.

The meridian survey

In 1792–1798 Méchain surveyed the southern portion of the Paris meridian, from Barcelona to Rodez, while Delambre covered the northern portion. The expedition, beset by war and Revolution, determined the length of the meridian quadrant from which the metre was defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from pole to equator. A small discrepancy in Méchain's latitude measurement at Barcelona troubled him for the rest of his life and was concealed in his notebooks — a story retold in Ken Alder's The Measure of All Things.

Death and legacy

Méchain returned to Spain in 1803 to extend the survey to the Balearic Islands, a triangulation that would fix the meridian more precisely. He died of yellow fever at Castellón de la Plana in 1804; the extension was completed by Jean-Baptiste Biot and François Arago. The lunar crater Méchain is named in his honour.
