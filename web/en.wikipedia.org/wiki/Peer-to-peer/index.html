Peer-to-peer

Peer-to-peer (P2P) computing or networking is a distributed application architecture that partitions tasks or workloads between peers — equally privileged, equipotent participants in the network. Peers make a portion of their resources, such as processing power, disk storage or network bandwidth, directly available to other participants, without the need for central coordination by servers or stable hosts. Peers are both suppliers and consumers of resources, in contrast to the traditional client-server model in which consumption is centralised.

A peer-to-peer network, in which nodes connect directly without a central server.

Historical development

Early networks such as Usenet (1979) and the protocols of the early Internet embodied peer-to-peer principles. P2P entered public consciousness in 1999 with Napster, a file-sharing service built around a central index that grew to tens of millions of users before being shut down by litigation. Decentralised successors — Gnutella, Kazaa and the BitTorrent protocol (2001) — removed the central server, making networks more resilient. Skype was originally built on P2P technology, and blockchain networks such as Bitcoin (2009) are peer-to-peer by design, using P2P gossip protocols to reach consensus without any central authority.

Architecture

P2P systems are commonly classified as unstructured (peers connect arbitrarily, as in Gnutella), structured (content is placed deterministically using distributed hash tables such as Kademlia), or hybrid (supernodes or central trackers assist discovery, as in early BitTorrent). Key technical problems include NAT traversal, churn (peers joining and leaving), free-riding, and ensuring content availability and integrity.

Applications and legal issues

Beyond file sharing, P2P underlies cryptocurrencies, distributed storage systems such as IPFS, content delivery, grid computing and privacy tools. Because decentralised file sharing was widely used to distribute copyrighted music and film, P2P became entangled in major copyright litigation — including the US Supreme Court's 2005 decision in MGM Studios v. Grokster — and in ongoing policy debates, even though the underlying technology has many lawful uses.
