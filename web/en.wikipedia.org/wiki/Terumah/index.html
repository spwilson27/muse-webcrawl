Terumah

Terumah (Hebrew: תְּרוּמָה, "an offering" or "gift") is a Hebrew word and a type of offering in Judaism. The word is generally used in the sense of a sacrifice, but in modern Hebrew also refers to a donation or contribution. In the Torah, terumah is a portion of produce, dough, or other goods set aside and given to the kohanim (priests). The most commonly discussed form, terumah gedolah ("great offering"), was separated from grain, wine, and oil grown in the Land of Israel and given to a priest, who had to consume it in a state of ritual purity.

Biblical basis

The obligation is described in several passages, including Numbers 18, which assigns the "heave offering" to the priests, and Deuteronomy 18. The Torah does not fix an exact proportion; rabbinic tradition set generous giving at one-fortieth of the produce, average giving at one-fiftieth, and stingy giving at one-sixtieth. Terumah is also the name of the seventh weekly Torah portion (parashah) in the Book of Exodus, which describes the donations of materials for building the Tabernacle.

Kinds of terumah

Beyond the great offering, sources discuss terumat ma'aser — the tenth of the Levite's tithe given onward to the priests — and the separation of challah from dough. Tractate Terumot of the Mishnah is devoted to the laws of these offerings. Because the ritual purity requirements cannot currently be met, terumah is still separated in Israel today but is not eaten.
