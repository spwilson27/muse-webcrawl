Reed switch

A reed switch is an electrical switch operated by an applied magnetic field. It consists of two ferromagnetic blades (reeds), usually of nickel–iron alloy, hermetically sealed inside a small glass tube filled with inert gas. When a magnet or electromagnet brings a field near the switch, the blades magnetise, attract each other and close the circuit; when the field is removed, the blades spring apart.

History

The reed switch was invented at Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1936 by W. B. Ellwood, and was patented in 1941. It was widely used in telephone exchanges and early computing equipment, and in reed relays.

Applications

Reed switches remain common in door and window contacts for burglar alarms, float switches for liquid-level sensing, bicycle speedometer sensors, and laptop lid-closure sensors. Form A switches are normally open, Form B normally closed, and Form C are changeover types. They are prized for low cost, small size and complete isolation of the contacts from the environment.
