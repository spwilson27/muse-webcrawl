Charisma

Charisma (from Greek χάρισμα, khárisma, "a gift or favour freely given") is a quality of an individual's personality or authority that inspires devotion, enthusiasm or loyalty in others. The German sociologist Max Weber introduced the term into social science, adapting it from early Christian usage in which it denoted a gift of grace — a spiritually conferred power or talent, such as the gifts of the Holy Spirit described in the New Testament.

Weber's charismatic authority

In Weber's typology, charismatic authority is one of three forms of domination, alongside traditional authority and rational-legal authority. It rests on devotion to the exceptional sanctity, heroism or exemplary character of an individual person, and on the normative patterns or order revealed or ordained by him. Charismatic leadership tends to arise in times of crisis and is inherently unstable: it depends on continued success and recognition by followers. Weber described the "routinization of charisma", by which a charismatic movement is transformed into stable traditional or bureaucratic forms after the founder's death or departure.

Social science and psychology

Modern researchers treat charisma variously as an innate trait, a set of learnable behaviours, or an attribution made by followers in particular contexts. Studies of charismatic leadership in organizations associate it with vision articulation, personal risk-taking, sensitivity to followers' needs and unconventional behaviour. Psychologists have also examined its "dark side", linking perceived charisma with narcissism, cult leadership and demagoguery.

Religious usage

In Christian theology, charisms are gifts of the Holy Spirit given for the good of the church, including prophecy, healing, tongues and teaching, as listed by Paul in 1 Corinthians 12. The modern Charismatic movement takes its name from this usage.
