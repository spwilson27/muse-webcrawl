Platitude

A platitude is a statement, generally about morality or social conduct, that is presented as wise or profound but has become trite and meaningless through overuse. Platitudes are often offered as consolation, advice or filler in conversation and speeches — "everything happens for a reason", "it is what it is", "time heals all wounds", "what doesn't kill you makes you stronger". The word derives from the French platitude, from plat, "flat": a remark that falls flat through dullness. Although a platitude may contain a grain of truth, its overuse has drained it of insight, and it is typically uttered as if it were fresh wisdom.

Characteristics

A platitude typically:

  • States something obvious or broadly unexceptionable, so that disagreement seems churlish;
  • Wears the dress of profundity — proverb-like brevity, second-person address, or the cadence of folk wisdom;
  • Functions socially rather than informationally: it fills silence, signals sympathy, ends discussion, or lets a speaker sound thoughtful without committing to anything specific;
  • Resists falsification — it is too vague to be wrong, which some analysts class with the broader family of "deepities" and empty rhetoric.

Distinctions

The platitude overlaps with several nearby notions. A cliché is any overused expression, which need not pose as wisdom ("at the end of the day"). A truism is a statement so obviously true it is hardly worth saying, without necessarily pretending to depth. A bromide (in H. L. Mencken's and Gelett Burgess's coinage) is a soothing, conventional remark intended to dull thought. An aphorism, by contrast, is a concise saying that retains genuine insight — the difference, as critics note, is that an aphorism surprises or sharpens thought, while a platitude merely repeats what everyone already assumes. Context matters: the same sentence may be a platitude in a graduation speech yet sincere and meaningful when spoken at a particular moment of grief, which is why scholars of rhetoric treat platitude as a property of use, not only of wording.

Usage and criticism

Orators, politicians and advertisers are frequent targets of the charge of trafficking in platitudes, since vague uplift offends no constituency. Gustave Flaubert's posthumous Dictionary of Received Ideas satirised the bourgeois platitudes of his century by cataloguing them as obligatory conversation. George Orwell, in "Politics and the English Language" (1946), attacked ready-made phrases that "construct your sentences for you" and anaesthetise thought. Style guides accordingly advise writers to replace platitudes with concrete observation — or, when a familiar saying must be used, to twist it so the reader notices it afresh.
