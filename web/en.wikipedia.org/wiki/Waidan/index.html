Waidan

Laboratory apparatus of external alchemy
Chinese外丹 (wàidān)
Literally"external elixir" / "external cinnabar"
TraditionTaoism
Key ingredientsCinnabar (mercuric sulfide), lead, realgar, saltpeter, sulfur
CounterpartNeidan (internal alchemy)

Waidan (Chinese: 外丹; pinyin: wàidān), literally "external elixir" or "external alchemy", is the Chinese tradition of laboratory alchemy devoted to compounding elixirs of immortality by heating minerals and metals — above all cinnabar (mercuric sulfide) — in sealed reaction vessels. Together with its counterpart neidan ("internal alchemy"), which reframed the same vocabulary as meditative and physiological practice, waidan forms one of the main branches of Taoist alchemy.

History

The search for an elixir of life predates Taoism as an organised religion: the First Emperor Qin Shi Huang famously sent expeditions in search of the elixir and is traditionally said to have died from elixir poisoning himself. The earliest surviving Chinese alchemical text, the Cantong qi (c. 2nd century CE), attributed to Wei Boyang, describes the compounding of elixirs in cosmological terms. The great fourth-century scholar Ge Hong devoted a large part of his Baopuzi to waidan recipes, regarding the ingestion of a properly prepared golden elixir (jindan) as a reliable path to immortality and transcendence.

Waidan flourished under Tang dynasty patronage, when emperors and aristocrats employed court alchemists. However, the elixirs — commonly containing mercury, lead, and arsenic compounds — were highly toxic, and a succession of Tang emperors (including Xianzong, Muzong, Wuzong, and Xuanzong, by some accounts) died of elixir poisoning. From the Song dynasty onward, neidan, which reinterpreted elixir-making as an internal process within the practitioner's body, progressively eclipsed waidan, though laboratory practices persisted in medicine and metallurgy.

Practice

Waidan operations were ritualised: the adept built a laboratory ("chamber of the elixirs"), chose auspicious times, made offerings, and observed taboos and secrecy. Ingredients were sealed in a reaction vessel (ding), often luted with the "six-one" clay mixture, and heated in carefully timed cycles of intensifying and diminishing fire (huohou, "fire times"). The prototypical elixir was "reverted cinnabar" (huandan) — cinnabar cyclically transformed through mercury and back, believed to concentrate cosmic potency with each cycle. Waidan experimentation contributed substantially to early Chinese chemistry, technology (including, famously, the discovery of gunpowder by alchemists seeking an elixir under the Tang), pharmacology, and metallurgy.
