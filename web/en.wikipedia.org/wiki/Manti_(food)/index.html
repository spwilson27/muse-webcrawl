Manti is a type of dumpling popular in Central Asian, West Asian (Turkish, Armenian, Azerbaijani), South Caucasus, and Balkan cuisines. Manti are filled with spiced meat — usually lamb or ground beef — wrapped in a thin dough wrapper and either steamed or boiled. They are closely related to East Asian dumplings and to Slavic dishes such as vareniki and pelmeni.

History and spread

The word derives from Chinese mantou (steamed bun), transmitted across the steppe by Turkic and Mongol peoples from the medieval period onward; dumplings of this family appear from Korea (mandu) to Anatolia. Nomadic horsemen valued dried or quickly cooked filled dough that could be steamed over a pot. By the Ottoman era, mantı was an established dish of Anatolian Turkish cuisine, with Kayseri famed for tiny dumplings said to fit forty on a single spoon.

Regional varieties

  • Central Asian manti (Uzbek, Kazakh, Uyghur, Tajik) — large, steamed in a tiered steamer (manti qazan), filled with lamb, onion and cumin, sometimes pumpkin; served with sour cream, vinegar sauce or chilli oil.
  • Turkish mantı — small, boiled, topped with garlic yogurt and melted butter infused with Aleppo pepper or sumac and dried mint; Kayseri style is the smallest.
  • Armenian manti — boat-shaped, baked until crisp, then moistened with broth and yogurt.
  • Afghan mantu — steamed beef dumplings with a split-pea tomato sauce and garlic yogurt.
  • Georgian khinkali — a large, twisted-top relative with spiced meat and herbs.

Preparation

A simple flour-and-water (sometimes egg) dough is rolled very thin, cut into squares, filled with a pinch of seasoned raw minced meat and onion, and sealed by pinching the corners together into a pouch or boat shape. Steaming preserves the juices; the dumplings are eaten hot with their sauces, which balance the richness of the meat.
