The conquest of the Canary Islands by the Crown of Castile was a protracted process spanning nearly a century, from 1402 to 1496. The islands were inhabited by the indigenous Guanches, Berber-related peoples of North African origin, who resisted fiercely in the larger, more mountainous islands.

Phases

The first phase (1402–1405) was a private expedition led by Jean de Béthencourt and Gadifer de la Salle with Castilian backing, which seized the eastern islands of Lanzarote, Fuerteventura and El Hierro. After decades of slave raids and disputes with Portugal, Castile resumed the conquest in earnest with a crown-financed second phase: Gran Canaria fell in 1483 after bitter fighting, La Palma in 1493, and Tenerife — the most heavily defended island, whose Guanches had defeated a Spanish force at the First Battle of Acentejo in 1494 — in 1496 after the Second Battle of Acentejo.

Aftermath

The conquest established Castile's Atlantic foothold that would underpin the later voyages of Columbus, who provisioned at the islands in 1492. The Guanche population was decimated by warfare, enslavement and European disease, and largely assimilated; their descendants are part of the Canarian people, and Canarian Spanish preserves traces of Guanche vocabulary.
