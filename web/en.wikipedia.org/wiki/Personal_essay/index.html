Personal essay

Personal essay
GenreNonfiction / creative nonfiction
Point of viewFirst person
OriginatorMichel de Montaigne, Essais (1580)
Notable practitionersMontaigne, Joan Didion, James Baldwin, Virginia Woolf, David Sedaris

A personal essay is a form of nonfiction writing in which the author explores a subject through the lens of their own experience, in the first person and in a distinctive voice. Unlike the formal essay, which argues a thesis impersonally, the personal essay uses anecdote, reflection and digression to make meaning from lived experience; its subject is as much the writer's mind in motion as the topic at hand.

History

The form descends from Michel de Montaigne's Essais (1580), in which the retired French judge made himself the subject of study: "I study myself more than any other subject. That is my metaphysics, that is my physics." English essayists from Francis Bacon and Charles Lamb to Virginia Woolf developed the familiar essay. In the 20th century the personal essay merged with journalism and memoir: James Baldwin's essays combined autobiography with social critique, and Joan Didion's cool, precise first-person reportage defined the "New Journalism" era.

Characteristics

Personal essays typically feature a conversational yet crafted voice, scenes rendered in detail, honest self-examination, and a movement from the particular to the universal. They range from a few hundred words to book length. The form overlaps with the memoir but is usually shorter and organised around an idea or question rather than a life story.

Contemporary practice

The internet era produced a boom in confessional personal essays on online magazines, sometimes criticised as the "first-person industrial complex" for incentivising writers — disproportionately women — to mine trauma for clicks. The personal essay remains a staple of literary journals, creative-nonfiction MFA programmes and collections such as The Best American Essays.
