Interrupted aortic arch


Specialty: Cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery
Type: Congenital heart defect

Interrupted aortic arch is a rare congenital heart defect in which the aorta is not completely developed — there is a complete absence of luminal continuity between the ascending and descending aorta. It is usually associated with a ventricular septal defect and a patent ductus arteriosus, on which blood flow to the lower body depends. It occurs in roughly 2 per 100,000 live births and accounts for about 1% of congenital heart disease.

Classification

The Celoria–Patton classification divides the condition by the site of interruption: Type A, distal to the left subclavian artery; Type B, between the left common carotid and left subclavian arteries (the most common, and strongly associated with DiGeorge syndrome / 22q11.2 deletion); and Type C, between the innominate and left common carotid arteries (the rarest).

Presentation and diagnosis

Affected newborns typically present in the first days of life, when the ductus arteriosus closes, with poor perfusion, metabolic acidosis, and shock. Diagnosis is usually by echocardiography, sometimes supplemented by CT or MRI angiography. Prostaglandin E1 infusion is given to keep the ductus open until surgery.

Treatment

Treatment is surgical repair in the neonatal period, usually by direct end-to-end anastomosis of the aorta combined with closure of the ventricular septal defect, performed through a median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass. Long-term follow-up is required for recoarctation and hypertension.
