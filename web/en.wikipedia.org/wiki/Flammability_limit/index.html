Flammability limit

Flammability limits, also called explosive limits, are the proportions of a combustible gas or vapor in air between which a mixture will burn or explode if ignited. The lower flammability limit (LFL), or lower explosive limit (LEL), is the lowest concentration of the fuel in air that will support combustion; below it, the mixture is too "lean" to burn. The upper flammability limit (UFL), or upper explosive limit (UEL), is the highest concentration that will burn; above it, the mixture is too "rich" in fuel and deficient in oxygen.

Factors affecting the limits

The limits depend on temperature, pressure, oxygen concentration, the presence of inert gases, and the size and geometry of the containing vessel. Raising temperature or pressure generally widens the flammable range. Enriching the air with oxygen dramatically raises the upper limit. Inerting — adding nitrogen or carbon dioxide — narrows the range and is a standard explosion-prevention technique.

Mixtures

For mixtures of several flammable gases, the limiting values can be estimated using Le Chatelier's mixing rule, which combines the limits of the individual components in proportion to their concentrations.

Safety applications

Flammability limits are fundamental to industrial safety. Ventilation systems are designed to keep concentrations below a fraction — commonly 25 percent — of the LFL. Combustible-gas detectors are calibrated in percent LEL. Operators of fuel tanks exploit the rich side of the range as well: the vapor space above liquid fuel in a nearly full tank is often above the UFL and so cannot ignite internally, whereas a partially emptied tank may pass through the flammable range.
