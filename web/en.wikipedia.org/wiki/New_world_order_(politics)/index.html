New world order (politics)

The United Nations headquarters; the phrase "new world order" is often associated with post-Cold War international arrangements.

The term "new world order" refers to a new period of history evidencing a dramatic change in world political thought and the balance of power in international relations. In political usage, it does not designate a single plan or conspiracy but has been applied to several major turning points, most notably the end of the Cold War.

Historical uses

Following the First World War, the phrase was associated with Woodrow Wilson's vision for a League of Nations and a rules-based international system. After the Second World War, it was occasionally used in connection with the founding of the United Nations.

Post-Cold War usage

The phrase entered wide public currency during the Gulf War of 1990–91. Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev used the term in a speech to the United Nations in December 1988, and U.S. President George H. W. Bush famously declared in an address to Congress on September 11, 1990: "Out of these troubled times, our fifth objective — a new world order — can emerge." For Bush, the phrase described an era in which the nations of the world, East and West, North and South, would cooperate through international law and the United Nations in the absence of superpower confrontation.

Other usages

More recent usage has applied the phrase to the emergence of a multipolar world, the rise of China, and debates over global governance. The phrase is also used in a distinct sense in conspiracy theories (see New World Order (conspiracy theory)), which allege a secret plan for a totalitarian world government; that usage is separate from the descriptive political-science sense covered here.
