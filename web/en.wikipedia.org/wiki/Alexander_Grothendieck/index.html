Alexander Grothendieck (28 March 1928 – 13 November 2014) was a German-born French mathematician who became the leading figure in the creation of modern algebraic geometry. His work vastly extended the scope of the field and introduced concepts — schemes, topoi, motives — that reshaped much of pure mathematics.

Early life

Grothendieck was born in Berlin to an anarchist father, the Russian writer Alexander Schapiro (Sascha Tanaroff), who died at Auschwitz, and a German mother. He grew up in France during the war, partly in internment camps and hiding, and discovered mathematics largely on his own before studying at Montpellier and Nancy, where he earned his doctorate in functional analysis under Laurent Schwartz and Jean Dieudonné.

The IHÉS years

From 1958, at the newly founded Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques (IHÉS), Grothendieck led a famous seminar that rebuilt algebraic geometry on the foundation of schemes. The resulting texts — the Éléments de géométrie algébrique (EGA, with Dieudonné) and the Séminaire de géométrie algébrique (SGA) — ran to thousands of pages. His framework enabled the eventual proof of the Weil conjectures by his student Pierre Deligne. He received the Fields Medal in 1966.

Withdrawal and later life

A committed pacifist, Grothendieck resigned from the IHÉS in 1970 on learning it received military funding, and progressively withdrew from mathematics and public life. He declined the Crafoord Prize in 1988. His vast autobiographical manuscript Récoltes et semailles reflects on his life and work. From 1991 he lived in seclusion in the Pyrenees until his death in 2014.
