Ballade


A medieval ballade manuscript.

The ballade (French: [balad]) is a form of medieval and Renaissance French poetry, and also the name of a musical setting of such a poem. As one of the three formes fixes (with the rondeau and the virelai), the ballade dominated French lyric poetry and song in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

Poetic form

A literary ballade typically consists of three stanzas of seven, eight, or ten lines, each ending with the same refrain line, followed by a short concluding stanza called the envoi, conventionally addressed to a prince or patron. The rhyme scheme of the first stanza is repeated throughout, and the same rhymes are used in each stanza. Guillaume de Machaut (c. 1300–1377), the form's greatest master, composed over two hundred ballades as both poet and composer. Later practitioners included Eustache Deschamps, Christine de Pizan, Charles d'Orléans, and François Villon, whose "Ballade des dames du temps jadis" contains the famous refrain "Mais où sont les neiges d'antan?" ("But where are the snows of yesteryear?").

Musical ballades

In the fourteenth-century ars nova and ars subtilior styles, ballades were usually composed for two or three voices, with only the first stanza sung in surviving sources. From the mid-fifteenth century the musical ballade declined as the formes fixes gave way to new styles.

Instrumental ballades

In the nineteenth century the term was revived for instrumental music. Frédéric Chopin composed four Ballades for solo piano (Opp. 23, 38, 47, and 52), the first works to use the title for an abstract instrumental composition; they are often heard as narrative in character without a specific programme. Johannes Brahms wrote four Ballades, Op. 10, and included a ballade in other sets; Franz Liszt, Edvard Grieg, and Gabriel Fauré also composed piano ballades. The term has since been applied to large-scale dramatic instrumental movements by many composers.
