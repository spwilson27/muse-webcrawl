Rotational speed

Rotational speed (also known as speed of revolution or rate of rotation) is the number of turns of an object divided by the time taken, specified as revolutions per minute (rpm), cycles per second (Hz), or radians per second. It is a scalar measure of how fast a body rotates or revolves relative to another point, distinct from angular velocity, which is treated as a vector (or pseudovector) pointing along the axis of rotation.

Units and conversion

The SI unit of rotational speed is the radian per second (rad/s), while the reciprocal second (s−1, equivalent to hertz) is used for rotational frequency. In engineering practice, revolutions per minute (rpm) is by far the most common unit, shown on tachometers for engines, motors, and machinery. The angular speed ω and rotational frequency f are related by:

ω = 2π f = 2π n / 60 (with n in rpm)

Relation to tangential speed

For a point at distance r from the axis, the tangential (linear) speed is the product of the angular speed and the radius:

v = ω r

Thus the rim of a large wheel moves faster than the rim of a small wheel at the same rotational speed — the principle behind gear ratios and the speed of a point on Earth's surface due to its rotation (about 465 m/s at the equator).

Examples

  • A vinyl LP record rotates at 33⅓ rpm; a 45 rpm single at 45 rpm.
  • A typical car engine idles near 600–1,000 rpm and redlines between about 6,000 and 8,000 rpm.
  • Hard-disk drives commonly spin at 5,400 or 7,200 rpm.
  • Earth rotates once per sidereal day, about 7.29 × 10−5 rad/s.
  • Ultracentrifuges can exceed 100,000 rpm to separate molecules by mass.
