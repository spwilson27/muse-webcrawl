River (typography)

In typography, a river (or river of white) is a conspicuous vertical or diagonal channel of white space running through a paragraph of justified text, formed when word spaces on successive lines happen to align. Rivers are considered a defect of typesetting: they distract the eye and interrupt the even grey "colour" of the page.

Causes and remedies

Rivers are most common in justified text set in narrow columns, where large word spaces are needed. Typesetters reduce them by adjusting hyphenation, allowing slight letter-spacing changes, choosing wider measures, or setting text flush left (ragged right) instead. Automated composition systems and proofreaders' practice of squinting at a page or turning it upside down help reveal them.

The term dates to early printing; related defects include "stacks" (repeated word alignment at line starts or ends).
