RSD-10 Pioneer

The RSD-10 Pioneer (Russian: РСД-10 Пионер; NATO reporting name: SS-20 Saber) was an intermediate-range ballistic missile with a nuclear warhead, deployed by the Soviet Union from 1976 to 1988. It featured a two-stage solid-fuel design mounted on a mobile transporter erector launcher, derived from the first stage of the RT-21 Temp 2S intercontinental ballistic missile.

Characteristics

The missile had a range of approximately 5,000–5,500 kilometres and in its most common form carried three multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs), each with a yield of about 150 kilotons. Its mobility, solid fuel, and MIRVed warheads made it far more capable than the aging SS-4 and SS-5 missiles it replaced, allowing it to strike targets throughout Europe and Asia from deep within Soviet territory.

Deployment and controversy

The deployment of the SS-20 from 1976 onward altered the European nuclear balance and provoked a major political controversy. NATO responded with its 1979 "Double-Track Decision": pursuing arms-control negotiations while preparing to deploy American Pershing II missiles and ground-launched cruise missiles in Western Europe if talks failed. The deployments triggered large protests across Western Europe in the early 1980s.

Elimination

The RSD-10 was eliminated under the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty signed by the United States and the Soviet Union in December 1987. All 654 Soviet missiles of the type, together with their launchers, were destroyed by May 1991 under the treaty's verification provisions, alongside the equivalent American systems.
