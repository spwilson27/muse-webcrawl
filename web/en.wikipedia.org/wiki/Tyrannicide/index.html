Tyrannicide

Tyrannicide
DefinitionThe killing of a tyrant or unjust ruler
Famous examplesHarmodius and Aristogeiton (514 BC); assassination of Julius Caesar (44 BC)

Tyrannicide is the killing or assassination of a tyrant — a ruler who has seized or exercises power unjustly or oppressively — usually carried out for the common good. The concept has a long history in political philosophy, stretching from classical antiquity through medieval scholasticism to early modern theories of resistance.

History

In ancient Athens, Harmodius and Aristogeiton, who killed the tyrant Hipparchus in 514 BC, were honoured with statues in the Agora and celebrated in popular song as liberators, even though the tyranny of Hippias continued for several more years. The most famous tyrannicide of antiquity is the assassination of Julius Caesar on the Ides of March, 44 BC, by a conspiracy led by Brutus and Cassius, who claimed to be restoring the Roman Republic.

Medieval and early modern thinkers debated whether subjects could lawfully kill a tyrant. John of Salisbury defended tyrannicide in certain circumstances in the Policraticus (1159), while Thomas Aquinas treated the question cautiously. The monarchomach writers of the Wars of Religion, and later John Locke's theory of the right of revolution, developed related arguments about resistance to tyranny.

Modern discussion

In modern law and ethics, tyrannicide is usually considered within the frameworks of assassination, just-war theory, and the targeted killing of heads of state, which is prohibited by many national laws and discouraged by international norms. The term survives mainly in historical and philosophical discussion.
