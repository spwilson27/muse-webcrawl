Caveolins are a family of integral membrane proteins that are the principal structural components of caveolae, the small flask-shaped invaginations found in the plasma membranes of many vertebrate cells, especially endothelial cells, adipocytes and muscle cells. Caveolin proteins insert into the inner leaflet of the membrane in a hairpin configuration, with both the N-terminus and C-terminus facing the cytoplasm.

Members

Three caveolin genes have been identified in mammals. Caveolin-1 (Cav-1) is the most widely expressed and is required for the formation of caveolae; it exists in alpha and beta isoforms produced by alternative translation initiation. Caveolin-2 is co-expressed with caveolin-1 in many tissues and depends on it for membrane targeting. Caveolin-3 is specific to muscle tissue, including skeletal and cardiac muscle, and is sometimes called M-caveolin.

Function

Caveolins oligomerise into high-molecular-weight complexes that bend the membrane into the characteristic caveolar shape. They bind cholesterol and interact with numerous signalling molecules through a region known as the caveolin scaffolding domain, which has led to the hypothesis that caveolae act as organising centres ("signalosomes") for pathways involving nitric oxide synthase, Src-family kinases, and G proteins. Caveolae also participate in endocytosis, transcytosis across endothelial cells, lipid regulation and mechanoprotection of the membrane.

Clinical significance

Mutations in CAV3 cause several muscle disorders, including limb-girdle muscular dystrophy type 1C, rippling muscle disease and some forms of long QT syndrome. Loss of caveolin-1 in mice produces pulmonary fibrosis, vascular abnormalities and insulin resistance, and altered Cav-1 expression has been reported in various cancers, though its role as tumour promoter or suppressor appears to depend on context and stage.
