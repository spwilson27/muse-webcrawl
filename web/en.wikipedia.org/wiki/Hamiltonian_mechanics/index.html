Hamiltonian mechanics

Hamiltonian mechanics is a formulation of classical mechanics developed by William Rowan Hamilton in 1833, reformulating Lagrangian mechanics in terms of generalised coordinates and conjugate momenta in phase space.

Overview

The Hamiltonian H(q, p, t), usually the total energy, generates motion through Hamilton's equations: dq/dt = ∂H/∂p and dp/dt = −∂H/∂q. The flow preserves phase-space volume (Liouville's theorem) and is described geometrically by symplectic geometry and Poisson brackets.

Applications

The framework underlies statistical mechanics, chaos theory, optics analogies, and the transition to quantum mechanics, where observables become operators and Poisson brackets become commutators. Canonical transformations simplify problems by choosing better coordinates.

History

Hamilton built on Lagrange and Laplace; his work also produced the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. The formulation is especially powerful for systems with conserved quantities and in celestial mechanics.
