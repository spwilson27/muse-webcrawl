Secretion

Secretion is the movement of material from one point to another, such as a secreted chemical substance from a cell or gland. In contrast, excretion is the removal of certain substances or waste products from a cell or organism. The classical mechanism of cell secretion is via secretory portals at the plasma membrane called porosomes. Secretion in bacterial species means the transport or translocation of effector molecules, for example proteins, enzymes or toxins, across the cytoplasmic membrane.

Mechanisms in eukaryotic cells

Eukaryotic cells secrete a wide variety of substances, including proteins, hormones, neurotransmitters, digestive enzymes, and components of the extracellular matrix. In the conventional secretory pathway, proteins destined for secretion are synthesised on the rough endoplasmic reticulum, transported to the Golgi apparatus for modification and sorting, and packaged into secretory vesicles that fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing their contents by exocytosis.

Types of secretion

Glandular secretion is classified by the mechanism by which products leave the cell. In merocrine secretion, products are released by exocytosis without loss of cellular material, as in pancreatic acinar cells. In apocrine secretion, a portion of the apical cytoplasm is pinched off with the product. In holocrine secretion, the entire cell disintegrates to release its contents, as in sebaceous glands.

Endocrine secretion releases hormones directly into the bloodstream, while exocrine secretion delivers products through ducts to a surface or cavity. Paracrine secretion acts on nearby target cells.

Bacterial secretion systems

Bacteria possess specialised secretion systems, numbered type I through type IX, that transport proteins across their membranes. The type III secretion system, found in many Gram-negative pathogens, injects effector proteins directly into host cells. The general secretory (Sec) pathway and the twin-arginine translocation (Tat) pathway move proteins across the inner membrane.
