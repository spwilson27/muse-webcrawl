Quadrupole ion trap

A quadrupole ion trap or Paul trap is a type of ion trap that uses dynamic electric fields to trap charged particles. It is named after Wolfgang Paul, who invented it and shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1989 for this work. Quadrupole traps are widely used as mass analysers in mass spectrometry and as platforms for quantum computing research and precision spectroscopy.

A three-dimensional Paul trap: a ring electrode between two endcap electrodes.

Principle

A static electric field cannot confine a charged particle in all three dimensions (a consequence of Earnshaw's theorem), so the Paul trap instead applies a radio-frequency (RF) oscillating quadrupole field, sometimes combined with a DC voltage. The rapidly alternating field creates an effective time-averaged "ponderomotive" potential with a saddle shape that continually refocuses the ion. Ion motion is described by the Mathieu equations, and stable trapping occurs only for suitable combinations of RF amplitude, frequency and the ion's mass-to-charge ratio.

Forms

The three-dimensional trap consists of a hyperboloidal ring electrode and two endcap electrodes; ions are confined near the centre and can be ejected selectively by mass for analysis. The linear ion trap uses four parallel rods, confining ions along a line; it offers greater capacity and is common in tandem mass spectrometry. In quantum information experiments, chains of laser-cooled ions in linear traps serve as qubits, their shared vibrational modes coupling the ions for entangling gates.

Applications

Quadrupole ion trap mass spectrometers are compact instruments able to perform multiple stages of tandem mass spectrometry (MSⁿ) by isolating, fragmenting and analysing ions in time. Trapped-ion systems have achieved some of the highest-fidelity quantum gates and are used in optical atomic clocks, tests of fundamental physics, and studies of cold ion–molecule chemistry.
