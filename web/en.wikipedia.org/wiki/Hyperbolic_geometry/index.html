Hyperbolic geometry

In mathematics, hyperbolic geometry (also called Lobachevskian geometry or Bolyai–Lobachevskian geometry) is a non-Euclidean geometry in which the parallel postulate of Euclidean geometry is replaced by an alternative: for any given line R and any point P not on R, in the plane containing both, there are at least two distinct lines through P that do not intersect R. The hyperbolic plane has constant negative Gaussian curvature, and the angles of a triangle in hyperbolic geometry sum to less than 180 degrees.

Properties

Many familiar Euclidean results fail in hyperbolic geometry. Through a point not on a given line there pass infinitely many lines parallel to it (in the sense of never meeting it). There are no similar triangles other than congruent ones: the angles of a triangle determine its sides, and there is an absolute unit of length. The area of a triangle is proportional to its angular defect — how far its angle sum falls below 180° — and is bounded above. Rectangles do not exist, and the circumference of a circle grows exponentially with its radius, C = 2π sinh(r), rather than linearly. Space effectively becomes "larger" near the boundary of any model of the plane.

History

For two thousand years mathematicians attempted to prove Euclid's parallel postulate from his other axioms. In the 1820s and 1830s, Nikolai Lobachevsky in Russia and János Bolyai in Hungary independently published geometries built on its denial, while Carl Friedrich Gauss had reached similar conclusions earlier without publishing. Acceptance grew after Eugenio Beltrami provided models in 1868 showing hyperbolic geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is, and after Henri Poincaré introduced his influential disc and half-plane models. Bernhard Riemann's framework of manifolds of constant curvature placed hyperbolic, Euclidean, and spherical geometry on a common footing.

Models

Several models represent the hyperbolic plane within Euclidean settings. In the Poincaré disc model, points lie inside a unit disc and "lines" are circular arcs meeting the boundary orthogonally (plus diameters); the model is conformal, preserving angles, but distances shrink towards the boundary, which lies at infinity. The Poincaré half-plane model uses the upper half-plane with vertical lines and semicircles as geodesics. The Klein disc model uses straight chords as lines but distorts angles. The hyperboloid model represents the plane as a sheet of a two-sheeted hyperboloid in Minkowski space.

Applications

Hyperbolic geometry underlies the geometry of saddle-shaped (pseudospherical) surfaces and plays a role in special relativity, where the space of velocities is hyperbolic (rapidity space). It appears in topology through the classification of surfaces and Thurston's geometrisation programme, in complex analysis via the uniformisation theorem, and more recently in network science and machine learning, where hyperbolic embeddings capture hierarchical data.
