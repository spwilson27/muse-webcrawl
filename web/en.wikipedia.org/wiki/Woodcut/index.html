Woodcut

Woodcut is a relief printing technique in printmaking. An artist carves an image into the surface of a block of wood — typically with gouges — leaving the printing parts level with the surface while removing the non-printing parts. The character of the lines is determined mainly by the direction of the wood grain, since the block is cut along the plank (side grain), distinguishing it from wood engraving, which is cut on the end grain.

History

Woodcut originated in China, where it was used for printing on textiles and later paper; the earliest dated printed book is the Chinese Diamond Sutra (868 AD). In Europe the technique appeared around 1400 and was used for playing cards, devotional images, and block books. Albrecht Dürer raised the woodcut to a major art form in the late 15th and early 16th centuries, with series such as the Apocalypse (1498).

Japanese woodcut

In Japan, the moku hanga tradition produced the famous ukiyo-e prints of the Edo period, using multiple blocks — one for each colour — carved from cherry wood and printed by hand with water-based inks. Artists such as Hokusai and Hiroshige achieved worldwide influence in the 19th century.

Technique

The design is drawn or transferred onto the block; knives and gouges cut away the areas to remain white; the surface is inked with a roller or brush; paper is laid on and pressure applied by press or by hand with a baren. For colour woodcuts, separate blocks are carved for each colour and printed in register.

Modern use

Woodcut influenced German Expressionism (Die Brücke artists such as Kirchner and Heckel) and remains practised by contemporary printmakers, valued for its bold graphic character.
