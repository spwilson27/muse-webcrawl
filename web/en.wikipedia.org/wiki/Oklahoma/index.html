Oklahoma (/ˌoʊkləˈhoʊmə/) is a state in the South Central region of the United States. It borders Texas to the south and west, Kansas to the north, Missouri to the northeast, Arkansas to the east, New Mexico to the west, and Colorado to the northwest. Partially in the western extreme of Tornado Alley, Oklahoma sits in a transition zone between the humid subtropical east and the semi-arid Great Plains of the west.

The state's name in Choctaw, Okla Humma, roughly translates to English as "Red People" or "Land of the Red People"; the name was chosen by Choctaw chief Allen Wright in 1866.

History

In the 1830s the United States forcibly removed the Cherokee, Muscogee, Seminole, Chickasaw and Choctaw nations — the "Five Civilized Tribes" — to what became Oklahoma, on journeys remembered as the Trail of Tears. After the Civil War the tribes lost their western lands, which were opened to non-Indian settlement in the Land Runs beginning in 1889; settlers who jumped the gun were nicknamed "Sooners." Oklahoma Territory and Indian Territory merged into the state of Oklahoma in 1907. Oil booms followed, then catastrophe in the 1930s, when the Dust Bowl drove tens of thousands of "Okies" west to California.

Geography and economy

Oklahoma contains more ecoregions per square mile than any other state, from Ozark forests and the Ouachita Mountains in the east to shortgrass prairie and mesas in the Panhandle. Its economy rests on energy (oil, natural gas, wind power), agriculture (wheat and cattle), aviation and maintenance (Tinker Air Force Base and the FAA's Mike Monroney Aeronautical Center), and tribal enterprises. Oklahoma has the second-largest Native American population of any state and is home to 38 federally recognised tribal nations, many headquartered there.

Cities

Oklahoma City, the capital, is the largest city; Tulsa, the former "Oil Capital of the World," is second. In 1995 Oklahoma City was the site of the deadliest act of domestic terrorism in U.S. history, the bombing of the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building.
