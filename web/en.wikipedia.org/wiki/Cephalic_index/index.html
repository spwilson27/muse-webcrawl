Cephalic index

The cephalic index or cranial index is the ratio of the maximum width (biparietal diameter) of an organism's head multiplied by 100, divided by its maximum length (occipitofrontal diameter) — that is, the ratio of the width of the head to its length. The term was coined by the Swedish anatomist Anders Retzius (1796–1860) and was widely used by anthropologists in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries to classify human populations.

The cephalic index compares the maximum breadth of the skull to its maximum length.

Classification

Skulls were traditionally grouped into three broad categories according to their index:

Historical use

Retzius used the index in his studies of ancient Scandinavian crania, and the measure was subsequently taken up across Europe and North America in attempts to map the distribution of supposed racial types. The American anthropologist Franz Boas showed in the early 1900s that the cephalic index of immigrants' children differed measurably from that of their parents, undermining the idea that it was a fixed hereditary marker of race. The index is now regarded as a poor tool for population classification, though it retains descriptive value in anatomy, archaeology and veterinary practice.

Modern use in medicine

A related cephalic index is still employed clinically to assess head shape in infants, for example in evaluating deformational plagiocephaly and brachycephaly, often measured from three-dimensional surface scans rather than callipers.

Use in animals

The term is also used descriptively for domestic animals, particularly dogs, where "brachycephalic" describes short-skulled breeds such as the Pug and the Bulldog, "mesaticephalic" breeds of intermediate proportions, and "dolichocephalic" long-skulled breeds such as the Greyhound and Collie.
