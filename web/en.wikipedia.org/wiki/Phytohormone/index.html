Phytohormone

Phytohormones (also called plant hormones or plant growth regulators) are signal molecules produced within plants that occur in extremely low concentrations and regulate cellular processes in targeted cells. Unlike animal hormones, which are often produced in dedicated glands, phytohormones are synthesized in many tissues and may act locally or be transported to other parts of the plant.

Major classes

Five groups of phytohormones are generally recognised:

  • Auxins — the first plant hormones discovered; indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) is the principal natural auxin. Auxins promote cell elongation, apical dominance, root initiation and phototropic bending.
  • Gibberellins — diterpenoid acids that stimulate stem elongation, seed germination and flowering; named after the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi, whose infection caused "foolish seedling" disease in rice.
  • Cytokinins — adenine derivatives such as zeatin that promote cell division (cytokinesis) and delay leaf senescence.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA) — a sesquiterpene that promotes dormancy, closes stomata during water stress and mediates responses to environmental stress.
  • Ethylene — a simple gaseous hydrocarbon that promotes fruit ripening, leaf abscission and the "triple response" in seedlings.

More recently recognised signalling compounds include brassinosteroids, jasmonates, salicylic acid, strigolactones and karrikins.

History

The concept of plant hormones grew out of experiments by Charles and Francis Darwin on phototropism in the 1880s, and the isolation of auxin by Frits Went in 1928, who demonstrated a diffusible growth-promoting substance from oat coleoptile tips in the classic Avena curvature test. Kinetin, the first cytokinin, was identified in 1955 by Folke Skoog and Carlos Miller. The ratio of auxin to cytokinin in tissue culture was shown by Skoog and Miller to control whether cultured cells form roots or shoots, a principle still central to plant tissue culture.

Mode of action

Phytohormones bind to specific receptor proteins — for example the TIR1/AFB family for auxin and the PYR/PYL/RCAR family for abscisic acid — triggering signal transduction cascades that alter gene expression. Auxin perception works by promoting the degradation of Aux/IAA repressor proteins via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, freeing auxin response factors (ARFs) to activate transcription.

Agricultural uses

Synthetic auxins such as 2,4-D are widely used as herbicides, while other growth regulators are used to root cuttings, thin fruit, delay or accelerate ripening, and dwarf cereal crops to prevent lodging.
