Robert Knox (surgeon)

Robert Knox (4 September 1791 – 20 December 1862) was a Scottish anatomist and ethnologist best known for his involvement in the Burke and Hare murders. Born in Edinburgh, Knox studied medicine there and served as an army surgeon at Waterloo and in South Africa before returning to become Edinburgh's most popular private teacher of anatomy.

Career

After losing an eye to smallpox, Knox graduated MD in 1814. He studied anatomy in Paris under Georges Cuvier and Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, whose transcendental anatomy influenced him deeply. In 1825 he took over John Barclay's extramural anatomy school in Edinburgh, attracting classes of several hundred students with brilliant, theatrical lectures.

Burke and Hare

Because the law allowed anatomists only the bodies of executed criminals, a shortage of corpses arose. In 1827–28 William Burke and William Hare murdered at least sixteen people in Edinburgh and sold the bodies to Knox, who paid up to £10 each and asked few questions. When the crimes were exposed, Hare turned king's evidence and Burke was hanged in 1829. Knox was never charged, though a committee found he had acted imprudently; an angry mob burned him in effigy and his reputation never recovered.

Later life

Student numbers collapsed and Knox drifted to Glasgow and then London, working as a pathologist and writing journalism and books, including The Races of Men (1850), a work of racial "science" now notorious for its doctrines of racial determinism. He died in Hackney in 1862 in obscurity.
