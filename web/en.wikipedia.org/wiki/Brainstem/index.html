The brainstem (or brain stem) is the stalk-like posterior part of the brain that connects the cerebrum with the spinal cord. In the human brain it comprises the midbrain, the pons, and the medulla oblongata. The brainstem is a very small component of the brain — making up around 2.6% of its total weight — yet it carries all the fibre tracts connecting the cerebrum and cerebellum with the spinal cord, gives rise to most of the cranial nerves, and contains the centres that regulate the vital autonomic functions of breathing, heart rate, and consciousness.

Anatomy

Medulla oblongata

The medulla is the lowest part of the brainstem, continuous with the spinal cord at the foramen magnum. It contains the cardiac, respiratory, vomiting, and vasomotor centres and so controls heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, swallowing, coughing, and sneezing. In its pyramids, most corticospinal fibres cross to the opposite side (decussate), which is why each cerebral hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body.

Pons

The pons — Latin for "bridge" — bulges anteriorly above the medulla and connects the two hemispheres of the cerebellum via massive transverse fibres. It relays signals between the cerebrum and cerebellum, houses nuclei of cranial nerves V–VIII, and, with the medulla, helps regulate breathing and sleep.

Midbrain

The midbrain (mesencephalon) is the shortest segment, surrounding the cerebral aqueduct. Its roof plate, the tectum, bears the superior and inferior colliculi, which process visual and auditory reflexes; its floor contains the substantia nigra, whose dopaminergic neurons degenerate in Parkinson's disease, and the red nucleus. The oculomotor and trochlear nerves arise here.

Functions

Running through the whole brainstem is the reticular formation, a network of nuclei that governs arousal and consciousness via the reticular activating system; damage producing bilateral brainstem dysfunction typically causes coma. The brainstem also originates cranial nerves III–XII, conveys all ascending sensory and descending motor pathways, and integrates reflexes of the head and neck including eye movements, gag, and pupillary responses — the basis of the clinical brainstem reflexes tested in the assessment of coma and brain death.

Clinical significance

Brainstem stroke — from occlusion of the basilar or vertebral arteries — produces characteristic "crossed" syndromes, in which cranial-nerve signs appear on one side of the face and motor or sensory signs on the opposite side of the body. Basilar occlusion can cause the locked-in syndrome, in which a conscious patient is almost completely paralysed. Brainstem tumours such as diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma are among the most lethal cancers of childhood. Because the brainstem controls the functions essential to life, irreversible loss of brainstem function is used as a legal criterion of death in several countries, including the United Kingdom.
