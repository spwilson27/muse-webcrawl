Antiqua

Antiqua is a term for the class of typefaces and letterforms that imitate the handwriting of Italian Renaissance humanists, as opposed to blackletter (Fraktur) scripts. Antiqua letterforms derive from the combination of Roman square capitals for uppercase letters and Carolingian minuscule for lowercase letters, developed in Italy in the early 15th century.

History

Humanist scribes such as Poggio Bracciolini and Niccolò de' Niccoli developed a clear, rounded hand based on Carolingian models, believing them to be Roman. Early printers in Italy adopted these forms, and Antiqua became the standard type for Latin texts across most of Europe. In Germany, however, blackletter remained dominant for German-language texts for centuries, leading to a long-running "Antiqua–Fraktur dispute" that lasted until the Nazi regime officially switched to Antiqua in 1941.

In modern German usage, "Antiqua" may refer to any Latin typeface as opposed to Fraktur, and also specifically to serif typefaces modelled on Renaissance forms.
