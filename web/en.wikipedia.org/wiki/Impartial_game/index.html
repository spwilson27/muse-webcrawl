Impartial game

Nim, the canonical example of an impartial game

In combinatorial game theory, an impartial game is a game in which the allowable moves depend only on the position of the game and not on which of the two players is currently moving, and where the payoffs are symmetric. In other words, the only difference between player 1 and player 2 is that player 1 goes first. The game is played until a terminal position is reached; the player who cannot move loses under normal play rules.

Theory

The Sprague–Grundy theorem states that every impartial game under the normal play convention is equivalent to a Nim heap of a certain size, called its Grundy value or nimber. A position is a losing position for the player to move exactly when its Grundy value is zero. This theorem reduces the analysis of sums of impartial games to computing Grundy values and combining them with the nim-sum (bitwise XOR).

Examples

Nim itself is the classic example: players alternately remove any positive number of counters from a single heap, and the position's Grundy value is simply the heap size. Other impartial games include Treblecross, Dawson's Kayles, Wythoff's game is partizan in appearance but effectively impartial variants, and the turning-turtles family of turning games such as Green Hackenbush.

Partizan games

Games in which the available moves differ between the players — such as chess, checkers, or Domineering — are called partizan games and require the broader theory developed by Conway, Berlekamp, and Guy.
