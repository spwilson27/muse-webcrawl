Evolutionary biology

Evolutionary biology is the subfield of biology that studies the evolutionary processes — natural selection, common descent, and speciation — that produced the diversity of life on Earth. It seeks to explain both the adaptation of organisms to their environments and the branching pattern of descent that unites all living things, and is sometimes summarised by Theodosius Dobzhansky's dictum that "nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution".

History

Ideas of organic transformation appeared in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, notably in Jean-Baptiste Lamarck's theory of inheritance of acquired characteristics (1809). The modern discipline began with Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace, who independently proposed evolution by natural selection, presented in Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859). Darwinism was fused with Mendelian genetics in the modern synthesis of the 1930s–40s by Fisher, Haldane, Wright, Dobzhansky, Mayr and others, establishing population genetics as the field's mathematical core. The discovery of DNA's structure and later genome sequencing added molecular evolution — including Motoo Kimura's neutral theory — and phylogenetics, while evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo") reconnected evolution with embryology.

Subfields and applications

Evolutionary biologists work across levels from molecules to ecosystems, in subfields such as speciation research, coevolution, life-history theory, sexual selection, paleobiology and experimental evolution — famously Richard Lenski's long-term evolution experiment with E. coli, begun in 1988. The field underpins medicine (antibiotic resistance, cancer evolution, pathogen tracking), agriculture (pest resistance, breeding) and conservation (genetic diversity, evolutionary significant units).
