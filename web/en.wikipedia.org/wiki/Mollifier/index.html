In mathematics, a mollifier (also called an approximate identity in this context) is a smooth function with special properties used to create sequences of smooth functions approximating nonsmooth (generalized) functions via convolution. Intuitively, given a function which is rather irregular, by convolving it with a mollifier it gets "mollified" — that is, its sharp features are smoothed, while still remaining close to the original nonsmooth function.

Definition

A mollifier is a function φ defined on Euclidean space that is smooth (infinitely differentiable), nonnegative, compactly supported (conventionally in the unit ball), and has integral equal to 1. The standard example is built from the bump function proportional to exp(−1/(1−|x|²)) for |x| < 1 and zero elsewhere. Rescaling gives a family φε(x) = ε−nφ(x/ε), whose support shrinks to the origin as ε → 0.

Convergence

Convolving a locally integrable function f with φε yields smooth functions f * φε that converge to f in Lp norm and pointwise at points of continuity (and uniformly on compact sets if f is continuous). The same smoothing technique applies to distributions, allowing nonsmooth objects to be approximated by smooth functions in arguments throughout partial differential equation theory.

Applications

Mollifiers are fundamental tools in analysis: they are used to prove that smooth functions are dense in Sobolev spaces, to justify the manipulation of weak derivatives, to define and regularise distributions, and in Friedrichs's theory of symmetric hyperbolic systems. The term was coined by Kurt Otto Friedrichs, who used mollifiers in his 1944 work on partial differential equations; related ideas appeared in the work of Sobolev and were further developed by Lars Hörmander and others.
