The northern gannet (Morus bassanus) is a seabird, the largest species of the gannet family, Sulidae. It is native to the coasts of the Atlantic Ocean, breeding in Western Europe and northeastern North America. It is distinguished by its white body, black-tipped wings, long pointed bill and tail, and a creamy yellow head and neck.

Behaviour and feeding

Northern gannets are spectacular plunge-divers: spotting shoals of mackerel, herring or sand eels from heights of up to 30 m, they fold their wings and strike the water at speeds approaching 100 km/h, cushioned by air sacs beneath the skin. They breed in dense colonies called gannetries on steep cliffs and rocky islands, laying a single chalky blue egg incubated on a mound of seaweed, held under their webbed feet. Notable colonies include Bass Rock in Scotland — from which the species takes its scientific name — St Kilda, Grassholm and Bonaventure Island in Quebec.

Conservation

Once persecuted for eggs, meat and feathers, the species recovered through the 20th century to roughly 1.5–1.8 million individuals. In 2022, however, highly pathogenic avian influenza devastated many colonies, killing tens of thousands of birds and sharply reducing numbers at Bass Rock and elsewhere; long-term recovery remains uncertain.
