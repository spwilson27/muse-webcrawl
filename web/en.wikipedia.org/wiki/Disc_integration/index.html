Disc integration

Disc integration, also known in integral calculus as the disc method, is a method of calculating the volume of a solid of revolution of a solid-state material when integrating along an axis "parallel" to the axis of revolution. This method models the resulting three-dimensional shape as a stack of an infinite number of discs of varying radius and infinitesimal thickness.

Method

If a region in the plane bounded by a curve y = f(x), the x-axis, and the vertical lines x = a and x = b is revolved around the x-axis, it generates a solid. Slicing the solid perpendicular to the axis at position x gives a circular disc of radius |f(x)|, hence cross-sectional area π[f(x)]2. Summing the discs and passing to the limit gives the volume

V = π ∫ab [f(x)]2 dx.

The same idea applied to a region between two curves produces the washer method, in which each slice is an annulus (a disc with a hole) whose area is the difference of two disc areas. Integration perpendicular to a horizontal axis handles revolutions about vertical lines analogously, with the roles of x and y exchanged.

Relation to other methods

Disc integration is an application of the general principle of computing volumes by cross-sections and is closely related to the method of cylindrical shells, which partitions the same solid into concentric tubes rather than slices. The two methods often apply to the same problem, with one or the other giving a simpler integrand depending on the geometry.
