Alpha diversity

In ecology, alpha diversity (α-diversity) is the mean species diversity in sites or habitats at a local scale. The term was introduced by R. H. Whittaker together with beta diversity (β) and gamma diversity (γ): alpha diversity describes diversity within a particular area or ecosystem, usually expressed by the number of species (species richness) and their relative abundances.

Measures

Common measures of alpha diversity include:

Species richness — the simple count of species in the sampled community.
Shannon index (Shannon entropy) — accounts for both richness and evenness of abundances.
Simpson index — the probability that two randomly drawn individuals belong to the same species (or its complement/inverse).
Fisher's alpha — a parameter of the log-series distribution fitted to species abundances.
Faith's phylogenetic diversity — the total branch length of the phylogenetic tree connecting all species in the community.

Scale considerations

Because alpha diversity depends on the size of the sampling area and sampling effort, comparisons between studies require standardisation, for example through rarefaction or species-accumulation curves. Whittaker's original scheme defined alpha diversity at the scale of a single habitat or sampling unit; beta diversity then measures the change in composition between habitats, and gamma diversity the total diversity of a landscape or region.

Applications

Alpha diversity is widely used in conservation biology to compare sites, in community ecology to test assembly theories, and in microbiome science, where within-sample alpha diversity (e.g. in gut microbiota studies) is routinely reported alongside between-sample beta diversity.
