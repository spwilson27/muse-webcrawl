Stan Brakhage

James Stanley Brakhage (January 14, 1933 – March 9, 2003), known as Stan Brakhage, was an American filmmaker and one of the most important figures in 20th-century experimental cinema. Over a career of more than five decades he made nearly 400 films, ranging from a few seconds to several hours, most of them silent and shot on 16 mm. His work explored birth, death, sexuality, and the nature of vision itself, and he developed influential techniques such as handheld camera movement, rapid montage, multiple exposures, and painting and scratching directly on film stock.

Early life

Brakhage was born in Kansas City, Missouri, and adopted as an infant. He sang as a boy soprano and studied at Dartmouth College briefly before dropping out to make films. He moved in circles that included filmmakers Kenneth Anger and Gregory Markopoulos, and settled in Colorado, where he lived for much of his life. He was married to Jane Collom (later Jane Brakhage), who appears in many early films, and the births of their children were documented in Window Water Baby Moving (1959), a landmark film.

Major works

Dog Star Man (1961–64), a feature-length cosmic epic in five parts, is often cited as his masterpiece. Other celebrated films include The Art of Vision (an expanded version of Dog Star Man), Scenes from Under Childhood, the "Songs" cycle, Mothlight (1963) — made by collaging moth wings, grass, and other materials directly onto film — The Text of Light (1974), filmed entirely through a glass ashtray's refractions, and the hand-painted The Dante Quartet (1987). His 1971 film The Act of Seeing with One's Own Eyes, shot in a Pittsburgh morgue, is regarded as one of the most powerful works of the American avant-garde.

Aesthetics and writings

In his book Metaphors on Vision (1963) Brakhage argued for a cinema of unmediated seeing — he asked readers to imagine "an eye unruled by man-made laws of perspective," free of the conventions of narrative film. He taught for many years at the University of Colorado Boulder, where he influenced generations of experimental filmmakers, and lectured widely. Diagnosed with bladder cancer (which he attributed in part to the dyes he used painting film), he died in Victoria, British Columbia, in 2003. His films are preserved and distributed by Canyon Cinema and the Film-Makers' Cooperative, and were the subject of the anthology By Brakhage released by the Criterion Collection.
