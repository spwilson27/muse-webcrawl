The God That Failed

The God That Failed is a 1949 anthology of essays by six writers — Arthur Koestler, Ignazio Silone, Richard Wright, André Gide, Louis Fischer and Stephen Spender — recounting their attraction to communism and their subsequent disillusionment with it. The collection was edited by the British Labour politician Richard Crossman and became a landmark text of the early Cold War.

Contents

Each contributor describes communism as a quasi-religious faith — the "god" of the title — adopted in the crisis years of the 1930s and abandoned in the face of the Moscow Trials, the Nazi–Soviet Pact, internal party discipline and direct experience of Soviet reality. Gide drew on his critical Return from the U.S.S.R. (1936); Wright wrote of racism within the American party; Koestler, once a Comintern agent, gave the volume's most intense account of conversion and apostasy.

Impact

The book was widely translated and shaped anti-communist liberal and social-democratic thought, offering a model of the confessional testimonial by ex-believers. Critics on the left questioned its framing and its use in Cold War cultural politics, some of it later linked to CIA-supported cultural programmes, though the essays' literary and historical influence has endured.
