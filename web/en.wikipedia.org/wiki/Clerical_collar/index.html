Clerical collar

A clerical collar, also called a dog collar or Roman collar, is an item of Christian clerical clothing. It is a small white band worn around the neck, attached to a (usually black) clerical shirt, and serves as a visible sign of ordained ministry. Collars come in two main styles: the full collar, a band encircling the neck entirely, and the tab collar, in which a small white square or "tab" of plastic or stiffened cloth shows at the throat of a specially made shirt.

History

Although popularly associated with Roman Catholic priests, the modern clerical collar is widely credited to the Church of Scotland minister Donald McLeod, who is said to have devised it in the mid-nineteenth century (the invention is often dated to 1865 in Glasgow). It spread through Protestant churches and was increasingly adopted by Catholic clergy as everyday dress in the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Use by denomination

Clerical collars are worn by clergy of the Roman Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, Presbyterian, Baptist, and many other churches, as well as by some chaplains and, in some traditions, seminarians and licensed lay ministers. The collar is sometimes nicknamed the "dog collar" in British usage. In popular culture, the unaccompanied collar on a black shirt functions as a universal visual shorthand for a member of the clergy, including in the dress of characters such as the Reverend in television dramas; colloquially, offences and defendants involving clergy have led to phrases such as "collar" for priests in some crime reporting.

Symbolism

Interpretations of the collar's meaning vary: it has been described as a sign of the wearer's obedience and servitude to God (likened to a slave's or dog's collar, from which the nickname derives), a yoke of service, and a reminder of the preacher's office. In practice it also functions pragmatically, making clergy identifiable in hospitals, prisons, and public ministry.
