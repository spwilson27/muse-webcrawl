WorldWide Telescope (WWT) is an open-source set of applications, data, and cloud services, originally created by Microsoft Research, that operates as a "virtual telescope": it renders terabytes of astronomical imagery — from ground- and space-based surveys — as a seamless, explorable sky, alongside 3D views of the Earth, planets, and the Solar System. It is widely used in astronomy education, planetariums, and research visualisation.

History

The project was conceived at Microsoft Research, influenced by Jim Gray's work on online sky databases such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey's SkyServer. WWT was announced at the TED conference in February 2008 and released publicly that spring, with Curtis Wong and Jonathan Fay among its principal creators. Microsoft made WWT open source in 2015, and stewardship passed to the American Astronomical Society (AAS), which now maintains the project with community contributions. The web client, rewritten using WebGL and the "pywwt" Python tools for Jupyter notebooks, became the primary platform.

Features

Users can pan and zoom across all-sky imagery from surveys including the Digitized Sky Survey, 2MASS, WISE, and Planck, switch wavelengths, view planets in 3D, and access data overlays from catalogues. A distinctive feature is guided tours: narrated, multimedia paths through the data authored by astronomers and educators. WWT powers many planetarium shows and supports the TOAST projection scheme for tiling the sphere, enabling dome displays. The AAS integrates WWT with its journals so that figures in papers can link to interactive data views.

Use in education and research

WWT is used in classrooms worldwide and in citizen-science and outreach programmes. Its data-visualisation engine has also been adapted for Earth and planetary science data. It is frequently compared with Google Sky, Stellarium, and Celestia, differing in its emphasis on research-grade survey imagery and tour authoring.
