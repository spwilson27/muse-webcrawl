Jensen–Shannon divergence

The Jensen–Shannon divergence measures how different two distributions are from their mixture.

In probability theory and statistics, the Jensen–Shannon divergence is a method of measuring the similarity between two probability distributions. It is based on the Kullback–Leibler divergence, but unlike that measure it is symmetric and always takes a finite value. The square root of the Jensen–Shannon divergence is a metric, often called the Jensen–Shannon distance. The measure is named for the Danish mathematicians Johan Jensen and Claude Shannon.

Definition

Given two probability distributions P and Q, let M = ½(P + Q) be their average (mixture) distribution. The Jensen–Shannon divergence is:

JSD(P ‖ Q) = ½ D(P ‖ M) + ½ D(Q ‖ M)

where D(· ‖ ·) denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Equivalently, it can be expressed in terms of Shannon entropy H:

JSD(P ‖ Q) = H(M) − ½ H(P) − ½ H(Q)

Properties

The divergence is symmetric, JSD(P ‖ Q) = JSD(Q ‖ P), and it is zero only when P = Q. With base-2 logarithms it is bounded between 0 and 1; with natural logarithms the bound is ln 2. Unlike the Kullback–Leibler divergence, it remains well-defined and finite when one distribution assigns zero probability to an event the other supports. Its square root satisfies the triangle inequality, making it a true metric on the space of probability distributions.

Applications

The Jensen–Shannon divergence is used in machine learning — notably as the quantity implicitly optimized by the original generative adversarial network objective — as well as in bioinformatics and genomics for comparing profiles, in image registration, in natural language processing for comparing word and document distributions, and in ecology for comparing species-abundance distributions. Generalizations to more than two distributions (weighted by mixing proportions) are common in Bayesian model comparison and clustering.
