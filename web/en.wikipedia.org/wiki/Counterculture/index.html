Counterculture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Hippies dancing at an open-air festival in 1969, flowers and long hair everywhere]
Hippies dancing at an open-air festival in 1969, flowers and long hair everywhere

A counterculture is a culture whose values and norms substantially oppose those of the mainstream society. The term is most associated with the youth movement of the 1960s in the West — crystallised around opposition to the Vietnam War, civil rights, psychedelic drugs, rock music, sexual liberation and communal experiments — but applies more broadly, from the Beat Generation and bohemians before it to punk, rave and digital cultures after.

The 1960s counterculture

Theorised by Theodore Roszak (who coined the term in The Making of a Counter Culture, 1969), the movement fused the New Left's politics with a cultural revolution of lifestyle: communes, underground press, be-ins and festivals (Woodstock, 1969), Eastern spirituality and psychedelics evangelised by Timothy Leary. Its fractures included the violence of 1968–70 — Chicago, Altamont, the Manson murders — and commercialisation that absorbed its styles into advertising within years.

Precursors and successors

Histories trace the lineage through Romanticism, Parisian bohemia, Dada, the Beats (Kerouac, Ginsberg) and British mods and rockers to the hippies, then to punk's negation of hippie optimism, hip hop's Bronx insurgency, rave culture, and internet-native countercultures. Each wave repeats the cycle of emergence, media panic, commodification and dispersal into the mainstream it opposed.

Legacy

The 1960s counterculture's victories are cultural commonplaces now — relaxed sexual mores, environmentalism, organic food, casual dress, world music — while its political projects largely stalled. Historians debate whether it liberated or merely entertained the societies it scandalised.
