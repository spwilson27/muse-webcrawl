Khalsa Army

The Khalsa Army (also called the Sikh Khalsa Army or Punjab Army) was the standing army of the Sikh Empire in the Punjab during the first half of the 19th century. Reorganised and modernised by Maharaja Ranjit Singh from the 1820s with the help of European officers, it became one of the most powerful indigenous armies in Asia, and fought the British East India Company in the two Anglo-Sikh wars of 1845–46 and 1848–49.

Origins

The Khalsa tradition of Sikh arms dates to Guru Gobind Singh, who founded the Khalsa order in 1699. Through the 18th century Sikh misls (confederacies) fielded largely cavalry forces against Mughal and Afghan invaders. When Ranjit Singh unified the Punjab and took Lahore in 1799, he inherited this military tradition but recognised the superiority of European-drilled infantry and artillery.

Modernisation

From 1822 Ranjit Singh employed former Napoleonic officers — including Jean-François Allard, Jean-Baptiste Ventura, Claude Auguste Court and Paolo Avitabile — to train his forces. The regular army, the Fauj-i-Ain, was organised into infantry regiments drilled on the French model, cavalry, and a powerful artillery arm (Fauj-i-Khas and the topkhana), with foundries at Lahore casting modern guns. At its peak the army numbered perhaps 70,000–100,000 men, including irregular cavalry (Fauj-i-Be Qawaid) and the feared Akali Nihang religious warriors.

Anglo-Sikh wars and dissolution

After Ranjit Singh's death in 1839, political instability and the army's own panchayats (soldiers' committees) destabilised the state. In the First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46) the Khalsa Army fought the British hard at Mudki, Ferozeshah, Aliwal and Sobraon before being defeated; in the Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–49), battles at Chillianwala and Gujrat ended with the British annexation of the Punjab in 1849 and the disbandment of the Khalsa Army. Many of its soldiers subsequently enlisted in the British Indian Army, where Sikhs became prized "martial race" recruits.
