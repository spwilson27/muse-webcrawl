Free church
Related termsNonconformism, dissenters, state church
Typical featuresVoluntary membership, congregational support, separation from the state

A free church is a Christian denomination or congregation that is independent of control by the state — "free" in the sense of being free from government establishment, funding and regulation of its doctrine and worship. The term stands in contrast to a state church (or established church), which is officially recognised and supported by the government.

Characteristics

Free churches are usually supported by the voluntary contributions of their members rather than by taxation, and membership is by personal choice — often sealed by believer's baptism or a public profession of faith — rather than by birth within a parish. Most free churches emphasise the autonomy of the local congregation and the "priesthood of all believers", and many have historically campaigned for the separation of church and state and for religious toleration.

History

The concept developed from the radical wing of the Reformation (the Anabaptists) and from English Dissent: after the Restoration of 1660 and the Act of Uniformity 1662, ministers and congregations who refused to conform to the Church of England formed independent churches, including Baptists, Congregationalists (Independents), Presbyterians outside Scotland, and later Methodists and the Society of Friends (Quakers). In England and Wales these bodies became known collectively as the "Free Churches", and in 1896 they formed the Free Church Federal Council to coordinate their work.

In Scotland, the Free Church of Scotland was created in the Disruption of 1843, when about a third of the Church of Scotland's ministers left over the issue of patronage and the spiritual independence of the church. In Scandinavia, free churches arose in the nineteenth century outside the Lutheran state churches. In the United States, the First Amendment's prohibition on an establishment of religion made all churches "free" in this sense, and the term is applied especially to independent and non-denominational congregations.
