Botrytis cinerea is a necrotrophic fungus that affects many plant species, causing the disease known as grey mould. It is one of the most economically important plant pathogens in the world, damaging grapes, strawberries, tomatoes, ornamentals and many other crops in the field and in storage. Under the right conditions on wine grapes, however, the same fungus produces the prized "noble rot" behind Sauternes, Tokaji and German Beerenauslese wines.

[ Image: Grey mould sporulating on a bunch of grapes, showing ashen conidiophores ]

Biology

The name Botrytis derives from the Greek botrys, a bunch of grapes, describing the clusters of conidia borne on branched conidiophores; cinerea refers to their ash-grey colour. The fungus overwinters as hard black sclerotia or mycelium in plant debris. Spores are dispersed by air and splashing water and germinate in cool, humid conditions, killing host cells in advance of colonisation with toxins and cell-wall-degrading enzymes.

Disease and control

Grey mould rots fruits, flowers and leaves, covering them with fuzzy grey-brown growth. It is especially severe in dense canopies and in stored produce. Management combines sanitation, ventilation and canopy management with fungicides — against which B. cinerea, with its high genetic variability, notoriously evolves multiple resistance — and biological control agents.

Noble rot

When infection of ripe white grapes is followed by dry conditions, berries shrivel as water is lost, concentrating sugars and flavours and adding distinctive honeyed, apricot notes. This benign form of infection is deliberately encouraged in several classic sweet-wine regions.
