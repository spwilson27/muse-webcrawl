Ancient Greek athletics occupied a central place in Greek culture, combining physical training, religious festival and civic prestige. From the traditional founding of the Olympic Games in 776 BC, athletic competitions were held at hundreds of festivals across the Greek world; the four Panhellenic "crown games" — the Olympics at Olympia, the Pythian Games at Delphi, the Nemean Games and the Isthmian Games — formed the most prestigious circuit (periodos).

Events

The programme centred on foot races (the stadion sprint of about 192 metres, the two-stade diaulos, and the long-distance dolichos), the pentathlon (discus, javelin, long jump, running and wrestling), and the combat sports: wrestling, boxing and the pankration, a near-no-holds-barred mixture of the two. Equestrian events, above all the four-horse chariot race, were open to wealthy owners who need not drive themselves. Athletes competed nude, coated in olive oil, a practice the Greeks regarded as distinctly Hellenic.

Training and social role

Cities maintained gymnasia where boys and young men trained under a paidotribes, supervised in athletics, music and letters as part of the ideal of balanced education. Victorious athletes won only wreaths at the crown games but received lavish rewards, statues, free meals and poetic commemoration (Pindar's victory odes) at home. By the Hellenistic and Roman periods athletics had become a professional pursuit, with athletes' guilds, star performers and a dense calendar of prize games. Famous athletes include Milo of Croton, the wrestler said to have won six Olympic titles, and Leonidas of Rhodes, winner of twelve sprint crowns in the 2nd century BC.
