Card stunt

Card stunt
TypeCoordinated crowd display
OriginAmerican college football, early 20th century
Also known asCard section display

A card stunt is a coordinated display in which members of a stadium crowd hold up coloured cards or boards on cue to form a picture, message or pattern visible across the stand. Each participant follows a numbered sequence of cards placed at their seat, changing them at signals so that the section acts as a low-resolution image screen.

History

Card stunts developed in American college football in the 1910s and 1920s. Lindley Bothwell, a cheerleader at Oregon Agricultural College and later USC, is credited with early organised card sections around 1910, and the University of California, Berkeley became famous for elaborate stunts directed by its student section. At their peak, major college card sections coordinated thousands of students flipping sequences of dozens of coloured cards.

The most infamous card-stunt moment was a prank: during the 1961 Rose Bowl, Caltech students altered the instruction sheets of the University of Washington card section so that it spelled out "CALTECH" — a celebrated hack in collegiate lore.

Modern use

Although traditional student card sections have declined, card displays remain common at major sporting events, Olympic ceremonies and football matches, where clubs or organisers pre-place cards and mosaics (choreographies known as tifo in European supporter culture) are unveiled in the stands.
