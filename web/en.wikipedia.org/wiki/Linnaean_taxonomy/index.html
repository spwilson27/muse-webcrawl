Linnaean taxonomy

OriginatorCarl Linnaeus
Key workSystema Naturae (1735)
BasisRanked hierarchy; binomial nomenclature

Linnaean taxonomy is a system of classification of organisms devised by the Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus and set out in his Systema Naturae (1735) and later works. It classifies living things into a nested hierarchy of ranks and names each species with a two-part Latinised binomial, consisting of a generic name and a specific epithet (for example, Homo sapiens).

Principles

Linnaeus originally recognised three kingdoms — animals, plants and minerals — and grouped organisms chiefly by shared physical characteristics, such as the number and arrangement of reproductive parts in plants. His classification of animals in the 10th edition of Systema Naturae (1758) is the starting point of zoological nomenclature, and his Species Plantarum (1753) plays the same role for most plants.

Modern use

Although modern taxonomy is grounded in evolutionary relationships and cladistics rather than overall resemblance, the Linnaean ranks and binomial names remain the universal framework of biological nomenclature, administered through codes such as the ICZN and ICN. Proposals such as the PhyloCode offer rank-free alternatives but have not displaced the Linnaean system.
