Fermat polygonal number theorem

The Fermat polygonal number theorem states that every positive integer is a sum of at most n n-gonal (polygonal) numbers. It was conjectured by Pierre de Fermat in the 17th century, who claimed to have a proof that was never found; the theorem was proved progressively over the following two centuries.

Special cases

  • Three, triangular numbers (n = 3): Every natural number is the sum of at most three triangular numbers. Proved by Gauss in 1796, who recorded the discovery in his diary with the note "ΕΥΡΗΚΑ! num = Δ + Δ + Δ".
  • Four, square numbers (n = 4): Every natural number is the sum of at most four square numbers—Lagrange's four-square theorem, proved by Lagrange in 1770.
  • Five, pentagonal numbers (n = 5): Proved by Cauchy in 1813, who completed the general theorem at the same time; the full result is sometimes called Cauchy's polygonal number theorem.

Statement

The n-gonal numbers are given by the formula Pn(k) = ((n − 2)k² − (n − 4)k) / 2 for k = 0, 1, 2, … (the sequence begins 0, 1, …). The theorem asserts that for every positive integer m, there exist nonnegative integers k1, …, kn such that m = Pn(k1) + … + Pn(kn).

History

Fermat stated the result in a letter and in marginal notes connected with Diophantus's Arithmetica, claiming a proof by "descent." Euler and Lagrange made progress; Gauss settled the triangular case; Legendre and Cauchy contributed, with Cauchy presenting a complete proof in 1813 using his lemma on representations with bounded numbers of large terms.
