Apparent retrograde motion

Apparent retrograde motion is the apparent motion of a planet in a direction opposite to that of other bodies within its system, as observed from a particular vantage point. While a planet moves eastward (prograde) against the background stars for most of the time as seen from Earth, at intervals it appears to slow, stop, and move westward for weeks or months before resuming its eastward course, tracing a loop or zigzag in the sky. This retrograde motion is an optical consequence of the relative positions and speeds of Earth and the planet: it does not involve any actual reversal in the planet's orbit.

As Earth overtakes an outer planet, the planet appears to loop backwards against the background stars.

Explanation

For a superior planet (one farther from the Sun than Earth, such as Mars, Jupiter, or Saturn), retrograde motion occurs around opposition, when the planet lies opposite the Sun in the sky. Because Earth travels faster in its smaller orbit, it overtakes the slower planet "on the inside lane". Just as a driver overtaking a slower car sees it drift backwards against the distant landscape, the outer planet appears to move backwards against the far more distant stars. For the inferior planets Mercury and Venus, which orbit inside Earth's orbit, retrograde motion occurs around inferior conjunction, when they pass between Earth and the Sun; their retrograde periods are shorter and coincide with their being difficult or impossible to observe in the Sun's glare.

Historical significance

Retrograde motion was a central puzzle of ancient astronomy. The planets were called planētai ("wanderers") precisely because of these irregular paths. To preserve the principle of uniform circular motion in a geocentric universe, Greek astronomers developed increasingly elaborate models: Apollonius of Perga introduced epicycles — small circles whose centres moved along larger circles (deferents) — and Ptolemy's Almagest (2nd century AD) refined this scheme with eccentrics and equants, reproducing retrograde loops with good accuracy. Copernicus's heliocentric model (1543) explained retrograde motion naturally as a perspective effect of Earth's own motion, one of the model's great conceptual advantages, though Copernicus retained circles and small epicycles for accuracy. Kepler's elliptical orbits finally rendered the phenomenon a straightforward geometric consequence.

Duration and frequency

Retrograde episodes recur once per synodic period for each planet. Mars retrogrades for about 72 days every 26 months; Jupiter for about 121 days every 13 months; Saturn for about 138 days every 12.4 months; Mercury, moving fastest, retrogrades three or four times a year for roughly three weeks each — a frequency that has made "Mercury retrograde" a fixture of popular astrology, though astronomers note the phenomenon has no known physical influence on terrestrial affairs.

Retrograde rotation and orbits

Apparent retrograde motion should not be confused with genuine retrograde rotation (as with Venus and Uranus, which spin in the opposite sense to most planets) or with retrograde orbits of satellites and some exoplanets, in which a body truly orbits opposite to the primary's rotation.
