Ancient Semitic religion

Ancient Semitic religion is an umbrella term for the polytheistic religions of the Semitic peoples of the ancient Near East and their diasporas, spanning Mesopotamia, the Levant, the Arabian Peninsula, Ethiopia, and North Africa from the third millennium BCE into the early centuries CE. It encompasses the religions of the Akkadians, Amorites, Ugarit and the Canaanites, Phoenicians, ancient Israelites and Judahites, Aramaeans, pre-Islamic Arabs, and others.

Common features

Despite wide regional diversity, the Semitic religions shared recognisable features: a pantheon headed by a high god (often El or a local equivalent), storm and fertility deities such as Baal/Hadad, astral gods of sun, moon, and Venus (Ishtar/Astarte), and a divine council or assembly of the gods. Worship centred on temples and high places, animal and grain sacrifice, libations, seasonal festivals keyed to agriculture, sacred stones and trees, and the veneration of ancestors. Semitic deities were deeply involved in kingship: the king was the god's earthly representative and temple-builder.

Regional traditions

In Mesopotamia, the Semitic Akkadians adopted and reshaped Sumerian religion, producing the traditions of Babylon and Assyria with gods such as Marduk and Ashur. In the Levant, the Ugaritic and Canaanite traditions centred on El, Baal, and Asherah. The Phoenicians carried their gods — Baal, Melqart, Astarte, and Tanit — across the Mediterranean to Carthage and beyond. In South Arabia, temple religions honoured Almaqah and the moon god; in the north, Nabataean and Palmyrene cults flourished into the Roman era.

Transition to monotheism

From within the West Semitic world emerged the monotheistic traditions that would transform global religion: Israelite Yahwism developed into Judaism, from which Christianity grew, and in Arabia the older polytheism gave way by the seventh century CE to Islam. Study of ancient Semitic religion, transformed by the decipherment of cuneiform and the discovery of the Ugaritic texts, illuminates the background of all three traditions.
