"The Nose" (Russian: Нос, romanized: Nos) is a satirical short story by Nikolai Gogol, written during 1835–1836 and first published in 1836 in the literary journal Sovremennik (The Contemporary), which Alexander Pushkin had founded. One of Gogol's "Petersburg tales," it tells of a minor St. Petersburg official whose nose leaves his face and develops a life of its own, attaining a higher rank than its owner. The story is a celebrated early example of absurdist and surrealist literature and a sharp satire of the rank-obsessed bureaucracy of Imperial Russia.

Plot

On March 25, the barber Ivan Yakovlevich finds a human nose baked inside his morning loaf of bread. Recognising it as belonging to his customer, Collegiate Assessor Kovalyov — who insists on being called "Major" — the terrified barber wraps it in a cloth and throws it into the Neva River from St Isaac's Bridge, only to be accosted by a police officer as he does so.

Meanwhile Kovalyov wakes to find his face completely smooth where his nose should be. Horrified, he hurries out to report the loss and encounters his own nose in Kazan Cathedral — dressed in the gold-braided uniform of a State Councillor, a rank three grades above Kovalyov's own, and absorbed in prayer. Too intimidated by his nose's superior status to make a scene, Kovalyov begs it to return to his face; the nose refuses to understand him and slips away. Kovalyov's attempts to advertise for the lost nose in a newspaper are rejected as undignified, a police official he petitions is unhelpful, and doctors refuse to reattach it even after the nose is caught trying to flee the city by stagecoach. Eventually the police return the nose in a handkerchief, but it will not stick to Kovalyov's face. On April 7, however, he wakes to find the nose inexplicably back in place, and life resumes its ordinary course. The narrator closes by marvelling at the absurdity — while insisting such things do happen in the world.

Themes and interpretation

The story lampoons the Table of Ranks that governed status in the Russian civil service: Kovalyov's distress is less about disfigurement than about the social impossibility of appearing without a nose before people of rank, and his humiliation at being outranked by his own appendage distils Gogol's critique of a society organised by hierarchy and appearances. Its deadpan narration of impossible events anticipated later absurdist and surrealist writing; Nabokov considered Gogol the greatest Russian writer and "The Nose" among his finest shorter works.

Adaptations

Dmitri Shostakovich based his first opera, The Nose (Op. 15, premiered 1930), on the story, blending it with other Gogol texts. The story has also been adapted for theatre, film and animation, and the Russian idiom "to be left with the nose" (ostat'sya s nosom, to be duped) resonates through the tale.
