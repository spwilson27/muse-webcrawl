A North American beaver (Castor canadensis)

The North American beaver (Castor canadensis) is one of two extant beaver species, alongside the Eurasian beaver (Castor fiber). It is native to North America and has been introduced to parts of Europe and South America. It is the national animal of Canada and appears on the Canadian five-cent coin.

Description

The North American beaver is the largest rodent in North America. It has a stout body, webbed hind feet, a large flat paddle-shaped tail covered in scales, and dense waterproof brown fur. Its continually growing incisors, stained orange by iron in the enamel, allow it to fell trees. Beavers are semiaquatic and mainly nocturnal.

Behaviour

Beavers are famed as ecosystem engineers. Family groups — monogamous pairs with offspring of two years — build dams of sticks, mud and stones across streams, creating ponds that protect their lodges from predators and provide access to food. They feed on the bark and cambium of trees such as aspen, willow and birch, as well as aquatic plants, caching branches underwater for winter.

History and conservation

Beaver pelts drove much of the North American fur trade; by 1900 trapping had nearly exterminated the species across much of its range. Reintroductions and regulation have restored populations, estimated at 10–15 million. Introduced beavers in Tierra del Fuego, by contrast, are an invasive pest damaging sub-Antarctic forests.
