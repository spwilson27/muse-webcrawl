Uranium-233

Uranium-233 is a fissile isotope of uranium bred from thorium-232 as part of the thorium fuel cycle. It does not occur in nature in significant quantities. Like uranium-235 and plutonium-239, it can sustain a nuclear chain reaction and is usable in nuclear reactors and, in principle, in nuclear weapons.

Production

U-233 is produced when thorium-232 absorbs a neutron to become thorium-233, which beta decays to protactinium-233 and then to uranium-233. It can be bred in thermal breeder reactors, molten salt reactors, and other designs using thorium. The liquid fluoride thorium reactor (LFTR) concept is built around this cycle. Chemically separating protactinium-233 and allowing it to decay outside the reactor improves breeding efficiency.

Properties and complications

U-233 has favourable neutron economy in thermal spectra — it releases more neutrons per neutron absorbed than U-235 or Pu-239 in thermal reactors — making thermal breeding feasible. However, bred U-233 is invariably contaminated with traces of uranium-232, whose decay chain includes thallium-208, an emitter of penetrating 2.6 MeV gamma radiation. This complicates handling and fabrication but also makes the material self-protecting and easier to detect, features cited in proliferation debates. The half-life of U-233 is about 159,200 years.

History

U-233 was produced in quantity during the Cold War for research, reactor experiments (such as the Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment at Oak Ridge), and limited weapons-related testing; the United States detonated a device using U-233 in the 1955 Operation Teapot MET shot. India, with large thorium reserves, has pursued a three-stage nuclear programme whose final stage envisions U-233-fuelled reactors, including the KAMINI research reactor, the world's only operating U-233-fuelled reactor.
