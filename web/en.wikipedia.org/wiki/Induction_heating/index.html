Induction heating

Induction heating is the process of heating an electrically conducting object (usually a metal) by electromagnetic induction, through heat generated in the object itself by eddy currents. An induction heater consists of an electromagnet and an electronic oscillator that passes a high-frequency alternating current (AC) through the electromagnet. The rapidly alternating magnetic field penetrates the object, generating eddy currents inside the conductor, and resistive heating (Joule heating) of the material results. In ferromagnetic materials such as iron, inductive heating is augmented by magnetic hysteresis losses.

The frequency of the current used depends on the object size, material type, coupling and the desired penetration depth. Frequencies from a few hundred hertz for large steel melting furnaces to several megahertz for small parts are common.

Principles

Because heat is generated inside the workpiece itself, induction heating is fast, repeatable and efficient. The depth to which the eddy currents penetrate is governed by the skin effect: penetration decreases as frequency increases. The reference depth at which current density falls to 1/e of its surface value is a key design parameter.

Applications

Applications include surface hardening of gears and shafts, brazing and soldering, shrink fitting, forging, melting of metals in crucible furnaces, sealing of containers, curing of coatings, and domestic induction cooking. In cooking, a coil beneath a glass-ceramic surface induces currents directly in a ferromagnetic pan, heating the food while the surface itself stays comparatively cool.

History

The principle was demonstrated in the 19th century following the work of Michael Faraday on electromagnetic induction. Early industrial furnaces appeared around 1900, and the development of solid-state power electronics in the later 20th century made compact, efficient induction heaters widely available.
