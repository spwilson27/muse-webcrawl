Aramaic is a Northwest Semitic language that originated among the Arameans of the ancient Near East. It rose to become the lingua franca of the Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid Persian empires, and remained a dominant language of the Levant and Mesopotamia for over a thousand years, until it was gradually displaced by Arabic following the Muslim conquests of the 7th century AD.

History

Old Aramaic is attested in inscriptions from the 9th century BC in the Aramean city-states of Syria. Adopted for administration by the Assyrian and Babylonian empires, it became under the Achaemenids a standardised chancery language — Imperial (or Official) Aramaic — written in a 22-letter consonantal alphabet derived from Phoenician. This alphabet is the ancestor, directly or indirectly, of the Hebrew, Syriac, Arabic and many other scripts. In the Hellenistic and Roman periods, Aramaic diversified into Eastern and Western dialects.

Religious significance

Parts of the Hebrew Bible — sections of Ezra and Daniel, and isolated verses elsewhere — are written in Biblical Aramaic. Aramaic was the everyday language of Jews in Roman Palestine and is generally regarded as the native language of Jesus; several of his sayings are preserved in Aramaic in the Gospels (such as talitha cumi and the cry Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani). The Jewish Targums (Aramaic translations of Scripture), much of the Talmud, and the Kaddish and Kol Nidre prayers are in Aramaic. Among Christians, the Syriac dialect produced a major literature and remains the liturgical language of several churches; the Peshitta is the standard Syriac Bible. Mandaic is the sacred language of Mandaeism.

Modern Aramaic

Today the Neo-Aramaic languages are spoken by an estimated several hundred thousand people, mainly Assyrian Christians in northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, northwestern Iran and Syria, and in a global diaspora — communities severely disrupted by the Assyrian genocide of 1915 and more recent conflicts, including the persecution under Islamic State. Many varieties are endangered. The village of Maaloula in Syria was long noted for preserving a Western Neo-Aramaic dialect close to the language of the Roman period.
