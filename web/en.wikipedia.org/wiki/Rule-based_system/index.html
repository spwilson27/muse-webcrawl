Rule-based system

In computer science, a rule-based system is a system in which knowledge is represented as a set of rules, typically of the form if (condition) then (action or conclusion), and an inference engine applies those rules to facts in working memory to derive new facts or trigger actions. Rule-based systems were a central technology of classical artificial intelligence and formed the core of most expert systems built in the 1970s and 1980s.

Architecture

A typical rule-based system has three components:

  • A knowledge base of rules and facts;
  • A working memory holding the current set of asserted facts;
  • An inference engine that matches rule conditions against working memory and fires rules whose conditions are satisfied.

Inference may proceed by forward chaining (data-driven: rules fire as their premises become true) or backward chaining (goal-driven: the system works backwards from a hypothesis to the facts needed to support it). When several rules match, a conflict-resolution strategy — such as specificity, recency or rule ordering — selects which fires.

History

Rule-based production systems grew out of Emil Post's production systems of the 1940s and were developed in the 1970s in systems such as DENDRAL and MYCIN at Stanford, and the OPS family of languages at Carnegie Mellon. The Rete algorithm, published by Charles Forgy in 1979, made matching large rule sets against changing working memories efficient and underlies many modern rule engines, including CLIPS, Jess and Drools.

Applications

Rule-based systems have been used for medical diagnosis, equipment configuration (such as DEC's XCON), credit assessment, business rules management, and industrial process control. Their main advantages are modularity — rules can be added or removed independently — and explainability, since the chain of fired rules can be shown as a justification. Their disadvantages include difficulty in maintaining large rule sets and brittleness outside the situations anticipated by the knowledge engineer.
