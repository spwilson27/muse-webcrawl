Hypovitaminosis

Hypovitaminosis is a deficiency of one or more essential vitamins, caused by inadequate intake, impaired absorption, increased requirements, or impaired metabolic activation. Severe deficiencies produce classic syndromes — scurvy (vitamin C), beriberi and Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome (thiamine, B1), pellagra (niacin, B3), rickets and osteomalacia (vitamin D), night blindness and xerophthalmia (vitamin A), megaloblastic anemia (B12 and folate), and bleeding disorders (vitamin K). Milder "subclinical" deficiencies may be common and harder to detect, with non-specific effects on immunity and health.

Causes and risk factors

Risk factors include poverty and restricted diets, alcohol use disorder, malabsorptive conditions (coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, bariatric surgery), chronic kidney or liver disease, pregnancy, advanced age, and medications that interfere with vitamin metabolism (such as methotrexate or isoniazid). Fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies cluster with fat malabsorption.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines clinical suspicion with dietary history and, where available, measurement of vitamin levels or functional markers (for example, methylmalonic acid for B12 status). Treatment is replacement of the deficient vitamin, oral or parenteral depending on severity and absorptive capacity, together with correction of the underlying cause. Excessive supplementation can produce the opposite problem, hypervitaminosis, particularly with fat-soluble vitamins A and D.
