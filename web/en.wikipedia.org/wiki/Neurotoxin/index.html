Neurotoxin

Neurotoxins are toxins that are destructive to nerve tissue, causing neurotoxicity. Neurotoxins are an extensive class of exogenous chemical neurological insults that can adversely affect function in both developing and mature nervous tissue. The term can also be used to classify endogenous compounds which, when abnormally contacted, can prove neurologically toxic. Neurotoxins are frequently, though not universally, proteins or peptides; some, such as tetrodotoxin and the heavy metals, are not.

Mechanisms of action

Neurotoxins act on the proteins that control neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission. Sodium channel toxins illustrate the range: tetrodotoxin and saxitoxin block voltage-gated sodium channels and prevent action potentials, while batrachotoxin and scorpion toxins hold them open. Botulinum toxins cleave SNARE proteins and so prevent acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, causing flaccid paralysis; tetanus toxin travels to inhibitory interneurons and blocks glycine and GABA release, causing spastic paralysis. Curare and α-bungarotoxin antagonise nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, and organophosphate compounds inhibit acetylcholinesterase.

Sources

Neurotoxins occur throughout nature: in snake, spider, scorpion, and cone snail venoms; in bacteria such as Clostridium botulinum and Clostridium tetani; in algae responsible for shellfish poisoning; and in plants such as hemlock. Some are synthesised deliberately as chemical weapons or pesticides, the nerve agents sarin and VX being extreme examples of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors.

Applications

Despite their danger, neurotoxins are invaluable research tools for dissecting ion channel function, and botulinum toxin, in minute controlled doses, is used therapeutically and cosmetically — for spasticity, dystonia, chronic migraine, and the treatment marketed as Botox.
