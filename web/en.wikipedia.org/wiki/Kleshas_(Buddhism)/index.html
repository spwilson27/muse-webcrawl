Kleshas (Buddhism)

Sanskritkleśa (क्लेश)
Palikilesa
TranslationsAfflictions, defilements, destructive emotions, poisons
Root threeGreed (rāga), aversion (dveṣa), delusion (moha)

Kleshas (Sanskrit: क्लेश, kleśa; Pali: kilesa) in Buddhism are mental states that cloud the mind and manifest in unwholesome actions. They are commonly translated as afflictions, defilements, negative emotions, or mind poisons, and they are understood to be the root cause of suffering (duḥkha) and continued rebirth in saṃsāra.

The three poisons

The most fundamental kleshas are the three poisons (or three unwholesome roots, akuśala-mūla):

  • Rāga — attachment, greed, or sensual desire (symbolized by a rooster)
  • Dveṣa — aversion, hatred, or anger (symbolized by a snake)
  • Moha — delusion or ignorance, closely related to avidyā, the misperception of reality (symbolized by a pig)

In the Tibetan Buddhist wheel of life (bhavacakra), the three animals chase one another in a circle at the hub, depicting how the poisons drive the cycle of rebirth. In the twelve nidānas, ignorance stands as the first link in dependent origination.

Classifications

Texts enumerate kleshas in various lists. The Abhidharma traditions analyze dozens of afflictive mental factors (caitta), including six root kleshas: attachment, aversion, ignorance, pride (māna), doubt (vicikitsā), and wrong view (dṛṣṭi). Earlier sources list the three unwholesome roots and the five hindrances (pañca nīvaraṇa) that obstruct meditation. The Yogācāra school of Asaṅga systematized fifty-two mental factors, of which a large group are afflictive.

Abandonment in practice

Kleshas are not merely suppressed but understood and uprooted through the Buddhist path: ethical discipline weakens their expression, concentration calms them, and insight (prajñā) into impermanence, suffering, and non-self eradicates them. In Abhidharma terms, they are abandoned in stages — by seeing (darśana) at stream-entry and by cultivation (bhāvanā) thereafter — until the arhat is free of all afflictions. Mahāyāna literature contrasts the afflictive obscurations (kleśāvaraṇa) with the cognitive obscurations (jñeyāvaraṇa) that a buddha alone removes.
