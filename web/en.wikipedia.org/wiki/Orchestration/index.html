Orchestration

Related terms: Instrumentation, arrangement

Classic treatises: Berlioz (1844); Rimsky-Korsakov, Principles of Orchestration

Practitioners: Orchestrators, arrangers, composers

Orchestration is the art and practice of writing music for an orchestra, or adapting music composed for another medium to the orchestra. It involves assigning notes to particular instruments and combining them so that balance, colour and expressive character serve the composition. The study of the ranges, techniques and timbres of instruments themselves is called instrumentation.

History

In the Baroque and early Classical eras, instrumentation was often flexible, but composers such as Haydn and Mozart fixed the orchestra's core of strings, paired winds and brass. The nineteenth century expanded the palette enormously: Hector Berlioz's Treatise on Instrumentation (1844, revised by Richard Strauss) systematised the field, while Wagner, Mahler and Strauss demanded ever larger forces and novel blends. Rimsky-Korsakov's Principles of Orchestration, drawn from his own scores, remains a standard text.

Technique

Orchestrators consider each instrument's range, dynamic capacity in each register, agility and characteristic colour; how doublings reinforce or blur a line; and how registers may be spaced so that parts remain clear. Maurice Ravel's orchestration of Mussorgsky's piano suite Pictures at an Exhibition is a celebrated example of re-composing another composer's music for orchestra.

Professional orchestrators

In film, television and musical theatre, where deadlines are tight, composers frequently delegate detailed orchestration to specialist orchestrators, who realise the composer's short score into full parts — a role recognised by credits on major productions and long filled by figures such as Edward Powell and Conrad Pope.
