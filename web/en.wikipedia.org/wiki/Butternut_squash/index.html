Butternut squash (Cucurbita moschata 'Butternut'), known in Australia and New Zealand as butternut pumpkin or gramma, is a type of winter squash that grows on a vine. It has a sweet, nutty taste similar to that of a pumpkin. It has tan to yellow skin and orange fleshy pulp with a compartment of seeds in the blossom end. When ripe, it turns increasingly deep orange and becomes sweeter and richer.

History

The butternut cultivar was developed in the 1940s by Charles A. Leggett of Stow, Massachusetts, who crossed gooseneck squash with other varieties to produce a compact, straight-necked squash that was easier to handle and pack than the crooked-necked types then available. He named it "butternut" because he said it was "smooth as butter and sweet as a nut." It became one of the most popular winter squashes in North America and widely grown elsewhere.

Culinary use

Butternut squash is versatile in the kitchen: it is roasted, sautéed, puréed into soups (butternut squash soup is a classic autumn dish), mashed, used in risottos, curries, pasta fillings such as ravioli, and baked goods. The seeds can be roasted like pumpkin seeds. The skin becomes tender when cooked, though it is often peeled first. It stores well for months in a cool place, a defining trait of winter squashes.

Nutrition

Butternut squash is an excellent source of vitamin A (as beta-carotene), a good source of vitamin C, potassium, folate, and fibre, and is low in calories.
