Authenticity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Authenticity is a concept in psychology, existential philosophy and aesthetics concerning the genuineness of a person, work or experience. In existentialism, authenticity is the degree to which a person's actions are congruent with their beliefs and desires, despite external pressures to conformity and in the face of their awareness of their own mortality and freedom. The concept forms a central part of the thought of Kierkegaard, Heidegger, Sartre and de Beauvoir.

Existential philosophy

Heidegger, in Being and Time (1927), contrasted Eigentlichkeit (authenticity) with the fallen everydayness of das Man ("the They"), in which individuals surrender their choices to anonymous social norms. Authentic existence involves owning one's finitude — being-toward-death — and resolutely taking over one's own possibilities. Sartre's notion of bad faith (mauvaise foi) describes the opposite condition: denying one's freedom by pretending to be bound by a fixed role or nature.

Psychology

In humanistic psychology, authenticity is associated with Carl Rogers's concept of congruence and with self-actualisation. Contemporary trait research treats authenticity as comprising self-awareness, unbiased processing of self-relevant information, congruent behaviour and relational openness, and links it to well-being and life satisfaction.

Art and culture

In the arts, authenticity concerns the genuineness of attribution and provenance — whether a work is truly by the hand it claims — and, more loosely, the perceived sincerity of performers and works. Walter Benjamin's essay "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) analysed the "aura" of the authentic original. In tourism studies, authenticity denotes the degree to which experiences and sites are perceived as genuine rather than staged.
