Locked-room mystery

The locked-room mystery (or impossible crime) is a subgenre of detective fiction in which a crime, usually a murder, is committed in circumstances under which it seems impossible for the perpetrator to have entered or left the scene: a room locked from the inside, a sealed train compartment, or a murder observed in an empty, watched space. The pleasure of the genre lies in the detective's ingenious explanation of how the "impossibility" was contrived.

History

The first locked-room story is generally considered to be Edgar Allan Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" (1841), in which C. Auguste Dupin solves the killing of two women in a room locked from the inside. Israel Zangwill's The Big Bow Mystery (1892) and Gaston Leroux's The Mystery of the Yellow Room (1907) established the form in novel length.

The "locked-room lecture"

John Dickson Carr, regarded as the master of the subgenre, included a famous "locked-room lecture" in The Three Coffins (1935), in which Dr Gideon Fell catalogues the general methods by which locked-room illusions are achieved: delayed-action devices, murder committed before the room was sealed, the victim killed from outside by mechanical means, suicide made to look like murder, and so on.

Common mechanisms

Standard solutions involve strings or ice used to manipulate bolts, hidden exits, the victim being killed earlier or later than believed, animals or automata as agents, and misdirection about which room or body is involved. The best examples play fair with the reader, presenting all clues needed for the solution.

Legacy

The form flourished in the Golden Age of Detective Fiction and was revived in Japan by the shin honkaku ("new orthodox") movement, notably Soji Shimada's The Tokyo Zodiac Murders (1981). It remains a staple of crime fiction, television, and escape-room games.
