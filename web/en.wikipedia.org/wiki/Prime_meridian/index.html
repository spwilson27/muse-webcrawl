A prime meridian is the line of longitude defined as 0° from which other longitudes are measured east and west. By international agreement since 1884, Earth's prime meridian passes through the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, London — the Greenwich meridian — and its antipodal line is the 180th meridian, roughly followed by the International Date Line.

History

Ancient and early modern mapmakers used various prime meridians, including the Fortunate Isles (Canary Islands) of Ptolemy, and later the meridians of Paris, Ferro (El Hierro) and numerous national observatories. The International Meridian Conference of 1884 in Washington, D.C. chose Greenwich, reflecting Britain's dominance in shipping and the fact that most nautical charts already used it. France, which maintained the Paris meridian on its maps for decades afterwards, abstained.

Modern definition

The reference meridian used by GPS and modern geodesy, the IERS Reference Meridian, lies about 102 metres east of the historic Airy Transit Circle at Greenwich, because it is defined by a geodetic datum (WGS 84) rather than by a single instrument. The prime meridian also defines Coordinated Universal Time's reference zone, successor to Greenwich Mean Time.

Other bodies

Prime meridians are defined for other Solar System bodies as well: the Moon's passes near the crater Bruce, and Mars's passes through the crater Airy-0.
