The Treaty of al-Hudaybiya (Arabic: صلح الحديبية, Ṣulḥ al-Ḥudaybiya) was a pivotal agreement concluded in March 628 CE (6 AH) between Muhammad, representing the Muslim community of Medina, and emissaries of the Quraysh tribe of Mecca, at the locality of al-Hudaybiya on the edge of the sacred territory of Mecca.

Background and terms

Muhammad had marched from Medina with some 1,400 followers, intending to perform the ʿumrah (lesser pilgrimage) rather than to fight; the Quraysh blocked his entry, and after negotiations — conducted for the Quraysh by Suhayl ibn Amr — a truce was drawn up. Its terms included: a cessation of hostilities for ten years; the Muslims would return home that year but could come back the next year to perform the pilgrimage and stay three days; any Meccan who defected to Medina without his guardian's permission would be returned, while defectors in the other direction would not; and the tribes of Arabia were free to ally with either side.

Significance

Though many of Muhammad's companions initially saw the terms as humiliating, the treaty amounted to Meccan recognition of the Medinan polity as an equal party. The two years of peace that followed allowed Islam to spread widely, and when the truce broke down in 630 — after a Quraysh-allied tribe attacked a tribe allied to Muhammad — Muhammad marched on Mecca and took the city almost without bloodshed. In Islamic tradition the episode is celebrated as a "manifest victory" (fatḥ mubīn), the phrase of the Quran's Surah Al-Fath, revealed in its aftermath.
