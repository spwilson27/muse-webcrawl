Industrial archaeology

Industrial archaeology is the systematic study of material evidence associated with industry, particularly the remains of the industrial period: factories, mills, mines, canals, railways, warehouses, workers' housing and machinery. It combines methods from archaeology, history, engineering and architecture to record and interpret how industrial societies worked and how their landscapes were formed.

Development

The field took shape in Britain in the 1950s, as rapid redevelopment threatened the remains of the Industrial Revolution; the term was popularised by scholars at the University of Birmingham. It spread internationally, with societies, journals and conferences devoted to recording industrial monuments, and it helped drive the preservation of sites from Ironbridge Gorge in England — often called the birthplace of the Industrial Revolution — to mill towns and mining landscapes now recognised as World Heritage Sites.

Scope

Industrial archaeologists survey and excavate sites, study artefacts and documents, and record processes through oral history and photography. Their subject matter ranges from early water-powered mills to twentieth-century power stations, and includes the social history of the workers who lived and laboured in industrial communities.
