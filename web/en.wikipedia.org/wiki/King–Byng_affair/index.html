The King–Byng affair (also called the King–Byng crisis) was a Canadian constitutional crisis in 1926. It arose when the Governor General of Canada, Lord Byng of Vimy, refused Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King's request to dissolve Parliament and call an election, instead inviting the opposition leader to form a government. The affair became a landmark in the evolution of the role of the governor general and of dominion autonomy within the British Empire.

Background

In the federal election of October 1925, Arthur Meighen's Conservatives won more seats than King's Liberals, but King retained power by governing with the support of the Progressive Party. By the summer of 1926, King's minority government was embroiled in a customs corruption scandal and faced a censure motion it expected to lose. King asked Byng for a dissolution; Byng refused, arguing that Meighen, as leader of the largest party, should first be given the chance to govern.

Crisis and election

King resigned, and Meighen formed a government — which promptly lost a confidence vote by a single vote after only a few days. Byng then granted Meighen the dissolution he had denied King. In the ensuing election campaign, King turned the campaign into a constitutional issue, attacking the governor general's intervention as British interference in Canadian self-government against an elected prime minister. The Liberals won the September 1926 election, and King returned to office.

Significance

No governor general of Canada has since refused a prime minister's request for dissolution. The affair fed directly into the Balfour Declaration of 1926 and the Statute of Westminster 1931, which established the equality and autonomy of the dominions — including the principle that the governor general represents the monarch, not the British government, and acts on the advice of dominion ministers.
