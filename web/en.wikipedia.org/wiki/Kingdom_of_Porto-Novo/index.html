The Kingdom of Porto-Novo was a West African kingdom centred on the coastal city of Porto-Novo, in what is now south-eastern Benin. Founded around 1600, according to tradition by Yoruba-speaking settlers led by Te-Agdanlin, it became the principal state of the Gun people and a significant trading power on the Slave Coast.

History

Porto-Novo grew prosperous through trade with Europeans — first in slaves and later, after the suppression of the Atlantic slave trade in the mid-19th century, in palm oil. The kingdom was long overshadowed by its powerful neighbours, the kingdoms of Allada and Dahomey, whose armies raided its territory, yet Porto-Novo preserved its independence by playing European powers against one another.

In 1863 King Sodji accepted a French protectorate, partly to shield the kingdom from Dahomey. After fluctuations in the relationship, Porto-Novo was placed under direct French protection again in 1883 and was used as a base during the Franco-Dahomean wars. The monarchy was abolished by France in 1908, although the royal dynasty continued to be recognised ceremonially, and Porto-Novo remains the official capital of Benin today.

Culture

The kingdom's Afro-Brazilian returnee community, descended from freed slaves who returned from Brazil, enriched Porto-Novo with Brazilian-style architecture, Catholicism and Islam alongside the traditional Vodun religion of the Gun and Yoruba peoples.
