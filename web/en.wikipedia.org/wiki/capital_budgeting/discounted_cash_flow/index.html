Discounted cash flow

The discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis, in financial analysis, is a method used to value a security, project, company, or asset, that incorporates the time value of money. Discounted cash flow analysis is widely used in investment finance, real estate development, corporate financial management, and patent valuation. Used in industry as early as the 1700s and 1800s, it was widely discussed in financial economics in the 1960s, and became widely used in American courts in the 1980s and 1990s.

Basic formula

The value of an asset is the sum of its expected future cash flows, each discounted to the present at a rate reflecting the risk of those flows and the time value of money:

DCF = CF1/(1+r)1 + CF2/(1+r)2 + … + CFn/(1+r)n

where CFt is the cash flow in period t and r is the discount rate. When the stream continues indefinitely, a terminal value is estimated, often by applying a growth model (such as the Gordon growth model) or an exit multiple.

Capital budgeting

In capital budgeting, DCF underpins the central decision rules: the net present value (NPV) of a project is its discounted inflows minus the initial investment, and projects with positive NPV are accepted; the internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes NPV zero and is compared with the firm's hurdle rate. The discount rate is usually the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), which blends the costs of debt and equity financing in proportion to their shares in the capital structure.

Valuation practice and limitations

For valuing companies, analysts project free cash flow to the firm or to equity over an explicit forecast period, then add a terminal value, which often dominates the result. Because DCF outputs are sensitive to assumptions about growth, margins, and the discount rate, small changes in inputs can swing the valuation substantially; practitioners therefore run sensitivity and scenario analyses, and cross-check DCF results against market multiples.
