Lambertian reflectance

Lambertian reflectance
Named afterJohann Heinrich Lambert
Governing lawLambert's cosine law
FieldOptics, photometry, computer graphics

Lambertian reflectance is the property defining an ideal diffusely reflecting surface: one that appears equally bright (has the same radiance) from all viewing directions, scattering incident light so that the apparent brightness depends only on the angle of illumination. Such a surface is called a Lambertian surface or ideal diffuse reflector, after Johann Heinrich Lambert, who introduced the concept in his 1760 work Photometria.

Lambert's cosine law

For a Lambertian surface, the radiant intensity of the reflected light in a given direction is proportional to the cosine of the angle between that direction and the surface normal — a relationship known as Lambert's cosine law. Combined with the inverse cosine falloff of irradiance as a surface tilts away from a light source, this means the reflected radiance is proportional to the cosine of the incidence angle and independent of the viewing angle. Matte materials such as unpolished plaster, matte paint, and paper approximate Lambertian behaviour, though none is perfect; integrating spheres and calibration standards such as pressed barium sulfate and Spectralon exploit near-Lambertian reflection.

Applications

In computer graphics, Lambertian shading is the simplest and most widely used diffuse lighting model: the colour of a surface point is computed as the surface albedo multiplied by the light colour and the dot product of the surface normal and the light direction (clamped at zero). It is a standard component of the Phong and Blinn–Phong reflection models and of physically based rendering workflows, where it forms the diffuse term of the bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF), which for a Lambertian surface is constant: fr = ρ/π, where ρ is the albedo.
