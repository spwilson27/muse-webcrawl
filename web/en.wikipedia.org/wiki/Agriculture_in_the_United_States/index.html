Agriculture in the United States is a major sector of the national economy and one of the world's largest producers and exporters of food. Around 880 million acres — roughly 40 percent of the US land area — are farmed by about 1.9 million farms, though production is concentrated: large farms account for the great majority of output, and direct farm employment is around 1 percent of the workforce.

Major commodities

The United States is the world's leading producer of corn (maize) and soybeans, much of it grown in the Midwestern Corn Belt. Other major products include wheat from the Great Plains, cotton, rice, dairy products, beef cattle from the Plains and Southwest, hogs, broiler chickens, and specialty crops such as California fruits, nuts, and vegetables, Florida citrus, and Washington apples. The US is a top exporter of soybeans, corn, wheat, cotton, and tree nuts.

History

Indigenous peoples cultivated maize, beans, and squash for millennia before European colonization introduced plantation agriculture — including tobacco and later cotton worked by enslaved people in the South. The Homestead Act of 1862, mechanization, hybrid seed, synthetic fertilizers, and the Green Revolution dramatically raised yields through the 20th century. Federal policy, shaped by recurring farm bills since the 1930s, supports prices, crop insurance, conservation, and nutrition programs.

Issues

Contemporary concerns include consolidation and farm debt, water scarcity (notably aquifer depletion in the Plains and drought in California), soil erosion, fertilizer runoff, labor supply, and the effects of climate change.
