Chamber tomb

A chamber tomb is a tomb for burial that is formed of a chamber or chambers cut into rock or built of stone, as distinct from a simple pit or cist grave. Chamber tombs were used by many cultures from the Neolithic onward and were generally intended for collective or repeated burial of families or elite groups, with grave goods deposited alongside the dead. The term covers a range of forms, including megalithic gallery graves, rock-cut hypogea, and vaulted masonry tombs.

Forms and distribution

  • Megalithic chamber tombs: In Neolithic and Bronze Age Europe, collective tombs were built of large stones — passage graves, gallery graves, and related dolmens — covered by mounds or cairns, as in Brittany, Ireland, Iberia, and Scandinavia.
  • Mycenaean chamber tombs: In Late Bronze Age Greece (c. 1500–1100 BC) the commonest form of family tomb was a chamber cut into soft rock, approached by a sloping open-air corridor (dromos) and a doorway (stomion) blocked with stones. Cemeteries of such tombs ring Mycenae, Athens, and other centres; they are distinct from the grander corbel-vaulted tholos tombs of rulers.
  • Etruscan chamber tombs: In Etruria, rock-cut chamber tombs in necropolises such as Cerveteri and Tarquinia imitated houses, with carved beams, furniture, and painted walls.
  • Rock-cut tombs of Egypt, Nubia, and the Near East: Chamber tombs hewn into cliffs served elites from pharaonic Egypt to Nabataean Petra and Lydian Anatolia.
  • Asia: Kofun-period Japan produced the celebrated keyhole-shaped mounds with stone burial chambers; Goguryeo tombs (in present-day North Korea and northeast China) contain mural-painted chambers.

Use

Chamber tombs were typically opened repeatedly for successive burials over generations, with earlier remains pushed aside or gathered into pits. Their assemblages — pottery, weapons, jewellery, and food offerings — are a primary source of archaeological information about social status, trade, and ritual. After final use, entrances were sealed or the dromos back-filled.
