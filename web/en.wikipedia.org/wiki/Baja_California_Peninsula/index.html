The Baja California Peninsula is a peninsula in northwestern Mexico that separates the Pacific Ocean from the Gulf of California (also called the Sea of Cortés). It stretches roughly 1,247 km (775 mi) from Mexicali in the north to Cabo San Lucas in the south and varies in width from about 40 km at its narrowest to 320 km at its widest, making it one of the longest peninsulas in the world. Politically it is divided between the Mexican states of Baja California (north) and Baja California Sur (south).

Geology

The peninsula was originally part of mainland Mexico and was separated from it by the rifting that opened the Gulf of California, a process that began several million years ago as the Pacific Plate moved northwest relative to the North American Plate along the San Andreas fault system. The peninsula is still drifting northwest at a rate of a few centimetres per year. Its spine is formed by the Peninsular Ranges batholith, an ancient granitic mountain chain that extends north into Southern California.

Geography and climate

Much of the peninsula is desert — the Baja California desert and Vizcaíno desert among them — with distinctive flora including the giant cardón cactus, the bizarre cirio (boojum) tree, and many endemic species. The western coast is cooled by the California Current, while the Gulf side is warm and humid in summer. Mountain ranges such as the Sierra de San Pedro Mártir and Sierra de la Laguna receive enough precipitation to support pine and oak forests.

History

Humans have lived on the peninsula for at least 10,000 years, leaving rock art sites such as the Great Murals of the Sierra de San Francisco, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Hernán Cortés's expedition reached the peninsula in 1535. For two centuries Europeans mistakenly mapped California as an island. Jesuit missionaries founded a chain of missions from 1697 onward, followed by Franciscans and Dominicans, which devastated indigenous populations through disease.

Marine life

The Gulf of California is among the richest marine environments on Earth, sometimes called "the world's aquarium". The peninsula's Pacific lagoons, including Ojo de Liebre and San Ignacio, are key calving grounds for gray whales. El Vizcaíno Biosphere Reserve and the Islands and Protected Areas of the Gulf of California are recognised by UNESCO.
