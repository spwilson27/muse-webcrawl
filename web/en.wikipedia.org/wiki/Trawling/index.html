Trawling is a method of fishing in which a large net (a trawl) is towed behind one or two boats through the water. The net is held open horizontally by otter boards, or "doors", and in midwater also vertically by weights and floats. Vessels that fish with trawls are called trawlers.

Types

Bottom trawling drags the net along or just above the seabed and targets demersal species such as cod, haddock, flatfish and shrimp. Midwater (pelagic) trawling fishes in the water column and targets schooling fish such as herring, mackerel and Alaska pollock; midwater trawls can be enormous, with mouths hundreds of metres across. Beam trawls, held open by a rigid beam, are used especially for flatfish and shrimp.

Environmental impact

Bottom trawling is controversial because heavy gear — particularly dredges and beam trawls with tickler chains — can flatten seabed habitats, damage cold-water corals and sponges, resuspend sediments and release stored carbon. Trawls are also relatively unselective, producing bycatch of juvenile fish and non-target species, though devices such as square-mesh panels, sorting grids and turtle excluder devices reduce this. Many jurisdictions restrict or ban bottom trawling in sensitive areas and marine protected areas.

History

Trawling with sailing vessels was practised in the North Sea by the 14th century and drew fishermen's complaints as early as 1376. Steam trawlers in the late 19th century, and later diesel factory trawlers with onboard processing, greatly increased fishing power and range.
