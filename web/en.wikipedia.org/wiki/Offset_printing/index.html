Offset printing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Offset printing is a widely used printing technique in which the inked image is transferred (or "offset") from a plate to a rubber blanket, and then to the printing surface. Combined with the lithographic principle — the mutual repulsion of oil-based ink and water on a flat aluminium plate — it is the dominant process for high-volume commercial printing: books, newspapers, magazines, catalogues, packaging and stationery.

Process

In a modern press unit, a thin plate bearing the image is wrapped around a cylinder. Dampening rollers apply fountain solution (water with additives) to the plate's non-image areas; ink rollers then apply ink, which sticks only to the dry image areas. The plate cylinder transfers the image to a rubber blanket cylinder, which in turn prints it onto paper. Colour work typically uses four such units in sequence with cyan, magenta, yellow and black inks (CMYK), plus optional spot colours. Presses are either sheet-fed (printing individual sheets) or web-fed (printing from a continuous roll, as for newspapers and with heatset or coldset configurations).

History

Offset printing on paper developed accidentally around 1903–1904 when American printer Ira Washington Rubel found that an image transferred from the plate to the rubber-covered impression cylinder produced a sharper print; the Harris brothers developed the idea commercially. Offset had displaced letterpress for most commercial work by the later twentieth century. Computer-to-plate (CTP) technology since the 1990s images plates directly from digital files, eliminating film.

Advantages

The compliant rubber blanket conforms to textured papers and produces consistent, high-quality images; plates are cheap and quick to make; and presses run at very high speeds, making unit costs extremely low at large quantities. For short runs, digital printing is now generally more economical.
