Traditional authority

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Traditional authority is a form of leadership in which the authority of an organisation or a government is largely tied to tradition or custom. It is one of the three sources of legitimate authority identified by the sociologist Max Weber in his tripartite classification of authority, the others being charismatic authority and rational-legal authority. The main reason for the given state of affairs is that "it has always been that way".

Weber's typology

Weber defined traditional authority as resting on "an established belief in the sanctity of immemorial traditions and the legitimacy of those exercising authority under them". The ruler commands obedience because of the traditional status he holds, not because of written rules or personal charisma. Authority is personal and patrimonial: administration is carried out by relatives, household officials or personal retainers, and there is no clear separation between the ruler's private and public resources. Gerontocracy (rule by elders), patriarchalism and hereditary monarchy are classic examples.

Weber subdivided traditional authority into forms that include patriarchalism, patrimonialism — in which the whole realm is treated as an extension of the ruler's household — and feudalism, where authority is mediated by vassals holding benefices. In its extreme form, "sultanism", patrimonial rule operates largely free of traditional restraint, at the ruler's arbitrary discretion.

Characteristics

Under traditional authority, obedience is owed to the person occupying a traditionally sanctioned position, and its scope is bounded by custom: a ruler who grossly violates tradition may lose legitimacy and provoke resistance justified in tradition's name. Innovation is difficult to legitimise, and change tends to be presented as restoration of an earlier order. There is no formal body of law standing above the ruler; disputes are settled by precedent and customary norms.

Modern persistence

Traditional authority persists in many institutions: reigning monarchies (including absolute ones such as Saudi Arabia and Eswatini), chieftaincies recognised within modern African states, clan and tribal leadership, religious hierarchies, and family firms. Weber regarded the long-run trend of modernisation as a shift toward rational-legal authority, but hybrid arrangements — for example, constitutional monarchies in which hereditary heads of state coexist with elected governments — remain common.

Criticism and analysis

Later scholars have noted that Weber's categories are ideal types rather than descriptions of real regimes, which usually mix all three; that "tradition" itself may be deliberately invented or revived (the "invention of tradition"); and that traditional authority can be an active political resource in post-colonial states, where chiefs may command more local trust than elected officials.
