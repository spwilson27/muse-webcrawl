Tell el-Amarna

Tell el-Amarna is an extensive ancient Egyptian archaeological site in Middle Egypt, on the east bank of the Nile, that represents the remains of Akhetaten, the capital city founded by the pharaoh Akhenaten of the late Eighteenth Dynasty around 1346 BC. The city was abandoned shortly after Akhenaten's death, and its brief occupation makes it uniquely informative about ancient Egyptian urban life.

History

Akhenaten founded Akhetaten ("Horizon of the Aten") in the fifth year of his reign as the centre of his worship of the sun disc, the Aten, abandoning Thebes and its priesthood of Amun. Boundary stelae carved into the surrounding cliffs defined the city's territory and recorded the founding oath. The court, administration and a population of perhaps 20,000–50,000 moved to the new site.

After Akhenaten's death around 1336 BC, the court of Tutankhamun returned to Thebes, and the city was largely deserted and never substantially reoccupied. Later pharaohs dismantled its temples, reusing the distinctive small sandstone blocks known as talatat as fill elsewhere.

Archaeology

The site includes the Great Aten Temple, the Great Palace, the royal tomb, officials' tombs decorated in the naturalistic Amarna art style, and the Workmen's Village. In 1887 local villagers discovered a cache of over 300 cuneiform tablets — the Amarna letters — the diplomatic correspondence of Akhenaten and his father Amenhotep III with rulers across the Near East. Excavations by Flinders Petrie in 1891–92 and later by the Egypt Exploration Society uncovered much of the city plan; the famous bust of Nefertiti was found in 1912 by a German expedition in the workshop of the sculptor Thutmose.
