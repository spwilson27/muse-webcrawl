Placebo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A placebo is a substance or treatment with no inherent therapeutic effect — such as a sugar pill or saline injection — given to mimic a real intervention. In research, comparing a treatment against a placebo isolates its true effect; in the placebo effect, patients genuinely improve after receiving a placebo, through expectation, conditioning and the context of care.

The placebo effect

Placebo responses are strongest for symptoms such as pain, nausea, fatigue and some mood and Parkinson's symptoms, and involve real neurobiology — expectation can release endogenous opioids (blocked by naloxone) and dopamine. Sham procedures can produce larger effects than pills. The nocebo effect is the mirror image: expecting harm produces real worsening of symptoms. Remarkably, even openly labelled 'honest' placebos show measurable effects in some trials.

Use and ethics

Placebo controls in clinical trials are ethical when no proven treatment is withheld or when added to standard care, and regulators generally require them where feasible. Using placebos deceptively in ordinary practice is considered unethical, but harnessing context effects — good communication, rituals of care — is part of good medicine.
