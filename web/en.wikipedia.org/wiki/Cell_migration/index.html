Cell migration

Cell migration is a central process in the development and maintenance of multicellular organisms. Tissue formation during embryonic development, wound healing and immune responses all require the orchestrated movement of cells in particular directions to specific locations. Cells often migrate in response to, and towards, specific external signals — a process called chemotaxis when the signal is a chemical gradient.

Mechanism

The standard cycle of crawling cell motility involves four steps: (1) polarisation of the cell, establishing a front (leading edge) and rear; (2) protrusion of the leading edge by polymerisation of actin filaments, forming lamellipodia, filopodia or blebs; (3) adhesion of the protrusion to the substrate through integrin-based focal adhesions, giving the cell traction; and (4) retraction, in which myosin II–driven contraction pulls the cell body forward and releases adhesions at the rear. Signalling by Rho-family GTPases (Rac, Rho, Cdc42) coordinates these steps.

Modes of migration

Cells migrate singly or collectively. Epithelial and endothelial sheets move as coherent groups in wound closure and angiogenesis; neural crest cells migrate in streams during development. Some cells, including many cancer cells, can switch between adhesion-dependent mesenchymal migration and rounded, amoeboid movement through confined spaces.

Significance

Defects in cell migration cause developmental disorders, immune deficiencies and chronic non-healing wounds. Conversely, pathological migration drives cancer metastasis, in which tumour cells invade surrounding tissue and colonise distant organs — the process responsible for most cancer deaths. Cell migration is studied with assays such as the scratch (wound-healing) assay, Boyden chambers and live-cell imaging.
