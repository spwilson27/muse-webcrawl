Dunhuang manuscripts

Dunhuang manuscripts
Discovered: 1900, Mogao Cave 17
Dates: c. 5th–11th centuries
Now held: British Library, National Library of China, Bibliothèque nationale de France, and others

The Dunhuang manuscripts are a cache of important religious and secular documents discovered in the Mogao Caves of Dunhuang, China. Dated from the late fourth to the early eleventh centuries, the manuscripts include works in Chinese and other languages such as Tibetan, Khotanese, Sogdian, and Sanskrit. They are a major resource for scholarship on medieval China and Central Asia.

Discovery

In 1900 the Daoist monk Wang Yuanlu, self-appointed caretaker of the caves, discovered a hidden chamber — Cave 17, the so-called Library Cave — sealed around the early 11th century and packed with tens of thousands of manuscripts. In 1907 the explorer Aurel Stein purchased thousands of documents for the British Museum; Paul Pelliot followed in 1908 for France, and expeditions from Japan, Russia, and China acquired further portions. The remainder are held chiefly by the National Library of China.

Contents

The majority are Buddhist sutras, including many copies of the Lotus and Diamond Sutras; the world's earliest dated printed book, a Chinese Diamond Sutra of 868 CE, comes from the cave. Other materials include Daoist, Manichaean, Nestorian Christian, and Jewish texts, contracts, letters, calendars, medical treatises, star maps, folksongs, and administrative records illuminating everyday life along the Silk Road.

Legacy

The International Dunhuang Project, founded in 1994 and based at the British Library, digitizes and reunites the scattered collection online. See Dunhuang and Mogao Caves.
