Little Moreton Hall, near Odd Rode in Cheshire, England, is one of the finest examples of a timber-framed moated manor house in England. Built for the prosperous Moreton family between about 1504 and 1610, its black-and-white half-timbered ranges surround a cobbled courtyard, and the whole is encircled by a moat fed by a nearby stream.

History

The earliest part of the house, the Great Hall range, was begun around 1504–1508 by William Moreton. Successive generations added the east and south ranges, culminating in the distinctive three-storey south range with its two projecting bays and the celebrated Long Gallery at the top of the house, built around 1580–1610. The weight of the Long Gallery caused the floors below to sag picturesquely — the house has been described as looking "lifted from a fairy story" and famously crooked.

The Moreton family's fortunes declined after the English Civil War, and the house was let to tenants for centuries, which preserved it from modernization. In 1937 it was given to the Bishop of Chester, and in 1938 it passed to the National Trust, which still owns and opens it to the public.

Architecture and features

  • The Long Gallery, running the length of the south range, retains its original plaster friezes depicting the story of Susanna and scenes from the Wheel of Fortune.
  • The Chapel, in the east range, has wall paintings discovered in the 1970s.
  • The Gatehouse and the brew house and other service buildings stand by the moat bridge.
  • Formal gardens include a knot garden laid out in the 20th century.

The writer and critic William Morris and later Lord Leverhulme were among the admirers who helped secure the hall's preservation; Charles Abraham, Bishop of Derby, undertook major restoration in the 1930s.
