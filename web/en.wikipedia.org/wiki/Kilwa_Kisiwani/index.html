Kilwa Kisiwani is an island and historic port city off the southern coast of present-day Tanzania. Between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries it was the most powerful of the Swahili city-states, controlling the trade of the southern East African coast and the gold exports of the interior kingdom centred on Great Zimbabwe. Its stone ruins, protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, include some of the finest surviving monuments of Swahili civilisation.

History

According to the Kilwa Chronicle, the city was founded around the tenth century by Ali ibn al-Hassan Shirazi, a prince said to have come from Shiraz in Persia, who purchased the island from its inhabitants. Under the Mahdali dynasty from the late thirteenth century, Kilwa reached its zenith: its sultans minted their own coins, extended their authority as far south as Sofala — the outlet for Zimbabwean gold — and rebuilt the city in coral stone.

The traveller Ibn Battuta, who visited in 1331, praised Kilwa as one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns in the world and its sultan, Abu al-Muzaffar Hasan, for his generosity. The Portuguese reached Kilwa in 1500 and under Francisco de Almeida sacked and occupied it in 1505, building the fort of Gereza; Portuguese domination, combined with shifting trade routes, triggered its long decline, and although it revived modestly in the eighteenth century as a slave and ivory port under Omani influence, the grand city was never rebuilt.

Monuments

The Great Mosque of Kilwa, begun in the eleventh century and greatly enlarged around 1300, was the largest mosque on the East African coast; its great domed prayer hall was an architectural landmark of the Islamic world. Husuni Kubwa, the sultan's palace built in the early fourteenth century on a headland outside the town, was a vast complex of courts, audience chambers and an octagonal bathing pool — the largest single building in sub-Saharan Africa of its era. The smaller Husuni Ndogo fort and the Makutani Palace also survive.
