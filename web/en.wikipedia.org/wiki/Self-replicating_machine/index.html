Self-replicating machine
Key theoristJohn von Neumann
Also known asVon Neumann machine; clanking replicator
ApplicationsSpace exploration probes, nanotechnology, 3D printing (RepRap)

A self-replicating machine is a type of autonomous robot that is capable of reproducing itself autonomously using raw materials found in its environment — analogous to self-replication in nature. The concept was rigorously developed by mathematician John von Neumann in the 1940s in his theory of self-reproducing automata, in which he showed that a machine combining a universal constructor with a stored description of itself could, in principle, build copies of itself.

Concepts and designs

Von Neumann's kinematic model distinguished the constructor, the description (or "tape"), and the copying mechanism — anticipating the roles of ribosomes, DNA, and enzymes in biological reproduction. A von Neumann probe applies the idea to space exploration: a spacecraft that lands on a planet or asteroid, mines local materials, and builds copies of itself that travel onward, enabling exponential exploration of the galaxy. The physicist Frank Tipler argued that the absence of such probes is evidence relevant to the Fermi paradox.

Studies and partial realizations

A 1980 NASA summer study, Advanced Automation for Space Missions, designed a self-replicating lunar factory in detail. In practice, engineers distinguish degrees of "closure" — the fraction of components a system can produce itself. The open-source RepRap 3D printer (from 2005) can print many of its own plastic parts and is often cited as the first partially self-replicating general-purpose machine, though it still requires human assembly and externally produced electronics and motors. At the molecular scale, self-replicating systems intersect with nanotechnology; K. Eric Drexler's vision of molecular assemblers raised both hopes and fears, notably the "grey goo" scenario of runaway replication, which Drexler himself later downplayed. Legal and ethical debates also surround self-replicating spacecraft and the governance of technologies that can copy themselves outside direct human control.
