Pitman–Yor process

The two-parameter Chinese restaurant process: customers join occupied tables with probability proportional to occupancy minus the discount parameter.

In probability theory, a Pitman–Yor process (also called the two-parameter Poisson–Dirichlet process) is a stochastic process that generalises the Dirichlet process. It was introduced by Jim Pitman and Marc Yor. The Pitman–Yor process is a distribution over distributions: samples from it are random probability measures, making it a useful prior in Bayesian nonparametric statistics.

Definition

A Pitman–Yor process is parameterised by a discount parameter 0 ≤ d < 1, a concentration parameter θ > −d, and a base distribution H over a probability space. It can be constructed through a stick-breaking representation: successive weights are drawn from Beta(1 − d, θ + kd) distributions for k = 0, 1, 2, …, each new weight taking a fraction of the remaining stick length. When d = 0 the construction reduces to the stick-breaking construction of the Dirichlet process with concentration θ.

Properties

The key distinguishing property of the Pitman–Yor process is its power-law behaviour: the number of distinct clusters in n samples grows roughly like nd, rather than the logarithmic growth of the Dirichlet process. The exchangeable partition probability function associated with the process is the two-parameter Ewens–Pitman sampling formula. In the corresponding Chinese restaurant process metaphor, a new customer sits at an occupied table with probability proportional to (nk − d), where nk is the number of customers already seated there, and at a new table with probability proportional to (θ + Kd), where K is the number of occupied tables.

Applications

Because many real-world datasets — notably word frequencies in natural language — exhibit power-law (Zipfian) behaviour, the Pitman–Yor process is widely used in language modelling, including hierarchical Pitman–Yor language models that smooth n-gram counts more effectively than Dirichlet-based models. It is also used in topic modelling, species sampling problems in ecology and population genetics, and other clustering applications where the number of clusters grows as a power of the sample size.
