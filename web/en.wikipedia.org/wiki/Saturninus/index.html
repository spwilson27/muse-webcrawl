Saturninus

Lucius Appuleius Saturninus (died late 100 BC) was a Roman populist politician and tribune of the plebs, a supporter and ally of Gaius Marius. Through a series of radical laws and violent political methods he became one of the most controversial figures of the late Roman Republic, and his death, following the Senate's first use of the senatus consultum ultimum against a Roman citizen, marked a turning point in the political violence of the era.

Early career and quaestorship

Saturninus first appears as quaestor in 104 BC, in charge of the grain supply at Ostia. Removed from that post by the Senate — reportedly for inefficiency during a period of high grain prices — and replaced by Marcus Aemilius Scaurus, he turned decisively against the senatorial aristocracy (optimates) and toward the popular party. He was elected tribune of the plebs for 103 BC.

First tribunate (103 BC)

During his first tribunate Saturninus carried legislation favourable to Marius and the veterans of the Cimbrian Wars, including a law reducing the price of grain and measures supporting Marius's colonies in Africa. He prosecuted political opponents through the popular courts and is credited by ancient sources with considerable violence and theatricality in his methods.

Second tribunate and death (100 BC)

Re-elected tribune for 100 BC with the support of Marius and in alliance with the praetor Gaius Servilius Glaucia, Saturninus proposed an extensive programme: land distributions in the provinces for Marius's veterans, colonies in Sicily, Achaea and Macedonia, the extension of his grain law, and a clause requiring all senators to swear an oath to uphold the laws within five days, on pain of exile. The exiled Quintus Caecilius Metellus Numidicus refused the oath and went into exile.

The elections for 99 BC descended into violence: Saturninus and Glaucia's supporters murdered the rival consular candidate Gaius Memmius. The Senate responded with the senatus consultum ultimum, and Marius, as consul, reluctantly moved against his former allies. Saturninus, Glaucia and their followers surrendered after being promised their lives, took refuge in the Senate house, and were stoned to death by a mob with roof tiles. Saturninus's laws were subsequently annulled.

Legacy

Saturninus's career is treated by historians as an early example of the populist, violence-driven politics that later consumed the Republic. His death under a decree of the Senate also set a precedent — that the Republic's ultimate decree could be turned on tribunes themselves — which echoed in the later crises surrounding the Gracchi's successors, Clodius, and the final civil wars.
