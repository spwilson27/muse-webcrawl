Audio signal processing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Audio signal processing is a subfield of signal processing concerned with the electronic manipulation of audio signals. It encompasses the analysis, synthesis, modification, and efficient transmission and storage of sound — speech, music, and environmental audio — in both analog and digital forms.

Analog and digital processing

Analog processing operates on continuous electrical signals representing sound, historically with filters, amplifiers, and tape machines. Digital signal processing samples audio into discrete values (commonly 44.1 or 48 kHz at 16 or 24 bits) and manipulates it with algorithms, implemented in general-purpose computers, dedicated DSP chips, or embedded processors. Digital methods allow operations impractical in analog, such as linear-phase filtering, spectral editing, and adaptive noise cancellation.

Core operations

  • Filtering and equalization — altering the frequency balance with FIR and IIR filters.
  • Dynamics processing — compression, limiting, expansion, and gating of dynamic range.
  • Time and pitch manipulation — delay, reverberation (convolution and algorithmic), time stretching, and pitch shifting.
  • Spatial processing — stereo imaging, panning, binaural rendering, and surround sound.
  • Analysis and recognition — Fourier and wavelet analysis, speech recognition, music information retrieval, and acoustic echo and feedback suppression.
  • Coding — perceptual compression for storage and streaming.

Applications

Audio signal processing underlies recording and music production, telecommunications (speech codecs, echo cancellation, noise suppression in calls), hearing aids and cochlear implants, active noise control, voice assistants, and entertainment systems. The field draws on psychoacoustics — knowledge of how hearing perceives loudness, pitch, masking, and localization — to design algorithms that achieve the desired percept with minimal data and computation.
