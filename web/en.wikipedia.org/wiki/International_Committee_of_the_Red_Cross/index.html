International Committee of the Red Cross

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

International Committee of the Red Cross
Founded: 17 February 1863, Geneva
Role: Guardian of international humanitarian law; neutral intermediary in armed conflict
Nobel Peace Prizes: 1917, 1944, 1963 (shared)

The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) is a Swiss-based humanitarian organisation founded in Geneva in 1863, following the proposals of Henry Dunant after the Battle of Solferino. It is the oldest component of the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement (see Red Cross).

Mandate

The ICRC's mandate, recognised in the Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols, is to protect and assist victims of armed conflict and other violence: visiting detainees and prisoners of war, restoring contact between separated families, providing medical care and clean water, and promoting respect for international humanitarian law. It acts as a neutral intermediary — for prisoner exchanges, evacuations and humanitarian access — and its confidential dialogue with all parties is a hallmark of its method.

Recognition

The ICRC received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1917 and 1944 for its work in the two World Wars, and shared the 1963 prize with the League of Red Cross Societies in its centenary year. It is governed by a committee of Swiss citizens and is legally distinct from the national societies and the IFRC, with which it co-ordinates in the Movement.
