Lokono language

Lokono
Native to: Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana, Venezuela
Ethnicity: Lokono (Arawak) people
Family: Arawakan (Maipurean)
Status: Endangered

Lokono, also known as Arawak (self-designation Lokono Dian, "the people's speech"), is an Arawakan language spoken by the Lokono people of the Guianas — Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana and eastern Venezuela. It is the language that gives the entire Arawakan family its conventional name, from the Arawak people encountered by Europeans in the Caribbean.

Lokono-speaking communities are concentrated along the coast and rivers of the Guianas.

Status

Once widely spoken, Lokono has declined sharply under pressure from English, Dutch, French and Creole languages. Estimates of fluent speakers are in the low thousands, mostly older people, out of an ethnic population of tens of thousands; UNESCO classifies it as endangered. In Suriname and Guyana it survives best in interior villages, and there are modest revitalisation efforts involving dictionaries, orthography standardisation and school materials.

Features

Lokono is an agglutinative, head-marking language typical of the Arawakan family, with prefixes marking subject and possessor, a distinction of gender (masculine/feminine) in third-person pronouns, and classifiers. Its word for people, lokono (singular loko), is the source of the endonym now preferred over the exonym "Arawak".
