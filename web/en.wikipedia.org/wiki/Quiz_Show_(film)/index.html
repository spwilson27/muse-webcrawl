Quiz Show (film)

Directed by: Robert Redford
Written by: Paul Attanasio
Starring: John Turturro, Rob Morrow, Ralph Fiennes, David Paymer, Paul Scofield
Release date: 14 October 1994
Running time: 133 minutes
Box office: approx. $24.8 million

Quiz Show is a 1994 American historical drama film directed and produced by Robert Redford. Based on Richard N. Goodwin's 1988 memoir Remembering America: A Voice from the Sixties, the screenplay by Paul Attanasio dramatises the quiz show scandals of the 1950s, in which the popular NBC programme Twenty-One was revealed to be rigged.

Plot

The film follows Twenty-One champion Herbert Stempel (John Turturro), a working-class ex-GI from Queens who is fed answers by producer Dan Enright and then ordered to lose to a more telegenic challenger: Charles Van Doren (Ralph Fiennes), a Columbia University instructor from a famous literary family. Embittered, Stempel blows the whistle but is dismissed as unstable. Congressional investigator Richard Goodwin (Rob Morrow) gradually uncovers the fraud, drawing Van Doren into a moral trap between his family's reputation and the truth. Van Doren finally confesses before a congressional committee, even as the networks and sponsors largely escape blame—leaving Goodwin to conclude that the public prefers the illusion.

Production and accuracy

Redford and Attanasio compressed events for dramatic effect; the real Van Doren did not testify in the manner shown, and details of Stempel's defeat—including the Marty question shown in the film—were altered. Van Doren, who declined to participate, broke decades of near-silence in a 2008 New Yorker essay. Paul Scofield was widely praised as Van Doren's father, the poet Mark Van Doren.

Reception and legacy

The film was acclaimed by critics for its screenplay, performances, and treatment of media ethics and American innocence, though it performed modestly at the box office against competition from Pulp Fiction and Forrest Gump the same year. It received four Academy Award nominations, including Best Picture, Best Director, Best Supporting Actor (Scofield), and Best Adapted Screenplay, and won the BAFTA for Best Adapted Screenplay. It is frequently cited as one of the finest films of 1994 and a defining dramatisation of early television's corruption.
