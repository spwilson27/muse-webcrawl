Geospatial intelligence

Geospatial intelligence (GEOINT) is the intelligence discipline concerned with the exploitation and analysis of imagery and geospatial information to describe, assess, and visually depict physical features and geographically referenced activities on the Earth. In the United States, the statutory definition (10 U.S.C. § 467) covers imagery, imagery intelligence, and geospatial information. The principal US agency is the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA), created in 1996 as the National Imagery and Mapping Agency and renamed in 2003.

Components

GEOINT fuses data from multiple sources: electro-optical and radar imagery from reconnaissance satellites and aircraft (imagery intelligence, IMINT), topographic mapping and geodesy, aeronautical and nautical charting, elevation data, and increasingly open sources such as commercial satellite imagery and social media geolocation. Analysts produce maps, 3-D models, target folders, and assessments of everything from military deployments to crop yields, disaster damage, and treaty compliance.

History

The discipline grew out of photographic reconnaissance in the two World Wars, U-2 and SR-71 overflights, and the Corona and KeyHole satellite programmes. The proliferation of high-resolution commercial imagery in the 2000s and 2010s democratised capabilities once monopolised by states, enabling open-source investigators to verify conflict events publicly. GEOINT also underpins precision weapons guidance, mission planning, humanitarian relief, and civil mapping.
