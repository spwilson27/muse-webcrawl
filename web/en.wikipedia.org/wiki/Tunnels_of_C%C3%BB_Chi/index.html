Tunnels of Củ Chi

The tunnels of Củ Chi are an immense network of connecting tunnels in the Củ Chi District of Ho Chi Minh City (Saigon), in Vietnam, and are part of a much larger network of tunnels that underlie much of the country. The Củ Chi tunnels were the location of several military campaigns during the Vietnam War, and were the Viet Cong's base of operations for the Tết Offensive in 1968.

History

The tunnels were dug by hand, only a short section at a time, beginning in the late 1940s during the war against French colonial rule. During the Vietnam War the network expanded and came to include subterranean living areas, storage facilities, weapons factories, field hospitals, command centres and kitchens. Trap doors, booby traps and ventilation shafts disguised as termite mounds protected the occupants.

Life in the tunnels

Fighters often lived underground for days at a time. Malaria, intestinal parasites and other diseases were rampant; a famous saying held that half of the guerrillas in the tunnels had malaria. Food such as cassava was grown nearby and cooked in kitchens whose smoke was vented far away through disguised chimneys.

American response

United States and Australian forces attempted to destroy the tunnels with explosives, defoliants and specially trained "tunnel rats" — soldiers who entered the tunnels armed only with a pistol, a knife and a flashlight. The tunnels nevertheless survived, and Củ Chi became a symbol of resistance. Today the preserved sections at Bến Dược and Bến Đình are popular tourist attractions.
