A celestial globe is a spherical model of the celestial sphere, depicting the positions of stars and the figures of the constellations as they would appear on the inside of an imaginary sphere surrounding the Earth — but conventionally drawn on the outside of the globe, so that the constellations appear reversed left-to-right compared with their appearance in the sky. Celestial globes were used for teaching astronomy, for astrological calculation, and for solving problems of spherical astronomy such as rising and setting times.

History

Star globes date to antiquity: the Farnese Atlas, a Roman marble statue, supports a globe marked with constellation figures, reflecting Hellenistic astronomy. In the Islamic world, brass celestial globes were produced from the early medieval period, some of remarkable precision, and the tradition passed into European instrument-making. During the 16th and 17th centuries, Dutch and German cartographers such as Willem Blaeu and the makers of the Gottorf globe produced elaborate paired terrestrial and celestial globes; the great Gottorf globe, over three metres in diameter, could be entered from within. By the 19th century printed gores pasted on plaster or wooden spheres made globes widely available for schools.

Construction and use

A typical celestial globe is mounted in a meridian ring within a horizon ring on a stand, allowing it to be set for the observer's latitude. The surface carries the equatorial coordinate grid of right ascension and declination, the ecliptic with the zodiac, and stars plotted to a limiting magnitude with constellation figures engraved or printed. Rotating the globe lets the user simulate the diurnal motion of the heavens.
