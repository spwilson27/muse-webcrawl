Liturgical colour

Chasubles in white, red, green and violet — the principal colours of the Roman Rite.

Liturgical colours are the specific colours used for vestments and hangings within the context of Christian liturgy. The symbolism of violet, white, green, red, gold and other colours helps mark the seasons of the liturgical year and the character of particular feasts and observances. Usage varies between the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, and other traditions, and even within them by period and place.

Roman Rite

The Roman Rite's sequence of colours was largely codified in the reforms of Pope Pius V in the sixteenth century and adjusted in the twentieth century. In current Roman Catholic usage:

ColourUse
White (and gold)Christmas and Easter seasons, feasts of Christ, Mary, angels and non-martyr saints; may replace other colours on solemnities
RedPalm Sunday, Good Friday, Pentecost, feasts of martyrs and of the Holy Spirit
GreenOrdinary Time
Violet (purple)Advent and Lent; may be used for funerals and offices for the dead
BlackTraditionally for funerals and All Souls; now optional in many places
RoseOptionally on Gaudete Sunday (third of Advent) and Laetare Sunday (fourth of Lent)

Other traditions

The Byzantine (Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic) tradition uses colour more flexibly, with broad conventions — gold as the default, light blue for feasts of the Theotokos, white for Pascha and funerals, dark red or purple for Lent — but no single binding system. Anglican and Lutheran churches generally follow the Roman sequence, sometimes with additions such as blue for Advent (the "Saracenic" or Sarum usage) in some Anglican parishes.

History

Colour symbolism in worship is ancient, but fixed sequences developed gradually. Innocent III described a scheme of white, red, green and black around 1200; regional uses (such as the Sarum Use in England, which included blue and yellow) flourished until post-Tridentine standardization in the Latin West.
