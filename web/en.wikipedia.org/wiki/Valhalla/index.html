Valhalla

In Norse mythology, Valhalla (from Old Norse Valhǫll, "hall of the slain") is a majestic hall located in Asgard and ruled over by the god Odin. Half of those who die in combat are chosen by Odin to travel to Valhalla, guided by valkyries, while the other half go to the goddess Freyja's field Fólkvangr. In Valhalla, the dead warriors — the einherjar — prepare to aid Odin during the events of Ragnarök.

Attestations

Valhalla is described in the Poetic Edda, compiled in the 13th century from earlier traditional material, and in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda and Heimskringla. The hall is said to have five hundred and forty doors, walls made of spears and a roof thatched with golden shields; a wolf hangs before its west door and an eagle hovers above. Each day the einherjar arm themselves and fight one another to the death, then rise whole again and feast on the ever-renewed boar Sæhrímnir and mead flowing from the udders of the goat Heiðrún, served by the valkyries.

Ragnarök and legacy

At Ragnarök the einherjar will march out of Valhalla's doors to fight the wolf Fenrir and the forces of chaos alongside the gods, in a battle in which most will perish with the world itself. Valhalla has had a powerful afterlife in art and politics: Richard Wagner's operas, the Walhalla memorial built by Ludwig I of Bavaria above the Danube (1842) to honour Germanic heroes, and countless works of popular culture draw on the image of the hall of the slain.
