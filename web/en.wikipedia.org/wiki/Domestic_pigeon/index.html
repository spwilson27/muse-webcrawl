The domestic pigeon (Columba livia domestica) is a pigeon subspecies derived from the rock dove. It is among the earliest birds domesticated by humans, probably in the Near East more than 5,000 years ago. Domestic pigeons were historically prized as messengers because of their powerful homing instinct, and are still kept for racing, exhibition, and food, while their escaped descendants — feral pigeons — populate cities worldwide.

History

Pigeons appear in Mesopotamian cuneiform records and Egyptian art. They carried messages in the ancient world, announced the winners of the Olympic Games, and served in both World Wars; the hen Cher Ami was decorated for saving a US battalion in 1918. Charles Darwin used domestic pigeon breeds extensively in On the Origin of Species as a model of artificial selection.

Breeds and uses

Hundreds of breeds exist, grouped as homing/racing pigeons, utility pigeons raised for meat (squab), and fancy pigeons bred for appearance — fantails, pouters, tumblers and frillbacks among them. Racing pigeons can navigate hundreds of kilometres home in a day, using the sun, Earth's magnetic field, landmarks and smell.

Feral pigeons

Escaped domestic birds have established feral populations in cities on every inhabited continent. They closely resemble wild rock doves but show far greater plumage variation. City flocks feed on spilled grain and food scraps and nest on building ledges that mimic cliffs.
