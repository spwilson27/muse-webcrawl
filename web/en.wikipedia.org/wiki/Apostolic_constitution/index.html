Apostolic constitution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An apostolic constitution (Latin: constitutio apostolica) is the most solemn form of legal enactment promulgated by the Pope in the Catholic Church. It is generally issued in the form of a papal bull and is used for the most weighty matters of doctrine and discipline — for example, promulgating dogmatic definitions, erecting or suppressing dioceses, issuing the Church's codes of law, and restructuring the Roman Curia. The term constitution comes from the Latin constitutio, originally denoting an imperial enactment.

Form and legal force

Apostolic constitutions are legislative acts of the supreme authority of the Church. They are conventionally given Latin incipit titles drawn from their opening words. Unlike an apostolic exhortation, which is pastoral and exhortatory in character, an apostolic constitution typically contains binding law or definitive doctrinal determinations. It should also be distinguished from the ancient Apostolic Constitutions, a fourth-century church order attributed pseudepigraphally to the Apostles through Clement of Rome, which is a quite different kind of document.

Notable examples

  • Munificentissimus Deus (1950), in which Pius XII defined the dogma of the Assumption of Mary — an ex cathedra definition.
  • Paenitemini (1966), Paul VI's constitution revising the discipline of fasting and abstinence.
  • Sacrae disciplinae leges (1983), by which John Paul II promulgated the current Code of Canon Law for the Latin Church.
  • Universi Dominici Gregis (1996), John Paul II's constitution governing the vacancy of the Apostolic See and the election of the Roman Pontiff.
  • Anglicanorum Coetibus (2009), Benedict XVI's constitution establishing personal ordinariates for former Anglicans entering full communion with the Catholic Church.
  • Praedicate Evangelium (2022), Francis's constitution reforming the Roman Curia, which replaced Pastor Bonus (1988).

Dogmatic constitutions of Vatican II

The Second Vatican Council issued four documents styled "constitutions", its most authoritative texts, although these were conciliar rather than solely papal acts: Sacrosanctum Concilium on the liturgy, Lumen Gentium (a dogmatic constitution on the Church), Dei Verbum (a dogmatic constitution on divine revelation), and Gaudium et Spes (a pastoral constitution on the Church in the modern world).
