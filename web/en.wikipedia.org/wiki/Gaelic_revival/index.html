The Gaelic revival (Irish: Athbheochan na Gaeilge) was a late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century movement to revive interest in the Irish language and in Gaelic culture, including literature, sport, music and folklore. It formed a major strand of Irish cultural nationalism in the decades before Irish independence and deeply influenced the generation that led the revolutionary period.

Origins

By the late nineteenth century the Irish language had retreated to the rural west under pressure from anglicisation, the national school system and the devastation of the Great Famine in the most strongly Irish-speaking districts. Scholarly and antiquarian interest in Irish manuscripts and oral tradition — from the Ordnance Survey letters of John O'Donovan to the work of Eugene O'Curry — laid the groundwork for revival. The Society for the Preservation of the Irish Language (1876) and the Gaelic Union (1880) published journals and textbooks, while the Gaelic Athletic Association (1884) revived native sports.

The Gaelic League

The decisive organisation was the Gaelic League, founded in 1893 by Douglas Hyde and Eoin MacNeill after Hyde's lecture "The Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland". The League promoted Irish through classes, branches in towns across Ireland and the diaspora, festivals (feiseanna) and publications, and campaigned successfully for Irish in schools and for its recognition by the National University. Writers such as Patrick Pearse, Pádraic Ó Conaire and Peadar Ua Laoghaire created a modern Irish literature, and the revival's publishing houses issued hundreds of titles.

Legacy

Though Hyde sought to keep the League non-political, many of its members — including Pearse — moved toward separatism, and the movement is often seen as the cultural seedbed of the Easter Rising and the Irish revolutionary period. In the independent Irish Free State after 1922, Irish became the first official language and a compulsory school subject; the revival's ambitions for a fully Irish-speaking nation were not realised, but the language survives in the Gaeltacht regions and in a growing urban revival movement today.
