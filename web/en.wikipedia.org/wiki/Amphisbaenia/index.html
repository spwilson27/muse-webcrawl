Amphisbaenia

Kingdom: Animalia

Phylum: Chordata

Class: Reptilia

Order: Squamata

Clade: Amphisbaenia (Gray, 1844)

The Amphisbaenia (called amphisbaenians or worm lizards) are a group of usually legless squamate reptiles, comprising over 200 extant species. Amphisbaenians are characterised by their long bodies, reduced or absent limbs, and rudimentary eyes, adaptations to a burrowing lifestyle spent largely underground in loose soil and leaf litter.

Anatomy

The head is stout, not set off from the neck, with a rounded snout in most species, though some have a shovel-shaped snout or a keel on the head used for tunnelling. The skin is divided into ring-like segments (annuli), giving the animals an earthworm-like appearance, and the loose skin allows them to move within tunnels almost as easily backwards as forwards — a trait reflected in their name, from the mythical two-headed serpent Amphisbaena. The tail is short and blunt, often resembling the head, which may confuse predators.

Distribution

Amphisbaenians are found in South America, the Caribbean, Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and around the Mediterranean in Europe and western Asia. A single species, the Florida worm lizard (Rhineura floridana), occurs in North America. Most species are poorly known because of their secretive, subterranean habits.

Taxonomy

The group is usually divided into six families: Amphisbaenidae, Bipedidae (the only family with well-developed forelimbs, found in Mexico), Blanidae, Cadeidae, Rhineuridae, and Trogonophidae. Molecular studies place amphisbaenians within the Lacertoidea, close to the lacertid lizards.

Diet and behaviour

Amphisbaenians are carnivorous, feeding on insects, earthworms, and other small invertebrates, and occasionally small vertebrates. Their powerful jaws and interlocking teeth allow them to tear pieces from larger prey. Most are oviparous, laying small clutches of eggs underground.
