Eclogue

An eclogue is a poem in a classical style on a pastoral subject. Poems in the genre are sometimes also called bucolics. The term derives from Greek eklogē, meaning a selection.

Eclogue
Genre: Pastoral poetry
Origin: Theocritus, Idylls
Major work: Virgil's Eclogues

History

The form was developed by the Greek poet Theocritus in his Idylls. The Roman poet Virgil imitated them in his Eclogues (Bucolica, c. 42–39 BC), ten poems featuring shepherds conversing and singing in an idealized Arcadian landscape, often with allegorical references to contemporary politics and poets, including Julius Caesar and Octavian.

Later tradition

Renaissance poets revived the form: Mantuan, Sannazaro, Edmund Spenser's The Shepheardes Calender (1579), and Alexander Pope's Pastorals. Eclogues could be dialogues, elegies, or singing contests between shepherds, blending rustic setting with sophisticated commentary on love, death, patronage, and poetry itself.

Modern uses

Louis MacNeice, Seamus Heaney and others adapted the eclogue for modern rural and political subjects. The term is also used musically for pastoral compositions.
