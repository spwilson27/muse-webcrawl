Computational linguistics

Computational linguistics is an interdisciplinary field concerned with the computational modelling of natural language, as well as the study of appropriate computational approaches to linguistic questions. In general, computational linguistics draws upon linguistics, computer science, artificial intelligence, mathematics, logic, philosophy, cognitive science, psychology, and anthropology. It is closely related to natural language processing (NLP), which emphasises practical applications; computational linguistics tends to stress the modelling of language itself and the use of computation to test linguistic theory, though the boundary is blurred and the professional community (organised around the Association for Computational Linguistics) largely shares both aims.

Subfields

  • Computational phonetics and phonology — modelling speech sounds and sound systems.
  • Computational morphology and syntax — parsing word structure and sentence structure, formal grammars such as context-free and dependency grammars.
  • Computational semantics and pragmatics — representing meaning, reference, discourse, and dialogue.
  • Corpus linguistics — statistical analysis of large text collections.
  • Machine translation — from rule-based systems through statistical methods to neural machine translation.
  • Speech technology — speech recognition and synthesis.

History

The field began in the 1950s with efforts in the United States to translate Russian scientific texts automatically (the Georgetown–IBM experiment of 1954). Early optimism was tempered by the ALPAC report (1966), which cut funding for machine translation. Theoretical work continued through formal language theory and parsing research; the 1980s and 1990s brought a statistical revolution based on large corpora and probabilistic models such as hidden Markov models. From the 2010s, deep learning transformed the field: word embeddings, sequence-to-sequence models, the transformer architecture (2017), and large language models such as BERT and the GPT family achieved major gains on translation, summarisation, question answering, and dialogue, while reviving theoretical questions about how much linguistic structure such models learn.

Applications

Search engines, machine translation, virtual assistants, sentiment analysis, information extraction, grammar checking, text summarisation, and tools for language documentation and education all draw on computational linguistics. The field's main venues include the ACL conferences and the journal Computational Linguistics.
