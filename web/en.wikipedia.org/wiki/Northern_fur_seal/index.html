The northern fur seal (Callorhinus ursinus) is an eared seal found in the cold waters of the North Pacific Ocean, the Bering Sea and the Sea of Okhotsk. It is the only living member of the genus Callorhinus and is distinguished by its dense underfur, which once made it one of the most commercially valuable of all seals.

Description and behaviour

The species is strongly sexually dimorphic: adult males weigh 180–270 kilograms and are much larger than females, which weigh 30–50 kilograms. Both sexes have long flippers and thick fur over most of the body. Northern fur seals are highly migratory and pelagic for most of the year, feeding on fish and squid; in summer they gather at traditional breeding rookeries, principally on the Pribilof Islands in Alaska and on Russia's Commander Islands and Robben Island. Males establish harems, and females, which give birth shortly after arriving ashore, make long foraging trips, nursing their pups between voyages.

Exploitation and conservation

Intensive hunting for skins in the 18th and 19th centuries — including pelagic sealing at sea — reduced the population drastically. The North Pacific Fur Seal Convention of 1911 between the United States, Britain (Canada), Japan and Russia banned pelagic sealing and is considered the first international wildlife conservation treaty; under regulated land harvesting the herds recovered to roughly two million animals. Commercial harvests later ended (except for subsistence takes by Alaska Natives), but the Pribilof population declined again from the late 20th century for reasons including prey changes, entanglement and predation, and the species is listed as Vulnerable.
