Northern dynasties

The Northern dynasties (Chinese: 北朝; pinyin: Běi Cháo) is the collective name for the succession of regimes that ruled northern China from 386 to 581 CE, during the Northern and Southern dynasties period. They were, in sequence: the Northern Wei (386–534), the Eastern Wei (534–550), the Western Wei (535–557), the Northern Qi (550–577), and the Northern Zhou (557–581).

Overview

The period began when the Tuoba (Tabgach) clan of the Xianbei unified the north under Northern Wei, ending the chaotic Sixteen Kingdoms era. Northern Wei moved its capital to Luoyang in 494 and pursued extensive sinicisation, while its equal-field land system and other institutions profoundly shaped later Chinese government. After civil war split Wei into eastern and western successor states, the east passed to Northern Qi and the west to Northern Zhou. In 577 Northern Zhou conquered Qi and reunified the north; in 581 Yang Jian usurped the Zhou throne to found the Sui dynasty, which reunified all of China in 589.

Society and culture

Northern dynasties society blended Han Chinese administrative traditions with Xianbei and other steppe customs; aristocratic clans dominated government, and Buddhism flourished with imperial patronage, producing the cave temples of Yungang and Longmen. The northern courts' military colonies and the fubing militia system under Western Wei and Northern Zhou laid institutional foundations inherited by Sui and Tang.
