Charles Émile Picard (24 July 1856 – 11 December 1941) was a French mathematician, one of the most prominent analysts of his generation. His work ranged over complex function theory, differential equations, and algebraic geometry, and his theorems — the Picard theorem on essential singularities and the Picard–Lindelöf theorem on the existence and uniqueness of solutions of ordinary differential equations — remain staples of the subject.

Life and career

Picard lost his father during the Siege of Paris in the Franco-Prussian War and was educated at the Lycée Henri-IV and the École Normale Supérieure, graduating first in his class in 1877. He taught at Toulouse and then at the École Normale and the Sorbonne in Paris, where he held the chair of analysis for most of his career. His three-volume Traité d'analyse became a standard reference for a generation of mathematicians.

Mathematical contributions

In 1879 Picard proved his famous theorem: an entire function that omits two values in the complex plane must be constant — a vast strengthening of Casorati–Weierstrass theory, with the "little theorem" (excluding one value allows only a constant as well when the function omits two points) and the "great theorem" on behaviour near an essential singularity. His method of successive approximations underlies the existence theory for differential equations, and the Picard group and Picard scheme in algebraic geometry are named for his work on integrals on algebraic surfaces, developed in his long collaboration with Georges Simart.

Honours

Picard was elected to the Académie des sciences in 1889 and served as its perpetual secretary from 1917; he was elected to the Académie française in 1924. He received the Poncelet Prize and was a foreign member of many learned societies. An asteroid, 7236 Émilepicard, is named after him.
