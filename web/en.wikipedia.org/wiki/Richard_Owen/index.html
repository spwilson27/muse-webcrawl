Sir Richard Owen KCB FRMS FRS (20 July 1804 – 18 December 1892) was an English biologist, comparative anatomist and palaeontologist. Owen is regarded as one of the most influential scientists of Victorian Britain: he coined the word Dinosauria, formalised the distinction between homology and analogy, described hundreds of fossil species, and led the campaign that created the Natural History Museum in London. He was also a prominent opponent of Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection.

Life and career

Owen was born in Lancaster and trained in medicine at Edinburgh and St Bartholomew's Hospital, London. In 1827 he became assistant curator of the Hunterian Museum of the Royal College of Surgeons, and in 1836 its Hunterian Professor; his catalogues of the collection made his reputation as the foremost comparative anatomist in Britain, sometimes called "the British Cuvier". In 1856 he became superintendent of the natural history departments of the British Museum, from which position he campaigned for a separate museum of natural history; the result, the Natural History Museum in South Kensington, opened in 1881.

Scientific work

Owen reconstructed numerous extinct animals, often from fragmentary remains: he described the giant moa of New Zealand from a single bone fragment, identified the first fossils of the mammal-like reptiles of South Africa, and in 1842 recognised that Megalosaurus, Iguanodon and Hylaeosaurus formed a distinct group, which he named Dinosauria. His 1843 definitions of homologue ("the same organ in different animals under every variety of form and function") and analogue remain foundational to comparative anatomy. He advised on the famous life-sized dinosaur models at the Crystal Palace. Invertebrate zoology also owes him major works, including the first description of Trichinella spiralis.

Evolution controversy

Owen accepted that species had changed over geological time but rejected natural selection, favouring a form of divinely guided derivation. His anonymous hostile review of On the Origin of Species and his support of Bishop Samuel Wilberforce at the 1860 Oxford evolution debate placed him at odds with Darwin, Huxley and Hooker. A bitter dispute over the anatomy of the ape brain — Owen claimed, wrongly, that structures such as the hippocampus minor were unique to humans — damaged his reputation, and his standing declined in later life even as his museum project triumphed.
