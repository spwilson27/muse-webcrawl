Luminiferous aether

Luminiferous aether (also spelled ether) was the postulated medium for the propagation of light in 18th- and 19th-century physics. It was proposed to explain how light waves could travel through the vacuum of space, by analogy with sound waves travelling through air. The hypothesis implied a fixed frame of reference — the "aether frame" — through which the Earth and other bodies were thought to move.

History

Christiaan Huygens proposed that light is a wave in an all-pervading medium, in contrast to Newton's corpuscular theory. After Thomas Young and Augustin-Jean Fresnel established the wave theory in the early 19th century, the aether became a central object of physical theory. Physicists struggled to reconcile its required properties: it had to be enormously rigid to support light's high wave speed, yet offer no detectable resistance to the motion of planets.

Michelson–Morley experiment

In 1887, Albert A. Michelson and Edward W. Morley attempted to detect the Earth's motion through the aether by measuring differences in the speed of light in different directions using an interferometer. The result was null: no "aether wind" was found. Attempts to rescue the hypothesis, such as the FitzGerald–Lorentz length contraction and Lorentz's aether theory, became increasingly ad hoc.

Abandonment

Albert Einstein's theory of special relativity (1905) explained the null result by postulating that the speed of light is constant in all inertial frames, making the aether superfluous. Einstein remarked that the introduction of a "light ether" would prove to be superfluous; the concept was abandoned by mainstream physics, although Einstein later used the word "ether" metaphorically for the physical properties of spacetime in general relativity.
