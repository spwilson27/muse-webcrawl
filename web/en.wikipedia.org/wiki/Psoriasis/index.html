Psoriasis is a long-lasting, noncontagious autoimmune disease characterised by patches of abnormal skin. These areas are typically red or purple, raised, dry and scaly, and result from the greatly accelerated production of skin cells — keratinocytes may be produced in days rather than the normal month — driven by an overactive immune response involving T cells and inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and interleukins 17 and 23. Psoriasis varies in severity from small localised patches to complete body coverage, and injury to the skin can trigger changes at the injured site (the Koebner phenomenon).

Types and signs

The most common form, plaque psoriasis (psoriasis vulgaris, about 90% of cases), presents as well-demarcated salmon-pink plaques with silvery-white scale, favouring the elbows, knees, scalp and lower back. Other forms include guttate psoriasis (drop-like lesions, often after streptococcal throat infection), inverse psoriasis in skin folds, pustular psoriasis, and the potentially life-threatening generalised redness of erythrodermic psoriasis. Nail changes (pitting, onycholysis, "oil drops") are common, and around a quarter to a third of patients develop psoriatic arthritis.

Causes and triggers

Psoriasis is a multifactorial disease with strong heritability; the susceptibility locus PSORS1 in the major histocompatibility complex, involving HLA-Cw6, accounts for a large share of genetic risk. Flares may be triggered by infections (especially streptococcal), skin trauma, stress, cold weather, alcohol, smoking and certain medicines including lithium, beta blockers and antimalarials. Psoriasis is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, depression and inflammatory bowel disease.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, based on the appearance and distribution of lesions; severity is scored with instruments such as the PASI (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index). There is no cure, but many treatments control symptoms:

  • Topical — emollients, corticosteroids, vitamin D analogues (calcipotriol), coal tar, dithranol and retinoids.
  • Phototherapy — narrowband UVB, and PUVA (psoralen plus UVA).
  • Systemic — methotrexate, ciclosporin, acitretin and apremilast.
  • Biologics — monoclonal antibodies targeting TNF-α (e.g. adalimumab, etanercept), IL-12/23 (ustekinumab), IL-17 (secukinumab, ixekizumab) and IL-23 (guselkumab, risankizumab), which have transformed outcomes in moderate-to-severe disease.

History and epidemiology

The disease was described in antiquity and long confused with leprosy — a source of enduring stigma. It affects roughly 2–3% of people worldwide, more commonly in northern populations and less near the equator; in the United States about 7.5 million adults are affected. World Psoriasis Day is observed on 29 October.
