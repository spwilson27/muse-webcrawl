Lung compliance, or pulmonary compliance, is a measure of the lung's ability to stretch and expand: the change in volume produced by a given change in transpulmonary pressure, C = ΔV/ΔP. Together with airway resistance it determines the work of breathing. Compliance is the reciprocal of elastance.

Physiology

The pressure–volume curve of the lung is non-linear and shows hysteresis: inflation and deflation follow different paths, partly because of the surface-tension properties of the alveolar lining and the action of pulmonary surfactant, a phospholipid (chiefly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) secreted by type II alveolar cells that lowers surface tension and stabilises alveoli of different sizes. Elastic fibres of elastin and collagen in the lung parenchyma also contribute; collagen limits expansion at high volumes, making the lung stiffer near total lung capacity. Static compliance is measured under no-flow conditions and reflects elastic properties, whereas dynamic compliance, measured during breathing, is also influenced by airway resistance.

Clinical significance

Compliance is decreased ("stiff lungs") in pulmonary fibrosis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), pneumonia, and pulmonary oedema, increasing the work of breathing. Compliance is increased in emphysema, where destruction of alveolar walls reduces elastic recoil; the lungs inflate easily but empty poorly. In neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, surfactant deficiency lowers compliance in premature infants. Compliance measurements guide mechanical ventilation settings in intensive care.
