Nuclear power in the United States

Nuclear power in the United States is provided by commercial nuclear power plants that together form the largest fleet of reactors in the world. The United States generates more nuclear electricity than any other country, and nuclear power supplies roughly one fifth of the nation's electricity and about half of its carbon-free electricity.

History

The first commercial nuclear power station in the United States, Shippingport Atomic Power Station in Pennsylvania, began operation in 1957. The industry expanded rapidly in the 1960s and 1970s. Growth stalled after the Three Mile Island accident of 1979, and for decades few new reactors were ordered. The fleet peaked at 112 operating reactors; by the 2020s around 93 reactors remained in operation as aging plants retired for economic reasons. The completion of Vogtle Units 3 and 4 in Georgia in 2023–2024, the first new American reactors in decades, marked a revival of interest, alongside work on small modular reactors.

Regulation

Nuclear power plants are licensed and overseen by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC). Many reactors have received license renewals extending their operating lives from 40 to 60 years, and some to 80 years. Spent nuclear fuel is stored on site, as the planned Yucca Mountain repository in Nevada was never completed.
