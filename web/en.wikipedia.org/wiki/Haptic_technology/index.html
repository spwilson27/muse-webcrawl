Haptic technology

Haptic technology (from Greek haptikos, "able to touch") is technology that interfaces with a user through the sense of touch, by applying forces, vibrations, or motions. Also called kinaesthetic communication or 3D touch, haptics is used in devices ranging from mobile phones and game controllers to surgical simulators, teleoperation systems, and virtual reality equipment.

Types

Haptic feedback falls broadly into two categories. Vibrotactile feedback, the most common, uses small motors or piezoelectric actuators—including eccentric rotating mass motors and linear resonant actuators—to produce vibration, as in phone notifications and the "rumble" of game controllers. Force feedback devices resist or assist the user's motion through motors and linkages, allowing the user to feel virtual weight, stiffness, and contact; examples include force-feedback joysticks and steering wheels, grounded haptic interfaces such as the PHANTOM arm, and surgical training simulators. Other approaches include electrotactile stimulation, ultrasonic mid-air haptics, and pneumatic systems in gloves and suits.

Applications

Haptics improves accessibility for visually impaired users, supports remote palpation and telesurgery, trains pilots and surgeons in simulators, and enriches virtual and augmented reality by adding touch to visual and auditory immersion. Automotive interfaces use haptic confirmation to reduce drivers' need to look at controls. Research continues into reproducing texture, temperature, and friction on touchscreens, for example by modulating surface friction with electrostatic or ultrasonic effects.

History

Early haptic systems appeared in aircraft controls and in teleoperation research of the mid-twentieth century. The field grew rapidly in the 1990s with commercial force-feedback devices and the founding of companies such as Immersion Corporation and SensAble Technologies, and vibration feedback became standard in mobile devices in the 2000s.
