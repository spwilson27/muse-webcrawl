Air–fuel ratio (AFR) is the mass ratio of air to a solid, liquid, or gaseous fuel present in a combustion process. The combustion may take place in a controlled manner such as in an internal combustion engine or industrial furnace, or the ratio may describe an uncontrolled event such as a dust explosion.

Stoichiometric ratio

The stoichiometric air–fuel ratio is the ratio at which exactly enough oxygen is present to burn all the fuel completely. For pure octane it corresponds to a reaction in which all carbon forms CO2 and all hydrogen forms H2O. For typical pump petrol the stoichiometric ratio is about 14.7 parts air to 1 part fuel by mass; for diesel about 14.5:1, for ethanol about 9:1, and for methanol about 6.4:1. Mixtures with more fuel than stoichiometric are called rich and those with less are called lean. Rich mixtures produce more power and cooler combustion but more carbon monoxide and unburned hydrocarbons; lean mixtures improve economy but raise combustion temperatures and nitrogen-oxide emissions.

Lambda and control

Engineers often express the ratio relative to stoichiometric as lambda (λ): λ = 1 is stoichiometric, λ < 1 is rich, and λ > 1 is lean. Modern engine management systems use zirconia or wideband oxygen sensors in the exhaust to hold the mixture near λ = 1 so that three-way catalytic converters can simultaneously oxidize CO and hydrocarbons and reduce NOx. Racing and performance applications use wideband AFR gauges for tuning across the load range.
