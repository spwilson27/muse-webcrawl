Arnhem Land

Arnhem Land
Country: Australia
Territory: Northern Territory
Area: approx. 97,000 km²
Named after: The Dutch ship Arnhem (1623)

Arnhem Land is a historical region of the Northern Territory of Australia, covering roughly the north-eastern corner of the territory's "Top End". It is one of the largest Aboriginal reserves in Australia and is known for its strong continuing Indigenous culture, rock art, and rugged sandstone escarpments and wetlands.

Geography

The region borders the Arafura Sea and the Gulf of Carpentaria, and includes the Cobourg Peninsula, the Gove Peninsula, and the western edge of the great Arnhem Land plateau. Its western boundary adjoins Kakadu National Park. Major communities include Nhulunbuy, a mining town on the Gove Peninsula, and Maningrida, Jabiru (on the Kakadu border), and Gunbalanya (Oenpelli).

History and culture

Aboriginal peoples, including the Yolngu of the north-east, have lived in Arnhem Land for tens of thousands of years. Shelters in the region preserve some of the world's richest rock art, including early depictions of contact with Macassan traders from Indonesia, who visited for trepang (sea cucumber) long before European settlement. The region was named by Matthew Flinders after the Dutch ship Arnhem, which charted the coast in 1623.

Land rights

Arnhem Land was declared an Aboriginal reserve in 1931. The Yolngu bark petitions of 1963, protesting bauxite mining at Gove, became a landmark in the Australian land rights movement, preceding the landmark Mabo-era reforms. Entry by non-Indigenous visitors generally requires a permit.
