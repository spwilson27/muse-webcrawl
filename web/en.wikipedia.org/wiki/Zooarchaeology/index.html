Zooarchaeology (also spelled archaeozoology) is a branch of archaeology that studies animal remains from archaeological sites. It examines the relationships between past human societies and the animals they hunted, herded, domesticated, traded, and consumed, reconstructing diet, economy, environment, and ritual practice from bones, teeth, shells, and other faunal remains.

History

The discipline developed from nineteenth-century studies of cave deposits and kitchen middens in Europe, where naturalists such as Ludwig Rütimeyer analyzed animal bones from Swiss lake dwellings. In the twentieth century, zooarchaeology became a standard component of excavation, particularly in the study of animal domestication in the Fertile Crescent and of hunting strategies among hunter-gatherers.

Methods

Zooarchaeologists identify specimens to species where possible and quantify assemblages using measures such as the Number of Identified Specimens (NISP) and the Minimum Number of Individuals (MNI). Cut marks, burning, gnawing, and breakage patterns — studied under taphonomy — help distinguish human activity from natural accumulation. Age and sex profiles reveal herd management and hunting selectivity, while isotopic analysis of bone collagen can reconstruct diet and mobility.

Applications

Faunal analysis has documented the origins of domesticated cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs; tracked the spread of dairying; identified seasonal occupation of sites; and reconstructed past environments from the species present. It also informs modern conservation by providing baselines for historical animal populations.
