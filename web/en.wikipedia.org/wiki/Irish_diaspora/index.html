Irish diaspora

The Irish diaspora refers to ethnic Irish people and their descendants who live outside the island of Ireland. Emigration has been a defining feature of Irish history for centuries, driven by conquest, religious persecution, poverty, and — most catastrophically — the Great Famine of 1845–1852, and has produced large Irish-descended populations in the United States, Britain, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Argentina.

Scale

Ireland's population fell from over 8 million in 1841 to about 4.4 million by 1911, a decline unmatched in Europe, and emigration continued through most of the twentieth century. Today the population of the island is around 7 million, while the number of people worldwide claiming Irish ancestry is estimated in the tens of millions — more than 30 million in the United States alone, where Irish Americans form one of the largest ancestry groups.

Waves of emigration

Early emigration included the "Wild Geese" — Jacobite soldiers who left for France and Spain after 1691 — and Scots-Irish Presbyterians who settled the American frontier in the 18th century. Mass Catholic emigration followed the Famine, sending millions to Liverpool, Glasgow, Boston, New York and beyond, where Irish communities built churches, political machines and labour movements. Later waves followed the economic cycles of independent Ireland, with significant outflows in the 1950s and 1980s, and a reversal during the Celtic Tiger boom of the 1990s–2000s, when returning emigrants and new immigrants arrived.

Culture and identity

The diaspora sustains Irish culture worldwide through music, dance, sport (the GAA has clubs on every continent), St Patrick's Day celebrations — the largest of which are held abroad — and political engagement, notably Irish-American involvement in the Northern Ireland peace process. The Irish government maintains formal ties with the diaspora, including an emigrant support programme and, since 2013, a minister of state for the diaspora.
