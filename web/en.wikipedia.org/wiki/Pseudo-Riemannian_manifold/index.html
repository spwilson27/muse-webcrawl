Pseudo-Riemannian manifold

Pseudo-Riemannian manifold
FieldDifferential geometry
StructureSmooth manifold with non-degenerate metric tensor
Special caseLorentzian manifold (signature (1, n−1) or (n−1, 1))
ApplicationGeneral relativity

In differential geometry, a pseudo-Riemannian manifold, also called a semi-Riemannian manifold, is a differentiable manifold equipped with a metric tensor that is everywhere non-degenerate but not necessarily positive-definite. When the metric is positive-definite the structure reduces to a Riemannian manifold. Because the metric may assign negative "lengths squared" to some tangent vectors, pseudo-Riemannian geometry admits directions of positive, negative and zero norm at every point.

Definitions

For a smooth manifold M, a metric tensor g is a symmetric, non-degenerate (0,2)-tensor field varying smoothly from point to point. By Sylvester's law of inertia the index of g — the number of negative eigenvalues in any diagonalisation — is the same in every tangent space (assuming M is connected) and defines the signature (p, q). A tangent vector v is classified as timelike, spacelike or null (lightlike) according to whether g(v, v) is negative, positive or zero (one common sign convention swaps the first two). The zero sets form a cone structure generalising the light cone of special relativity.

Lorentzian manifolds

The case of signature (1, n−1) — one direction of one sign and the rest of the other — is called Lorentzian. Lorentzian manifolds are the mathematical setting of Einstein's general relativity: spacetime is modelled as a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold whose metric obeys the Einstein field equations, and freely falling observers follow timelike geodesics. Minkowski space is the flat Lorentzian manifold of special relativity.

Geometry

Much of the Riemannian machinery carries over: the Levi-Civita connection exists and is unique, geodesics are defined, and curvature tensors are constructed identically. Key differences arise from the indefinite metric — the manifold has no canonical distance function, Hopf–Rinow-type results fail, and causal structure (the relations "can be connected by a timelike curve") becomes a central object of study, culminating in the singularity theorems of Penrose and Hawking.
