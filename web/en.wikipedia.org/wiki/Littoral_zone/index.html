Littoral zone

A rocky shore littoral zone at low tide, showing zonation of algae and barnacles.

The littoral zone, also called the intertidal zone in marine contexts, is the part of a sea, lake, or river shore that lies close to the water's edge and is influenced by its proximity to the water. In marine environments the term usually denotes the area between the highest high-tide line and the lowest low-tide line, which is alternately flooded and exposed by the tides. In limnology, the littoral zone of a lake is the shallow near-shore region where sunlight reaches the bottom and rooted plants can grow.

Marine littoral zone

Marine littoral habitats include rocky shores, sandy beaches, mudflats, and salt marshes. Organisms living there must tolerate immersion in seawater, exposure to air and sun, wave action, and rapid swings in temperature and salinity. Communities typically show vertical zonation: distinct bands of barnacles, mussels, seaweeds, limpets, and periwinkles occupy levels set by their tolerance of desiccation and by competition and predation. Subdivisions include the supralittoral (splash) zone above high tide, the midlittoral zone, and the infralittoral fringe exposed only at the lowest tides.

Lake littoral zone

In lakes, the littoral zone extends from the shore to the depth at which light no longer supports rooted macrophytes; beyond it lies the deeper profundal zone. Emergent, floating, and submerged plants shelter fish fry, invertebrates, and amphibians, making the littoral the most biologically productive and biodiverse part of most lakes. Wave action and wind-driven currents sort its sediments from gravel near shore to fine silt in deeper water.

Ecology and human impact

Littoral zones are nursery grounds, feeding areas, and buffers that filter runoff and protect shores from erosion. They are vulnerable to coastal development, pollution, trampling, invasive species, and sea-level rise, which can squeeze the zone against fixed defences ("coastal squeeze").
