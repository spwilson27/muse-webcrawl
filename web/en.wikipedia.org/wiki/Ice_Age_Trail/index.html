Ice Age Trail

The Ice Age Trail is a National Scenic Trail stretching approximately 1,200 miles (1,900 km) through the U.S. state of Wisconsin. It follows the terminal moraine left by the last continental glacier, which retreated from the region roughly 12,000 years ago, and passes through some of the most distinctive glacial landscapes in North America. The trail is one of only eleven trails designated as National Scenic Trails by the United States Congress.

History

The trail was conceived in the 1950s by Ray Zillmer, a Milwaukee attorney and conservationist who imagined a long-distance "Ice Age National Park" tracing Wisconsin's glacial features. The Ice Age Park and Trail Foundation (now the Ice Age Trail Alliance) was founded in 1958 to advance the idea. Congress authorized the Ice Age Trail as a National Scenic Trail in 1980, and in 1987 the State of Wisconsin designated it a State Trail as well. The trail is administered in partnership by the National Park Service, the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources, and the Ice Age Trail Alliance, with thousands of volunteers building and maintaining segments.

Route

From its western terminus at Interstate State Park on the St. Croix River, the trail winds south and east through northern Wisconsin forests, crosses the Baraboo Range and the Kettle Moraine — a landscape of kames, kettles, eskers, and drumlins deposited by the glacier — and reaches its eastern terminus at Potawatomi State Park on Green Bay. Along the way it passes through more than thirty Wisconsin counties and connects numerous state parks, state forests, and county lands. Roughly 700 miles of the route are completed trail; the remainder follows temporary connectors on rural roads.

Geology

The trail showcases the evidence of continental glaciation: the terminal moraine marking the glacier's farthest advance, outwash plains, glacial erratics, and kettle lakes. It passes near Devil's Lake State Park, the Dells of the Eau Claire River, and the Chippewa Moraine State Recreation Area, where interpretive centers explain the glacial story.

Use

Hikers who complete the entire trail are known as "Thousand-Milers" and are recognized by the Ice Age Trail Alliance. The trail supports day hiking, backpacking, and, on many segments, snowshoeing; dispersed camping areas and campgrounds are located along completed sections.
