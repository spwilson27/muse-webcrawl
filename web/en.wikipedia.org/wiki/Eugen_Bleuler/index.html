Eugen Bleuler

Image: Eugen Bleuler (placeholder)

Paul Eugen Bleuler (30 April 1857 – 15 July 1939) was a Swiss psychiatrist best known for coining the term schizophrenia and for his contributions to the understanding of mental illness. He served for decades as director of the Burghölzli psychiatric clinic in Zurich, one of the most influential psychiatric institutions in Europe.

Life and career

Bleuler was born in Zollikon, near Zurich, into a family of wealthy farmers. He studied medicine at the University of Zurich and later worked under the psychiatrist Auguste Forel at the Burghölzli asylum, succeeding him as director in 1898. He held the post until 1927 and also served as professor of psychiatry at the University of Zurich.

Schizophrenia

In 1908 Bleuler introduced the term schizophrenia, derived from the Greek words for "split" and "mind", to replace Emil Kraepelin's earlier term dementia praecox. Bleuler argued that the condition was not necessarily a dementia and did not always begin early in life. His 1911 monograph Dementia Praecox or the Group of Schizophrenias described the "four A's" of the disorder: disturbed associations, autism, abnormal affect, and ambivalence. Bleuler also coined the term autism, later developed by others in the context of developmental disorders.

Influence

Bleuler's ideas influenced psychoanalysis; he was an early supporter of Sigmund Freud's work and employed Carl Jung as an assistant physician at the Burghölzli. His more psychological and less purely prognostic approach to psychosis contrasted with Kraepelin's and reshaped twentieth-century psychiatry.
