Maoism

Maoism, officially known in China as Mao Zedong Thought, is a variety of Marxism–Leninism developed by Mao Zedong, leader of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), for realising a socialist revolution in the agricultural, pre-industrial society of China. It differs from other forms of Marxism–Leninism in its emphasis on the peasantry as the main revolutionary force, on protracted people's war, and on the mass line in political practice.

Key ideas

Central concepts include: the theory of contradictions and their analysis "On Contradiction" (1937); protracted people's war, in which a revolutionary army based in the countryside gradually encircles and captures the cities; the mass line — "from the masses, to the masses"; New Democracy, an alliance of classes in the first stage of revolution; and the theory of continuing revolution under socialism, which underlay the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976). Mao also championed self-reliance and criticised Soviet "revisionism" during the Sino-Soviet split.

History and influence

Mao Zedong Thought was enshrined as the guiding ideology of the CCP and guided policy in the People's Republic of China after 1949, including the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution, both associated with mass deaths and upheaval. After Mao's death, Deng Xiaoping's reforms retained Mao Zedong Thought formally while reversing many of its economic policies.

Internationally, Maoism inspired insurgent movements such as the Shining Path in Peru, the Naxalites in India, and the Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist), as well as New Left currents in the West. The designation "Marxism–Leninism–Maoism" is used by parties that regard Maoism as a higher stage of Marxism, a point of dispute among communist movements.
