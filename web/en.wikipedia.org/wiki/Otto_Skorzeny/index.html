Otto Johann Anton Skorzeny (12 June 1908 – 5 July 1975) was an Austrian-born German SS officer and commando leader in the Waffen-SS during the Second World War. He became famous for leading the glider-borne raid that rescued the deposed Italian dictator Benito Mussolini from imprisonment in 1943, and Allied propaganda dubbed him "the most dangerous man in Europe."

Early life

Skorzeny was born in Vienna into a middle-class family of Polish descent. He studied engineering at the University of Vienna, where he was a keen duelling fencer — a scar on his cheek from a student duel became his trademark. He joined the Austrian Nazi Party in 1931 and the SA, and after the Anschluss transferred into the SS.

Second World War

Serving first with the Leibstandarte SS Adolf Hitler and then the Das Reich division, Skorzeny fought in the Netherlands, France, the Balkans and on the Eastern Front, where he was wounded in 1941. Recalled to Germany, he was put in charge of commando training at Oranienburg and built up special units intended to imitate the British Commandos.

Gran Sasso raid

After Mussolini was overthrown and arrested in July 1943, Skorzeny was ordered to find and free him. On 12 September 1943 he led a force that landed by glider on the Gran Sasso plateau in the Apennines and released Mussolini from the Campo Imperatore hotel without a shot being fired, flying him out in a small Fieseler Storch aircraft. The raid made Skorzeny a celebrity in Germany; he was promoted and awarded the Knight's Cross of the Iron Cross. (The role of the accompanying Luftwaffe paratroopers of Major Harald Mors has since been stressed by historians.)

Later operations

In 1944 Skorzeny directed Operation Panzerfaust, the abduction of Miklós Horthy Jr. that forced Hungary's regent to abdicate and kept Hungary in the Axis. During the Ardennes Offensive he commanded Operation Greif, in which English-speaking German soldiers in American uniforms infiltrated behind US lines to sow confusion — a mission that, though militarily minor, caused panic and led to captured commandos being executed as spies.

Postwar life

Skorzeny surrendered to the Americans in May 1945. Tried at Dachau in 1947 for war crimes connected with Operation Greif, he was acquitted; held thereafter for denazification proceedings, he escaped from an internment camp in 1948. He settled in Franco's Spain, worked as an engineer and businessman, advised Egypt's President Nasser on military matters in the 1950s, and published memoirs presenting himself as an apolitical soldier — a self-image historians treat sceptically. He died of lung cancer in Madrid on 5 July 1975.
