History of the camera

The history of the camera traces the development of devices for recording images, from the ancient camera obscura to modern digital cameras. The camera obscura — a darkened room or box with a small aperture projecting an inverted image — was described by scholars including Ibn al-Haytham in the eleventh century and was used by artists for centuries as a drawing aid.

Photographic cameras

In 1816 Nicéphore Niépce produced the first partially successful photographs on paper coated with silver chloride, and around 1826–1827 he made the earliest surviving permanent photograph, View from the Window at Le Gras, using a bitumen-coated pewter plate. His partner Louis Daguerre developed the daguerreotype process, announced in 1839, which created detailed images on silver-plated copper. In England, William Henry Fox Talbot invented the calotype paper-negative process, allowing multiple prints.

Roll film and mass photography

George Eastman's Kodak company introduced flexible roll film in 1888 with the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest", bringing photography to amateurs. The 35 mm format, popularised by the Leica cameras of the 1920s, became the standard for photojournalism. Instant cameras from Polaroid (1948) and single-lens reflex designs from Japanese makers such as Nikon and Canon dominated the postwar decades.

Digital era

The first digital camera was built by Kodak engineer Steven Sasson in 1975. Commercial digital cameras appeared in the 1990s, and by the 2000s digital sensors had displaced film for most purposes. Camera phones, beginning around 2000, made photography ubiquitous, and modern smartphones combine multi-lens systems with computational photography.
