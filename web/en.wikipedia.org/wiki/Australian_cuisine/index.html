Australian cuisine

Australian cuisine is the food and culinary practices of Australia and its inhabitants. It has been transformed over the late 20th century by successive waves of immigration, evolving from its British and Irish colonial foundations — meat, pies, and roasts — into one of the world's most multicultural food cultures, increasingly incorporating Indigenous "bush tucker" ingredients.

Indigenous foods

For more than 60,000 years Aboriginal Australians developed a sophisticated food culture based on native plants and animals — kangaroo, emu, witchetty grubs, quandong, wattle seed, bush tomato, and lemon myrtle. Since the 1980s these ingredients have been taken up by chefs and food producers, though questions of benefit-sharing with Indigenous communities remain prominent.

Colonial and traditional dishes

Iconic Australian foods include the meat pie, Vegemite (1922), the pavlova (whose origin is disputed with New Zealand), lamingtons, Anzac biscuits, damper, and the sausage sizzle. Seafood (barramundi, Moreton Bay bugs, Sydney rock oysters) and barbecued meats are staples of outdoor eating; "throwing a shrimp on the barbie" became a global catchphrase through 1980s tourism advertising.

Modern Australian cuisine

Post-war immigration — Italian, Greek, Lebanese, Vietnamese, Thai, Chinese and many others — reshaped Australian eating. "Mod Oz" (modern Australian) fusion cooking emerged in the 1990s, combining European technique with Asian flavours and local produce. Australia is also known for its café culture, credited with popularising the flat white coffee, and for a major wine industry producing Shiraz, Chardonnay and Riesling from regions such as the Barossa Valley, Hunter Valley, and Margaret River.
