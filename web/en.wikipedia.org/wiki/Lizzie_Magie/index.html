Elizabeth J. Magie Phillips (née Magie; 9 May 1866 – 2 March 1948), known as Lizzie Magie, was an American game designer, writer, and Georgist. She invented The Landlord's Game, the precursor to Monopoly, to illustrate the economic theories of Henry George, in particular his ideas about land taxation and the harms of land monopolies.

Lizzie Magie, inventor of The Landlord's Game

Early life

Magie was born in Illinois in 1866. Her father, James Magie, was a newspaper publisher and abolitionist who had travelled with Abraham Lincoln. She worked as a stenographer and typist, wrote stories and poetry, performed on stage, and was an outspoken feminist and Georgist — once staging a mock slave auction of herself to protest women's low wages.

The Landlord's Game

Magie received U.S. Patent 748,626 for her board game on 5 January 1904. In The Landlord's Game, players travelled around a square board buying properties, railroads, and utilities and charging rent. Magie designed two rule sets: a monopolist version, in which one player bankrupts the rest, and an anti-monopolist "Prosperity" version, in which wealth creation is shared — intending to show the moral and practical superiority of Georgist land-value taxation. The game was manufactured commercially in 1906 and spread through Georgist communities, universities, and folk copying.

Relation to Monopoly

Hand-made versions of Magie's game circulated for decades and were adapted in Atlantic City, where the street names of the modern game were applied. Charles Darrow learned a version of the game, patented a variant as Monopoly in 1935, and sold it to Parker Brothers, which bought Magie's patent rights for $500 without royalties. Darrow was promoted as the game's sole inventor for decades; Magie's role was restored to public attention by economist Ralph Anspach during his 1970s Anti-Monopoly litigation, and confirmed by research into the patent record. She died in 1948, largely unrecognised, and is now widely credited as Monopoly's originating inventor.
