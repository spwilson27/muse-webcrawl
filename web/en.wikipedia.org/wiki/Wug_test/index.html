Wug test

Devised byJean Berko Gleason
Year1958
FieldPsycholinguistics; language acquisition
PurposeTesting children's productive knowledge of morphology

The Wug test is a famous experiment in language acquisition devised by psycholinguist Jean Berko (later Jean Berko Gleason) in 1958. It demonstrated that young children have internalized the rules of English morphology — such as how to form plurals and past tenses — rather than merely memorizing word forms.

The experiment

Children were shown simple line drawings of imaginary creatures and objects with nonsense names. In the classic item, the experimenter shows a bird-like creature and says: "This is a wug." A second creature appears: "Now there is another one. There are two of them. There are two ___?" Children who answer "wugs" (pronounced with a /z/ sound) demonstrate productive control of the plural rule, since they cannot have heard the word before. Other items tested past tense (-ed), possessives, comparatives, and agentive suffixes, using nonce words like "rick," "mot," and "bing."

Findings

Preschool and first-grade children performed very well on plurals and progressives but less well on forms requiring more complex phonological conditioning, such as producing the correct allomorph of the past tense for unfamiliar verbs. The study, published as "The Child's Learning of English Morphology" (1958), became a landmark in the study of first-language acquisition and evidence for the rule-governed, creative character of children's language.

Legacy

The wug became an informal mascot of linguistics. The technique of eliciting inflections of nonce words has been replicated across languages and adapted for clinical assessment, and "wug" tests are still used to study morphology, second-language learning, and language disorders. It is unrelated to the "worse is better" design philosophy also found in technical culture.
