A medical examiner is a physician — usually a board-certified forensic pathologist — appointed to investigate deaths that are sudden, violent, suspicious, unattended or otherwise fall under the jurisdiction of a death-investigation system. The medical examiner determines the cause and manner of death, performs autopsies, and certifies death certificates. The term and system are used chiefly in the United States, where jurisdictions are divided between medical examiner systems and the older office of coroner.

Medical examiner vs coroner

The coroner is an ancient English office; in many US counties the coroner is an elected official who may not be a physician and must contract with pathologists to perform autopsies. Medical examiners, by contrast, are appointed and are required to be licensed physicians, typically with subspecialty training in forensic pathology. Massachusetts created the first statewide medical examiner system in 1877, and New York City established its influential Office of Chief Medical Examiner in 1918, replacing the coroner system there. Reform advocates generally favour medical examiner systems for their professional qualifications, while critics note cost and the difficulty of recruiting forensic pathologists to rural areas.

Duties

  • Investigating the circumstances of deaths reported to the office, including scene investigation.
  • Performing autopsies and ordering toxicology and microscopic examinations.
  • Determining the cause of death (the disease or injury) and manner of death (natural, accident, suicide, homicide or undetermined).
  • Identifying unknown deceased persons and notifying next of kin.
  • Testifying in criminal and civil proceedings arising from investigated deaths.

Other countries

In England and Wales, a new statutory role of "medical examiner" was introduced (rolled out to all NHS trusts from 2019 and made statutory in 2024) — but here the medical examiner is a senior doctor who scrutinises the stated causes of non-coronial deaths and supports bereaved families, a function quite different from the American forensic role, which in Britain is performed by Home Office registered forensic pathologists acting for coroners. Similar death-investigation functions are performed elsewhere by forensic institutes attached to police or universities.
