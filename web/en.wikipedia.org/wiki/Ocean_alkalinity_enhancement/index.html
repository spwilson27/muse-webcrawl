Ocean alkalinity enhancement

Adding alkaline minerals to seawater increases its capacity to absorb and store atmospheric CO2 as bicarbonate.

Ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE) is a proposed carbon dioxide removal (CDR) approach that seeks to accelerate the ocean's natural uptake of carbon dioxide by adding alkalinity — typically in the form of finely ground silicate or carbonate minerals, or electrochemically produced bases — to seawater. Increasing alkalinity shifts the marine carbonate equilibrium so that more CO2 is stored as dissolved bicarbonate, and it counteracts ocean acidification.

Mechanism

Surface seawater absorbs CO2, which reacts to form carbonic acid and lowers pH. Raising total alkalinity converts dissolved CO2 and carbonic acid into bicarbonate (HCO3−) and carbonate (CO32−) ions, allowing the ocean to draw down additional atmospheric CO2 to restore equilibrium. The carbon is stored on timescales of tens of thousands of years.

Approaches

Proposed delivery methods include spreading crushed olivine or other silicate rocks on beaches and in coastal waters ("coastal enhanced weathering"), dissolving limestone with CO2-rich flue gas, spreading hydrated lime produced from limestone, and electrochemical processes that split seawater into acidic and basic streams, returning the base to the ocean.

Potential and concerns

Model studies suggest OAE could sequester gigatonnes of CO2 per year if deployed at very large scale, with the co-benefit of locally reducing acidification. Concerns include the energy and emissions cost of mining, grinding, and transporting minerals; trace-metal contamination (e.g., nickel from olivine); effects on marine ecosystems and calcifying organisms; and the difficulty of monitoring, reporting, and verifying net carbon removal. As of the mid-2020s the technique remains at the research and small field-trial stage, governed in part by the London Protocol's rules on marine geoengineering.
