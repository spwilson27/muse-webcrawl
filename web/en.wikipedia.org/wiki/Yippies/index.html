Youth International Party

Founded: 31 December 1967
Key figures: Abbie Hoffman, Jerry Rubin, Paul Krassner
Ideology: Counterculture, anti-war, libertarian socialism
Succeeded by: Grassroots Party (and informal Yippie networks)

The Youth International Party, whose adherents were known as Yippies, was an American youth-oriented countercultural revolutionary organisation formed in 1967. Founded by Abbie Hoffman, Jerry Rubin, Paul Krassner and others, the Yippies blended theatrical street protest with anarchist and anti-capitalist politics, using absurdity and media spectacle to attack the Vietnam War and the American establishment.

Founding and tactics

The party was announced on New Year's Eve 1967, partly as a deliberate media invention designed to attract the counterculture to protests planned for the 1968 Democratic National Convention in Chicago. Yippie actions included throwing dollar bills onto the floor of the New York Stock Exchange in 1967, causing traders to scramble for the money, and nominating a pig, Pigasus, for president in 1968. Their motto-laden manifestos treated revolution as performance: "We are a people who make up a nation of theatre."

Chicago 1968 and the trial

At the Chicago convention, clashes between demonstrators and police — later officially characterised as a "police riot" — led to the conspiracy trial of the Chicago Eight (later Seven), among them Hoffman and Rubin. The trial became a cause célèbre, with the defendants' courtroom antics, including appearing in judicial robes, turning the proceedings into a national spectacle about free speech and state repression.

Later years

The Yippies remained active through the 1970s, campaigning for marijuana legalisation (including smoke-ins and the "Yippie vs. marijuana laws" campaigns) and against the Rockefeller drug laws. A splinter formed the Grassroots Party in Minnesota. Yippie institutions included New York's Yippie Museum and Cafe at 9 Bleecker Street, and veteran members continued to organise events into the 21st century, though the movement never became a conventional electoral party.
