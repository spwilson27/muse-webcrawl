Path analysis (statistics)

In statistics, path analysis is a form of multiple regression analysis used to evaluate causal models by examining the relationships between a dependent variable and two or more independent variables. The method treats a hypothesised network of causal influences among observed variables as a system of simultaneous regression equations, allowing a researcher to decompose correlations into direct effects, indirect (mediated) effects, and spurious components.

History

Path analysis was invented by the geneticist Sewall Wright in a series of papers beginning in 1918–1921 (notably his 1921 paper "Correlation and Causation"), originally to disentangle the relative contributions of heredity and environment to traits such as the coat patterns of guinea pigs. The method was later taken up by sociologists such as Otis Dudley Duncan in the 1960s, became central to status-attainment research, and was generalised into modern structural equation modelling (SEM), in which path analysis is the special case using only observed variables (no latent factors).

Path models and diagrams

Conventions
Single-headed arrowHypothesised direct causal effect
Double-headed arrowCorrelation without specified direction
Path coefficientStandardised regression weight on an arrow

A path model is drawn as a path diagram: variables appear in boxes (observed variables in rectangles), arrows encode assumed causal direction, and each arrow carries a path coefficient. Using Wright's tracing rules, the correlation between any two variables can be decomposed by summing, over all legitimate compound paths connecting them, the products of their coefficients. Models may be recursive (effects flow one way) or non-recursive (with feedback loops or reciprocal effects). A model is tested by comparing the correlations it implies with those observed in the data; models with as many parameters as observed relationships are "just-identified" and fit perfectly by construction.

Assumptions and limitations

Path analysis assumes linearity, correct causal ordering, no omitted common causes of connected variables, and reliable measurement. It cannot by itself prove causation: a well-fitting model is consistent with the assumed structure, but alternative equivalent models may fit the same data. Within structural equation modelling software, path models are estimated alongside measurement models, with fit assessed by indices such as chi-square tests, RMSEA, and CFI.
