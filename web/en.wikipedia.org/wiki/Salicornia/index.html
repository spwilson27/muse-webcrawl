Salicornia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[hero image: Salicornia]
Glasswort in a coastal marsh

Salicornia is a genus of succulent, halophytic (salt-tolerant) flowering plants in the family Amaranthaceae that grow in salt marshes and on mudflats and beaches in temperate and subtropical regions worldwide. Common names include glasswort, pickleweed, samphire, and sea asparagus. The plants have leafless, jointed, fleshy green stems that turn red in autumn, and tiny inconspicuous flowers.

Description and taxonomy

Species of Salicornia are annual herbs, typically 10–60 cm tall, with opposite branches and scale-like leaves fused into the stem, giving them an appearance likened to miniature cacti or strings of beads. The genus is taxonomically difficult because the reduced morphology offers few distinguishing characters; depending on the authority, 25–60 species are recognised, including S. europaea in Europe and S. virginica and S. bigelovii in the Americas.

Uses

Historically, the ash of glasswort ("barilla") was a source of soda used in glass and soap making — hence the name. Today the tender tips are eaten as a delicacy, raw, pickled, or blanched, with a crisp texture and salty taste. Salicornia bigelovii has attracted research as a seawater-irrigated crop for biofuel and animal fodder in arid coastal deserts: its seeds are rich in oil, and pilot projects have tested Salicornia-based jet fuel. Salicornia also stabilises salt-marsh sediments and provides habitat for birds and invertebrates.
