Mimbres culture
Region: Southwestern New Mexico, US
Period: c. 200–1450 CE
Branch of: Mogollon culture
Noted for: Black-on-white pottery

The Mimbres culture (or Mimbres branch) was a part of the prehistoric Mogollon culture of the American Southwest, centred on the Mimbres Valley of southwestern New Mexico. It flourished especially between about 1000 and 1130 CE, the "Classic Mimbres" period, and is celebrated for its distinctive black-on-white painted pottery.

Pottery

Mimbres black-on-white bowls are among the most famous ceramics of the ancient Southwest. Painted in iron-based black pigment on a white slip, they depict geometric designs as well as striking figurative scenes — animals, humans, mythic beings and everyday activities. Many bowls were ritually "killed" (punched through the base) and placed over the faces of the dead in burials beneath house floors. The imagery provides rare insight into the cosmology and daily life of a prehistoric farming people.

Settlements

Early Mimbres people lived in pit-houses; by the Classic period they built large adobe pueblos of hundreds of rooms around plazas, farming maize, beans and squash with irrigation along the Mimbres River and its tributaries. Major sites include the Swarts Ruin and Mattocks Ruin. After about 1130 the Classic tradition ended amid drought and reorganisation; later Mimbres-region pueblos continued in changed forms until the 1400s.
