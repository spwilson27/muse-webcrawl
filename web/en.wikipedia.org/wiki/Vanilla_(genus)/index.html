Vanilla is a genus of flowering plants in the orchid family, Orchidaceae, comprising about 110 species of climbing vines distributed throughout the tropics of the Americas, Africa and Asia. It is one of the few orchid genera of major economic importance: the cured seed pods of Vanilla planifolia and a few relatives yield the spice and flavouring vanilla, second in value among spices only to saffron.

Description

Vanilla species are terrestrial or hemiepiphytic vines that climb by aerial roots, sometimes reaching 30 metres or more. Stems are fleshy and green; leaves are usually thick, leathery and alternate, though in some species reduced to scales. The large, fragrant, pale greenish-yellow flowers open for a single day and, in their native range, are pollinated by specific bees (historically attributed to stingless Melipona bees). Outside Mexico the flowers must be pollinated by hand for fruit to set — a labour-intensive technique first devised in 1841 by Edmond Albius, an enslaved boy on Réunion.

Cultivation and curing

The immature green pods are harvested and subjected to a curing process of killing, sweating, slow drying and conditioning over several months, during which the flavour compound vanillin develops and the pods turn dark brown and supple. Madagascar, Indonesia, Mexico, Tahiti and Uganda are leading producers. Natural vanilla is costly because of the hand pollination and long curing; most vanilla flavour in commerce is synthetic vanillin derived from lignin, guaiacol or other feedstocks.

History

The Totonac people of Mexico's Gulf coast first cultivated vanilla; the Aztecs adopted it as a flavouring for chocolate drinks. Hernán Cortés's expedition carried it to Europe in the 1520s, but production remained a Mexican monopoly until the pollination breakthrough allowed cultivation in the Indian Ocean colonies.
