Psychological warfare (PSYWAR), in United States usage psychological operations (PSYOP)—renamed military information support operations (MISO) in 2010—is the planned use of communications and other means to influence the perceptions, attitudes and behaviour of foreign target audiences in support of military and national objectives. Its classic instruments are leaflets, loudspeaker broadcasts, radio and television programming, newspapers, posters and, in the internet era, websites and social media. Practitioners distinguish white propaganda (openly attributed to its true source), grey (source unacknowledged) and black (falsely attributed, often coupled with deception), and levels from strategic campaigns aimed at whole populations down to tactical loudspeaker teams urging encircled soldiers to surrender. The aim is classically summarised as winning "hearts and minds" and, in the maxim attributed to practitioners, to fight "the war for the mind" without firing a shot where possible.

History

Psychological warfare is ancient: Sun Tzu taught that "all warfare is based on deception" and that subduing the enemy without fighting is the acme of skill; Genghis Khan's armies spread terror by design and used agents to magnify reports of Mongol numbers and ferocity; commanders from Cyrus to Alexander cultivated reputations for clemency toward those who submitted. The modern discipline dates from the First World War, when both sides dropped millions of leaflets over enemy trenches and Britain's Crewe House organisation (under Lord Northcliffe) systematised propaganda against Germany. In the Second World War the belligerents built large bureaucracies—the U.S. Office of War Information and OSS Morale Operations branch, Britain's Political Warfare Executive, German propaganda under Goebbels, Japanese efforts such as the "Tokyo Rose" broadcasts—and developed signature tactics: safe-conduct surrender passes dropped by the billion; radio personalities addressing enemy troops by name of unit; and tactical deception, including the U.S. 23rd Headquarters Special Troops (the "Ghost Army") whose inflatable tanks and sound effects impersonated real formations. The Korean War added mass leaflet campaigns over a literate conscript army, and Communist "brainwashing" fears in turn shaped Western doctrine.

During the Cold War, psychological warfare merged with political warfare and public diplomacy: Radio Free Europe and Radio Liberty, the Voice of America, covertly funded cultural fronts, and "active measures" by the KGB. The Vietnam War's "Chieu Hoi" (Open Arms) programme induced more than 100,000 defections with amnesty leaflets and loudspeaker appeals, yet the broader hearts-and-minds campaign failed to legitimise the Saigon government—an enduring lesson that propaganda cannot substitute for political reality. The 1991 Gulf War saw one of the most effective tactical campaigns on record: coalition leaflets accurately predicting bombing raids, followed by the raids themselves, contributed to mass Iraqi surrenders, including soldiers surrendering to a remotely piloted vehicle. In Afghanistan and Iraq after 2001, PSYOP forces—centred on the U.S. Army's groups at Fort Bragg (now Fort Liberty)—produced radio stations, newspapers and text-message campaigns, while insurgent and later ISIS media operations demonstrated that non-state actors could match state capabilities online.

Methods and doctrine

Professional PSYOP follows a cycle: target-audience analysis (beliefs, vulnerabilities, symbols, media habits), product development and pretesting, approval through command channels, dissemination, and measurement of effect (defection rates, attitude surveys, observed behaviour). Themes are usually built on truth—practitioners argue that demonstrably false white propaganda destroys credibility—but black operations deliberately deceive, and military deception (MILDEC) is a separate, coordinated discipline. In U.S. doctrine, PSYOP forces may not target American audiences; the Smith–Mundt Act of 1948 long restricted domestic dissemination of government information materials aimed abroad, a firewall eroded by global media and partly amended in 2012. Allies have analogous structures: NATO doctrine speaks of PsyOps as a supporting capability within "information operations" and "strategic communications".

The internet era

Social media collapsed the cost and attribution barriers of psychological warfare. Russia's Internet Research Agency conducted large-scale influence operations around the 2016 U.S. election; states field "troll farms", bot networks and AI-generated personas; deepfakes threaten fabricated orders and surrender appeals—Ukraine's 2022 war effort, conversely, used rapid, open social-media communication (including President Zelenskyy's selfie videos) as strategic psychological defence, while Russian and Ukrainian units exchanged text messages and TikTok content aimed at enemy morale. Analysts now treat psychological warfare as one strand of broader "cognitive warfare", and militaries invest in counter-disinformation, media literacy and rapid rebuttal as defensive counterparts.

Criticism and law

Psychological warfare directed at enemy combatants and occupied populations is broadly lawful under the laws of armed conflict—ruses of war are expressly permitted, though perfidy (feigning protected status to kill) is not, and broadcasts inciting genocide have been prosecuted as crimes (the RTLM radio case at the Rwanda tribunal). Ethical criticism focuses on manipulation of civilian populations, the blurring of lines between foreign influence and domestic politics, and the use of psychological techniques against one's own citizens in counterinsurgency, as in controversies over British information operations in Northern Ireland and U.S. domestic blowback from foreign-targeted campaigns.
