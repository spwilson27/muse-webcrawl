Valuation (finance)

In finance, valuation is the process of determining the present value of an asset or a company. Valuation can be done on assets (for example, investments in marketable securities such as stocks, options, business enterprises, or intangible assets such as patents and trademarks) or on liabilities (e.g., bonds issued by a company). Valuations are needed for many reasons, including investment analysis, capital budgeting, merger and acquisition transactions, financial reporting, and taxation.

Overview

Common terms for the value of an asset or liability are market value, fair value, and intrinsic value. The meanings of these terms differ: intrinsic value is an analyst's estimate of what an asset is worth based on its fundamentals, whereas market value is what buyers and sellers currently transact at. Valuation is often uncertain and model-dependent, so results are commonly expressed as a range.

Approaches

Discounted cash flow

The income approach, most commonly discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis, estimates value as the sum of expected future free cash flows discounted at a rate reflecting their risk, often the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). A terminal value captures cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period, frequently via the Gordon growth model.

Relative valuation

The market approach values an asset by comparison with similar assets, using multiples such as price-to-earnings (P/E), EV/EBITDA, or price-to-sales drawn from comparable publicly traded companies or precedent transactions.

Asset-based valuation

The asset approach totals the fair values of a company's assets less its liabilities. It is most applicable to holding companies, real estate, and liquidation scenarios.

Option-based methods

Contingent-claims techniques apply real options reasoning to assets whose payoffs depend on uncertain future events, such as undeveloped natural resources or early-stage technology.

Valuation of specific instruments

Bonds are valued by discounting coupons and principal at the market yield for comparable credit risk. Equities may be valued with dividend discount models. Private companies pose additional difficulties because of illiquidity and limited disclosure, often leading to discounts for lack of marketability.
