Megadiverse countries

The megadiverse countries are a group of countries that harbour the majority of Earth's species and high numbers of endemic species. Conservation International identified 17 megadiverse countries in work beginning with Russell Mittermeier's 1988 analysis of global biodiversity priorities. Together, these countries are estimated to hold more than 70% of the world's biological diversity while occupying a minority of its land surface.

The 17 countries

  • Australia
  • Brazil
  • China
  • Colombia
  • Democratic Republic of the Congo
  • Ecuador
  • India
  • Indonesia
  • Madagascar
  • Malaysia
  • Mexico
  • Papua New Guinea
  • Peru
  • Philippines
  • South Africa
  • United States
  • Venezuela

Criteria

To qualify, a country must have at least 5,000 endemic plants and possess a marine ecosystem within or bordering its territory. Most megadiverse countries are tropical or subtropical, reflecting the latitudinal diversity gradient.

Like-Minded Megadiverse Countries

In 2002 many of these states formed the group of Like-Minded Megadiverse Countries (LMMC), formalised in the Cancún Declaration, to cooperate in negotiations under the Convention on Biological Diversity, particularly on access to genetic resources and benefit-sharing, later embodied in the Nagoya Protocol (2010).
