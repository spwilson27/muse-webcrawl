Bushveld Igneous Complex

The Bushveld Igneous Complex (BIC) is the largest layered igneous intrusion in the Earth's crust, located in the northern part of South Africa in the provinces of Limpopo, Mpumalanga, North West, and Gauteng. It was tilted and eroded to form the outcrops around the edges of a great geological basin, and it has been intensively mined for its enormous reserves of platinum-group metals, chromium, vanadium, iron, tin, and other minerals.

Geology

The complex formed about 2.06 billion years ago (Paleoproterozoic) when vast volumes of magma intruded into the rocks of the Kaapvaal Craton beneath the Transvaal Basin. It covers roughly 66,000 km². Its layered sequence, the Rustenburg Layered Suite, is divided into the Lower, Critical, Main, and Upper Zones, overlain and flanked by granites of the Lebowa Granite Suite and the Rooiberg Group volcanic rocks.

Ore deposits

The Critical Zone contains the world's largest deposits of platinum-group elements in two thin, extraordinarily continuous chromitite-bearing layers: the Merensky Reef and the UG2 chromitite reef. The complex supplies the majority of the world's platinum and a large share of its palladium, rhodium, chromium, and vanadium. The Upper Zone's magnetite layers are mined for vanadium and iron, and deposits of tin, fluorite, and dimension stone also occur.

Mining

Major mining centres include Rustenburg, Brits, Thabazimbi, and Steelpoort, with operations run by companies such as Anglo American Platinum, Impala Platinum, and Sibanye Stillwater. The complex was first recognized as a major platinum province after Hans Merensky's discoveries of 1924.
