Buffer solution

Titration curve of a buffer solution: pH changes only slowly near the pKa

A buffer solution is an aqueous solution consisting of a mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or vice versa. Its pH changes very little when a small amount of strong acid or base is added to it. Buffer solutions are used as a means of keeping pH at a nearly constant value in a wide variety of chemical applications. In nature, there are many living systems that use buffering for pH regulation; for example, the bicarbonate buffering system is used to regulate the pH of blood.

Principles of buffering

When a strong acid is added to an acidic buffer, the added hydrogen ions are consumed by the conjugate base to form the weak acid, so free H+ concentration barely rises. When a strong base is added, hydroxide ions react with the weak acid to form water and the conjugate base. The pH of a buffer can be estimated with the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]). A buffer is most effective when the pH is close to the pKa of the weak acid and when the concentrations of acid and conjugate base are both large; this capacity is quantified as buffer capacity.

Common buffer systems

Common laboratory buffers include acetic acid/sodium acetate, phosphate buffers, Tris, and HEPES. In biochemistry, buffers maintain the pH conditions required for enzyme activity, and in medicine, blood plasma is buffered principally by the carbonic acid–bicarbonate system, assisted by proteins and phosphate.

Applications

Buffers are used in fermentation, in calibrating pH meters, in pharmaceutical formulations, in shampoos and other personal-care products, and in industrial processes where pH control is essential.
