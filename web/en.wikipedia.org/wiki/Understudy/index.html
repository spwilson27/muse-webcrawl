Understudy

An understudy rehearses so as to be ready to perform at a moment's notice.

In theatre, an understudy is a performer who learns the lines and blocking of a leading role in a play, musical, or opera and is prepared to perform that role if the regular actor is unable to appear — because of illness, injury, or other absence. Understudies are usually members of the production's ensemble or hold smaller roles of their own, and step up ("go on") when required, sometimes with only a few hours' notice.

Role and practice

Understudying is a standard feature of professional theatre, particularly in long-running productions on Broadway and in London's West End, where a show may run eight performances a week for years. Producers are generally expected to announce when an understudy performs a principal role, often through an insert in the programme and a notice in the lobby; audiences are typically entitled to a refund if a star performer is replaced. Major roles may have more than one understudy, sometimes ordered as first and second cover.

Swings and standbys

Related positions include the swing, a performer who learns multiple ensemble tracks and fills in for any of them, and the standby, who covers a principal role but does not otherwise perform in the show, waiting backstage (or on call) in case they are needed. Unlike an understudy, a standby has no regular role in the production.

Famous understudy performances

Several celebrated careers began when an understudy went on for an absent star. Shirley MacLaine was "discovered" when she replaced an injured Carol Haney in The Pajama Game (1954) and was spotted by a Hollywood producer. Sutton Foster was an understudy in Thoroughly Modern Millie during its out-of-town tryouts and took over the title role before the Broadway opening, winning a Tony Award. In 1980, a young understudy's readiness in the London production of Evita-era musicals became part of theatrical lore, and Anthony Warlow, Jonathan Groff and others have spoken of understudy work as essential training. The drama of the understudy's sudden opportunity is itself a theatrical trope, dramatised in the film All About Eve and the musical 42nd Street, whose plot turns on a chorus girl replacing the injured star.
