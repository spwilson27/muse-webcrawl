Time travel

Time travel is the concept of moving between different points in time in a manner analogous to moving between different points in space, by an object or a person, typically with the use of a hypothetical device known as a time machine. Time travel is a widely recognized concept in philosophy and fiction, particularly science fiction.

History of the concept

The idea of traveling in time has roots in ancient myths and folklore, but it gained its modern form in the 19th century. Mark Twain's A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court (1889) sent its protagonist back to the age of Camelot, and H. G. Wells's The Time Machine (1895) popularized the notion of a vehicle that allows an operator to travel purposely through time.

Time travel in physics

Some interpretations of special relativity suggest that travel into the past might be possible through closed timelike curves, solutions of Einstein's field equations first described by Kurt Gödel in 1949. Stephen Hawking proposed the chronology protection conjecture, suggesting that the laws of physics prevent time travel on all but microscopic scales.

Philosophy and paradoxes

The possibility of time travel raises paradoxes, most famously the grandfather paradox, in which a traveler kills their own grandfather before their parent's conception, creating a logical inconsistency. Philosophers have proposed resolutions including the Novikov self-consistency principle and branching many-worlds models.
