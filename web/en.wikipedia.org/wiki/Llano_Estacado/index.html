The Llano Estacado (Spanish for "Staked Plain") is a vast mesa and high plains region in eastern New Mexico and northwestern Texas, forming the southern portion of the Great Plains. It is one of the largest mesas in North America, a remarkably flat tableland bounded by escarpments: the Caprock Escarpment to the east, the Mescalero Ridge to the west, and the Pecos River valley beyond.

Name and history

The Spanish explorer Francisco Vázquez de Coronado crossed the region in 1541 and described a plain so featureless that his men used piles of bones and dung to mark their path. The name is often said to refer to stakes used as trail markers, though it may also derive from the stockade-like appearance of the escarpment or from yucca stalks ("estacas"). For centuries the Llano was the domain of nomadic Plains peoples, including the Comanche (Comancheria), and was crossed by cattle trails in the 19th century.

Geography and water

The surface is underlain by a caprock caliche layer over the Ogallala Formation. There are almost no permanent streams; water collects in thousands of shallow playa lakes after rain. Agriculture depends heavily on the Ogallala Aquifer, whose decline is a major regional concern. Principal cities include Lubbock and Amarillo (on its northern edge), Midland, and Odessa in Texas, and Clovis and Hobbs in New Mexico.

Economy

The region is a major producer of cotton, sorghum, cattle, and petroleum, and increasingly of wind energy. Its flat, windy terrain makes it one of the leading wind-power regions in the United States.
