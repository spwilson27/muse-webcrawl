Methodological individualism

In the philosophy of the social sciences, methodological individualism is the principle that social phenomena must be explained by showing how they result from individual actions, which in turn must be explained through reference to the intentional states — beliefs, desires, and opportunities — that motivate individual actors. In other words, it denies that a social scientist can make genuine explanatory progress by appealing to higher levels of social structure without specifying the individuals involved.

Origins

The term was introduced by Joseph Schumpeter in 1908, and the doctrine was developed by Max Weber, who held that social collectivities (states, nations, corporations) should be treated as resultants of the particular acts of individual persons. Karl Popper defended it in The Poverty of Historicism and The Open Society and Its Enemies, arguing against the "holistic" explanation of history by collective forces.

In economics

Methodological individualism is foundational to mainstream economics: market outcomes are explained through the choices of individual consumers and firms. Friedrich Hayek extended the idea to argue that social order is a spontaneous result of dispersed individual action rather than design. In sociology, Jon Elster championed "mechanism-based" explanation consistent with the doctrine, in opposition to functionalism.

Criticism

Critics in the Durkheimian tradition hold that social facts are external to and constraining on individuals, and cannot be reduced to individual psychology. Others object that institutions, socialisation and material structures shape the very beliefs and desires the individualist takes as starting points, so explanations must move in both directions.
