Mutual knowledge

Mutual knowledge in game theory occurs when each player or agent in a game is aware of an event, and every other player is also aware of that event. However, mutual knowledge by itself does not imply that it is known that the knowledge is mutual. If that further condition holds — that is, if everyone knows that everyone knows the event, everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on ad infinitum — then the event is said to be common knowledge.

Levels of knowledge: mutual knowledge requires only that all parties know the fact; common knowledge requires infinitely nested shared awareness.

Definition

Formally, let an event E be given. E is mutual knowledge among a group of agents if every agent knows E. E is common knowledge if every agent knows E, every agent knows that every agent knows E, and so forth for every finite level of iteration. Mutual knowledge is thus the first level of this hierarchy.

Importance

The distinction matters because many results in game theory — such as coordination outcomes and the unraveling of cooperation in finitely repeated games by backward induction — depend on common knowledge assumptions, in particular the common knowledge of rationality. Behaviour can change dramatically when an event is merely mutual knowledge rather than common knowledge.

The coordinated attack problem

A classic illustration is the coordinated attack problem (also known as the two generals' problem): two generals must attack simultaneously to succeed, but they can communicate only by messengers who may be captured. No finite sequence of messages can ever establish common knowledge of the attack plan, so perfectly guaranteed coordination is impossible — even though many rounds of acknowledgment create very deep levels of mutual knowledge.
