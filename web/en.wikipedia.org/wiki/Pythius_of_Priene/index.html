Pythius of Priene (Greek: Πύθιος), also rendered Pytheos, was a Greek architect of the 4th century BC from the Ionian city of Priene. He was, with Satyros of Paros, the architect of the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, and he designed the celebrated Temple of Athena Polias at Priene — regarded as the canonical example of the Classical Ionic temple.

Mausoleum at Halicarnassus

Vitruvius and Pliny name Pythius alongside Satyros as designer of the tomb of Mausolus at Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum), completed under Artemisia II about 350 BC and counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. The architects also published a written account of the building, following the Greek custom of architects issuing treatises on their masterworks; the work does not survive.

Temple of Athena at Priene

Pythius designed the Temple of Athena Polias at his home city of Priene, dedicated in part by Alexander the Great in 334 BC, whose dedicatory inscription survives in the British Museum. The temple's refined proportions — based on a modular grid in which the column's lower diameter governed the whole design — became a model of the Ionic order. Unusually, Pythius wrote about his methods: Vitruvius reports that Pythius disapproved of the Doric order for temples because its triglyph arrangement created awkward corner problems, and his preference for Ionic helped define late Classical taste. The temple's remains at Priene, excavated by German archaeologists, show his plan of a peripteral hexastyle temple on a stepped platform.

Influence

Through his buildings and his lost theoretical writings, Pythius transmitted a system of proportional design — symmetria — that Vitruvius drew upon three centuries later. The Priene temple's grid plan and the Mausoleum's influential tomb form both left long legacies: Priene became a textbook of Ionic architecture, and "mausoleum" entered every language as the word for a monumental tomb.
