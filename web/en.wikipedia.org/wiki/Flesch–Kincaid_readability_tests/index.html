Flesch–Kincaid readability tests


Readability scored from sentence and word length

The Flesch–Kincaid readability tests are two related measures of how easy a passage of English text is to read. The Flesch Reading Ease score rates text on a 100-point scale: the higher the score, the easier the text is to read. The Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level instead expresses readability as a United States school grade level. Both are computed from two variables only — the average sentence length and the average number of syllables per word.

History and formulas

Rudolf Flesch published the Reading Ease formula in 1948 in "A new readability yardstick". In the 1970s, J. Peter Kincaid and colleagues, under contract to the United States Navy, recalibrated the formula against the comprehension of Navy personnel, producing the grade-level version for evaluating technical manuals. Reading Ease equals 206.835 minus 1.015 times the average sentence length (in words) and minus 84.6 times the average syllables per word. The Grade Level formula uses weights of 0.39 for sentence length and 11.8 for syllables per word, minus 15.59.

Use and criticism

The tests are built into word processors, readability software and some government plain-language programmes; several U.S. states require insurance policies to meet a minimum Reading Ease score. A score of 60–70 is considered plain English suitable for most adults, while scores below 30 characterize dense academic or legal prose. Critics note that syllable counts and sentence length are crude proxies: the tests ignore word familiarity, coherence and layout, and writers can game them by chopping sentences mechanically.
