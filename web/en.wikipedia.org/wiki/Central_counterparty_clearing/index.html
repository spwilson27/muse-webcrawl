Central counterparty clearing

Central counterparty clearing (CCC) or central clearing is the process by which financial transactions in securities, derivatives and other instruments are cleared through a central counterparty (CCP) — an entity that interposes itself between counterparties to a trade, becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. This process, called novation, replaces a web of bilateral exposures with a single exposure to the CCP.

How it works

When a trade is registered with a clearing house, the original contract between the two traders is replaced by two contracts with the CCP. The CCP then manages risk by netting positions, collecting margin (initial margin as a buffer and variation margin reflecting daily price changes), maintaining a default fund contributed by clearing members, and operating a default waterfall that specifies the order in which resources absorb losses if a member fails. Major CCPs include LCH (London), CME Clearing, Eurex Clearing, ICE Clear and DTCC subsidiaries.

History and regulation

Central clearing grew out of 19th-century futures exchanges in Chicago and Liverpool. After the 2008 financial crisis, the G20 Pittsburgh Summit (2009) mandated that standardised over-the-counter derivatives be centrally cleared, a policy implemented through the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and the European Market Infrastructure Regulation (EMIR) in the EU. This greatly expanded CCPs' systemic importance.

Benefits and risks

Clearing reduces bilateral counterparty credit risk, enables multilateral netting, and increases transparency to regulators. Critics note that it concentrates risk in a few systemically critical institutions, can be procyclical through margin calls in stressed markets, and creates moral hazard if CCPs are expected to be rescued. CCP resolution regimes and "skin in the game" requirements for operators are responses to these concerns.
