Battle of the Somme

The Battle of the Somme, also known as the Somme offensive, was fought from 1 July to 18 November 1916 on both sides of the upper reaches of the river Somme in France. One of the largest battles of the First World War, it pitted the British Empire and French forces against the German Empire. More than three million men fought in the battle, of whom more than one million were killed or wounded, making it one of the deadliest in human history. The first day, 1 July 1916, remains the bloodiest day in the history of the British Army, with over 57,000 casualties, including 19,240 dead.

Background

The offensive was planned at the Chantilly Conference of December 1915 as part of coordinated Allied attacks. Its purpose shifted after the German assault on Verdun began in February 1916: the Somme became primarily a British effort to relieve pressure on the French. General Haig's Fourth Army under Rawlinson, built largely from Kitchener's volunteer "Pals battalions", would attack after a week-long artillery bombardment.

Course of the battle

The preliminary bombardment failed to destroy German deep dugouts and wire. On 1 July, infantry advancing in waves were cut down by machine guns; only in the south, near Montauban, were objectives taken. Fighting continued through the summer — Bazentin Ridge, Pozières, Delville Wood — with the first use of tanks on 15 September at Flers–Courcelette. By November, mud and exhaustion ended the offensive after an advance of roughly 10 km (6 miles).

Legacy

The Somme became a byword for futile slaughter in British memory, though historians debate its strategic effects: it relieved Verdun, wore down the German army, and forced tactical learning that shaped the victories of 1918. The Thiepval Memorial commemorates over 72,000 missing British and South African soldiers of the Somme.
