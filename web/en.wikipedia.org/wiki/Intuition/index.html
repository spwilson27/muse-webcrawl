Intuition

Intuition is the ability to acquire knowledge or understanding without conscious reasoning or immediate sensory evidence. It is often described as a "gut feeling" — a judgment or insight that arrives suddenly and without deliberate analysis. Intuitions may arise from unconscious pattern recognition, prior experience, or implicit learning, and they play a role in decision-making, creative work, and philosophical inquiry.

Philosophy

Philosophers have long debated the evidential status of intuitions. René Descartes and other rationalists appealed to intuition as a source of self-evident truths, while empiricists were more sceptical. Kant distinguished sensible intuition — the immediate presentation of objects in space and time — from intellectual intuition. In contemporary philosophy, appeals to intuition are central to thought experiments, though experimental philosophy has questioned how universal and reliable such judgments are.

Psychology

In psychology, intuition is associated with fast, automatic processing (System 1 in Daniel Kahneman's dual-process theory), in contrast to slow, deliberate reasoning (System 2). Research by Gary Klein on naturalistic decision-making found that experienced professionals, such as firefighters, often make effective decisions by recognising patterns rather than comparing options analytically. Intuition can, however, be distorted by cognitive biases.
