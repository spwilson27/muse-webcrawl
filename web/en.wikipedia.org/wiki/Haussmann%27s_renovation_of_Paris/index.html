Haussmann's renovation of Paris

Haussmann's renovation of Paris was a vast public works programme commissioned by Emperor Napoleon III and directed by Georges-Eugène Haussmann, prefect of the Seine, between 1853 and 1870. It transformed Paris from a dense medieval city into a modern capital of wide boulevards, parks, sewers, and uniform stone buildings.

Works

Haussmann cut broad, straight avenues through the old quarters, including the Boulevard Saint-Michel, Boulevard de Sébastopol, and the completed Rue de Rivoli, and created grand squares such as the Place de l'Étoile with twelve radiating avenues. The programme built hundreds of kilometres of sewers and water mains, new markets (Les Halles), railway stations, the Palais Garnier opera house, and great parks — the Bois de Boulogne, Bois de Vincennes, Parc des Buttes Chaumont, and Parc Montsouris — while annexing surrounding communes in 1860 to create the modern 20 arrondissements.

Motives and criticism

The renovation aimed at public health (fighting cholera and overcrowding), circulation of traffic and commerce, beautification, and, some historians argue, military control of insurrection-prone streets. It displaced hundreds of thousands of poorer residents, ran up enormous debts, and contributed to Haussmann's dismissal in 1870; much work continued under the Third Republic. The resulting urban form — cream stone facades, mansard roofs, tree-lined boulevards — still defines the image of Paris.
