Racial segregation in the United States is the practice of restricting people to certain residential areas and to separate institutions — schools, parks, restaurants, transportation, churches, cemeteries, and other facilities — on the basis of race or alleged race. Historically, the most legally codified form was the system of Jim Crow laws in the Southern states, which enforced racial separation in public life from the late 19th century until the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s.

History

Segregation in some form predates the United States, but it took on its most systematic legal form after Reconstruction. Following the Compromise of 1877 and the withdrawal of federal troops from the South, Southern states adopted statutes requiring separate facilities for Black and white residents. In Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), the Supreme Court upheld state segregation laws under the doctrine of "separate but equal," which remained federal constitutional law for nearly six decades.

In practice, facilities for Black Americans were chronically underfunded and inferior. Segregation was maintained not only by law but by extra-legal violence, including lynching, and by private practices such as restrictive covenants and redlining by federal housing agencies, which denied mortgages in Black neighborhoods and deepened residential segregation nationwide — including in Northern cities that had no de jure segregation statutes.

Desegregation

The modern civil rights movement attacked segregation on multiple fronts: litigation by the NAACP, mass protests such as the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56), sit-ins, and the Freedom Rides. In Brown v. Board of Education (1954), the Supreme Court unanimously overturned Plessy in public education, declaring state-sponsored school segregation unconstitutional. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed segregation in public accommodations and employment discrimination; the Voting Rights Act of 1965 dismantled barriers to Black voting; and the Fair Housing Act of 1968 prohibited housing discrimination. Loving v. Virginia (1967) struck down state bans on interracial marriage.

Legacy

Although de jure segregation was abolished, de facto segregation persists in housing patterns and school enrollments in many metropolitan areas, a legacy of redlining, white flight, and zoning. Scholars continue to study its effects on wealth gaps, health outcomes, and educational inequality.
