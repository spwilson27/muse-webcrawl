Constitution of the Netherlands

The Constitution of the Netherlands (Grondwet voor het Koninkrijk der Nederlanden) is the fundamental law of the European territory of the Kingdom of the Netherlands. It provides the framework for the organisation of the Dutch state and for its relationship to the individual. Of all constitutions in the world, it is the second oldest still in effect; the original constitution was enacted in 1814 and drew strongly on the influence of the Batavian Republic's "Staatsregeling" of 1798.

History

The first constitution of the Netherlands as a whole was the Staatsregeling voor het Bataafsche Volk of 1798. The present constitution dates from 1814, when the Netherlands was established as a sovereign principality after French rule, and a committee chaired by Gijsbert Karel van Hogendorp drafted it. In 1815 it was replaced by a new constitution for the United Kingdom of the Netherlands, which united the Netherlands with Belgium.

The most important revision took place in 1848, when Johan Rudolph Thorbecke rewrote the constitution, transforming the Netherlands into a parliamentary democracy with ministerial responsibility and greatly limiting the power of the king. The 1848 revision is often considered the true birth of the modern Dutch constitutional order.

Structure and contents

The constitution consists of eight chapters and additional articles. Chapter 1 deals with fundamental rights, including equality before the law, freedom of religion, freedom of expression and the right to privacy. Chapter 2 concerns the government, comprising the King and the ministers, who together form the government; the Council of State is also covered. Chapter 3 describes the States General (parliament), consisting of the Senate (Eerste Kamer) and the House of Representatives (Tweede Kamer). Chapter 4 covers the Council of State, the Court of Audit and the National Ombudsman. Chapter 5 deals with legislation and administration, Chapter 6 with the administration of justice, Chapter 7 with provinces, municipalities and water boards, and Chapter 8 with the procedure for constitutional revision.

Amendment

Amending the constitution is a two-stage process. A proposal must first be adopted by both houses of the States General by simple majority; parliament is then dissolved and elections held, after which the new parliament must adopt the proposal by a two-thirds majority. The last comprehensive revision was in 1983, which modernised the text and reorganised the fundamental rights into the opening chapter.

Judicial review

Uniquely, Article 120 of the constitution prohibits courts from reviewing the constitutionality of Acts of Parliament and treaties. This prohibition of constitutional review means the Netherlands has no constitutional court, and statutes cannot be tested against the constitution, although they can be tested against international treaties such as the European Convention on Human Rights.
