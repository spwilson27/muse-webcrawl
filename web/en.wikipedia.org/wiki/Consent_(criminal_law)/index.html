Consent (criminal law)

Consent in criminal law is a defence or element that negates criminal liability when a person legally capable of giving consent agrees to conduct that would otherwise be an offence — for example, contact in sport, medical treatment, or sexual activity. Absence of consent is an element of offences such as rape, sexual assault and, in many settings, battery.

Principles

Valid consent must generally be given freely by a person with capacity, with sufficient understanding, and may be withdrawn. Consent obtained by coercion, deception as to fundamental matters, or from someone below the age of consent or lacking mental capacity is not legally effective. Public policy limits consent: in many jurisdictions one cannot consent to serious bodily harm, so consent is no defence to grievous injury outside recognised exceptions such as surgery, contact sports and tattooing.

Sexual offences

Modern statutes increasingly define consent affirmatively as free agreement, list circumstances where consent is absent (unconsciousness, intoxication, threats), and set ages of consent. English law was reshaped by the Sexual Offences Act 2003; the House of Lords in R v Brown held consent no defence to actual bodily harm inflicted in sadomasochistic encounters, a frequently debated precedent.
