Plenoptic camera

A plenoptic camera, also called a light-field camera, is a camera that captures information not only about the intensity of light at each point of an image, but also about the direction in which light rays travel in space. The recorded light field allows photographs to be refocused and their viewpoint shifted slightly after capture.

Principle

The most common design places an array of microlenses between the main lens and the image sensor. Each microlens forms a small image of the main lens's aperture, so the sensor records both where each ray lands and the direction it came from. Software then reconstructs images focused at arbitrary depths, depth maps, and limited three-dimensional information, at the cost of effective spatial resolution.

History and products

The underlying idea goes back to integral photography, proposed by Gabriel Lippmann in 1908, and was formalised in the 1990s with the plenoptic function and light-field rendering research at Stanford. Ren Ng founded Lytro, which sold consumer light-field cameras from 2012; the company closed in 2018. Light-field sensors continue to be used in industrial inspection and microscopy, while software depth estimation and multi-camera arrays have taken over most consumer applications such as synthetic depth of field.
