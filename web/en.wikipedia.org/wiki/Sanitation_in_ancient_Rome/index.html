Sanitation in ancient Rome

Sanitation in ancient Rome was remarkably advanced for its time. The Romans built extensive systems of aqueducts, sewers, public latrines, and baths that supplied clean water to, and removed waste from, cities across their empire.

Sewers

The Cloaca Maxima, originally an open drain built around the 6th century BC to empty the marshes between Rome's hills, was gradually covered and enlarged into one of the world's earliest great sewer systems, discharging into the Tiber. Branch drains carried stormwater and effluent from public latrines and baths. Smaller household drains were less universal: many dwellings relied on cesspits, and chamber pots emptied into the street or collected for use as fertiliser and in fulling cloth with urine.

Public latrines and baths

Public latrines (foricae) provided rows of marble or stone seats over running water channels, used communally without partitions; a sponge on a stick (tersorium) rinsed in a channel of running water served for cleaning. Roman baths combined hygiene, exercise, and social life, and their wastewater helped flush the latrines.

Water supply

Rome's aqueducts delivered an estimated billion litres of water a day at their height, feeding fountains, baths, and some private homes through lead or terracotta pipes. Frontinus, as water commissioner, documented the system in De aquaeductu. Despite these works, Roman cities remained unhealthy by modern standards, with high rates of waterborne and parasitic disease.
