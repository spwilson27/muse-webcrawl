Spreadsheet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A spreadsheet is a computer application for computation, organisation, analysis, and storage of data in tabular form. A spreadsheet presents data in a grid of cells arranged in rows and columns; each cell may contain text, numbers, or a formula that automatically calculates a value from the contents of other cells. When an input value changes, dependent formulas recalculate immediately — the feature that made the spreadsheet the first "killer application" of the personal computer and transformed financial modelling.

History

The word "spreadsheet" originally referred to a large sheet of paper with columns and rows used by accountants. Electronic precursors ran on mainframes in the 1960s, including LANPAR (1969). The first interactive spreadsheet for a personal computer was VisiCalc, created by Dan Bricklin and Bob Frankston and released for the Apple II in 1979; it is widely credited with driving business adoption of microcomputers. Lotus 1-2-3 (1983) dominated the IBM PC era, and Microsoft Excel, released for the Macintosh in 1985 and Windows in 1987, eventually became the market standard. Free and web-based alternatives include LibreOffice Calc and Google Sheets (2006), which added real-time collaboration.

Concepts

Cells are addressed by column letter and row number (for example, B7). Formulas begin with an equals sign and may use hundreds of built-in functions — SUM, AVERAGE, IF, VLOOKUP — as well as cell ranges and absolute or relative references. Modern spreadsheets support multiple worksheets per workbook, charts, pivot tables for summarising data, macros and scripting, conditional formatting, and data-validation rules. Spreadsheet models underpin budgeting, accounting, engineering calculations, statistics, and much ad-hoc data analysis throughout business and science.

Risks and criticism

Field audits consistently find errors in a large share of operational spreadsheets — wrong formulas, overwritten constants, and broken references — occasionally with serious consequences, as in several widely reported financial and public-health incidents. Researchers in the "spreadsheet error" literature advocate testing, version control, and documentation practices borrowed from software engineering.
