The Mithridatic Wars were three conflicts fought between the Roman Republic and King Mithridates VI of the Kingdom of Pontus between 88 BC and 63 BC. They ended with the total defeat of Pontus, the reorganization of the Roman East, and the death of Mithridates, one of Rome's most persistent enemies. The wars brought Rome into direct control of most of Anatolia and drew Roman armies deep into Armenia and the Caucasus.

Mithridates VI (reigned 120–63 BC) inherited a small kingdom on the southern shore of the Black Sea and expanded it aggressively into Colchis, the Crimea, and the Roman province of Asia. Roman interference in Cappadocia and Bithynia, both of which Mithridates sought to dominate, brought the two powers into collision.

First Mithridatic War (88–85 BC)

In 88 BC Mithridates invaded Roman Asia and ordered the massacre of Roman and Italian residents throughout the province — the so-called Asiatic Vespers, in which tens of thousands were killed. He then crossed into Greece, where Athens welcomed him as a liberator from Roman rule. The Roman commander Lucius Cornelius Sulla, diverted from the civil war at home, besieged and stormed Athens in 86 BC and defeated the Pontic armies at Chaeronea and Orchomenus. The war ended with the Treaty of Dardanos (85 BC), by which Mithridates surrendered his conquests but kept his kingdom.

Second Mithridatic War (83–81 BC)

The brief Second Mithridatic War began when the Roman governor of Asia, Lucius Licinius Murena, invaded Pontus without authorization, suspecting Mithridates of rearming. Mithridates defeated Murena, and Sulla ordered the Romans to withdraw; the conflict ended with the status quo restored.

Third Mithridatic War (73–63 BC)

The final war began in 73 BC after the death of Nicomedes IV of Bithynia, who bequeathed his kingdom to Rome; Mithridates invaded to prevent the annexation. Lucius Licinius Lucullus campaigned brilliantly, driving Mithridates from Pontus into the protection of his son-in-law, Tigranes the Great of Armenia, and defeating Tigranes at Tigranocerta in 69 BC. Mutiny and political intrigue at Rome cost Lucullus his command, and in 66 BC Pompey the Great, armed with sweeping authority under the lex Manilia, took over. Pompey defeated Mithridates at Nicopolis and pursued him through the Caucasus. In 63 BC, betrayed by his son Pharnaces, Mithridates died in the Crimea.

Aftermath

Pontus was annexed and combined with Bithynia as a Roman province. Pompey reorganized the East, creating the province of Syria, redrawing client kingdoms, and enormously increasing Roman revenues. The wars were a milestone in Rome's transformation into the dominant power of the eastern Mediterranean.
