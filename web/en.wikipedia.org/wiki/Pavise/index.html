A pavise (also pavis) is a large shield used in late medieval warfare to provide cover for archers and crossbowmen while they reloaded. Tall enough to shelter a standing or kneeling man, the pavise was typically propped upright on a wooden strut or iron spike rather than held, functioning as a portable wall on the battlefield and in siege operations.

Description

Pavises were usually made of wood, sometimes covered with canvas, parchment, or leather and painted — frequently with the arms of the owner's city, a patron saint, or a religious image. A common form was slightly convex with a central vertical rib to deflect missiles. Many had a support leg or prop that folded out from the back, and some were fitted with a small window or loophole through which a crossbowman could spy or shoot. The name derives from Pavia in Italy, one of the centres of their manufacture.

Use

The pavise was especially associated with Genoese crossbowmen, the premier mercenary missile troops of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, whose slow-shooting steel crossbows required a shelter behind which to span the weapon with a windlass or cranequin. A crossbowman often worked with an attendant, the pavesarius, who carried and positioned the shield. Pavises were also erected in rows to protect troops during sieges and were used by Hussite forces in the fifteenth century. English longbowmen sometimes achieved a similar effect with planted stakes. The type declined as handheld firearms replaced bows and crossbows in the sixteenth century.
