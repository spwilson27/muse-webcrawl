Archduke Franz Ferdinand Carl Ludwig Joseph Maria of Austria (18 December 1863 – 28 June 1914) was the heir presumptive to the throne of Austria-Hungary. His assassination in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 by the Bosnian Serb student Gavrilo Princip, a member of Young Bosnia, triggered the July Crisis and Austria-Hungary's declaration of war on Serbia — the sequence of events that led directly to the First World War.

Life and position

The eldest son of Archduke Karl Ludwig, Franz Ferdinand became heir after the suicide of Crown Prince Rudolf in 1889 and the death of his father in 1896. A passionate huntsman (his game books record nearly 275,000 kills), he built up the palace of Konopiště in Bohemia and gathered a military chancellery at the Belvedere in Vienna that functioned as a shadow government. He advocated of reforming the Dual Monarchy — variously described as "trialism" or federalisation into a United States of Greater Austria — he nonetheless held deeply conservative, clerical views and a notorious contempt for Hungarian political dominance.

Marriage

His love match with Countess Sophie Chotek, a lady-in-waiting from an aristocratic but non-reigning Bohemian family, was bitterly opposed by Emperor Franz Joseph I. In 1900 Franz Ferdinand was permitted a morganatic marriage only after renouncing his descendants' rights to the throne; Sophie, later Duchess of Hohenberg, was excluded from many court honours — a humiliation the couple felt keenly, not least in Sarajevo, where she was exceptionally allowed to ride beside him because the visit fell on their wedding anniversary.

Assassination

On 28 June 1914 — Vidovdan, St Vitus Day, a date charged with Serbian nationalist meaning — the couple visited Sarajevo. After a bomb thrown by Nedeljko Čabrinović injured members of their entourage, the motorcade's changed route was not communicated to their driver, who turned into Franz Joseph Street and stalled in front of Princip. The teenager shot Sophie in the abdomen and Franz Ferdinand in the neck; both died within the hour. The chain of ultimatums that followed escalated within five weeks into a general European war.
