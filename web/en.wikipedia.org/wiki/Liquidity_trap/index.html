Liquidity trap

A liquidity trap is a situation, described in Keynesian economics, in which, "after the rate of interest has fallen to a certain level, liquidity preference may become virtually absolute in the sense that almost everyone prefers holding cash rather than holding a debt which yields so low a rate of interest."

Concept

A liquidity trap is caused when people hoard cash because they expect an adverse event such as deflation, insufficient aggregate demand, or war. According to modern theories, a liquidity trap is characterised by zero or negative nominal interest rates, an inability of a country's central bank to stimulate the economy, and persistent deflation or dismal growth.

Economist John Maynard Keynes introduced the concept in his 1936 work The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money. Keynes assumed that monetary policy has less control over the situation, because the increase in money supply, printed by the central bank to improve the economy, is offset by a decrease in the velocity of money.

Zero lower bound

A related concept is the zero lower bound (ZLB): because nominal interest rates cannot fall much below zero (people would simply hold cash), monetary policy loses traction once rates approach zero. Following the financial crisis of 2007–2008, several central banks — including the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, and the Bank of Japan — resorted to unconventional measures such as quantitative easing and negative interest rate policies.

Japan's "Lost Decade"

Japan's experience during the 1990s, often called the Lost Decade, is frequently cited as a modern example. Despite near-zero interest rates set by the Bank of Japan, the economy stagnated, leading economists such as Paul Krugman to revive the liquidity trap concept in the late 1990s.

Criticism

Classical and monetarist economists have questioned the significance of the liquidity trap. Some argue that even at zero short-term rates, central banks can influence longer-term rates and asset prices through expectations management and purchases of longer-maturity assets.
