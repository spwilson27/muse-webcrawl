The Two Cultures is the first part of a 1959 Rede Lecture by British scientist and novelist C. P. Snow, published in book form the same year. Its thesis was that the breakdown of communication between "the two cultures" of modern society—the sciences and the humanities—was hindering Britain's ability to solve the world's problems.

Thesis

Snow argued that literary intellectuals and physical scientists had become so estranged that they could scarcely communicate at all, describing the gap as a gulf of mutual incomprehension. He famously observed that many humanities scholars could not state the Second Law of Thermodynamics—an ignorance he compared to a scientist never having read Shakespeare. Snow blamed the British education system, which he believed specialized too early and rewarded literary culture while undervaluing science and engineering.

Response and debate

The lecture provoked a celebrated controversy, including a fierce attack by the literary critic F. R. Leavis in his 1962 Richmond Lecture. Snow replied with The Two Cultures: A Second Look (1963), in which he suggested a mediating "third culture" might emerge. The phrase "the two cultures" entered common usage, and the debate has been revisited whenever curricula, science funding, or the social role of science is discussed.
