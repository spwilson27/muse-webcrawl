Chemotaxis

Cells migrate up a gradient of chemoattractant

Chemotaxis is the directed movement of a cell or organism in response to a chemical gradient in its environment. Movement toward a chemical stimulus is positive chemotaxis; movement away from a repellent is negative chemotaxis. Chemotaxis is fundamental to bacterial foraging, immune-cell trafficking, wound healing, fertilisation and the development of multicellular organisms.

Bacterial chemotaxis

The best understood system is that of the bacterium Escherichia coli, studied extensively by Julius Adler and others from the 1960s. E. coli swims in a "run-and-tumble" pattern: counterclockwise rotation of its flagella bundles them for a smooth run, while clockwise rotation makes the cell tumble and reorient. Membrane receptors (methyl-accepting chemotaxis proteins) detect attractants such as aspartate and repellents such as phenol. A two-component signalling pathway — the Che proteins — couples receptor occupancy to the flagellar motor, and adaptation by methylation and demethylation of the receptors lets the cell sense changes in concentration over time rather than absolute levels, producing a biased random walk up the gradient.

Eukaryotic chemotaxis

Eukaryotic cells move by crawling, extending actin-rich pseudopods in the direction of the gradient. Neutrophils and macrophages chase bacteria and debris guided by formyl peptides, complement fragment C5a and chemokines such as IL-8; the slime mould Dictyostelium discoideum aggregates toward cyclic AMP when starving. Sperm of many species are guided to the egg by chemoattractants, and during embryonic development chemotactic signals position neurons and primordial germ cells.

Significance and study

Defective chemotaxis impairs immune defence and wound healing, while tumour cells can hijack chemokine signals during metastasis. Chemotaxis is measured with assays such as capillary tubes, the Boyden chamber and microfluidic gradient generators. Mathematically, populations undergoing chemotaxis are described by models of the Keller–Segel type, which couple cell density to the diffusion and consumption of the attractant and can produce striking aggregation patterns.
