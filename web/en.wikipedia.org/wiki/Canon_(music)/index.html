Canon (music)

In music, a canon is a contrapuntal (counterpoint-based) compositional technique that employs a melody with one or more imitations of the melody played after a given duration (e.g., quarter rest, one measure). The initial melody is called the leader (or dux), while the follower voice or voices, which imitate the leader at a fixed time interval and usually at the same pitch or a fixed interval of transposition, are called the comes. For the canon to be musically satisfying, the imitating voices must combine harmoniously with the original melody as it unfolds.

Types

A round, such as "Frère Jacques" or "Row, Row, Row Your Boat", is a canon in which the voices enter at the unison and the piece can repeat indefinitely. Canons may be classified by the interval of imitation (at the fifth, at the octave), by the number of voices, or by transformation: in a canon by inversion (mirror canon) the follower inverts the leader's intervals; in a retrograde or crab canon the follower plays the melody backwards; an augmentation canon stretches the follower's note values.

History

Canon was cultivated from medieval times through the Renaissance — the earliest known canon is the English rota "Sumer is icumen in" (c. 1250) — and reached virtuosic heights in the works of Johann Sebastian Bach, including the canons of the Goldberg Variations, The Musical Offering and The Art of Fugue. Johann Pachelbel's Canon in D (c. 1694–1706) became one of the most popular pieces of Baroque music in the modern era, although it is strictly a canon over a ground bass (passacaglia).
