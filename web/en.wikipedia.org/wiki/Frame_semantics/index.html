Frame semantics

Frame semantics is a theory of linguistic meaning developed by Charles J. Fillmore, extending his earlier case grammar. It relates linguistic semantics to encyclopedic knowledge: the central idea is that one cannot understand the meaning of a single word without access to all the essential knowledge that relates to that word. A word represents a category of experience, and the meaning of the word is understood against a background frame — a structured body of knowledge or conceptualization of experience.

Basic principles

A semantic frame is a conceptual structure describing a particular type of situation, object, or event, together with its participants and props, called frame elements. For example, the commercial transaction frame involves a buyer, a seller, goods, and money. Words such as buy, sell, pay, and charge evoke this same frame but profile different parts of it: buy focuses on the buyer and the goods, while sell focuses on the seller and the goods. Frame semantics thus explains how a speaker's choice of word reflects a perspective on a scene.

Unlike approaches that treat meaning as a list of necessary and sufficient conditions or as decomposed into primitive features, frame semantics holds that word meanings are relativized to frames, which are themselves grounded in recurring human experience, social institutions, and background practices.

Applications

The theory underlies the FrameNet project at the International Computer Science Institute in Berkeley, California, a lexicographic database that documents the semantic and syntactic combinatory possibilities of English words in terms of frames. Frame semantics has influenced cognitive linguistics, construction grammar, computational lexicography, and natural language processing, where frame-based annotation is used for semantic role labeling and information extraction.
