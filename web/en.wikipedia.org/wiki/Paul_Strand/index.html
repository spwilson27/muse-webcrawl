Paul Strand (October 16, 1890 – March 31, 1976) was an American photographer and filmmaker who, along with Alfred Stieglitz and Edward Weston, helped establish photography as an art form in the 20th century. His diverse body of work, spanning six decades, covers numerous genres and subjects throughout the Americas, Europe, and Africa.

Early life and education

Strand was born Nathaniel Paul Strand in New York City to Bohemian immigrant parents. He studied photography under Lewis Hine at the Ethical Culture Fieldston School, where Hine introduced him to the work of Alfred Stieglitz at the 291 gallery. Strand became a regular visitor to 291 and decided to pursue photography seriously.

Career

In 1915 Strand began to move away from the soft-focus Pictorialist style toward a harder, more abstract approach that Stieglitz championed as "straight photography." His photographs of that year, including Wall Street and From the El, and his 1916 candid street portraits such as Blind Woman, are considered landmarks of modernist photography. Stieglitz devoted the final issue of Camera Work (1917) to Strand's work, hailing him as the future of the medium.

In the 1920s Strand worked as a cinematographer, contributing to films including Manhatta (1921), a portrait of New York City co-directed with Charles Sheeler, and later The Plow That Broke the Plains (1936) and Native Land (1942). He helped found the Photo League in 1936.

Later work

After moving to France in 1949, partly in reaction to McCarthy-era political pressures in the United States, Strand produced a series of celebrated photographic books made with collaborators, including Time in New England (1950), La France de profil (1952), Un Paese (1955, with Cesare Zavattini), Tir a'Mhurain (1962), and Living Egypt (1969). He spent his final years in Orgeval, France, where he died in 1976.

Legacy

Strand's commitment to unmanipulated, sharply focused images and to printing his own work with great technical care made him one of the most influential figures in the history of photography. Major retrospectives of his work have been held at the Museum of Modern Art and the Philadelphia Museum of Art.
