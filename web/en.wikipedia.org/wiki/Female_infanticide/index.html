Female infanticide

Female infanticide is the deliberate killing of newborn female children or the termination of a female fetus. In societies where women are valued less than men, or where dowry and patrilineal inheritance make daughters an economic burden, son preference has historically led to the killing, abandonment, or fatal neglect of infant girls. Related modern practices include sex-selective abortion, which has skewed sex ratios at birth in parts of Asia and the Caucasus.

Historical and regional patterns

Female infanticide has been documented historically in parts of China and India, among some Arab tribes before Islam, and in other patriarchal societies. Colonial administrators in India recorded the practice among certain clans in the 19th century, and legislation such as the Female Infanticide Prevention Act of 1870 attempted to suppress it. Economist Amartya Sen drew attention to the phenomenon of "missing women" — the deficit of females relative to expected numbers in parts of Asia and North Africa.

Modern responses

Governments have banned sex determination for the purpose of selective abortion, as in India's Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act (1994) and China's regulations, and campaigns promote the value of daughters. Skewed ratios persist in some regions, though they have begun to normalise in countries such as South Korea.
