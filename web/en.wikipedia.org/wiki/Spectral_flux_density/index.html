Spectral flux density

Spectral flux density
SymbolSν or Fν
SI unitW·m−2·Hz−1
Common unitJansky (Jy): 1 Jy = 10−26 W·m−2·Hz−1

In spectroscopy, spectral flux density is the quantity describing the rate at which energy is transferred by electromagnetic radiation through a real or virtual surface, per unit surface area and per unit wavelength or frequency. It is a radiometric quantity, often used in astronomy, and should not be confused with spectral irradiance or spectral radiance. The total flux density is the integral of the spectral flux density over all wavelengths or frequencies.

Definitions

Two conventions exist, depending on whether the spectrum is expressed per unit wavelength (Fλ, in units such as W·m−2·μm−1) or per unit frequency (Fν, W·m−2·Hz−1). The two are related by Fλ = (c/λ2) Fν, so a spectrum flat in Fν is not flat in Fλ. In radio astronomy the standard unit is the jansky (Jy), defined as 10−26 W·m−2·Hz−1; bright radio sources are measured in thousands of janskys, while faint sources reach microjanskys.

Astronomical use

Spectral flux density as a function of frequency constitutes a source's spectral energy distribution (in part), characterizing stars, galaxies, quasars, and nebulae. The AB magnitude system used in optical astronomy is defined directly from spectral flux density: a source with Fν = 3,631 Jy has AB magnitude zero at all frequencies. Photometry in a bandpass effectively measures the flux density averaged over the filter response.
