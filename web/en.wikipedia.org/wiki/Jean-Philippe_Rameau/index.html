Jean-Philippe Rameau (25 September 1683 – 12 September 1764) was one of the most important French composers and music theorists of the 18th century. He replaced Jean-Baptiste Lully as the dominant composer of French opera and is also considered the leading French composer for the harpsichord of his time, alongside François Couperin.

Music theory

Rameau's Traité de l'harmonie (Treatise on Harmony, 1722) was the first theory of tonal music to systematically describe chord inversion and the fundamental bass, laying the groundwork for modern harmonic analysis. Later works, including Génération harmonique (1737), attempted to derive harmony from acoustical principles such as the harmonic series.

Operas

Rameau turned to opera relatively late. His first, Hippolyte et Aricie (1733), provoked the "Lulliste–Ramiste" controversy between defenders of Lully's tradition and Rameau's harmonically daring style. His masterpieces include Les Indes galantes (1735), Castor et Pollux (1737), Dardanus (1739), and Platée (1745).

Harpsichord music

His three books of Pièces de clavecin (1706, 1724, 1726–28) contain character pieces such as "Les Sauvages", "Les Cyclopes", and "La Poule", ranking among the finest French keyboard music of the Baroque.
