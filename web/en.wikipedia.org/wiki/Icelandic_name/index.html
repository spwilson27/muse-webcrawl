Icelandic name

An Icelandic name follows a naming system quite unlike that of most Western countries: with few exceptions, Icelanders do not have inherited family surnames. Instead, a person's second name is a patronymic — or occasionally a matronymic — formed from the given name of a parent, indicating "son of" or "daughter of" that parent.

The patronymic system

A boy named Jón whose father is named Einar becomes Jón Einarsson ("Jón, Einar's son"); his sister Anna becomes Anna Einarsdóttir ("Anna, Einar's daughter"). The suffixes -son (son) and -dóttir (daughter) are attached to the parent's name in the genitive case. Because the patronymic changes every generation, siblings of different sexes have different "surnames," and a family has no single shared family name. Icelanders are consequently listed alphabetically by given name in directories such as the telephone book.

Regulation

Given names are traditionally governed by the Icelandic Naming Committee (Mannanafnanefnd), which approves names that can be grammatically integrated into the Icelandic language and are not thought likely to cause the bearer embarrassment; naming laws were liberalized in 2019, in part granting parents greater freedom and allowing gender-neutral suffix options. A small number of old family names survive, mostly inherited from Danish-era families or adopted by Icelanders who lived abroad.

Usage

In Iceland a person is addressed by their given name, even formally — "President Guðni" rather than "President Jóhannesson." Icelanders travelling abroad sometimes adapt their patronymics as surnames for convenience. The system descends from the common Scandinavian naming practice of the Viking Age, which Iceland alone has preserved as its universal rule.
