The Beeching cuts, also colloquially known as the Beeching Axe, were a major programme of railway closure in Great Britain in the 1960s. The name refers to Dr Richard Beeching, chairman of the British Railways Board, whose 1963 report The Reshaping of British Railways recommended closing about a third of the network. Some 55,000 staff lost their jobs, and thousands of communities lost their rail service.

Background

By the early 1960s British Railways was making heavy losses as road transport, encouraged by motorway building, took passengers and freight. The Conservative government appointed Beeching, a physicist on secondment from ICI, to make the railways pay. His statistical analysis showed that half the network carried only a small fraction of traffic: a third of stations produced less than 1 per cent of passenger revenue.

The reports and closures

The 1963 report proposed closing 2,363 stations and some 5,000 route-miles. A second report in 1965, The Development of the Major Railway Trunk Routes, selected 3,000 miles for intensive investment. The Labour government elected in 1964 continued most closures, though Transport Minister Barbara Castle reprieved some lines. Implementation ran through the decade; the last major losses of the era came in the early 1970s.

Legacy

The cuts left Britain with a much-reduced network that later growth made congested; some closed routes have since reopened (for example lines in Scotland and Wales, and the Borders Railway in 2015), and some survive as heritage railways, footpaths or cycleways. Others were built over, making reinstatement difficult. Debate continues about whether the programme was a necessary rationalisation or a short-sighted one shaped by road-lobby interests; it framed the network inherited at privatisation in the 1990s.
