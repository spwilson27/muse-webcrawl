Nanook of the North is a 1922 American silent film that combines elements of documentary and docudrama/docufiction, at a time when the concept of a documentary film did not yet exist. Directed by Robert J. Flaherty, it depicts the life of the Inuk hunter Nanook and his family in the Canadian Arctic, and is widely regarded as the first feature-length documentary film.

Production

Flaherty, funded by the French fur company Revillon Frères, spent 1920–21 near Port Harrison (now Inukjuak) in northern Quebec. His star, "Nanook" (Inuktitut for "polar bear"), was in reality a hunter named Allakariallak; the women presented as his wives were not his wives, and several scenes — the walrus hunt by harpoon, the building of an oversized igloo with a cutaway wall for light — were staged to show traditional practices that rifles and modern trade goods had already displaced. Flaherty defended these methods as recovering a reality that would otherwise be lost, an approach now called salvage ethnography.

Reception and legacy

Distributed by Pathé, the film premiered in New York on June 11, 1922, and was a worldwide commercial and critical success, sparking a cycle of exotic travelogue films and popularizing "Nanook" in songs, ice cream bars, and slang. Allakariallak died of starvation on a hunting trip less than two years after filming, a fact that later deepened the film's melancholy reputation. In 1989 Nanook of the North was among the first 25 films selected for the United States National Film Registry as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant." It continues to anchor debates about documentary ethics, staging, and the representation of Indigenous peoples.
