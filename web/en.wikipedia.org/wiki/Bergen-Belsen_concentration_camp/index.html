Bergen-Belsen (pronounced [ˈbɛʁɡn̩ ˈbɛlsn̩]), commonly called Belsen, was a Nazi concentration camp in present-day Lower Saxony, south of the towns of Bergen and Celle. Originally established in 1940 as a prisoner-of-war camp, from 1943 it also operated as a concentration camp. Between 1941 and 1945 an estimated 20,000 Soviet prisoners of war and some 52,000 other prisoners died there, most from starvation, disease and neglect rather than systematic gassing — Bergen-Belsen had no gas chambers.

History

The site began as a Wehrmacht camp (Stalag XI-C/321) for Belgian, French and then Soviet prisoners of war, tens of thousands of whom perished in 1941–42. In April 1943 the SS took over part of the complex to establish a "residence camp" (Aufenthaltslager) for Jews intended for exchange for German nationals abroad. As the war turned, the camp absorbed prisoners from other sections and, from late 1944, tens of thousands evacuated from camps in the east on death marches and transports.

Overcrowding, a collapsed administration and epidemics of typhus, typhoid and dysentery made conditions in early 1945 catastrophic: some 35,000 people died between January and mid-April 1945 alone. Among them, in February or March 1945, were Anne Frank and her sister Margot, transferred from Auschwitz.

Liberation

British troops of the 11th Armoured Division reached the camp on 15 April 1945 under a locally negotiated truce, finding some 60,000 prisoners, most starving or gravely ill, and thousands of unburied corpses. BBC correspondent Richard Dimbleby's broadcast report brought the horrors to a worldwide audience. Despite medical efforts, about 14,000 prisoners died after liberation. The British burned the camp in May 1945 to stop the spread of typhus. A displaced persons camp, the largest Jewish DP camp in Germany, then operated nearby until 1950.

Trials and memorial

The first Belsen trial at Lüneburg in autumn 1945 prosecuted camp personnel including commandant Josef Kramer, who was hanged. The site is now a memorial and documentation centre, with mass graves, a Wall of Inscriptions and exhibitions recording the camp's history.
