Obesity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Obesity is a medical condition in which excess body fat accumulates to an extent that increases the risk of many diseases. In adults it is commonly defined by a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher (overweight being 25 or higher), though BMI is an imperfect proxy that does not measure fat directly or its distribution. Obesity has become a global epidemic: over a billion people now live with it, rates having roughly tripled since 1975 in many measures, including among children.

Its causes are a web of genetics (appetite and metabolism genes, e.g. FTO), abundant energy-dense food environments, sedentary work and transport, poor sleep, medications and socioeconomic factors — no population has grown more 'weak-willed' in two generations; environments changed. Consequences include type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, several cancers, sleep apnoea, fatty liver disease, osteoarthritis and stigma itself, which harms health and care.

Treatment

Care is chronic-disease management, not a single diet: structured lifestyle change yields modest average losses; anti-obesity medicines — the GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide) — produce substantial losses (on the order of 15–20% of body weight in trials) and have reshaped treatment in the 2020s; bariatric surgery (gastric bypass, sleeve gastrectomy) gives the largest durable losses and diabetes remission for severe obesity. Prevention policy targets food environments, marketing to children and urban design.

Measurement debate

BMI misclassifies muscular people and misses central (visceral) fat, which carries much of the risk; waist circumference and body-composition methods refine it. At the same time, large epidemiology consistently links high BMI classes to mortality, with the lowest risk near the 'normal' band — nuances actively debated in the literature.
