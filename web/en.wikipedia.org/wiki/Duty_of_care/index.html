Duty of care

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In English tort law, a duty of care is a legal obligation owed by one person to another to take reasonable care to avoid causing them foreseeable harm. It is the first element of the tort of negligence: without a duty, there is no liability however careless the conduct. The modern doctrine begins with Lord Atkin's “neighbour principle” in Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) — you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions you can reasonably foresee would injure persons so closely affected by your acts that they ought to be in your contemplation.

Since Caparo Industries v Dickman (1990), English courts ask whether harm was foreseeable, whether the relationship was sufficiently proximate, and whether it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. Established categories (road users, employers, doctors, manufacturers, occupiers) are applied by analogy; novel duties are developed incrementally. American law uses a similar duty analysis within negligence (Judge Cardozo's zone-of-danger reasoning in Palsgraf, 1928). Pure economic loss and omissions are generally outside the duty, subject to exceptions such as assumption of responsibility.
