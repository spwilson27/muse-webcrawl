Escherichia coli (commonly abbreviated E. coli) is a Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic, rod-shaped, coliform bacterium of the genus Escherichia that is commonly found in the lower intestine of warm-blooded organisms. Most E. coli strains are harmless and form part of the normal gut flora, but some serotypes — such as O157:H7 — can cause serious food poisoning, urinary tract infections and other disease. The bacterium was discovered by German-Austrian paediatrician Theodor Escherich in 1885.

Biology

E. coli cells are about 2.0 μm long and 0.5 μm in diameter and typically move by peritrichous flagella. They can grow rapidly on simple media, dividing as fast as every 20 minutes under optimal conditions, and ferment lactose with gas production — a trait used to distinguish them in water-quality testing, where their presence indicates faecal contamination. The genome of the laboratory strain K-12 MG1655, sequenced in 1997, contains about 4.6 million base pairs and roughly 4,300 protein-coding genes.

Role in science and disease

E. coli is the most widely studied prokaryotic model organism and a workhorse of biotechnology: it was central to experiments establishing DNA replication, the genetic code and gene regulation (the lac operon), and it is used industrially to produce recombinant insulin, enzymes and biofuels. Pathogenic strains are grouped into pathotypes (e.g. ETEC, EPEC, EHEC) responsible for diarrhoeal disease worldwide; the 2011 O104:H4 outbreak in Germany caused over 50 deaths. Most infections are treated supportively, and prevention rests on food hygiene and thorough cooking.
