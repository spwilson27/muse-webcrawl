Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is a genetic disorder of the heart muscle in which myocardium is progressively replaced by fibrofatty tissue, predisposing to ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death, particularly in the young and in athletes. It was originally described as arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia/cardiomyopathy (ARVD/C) because early disease most visibly affects the right ventricle, but left-dominant and biventricular forms are now recognised under the broader ACM umbrella.

Cause

Most cases are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern and are caused by mutations in genes encoding desmosomal proteins that anchor heart muscle cells together — plakophilin-2 (PKP2) most commonly, along with desmoplakin, desmoglein-2, desmocollin-2, and plakoglobin. Mechanical stress, especially endurance exercise, accelerates cell detachment, inflammation, and fibrofatty repair, which explains why sport both unmasks and worsens the disease. Rare recessive forms are associated with skin and hair abnormalities, as in Naxos disease and Carvajal syndrome.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis uses combinations of criteria (the Task Force and later Padua criteria): ECG abnormalities such as T-wave inversion in the right precordial leads and epsilon waves, ventricular arrhythmias of left-bundle-branch morphology, imaging showing ventricular dilation, aneurysms and dysfunction on echocardiography or cardiac MRI, tissue characterisation, and family history or genetic testing.

Management

There is no cure. Management centres on preventing sudden death: restriction from competitive and high-intensity endurance sport, beta-blockers, antiarrhythmic drugs, catheter ablation for recurrent ventricular tachycardia, and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) for high-risk patients. Family screening of first-degree relatives is recommended.
