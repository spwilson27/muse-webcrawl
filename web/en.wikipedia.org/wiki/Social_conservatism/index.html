Social conservatism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Social conservatism is a political philosophy that prioritises traditional values, established institutions and gradual social change. Social conservatives emphasise the importance of the family, religion, patriotism and conventional morality, and are typically sceptical of rapid changes in social norms concerning sexuality, gender roles, abortion, drugs and crime policy.

As an orientation rather than a single doctrine, social conservatism is found in conservative, Christian democratic and some populist parties worldwide. Its adherents argue that social institutions embody practical wisdom, that strong families and communities are preconditions of liberty and order, and that moral norms cannot be redesigned at will without unintended harm. Its critics argue that appeals to tradition entrench discrimination and that the state should not legislate private morality. In the United States, socially conservative voters became a central constituency of the Republican coalition from the late 1970s, organised through movements opposed to abortion rights and supportive of school prayer and traditional marriage; in other countries similar positions are carried by confessional parties.

Relation to other conservatisms

Social conservatism pairs naturally with national conservatism but is independent of economic doctrine: social conservatives may support welfare provision and economic intervention (as in much Christian democracy) or free markets (as in American fusionism). Libertarians, by contrast, generally oppose the legal enforcement of traditional morality, producing a standing tension on the right.
