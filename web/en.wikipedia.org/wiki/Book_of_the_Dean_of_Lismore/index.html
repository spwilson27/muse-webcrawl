The Book of the Dean of Lismore (Scottish Gaelic: Leabhar Deathan Lios Mòir) is a famous Scottish manuscript, compiled in the first half of the 16th century. It is the largest and most important surviving collection of early Scottish Gaelic poetry, and a key source for the shared Gaelic literary tradition of Scotland and Ireland in the late medieval period.

Compilation

The manuscript was compiled at Fortingall in Perthshire mainly between 1512 and 1526 by Sir James MacGregor (c. 1482–1551), vicar of Fortingall and Dean of Lismore, with contributions from his brother Duncan, a notary. Rather than the classical Gaelic orthography used by professional bards, the Gaelic verse is written in a phonetic spelling based on Middle Scots conventions — which makes the manuscript a uniquely valuable record of how Scottish Gaelic was actually pronounced at the time.

Contents

The book contains bardic poetry attributed to poets of both Scotland and Ireland — including Ossianic (Fionn cycle) ballads, panegyrics to Scottish clan chiefs such as the MacGregors and Campbells, religious and satirical verse, and poems by Irish poets such as Muireadhach Albanach. It also preserves early poems by women, including Aithbhreac inghean Coirceadal, whose elegy for her husband (c. 1475) is among the earliest known Gaelic poems by a named Scottish woman. Some material is in Scots and Latin.

Significance

Now held by the National Library of Scotland, the manuscript is the single most important source for Scottish Gaelic literature before 1600 and for the study of the Fenian ballad tradition. Scholarly editions appeared in the 19th and 20th centuries, beginning with the transcription published in 1862.
