Integrated pest management

Integrated pest management (IPM) is an ecosystem-based strategy for the long-term prevention and control of pests through a combination of techniques — biological control, habitat manipulation, cultural practices, resistant varieties, and judicious use of pesticides — chosen to minimise risks to human health, beneficial organisms, and the environment. Rather than aiming to eradicate pests, IPM seeks to keep pest populations below levels that cause unacceptable economic or aesthetic damage.

Beneficial predators such as ladybirds are a mainstay of IPM programmes.

Principles and practice

An IPM programme typically proceeds through monitoring and correct identification of pests, setting action thresholds (such as economic injury levels), applying preventive cultural methods (crop rotation, sanitation, resistant cultivars), and evaluating control options in order of increasing risk — mechanical and physical controls and pheromone mating disruption before broad-spectrum chemical insecticides, which are used selectively and in targeted applications when necessary. Record-keeping and evaluation close the loop.

History

IPM emerged in the 1950s–1970s in response to the drawbacks of calendar-based spraying: pest resistance, resurgence, secondary pest outbreaks, and environmental harm highlighted by Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962). Entomologists such as Vernon Stern and colleagues formalised the "integrated control" concept in 1959, introducing the economic threshold. Since then, IPM has been adopted in agriculture, forestry, public health, and urban pest control worldwide, and is promoted by the FAO and the European Union's sustainable-use framework.
