Académie royale de peinture et de sculpture

The Académie royale de peinture et de sculpture (Royal Academy of Painting and Sculpture) was France's premier art institution during the Ancien Régime. Founded in Paris in 1648 under King Louis XIV, it dominated French art education, exhibitions and artistic taste for nearly a century and a half, until it was abolished during the French Revolution in 1793.

History

The Academy was founded in 1648 by a group of twelve painters and sculptors, led by Charles Le Brun, who sought to free artists from the guild system of the Paris painters' guild (the Maîtrise) and to raise the status of painting and sculpture to that of liberal arts. In 1661, under the patronage of Louis XIV's minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert, the Academy was reorganised and placed under state control, becoming the instrument through which the monarchy directed official art.

Members met in the Louvre from 1692. The Academy administered the Prix de Rome, which sent the most promising students to study at the French Academy in Rome, founded in 1666. It also organised the famous Salon exhibitions, held in the Salon Carré of the Louvre, which from 1737 became the principal public exhibitions of contemporary art in Europe.

Organisation and teaching

The Academy imposed a strict hierarchy of genres, formalised by André Félibien in 1667, which ranked history painting highest, followed by portraiture, scenes of everyday life, landscape, animal painting and still life. Teaching centred on drawing from the live model and from casts of classical sculpture. Membership was restricted and fiercely contested; women were admitted only rarely — only fifteen women were members across the Academy's entire existence.

Abolition and legacy

Attacked during the Revolution as an instrument of royal privilege, the Academy was suppressed by the National Convention on 8 August 1793. Its functions were partly revived in 1795 with the creation of the Institut de France and its class of fine arts, ancestor of the present-day Académie des Beaux-Arts. Its doctrines — academic art — shaped European art teaching well into the nineteenth century.
