Copyright Term Extension Act

Long title: An Act to amend the provisions of title 17, United States Code, with respect to the duration of copyright, and for other purposes

Enacted: October 27, 1998

Citations: Pub. L. 105-298; 112 Stat. 2827

Introduced by: Sen. Orrin Hatch (R–UT); Rep. Howard Coble (R–NC)

The Copyright Term Extension Act (CTEA) of 1998 — also known as the Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act, the Sonny Bono Act, or, pejoratively, the Mickey Mouse Protection Act — extended copyright terms in the United States by 20 years. It is codified principally at 17 U.S.C. § 302 and § 304.

Provisions

Under the Copyright Act of 1976, copyright in a work created by an individual author lasted for the author's life plus 50 years, and copyright in works made for hire (and anonymous and pseudonymous works) lasted 75 years from publication or 100 years from creation, whichever was shorter. The CTEA added 20 years to each of these terms: life of the author plus 70 years for individual works, and 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation for works for hire. The extension applied both to works already under copyright and to future works, so that no then-copyrighted work entered the public domain again until January 1, 2019, when works published in 1923 finally expired.

Background and lobbying

The Act followed similar extensions in the European Union, which had harmonized terms at life plus 70 in 1993, and supporters argued that matching terms would protect American works abroad and encourage the restoration and distribution of older works. The bill was named posthumously for Sonny Bono, the entertainer and congressman who had favored even longer — ideally perpetual — copyright. Music and film industry groups, publishers, and the estate interests of prominent composers lobbied for passage; The Walt Disney Company was among the most prominent corporate supporters, since its early Mickey Mouse cartoons, beginning with Steamboat Willie (1928), were approaching expiration — the source of the "Mickey Mouse Protection Act" nickname.

Constitutional challenge

The Act was challenged as exceeding Congress's power under the Copyright Clause's "limited Times" requirement in Eldred v. Ashcroft, 537 U.S. 186 (2003). The Supreme Court upheld the CTEA 7–2, holding that the text, history, and precedent did not support striking down a term extension that remained finite, even though it applied retroactively to existing works. A later challenge to a related restoration statute in Golan v. Holder (2012) also failed. Critics including the plaintiffs' counsel Lawrence Lessig argued that repeated retroactive extensions effectively make copyright terms perpetual on the installment plan, while supporters maintained that Congress deserves deference in calibrating intellectual property policy.

Effects

Because of the Act, no published works entered the U.S. public domain through expiration between 1998 and 2019. Notable works that eventually entered on January 1, 2019 included films, books, and music first published in 1923. Steamboat Willie itself entered the public domain on January 1, 2024.
