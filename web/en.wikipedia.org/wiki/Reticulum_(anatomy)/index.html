Reticulum (anatomy)

The reticulum is the second chamber in the four-chamber stomach of ruminant animals, lying immediately behind the diaphragm and against the rumen, with which it forms the reticulorumen. Its interior lining is raised into a distinctive honeycomb pattern of intersecting ridges, giving it the nickname the "honeycomb" in butchery, where it is sold as a form of tripe.

Function

Together with the rumen, the reticulum hosts microbial fermentation of ingested plant material. Its contractions sort particles: fine, well-fermented material passes on through the reticulo-omasal orifice to the omasum, while larger particles are returned to the rumen and regurgitated as cud for further chewing (rumination). The reticulum also traps dense foreign objects swallowed by cattle, such as pieces of wire or nails; because it lies close to the heart, sharp objects can penetrate its wall and cause traumatic reticuloperitonitis ("hardware disease"), which is why many dairy cows are given a prophylactic magnet that sits in the reticulum and collects metal.
