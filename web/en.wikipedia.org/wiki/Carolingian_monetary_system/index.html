The Carolingian monetary system was the currency framework established in the Frankish Empire by Pepin the Short and his son Charlemagne in the later 8th century. It fixed the relationship £1 = 20 shillings = 240 pence (from the Latin libra, solidus, and denarius), a structure that persisted in much of Europe—and in Britain until decimalisation in 1971—for more than a thousand years.

The reform

Around 755 Pepin restored royal control of minting and fixed the silver denier as the standard coin. Charlemagne's reform of about 793/794 increased the weight of the Carolingian pound to roughly 408 grams of silver and decreed that 240 deniers be struck from one pound of silver. The pound and the shilling then functioned mainly as units of account—money of reckoning—while the only coin actually minted in large numbers was the silver penny (denier). Gold coinage largely disappeared from circulation north of the Alps, making the system monometallic.

Spread and legacy

The system spread with Carolingian influence into England, where Offa of Mercia and his successors adopted the silver penny and the 240-pence pound, and it underlay the currency systems of medieval and early modern Europe. The symbols £, s, and d, derived from the Latin names of the units, were still used for pounds, shillings, and pence in Britain until 1971, and "denier"-derived pennies circulated in France until the Revolution. In this sense the Carolingian reform is one of the longest-lived monetary standards in history.
