Sporangium


Fern sporangia clustered in sori.

A sporangium (plural: sporangia) is an enclosure, found in many plants and fungi, in which spores are formed. Within the sporangium, diploid sporophyte cells undergo meiosis to produce haploid spores, which are released when the sporangium ruptures or opens. Sporangia occur in nearly all land plants and in many fungal lineages.

In plants

In ferns, sporangia typically develop on the undersides of fronds, grouped in clusters called sori; many have a ring of specialised cells, the annulus, that catapults spores out as it dries. In seed plants, the pollen sacs of anthers are microsporangia and the ovule contains the megasporangium (nucellus). Mosses and liverworts bear a single sporangium — the capsule — at the tip of the sporophyte stalk.

In fungi

In zygomycete fungi such as Rhizopus, asexual sporangia are produced at the tips of upright hyphae called sporangiophores; each sporangium releases numerous sporangiospores. Some fungi, by contrast, disperse naked spores called conidia instead.
