Fort Mose (Spanish: Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose) was the first legally sanctioned free African settlement in what would become the United States. Founded in 1738 by the Spanish governor of Florida, Manuel de Montiano, just north of St. Augustine, it housed formerly enslaved people who had escaped from the British colonies of South Carolina and Georgia and sought freedom in Spanish Florida.

History

From 1687, enslaved Africans fleeing the Carolinas reached St. Augustine, where Spanish policy — confirmed by royal decrees from 1693 onwards — offered freedom to escapees who converted to Catholicism and served in the militia. By the 1730s the community was organised as a fortified town under its captain, Francisco Menéndez, himself an escapee from South Carolina. In 1740, during the War of Jenkins' Ear, British forces under James Oglethorpe captured and destroyed the fort; its inhabitants helped repel the subsequent siege of St. Augustine, and the town was rebuilt in 1752. When Spain ceded Florida to Britain in 1763, the residents of Mose evacuated with the Spanish to Cuba.

Legacy

The site, long forgotten, was rediscovered by archaeologists in the 1980s and is now Fort Mose Historic State Park, a US National Historic Landmark recognised as a precursor site of the Underground Railroad and a landmark of African-American history.
