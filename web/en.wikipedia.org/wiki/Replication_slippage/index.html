Replication slippage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Replication slippage, also known as slipped-strand mispairing (SSM), is a mutation process which occurs during DNA replication. It involves denaturation and displacement of the DNA strands, resulting in mispairing of the complementary bases. Replication slippage is best described as the addition or deletion of one or more base pairs, particularly in tandem repetitive sequences such as microsatellites, and is a major source of small insertions and deletions (indels) in genomes.

Mechanism

During replication of a repetitive tract, the newly synthesized (nascent) strand can transiently dissociate from the template and reanneal out of register. If the nascent strand loops out, replication resumes with extra repeat units inserted; if a loop forms in the template strand, repeat units are deleted. Because the mispaired intermediate is stabilized by correct base pairing on both sides of the repeat, the error can escape polymerase proofreading.

Cells normally correct most slippage events through the DNA mismatch repair system. Defects in mismatch repair — as in Lynch syndrome — cause microsatellite instability, a hallmark of certain hereditary and sporadic cancers.

Consequences

Replication slippage drives the high mutation rates of microsatellite loci, which makes them useful as genetic markers in population genetics, forensics, and molecular ecology. Expansion of trinucleotide repeats beyond critical thresholds causes human diseases: for example, expansion of CAG repeats in the huntingtin gene causes Huntington's disease, and CTG expansion in the DMPK gene causes myotonic dystrophy. Slippage at repeats within coding regions also produces frameshift mutations, and in bacteria it mediates phase variation of surface antigens, allowing populations to switch gene expression rapidly.
