John Locke (1632–1704) was an English philosopher and physician, among the most influential thinkers of the Enlightenment. His Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689) argued that the mind at birth is a blank slate (tabula rasa) written on by experience, founding British empiricism. His Two Treatises of Government defended natural rights to life, liberty and property and a social contract revocable when government breaks its trust — ideas foundational to liberalism and the American and French revolutions.

Epistemology

Against the doctrine of innate ideas, Locke held that all ideas come from sensation and reflection. He distinguished primary qualities (such as shape and motion, resembling qualities in objects) from secondary qualities (colour, taste, sound — powers to produce ideas in us). His account of personal identity, based on continuity of consciousness and memory, launched a debate continued by George Berkeley, David Hume and modern philosophers.

Political thought

The First Treatise refutes divine-right absolutism; the Second describes a state of nature governed by natural law, in which people form societies to secure their rights, retaining a right of resistance to tyranny. Locke's Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) argued for religious toleration (though with limits typical of his age). He was also involved in colonial administration and investment, a record modern scholars scrutinise alongside his philosophy.
