Vocal fry register

The vocal fry register (also known as pulse register, creak, creaky voice, or glottal fry) is the lowest vocal register and is produced through a loose glottal closure that permits air to bubble through slowly with a popping or rattling sound at a very low frequency. During this phonation, the arytenoid cartilages in the larynx are drawn together, causing the vocal folds to become slack, compact, and thick.

Physiology

In vocal fry, the vocal folds vibrate slowly and irregularly, often at rates of roughly 20–50 pulses per second — about an octave below normal speech — with long closed phases between brief openings. The resulting sound is perceived as a creaking or frying noise. Vocal fry can be produced by most speakers and singers at the bottom of their range and forms the basis of the country-music "creak" and some styles of throat singing.

Use in language and singing

Although in mainstream American English vocal fry is often a stylistic or prosodic feature — frequently occurring at the ends of phrases — in some languages creaky phonation is phonemic, distinguishing word meaning (as in Danish stød or the tones of Vietnamese and Burmese). Vocal fry is used deliberately in singing for effect, notably in country, metal, and contemporary pop.

Social perception

Since the early 2010s, vocal fry among young American women has attracted extensive media commentary and some criticism, with studies finding that heavy use can be perceived negatively in professional contexts, even as usage spreads as a marker of casual, in-group speech. Speech-language researchers generally caution that habitual fry at comfortable pitch is not necessarily harmful to the voice, though fry produced with strain can accompany vocal fatigue.
