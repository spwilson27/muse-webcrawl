Hugh Esmor Huxley (25 February 1924 – 25 July 2013) was a British molecular biologist who made important discoveries in the physiology of muscle. Together with Jean Hanson, who worked independently, he proposed the sliding filament theory of muscle contraction in 1954 — a discovery paralleled by Andrew Huxley (no close relation) and Rolf Niedergerke — and he later pioneered the structural analysis of how myosin cross-bridges interact with actin filaments.

Life and career

Huxley studied physics at Christ's College, Cambridge, before his studies were interrupted by war service in the RAF working on radar. He returned to Cambridge and completed his PhD in the new field of molecular biology at the Medical Research Council unit, studying muscle structure with X-ray diffraction. He spent time at MIT in the early 1950s, where with Jean Hanson he used phase-contrast and electron microscopy on rabbit muscle.

From 1962 he worked at King's College London and then as a professor at University College London, before joining the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge in 1967 as deputy director, where he remained until his formal retirement in 1987. He then moved to Brandeis University in Massachusetts as professor of biology, continuing research into his late eighties. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1960 and won the Copley Medal in 1997.

The sliding filament theory

Before 1954 it was widely assumed that muscle shortened because its protein filaments themselves contracted, like elastic bands. Huxley and Hanson's microscopical observations showed instead that during contraction the A-bands of the sarcomere stay the same length while the I-bands shorten. They concluded that thin actin filaments slide between thick myosin filaments, pulled by projections (cross-bridges) from the myosin — filaments themselves unchanged in length. Two papers in Nature in May 1954 — by Huxley and Hanson, and simultaneously by Andrew Huxley and Niedergerke — established the model that underlies all modern muscle physiology, including the roles of the sarcoplasmic reticulum in calcium release and of tropomyosin and troponin in regulation.

Later, Huxley proposed the "swinging cross-bridge" model (1969) for how myosin heads generate force, and used X-ray diffraction on living muscle at synchrotron sources to follow cross-bridge movements in real time.
