Xiphoid process

The xiphoid process (from Greek ξίφος xiphos, "sword"), also called the ensiform process or xiphisternum, is a small cartilaginous extension at the lower end of the sternum (breastbone) in humans. It typically ossifies — hardens into bone — later in adult life, often around the age of 40, though the timing varies widely.

Structure

The xiphoid process is the smallest and most variable of the three parts of the sternum, after the manubrium and the body. It may be broad and thin, pointed, bifid (split into two), curved or perforated. It lies at the level of the ninth thoracic vertebra and attaches to the body of the sternum at the xiphisternal joint. Though it holds no ribs, it serves as an attachment site for the linea alba, parts of the rectus abdominis and transverse abdominis muscles, and fibres of the diaphragm. In infants and children it is entirely cartilaginous.

Clinical significance

The xiphoid is an anatomical landmark marking the lower limit of the thoracic cavity and the upper limit of the liver. During cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), compressions must be delivered on the body of the sternum rather than the xiphoid, because pressure on the xiphoid can fracture it and drive it into the underlying liver, causing dangerous internal bleeding. Pain localised to the xiphoid (xiphoidalgia or xiphoid syndrome) can mimic cardiac or gastric pain. An unusually prominent, forward-projecting xiphoid is a normal variant but is sometimes mistaken for an abdominal mass.
