The River Vyrnwy (Welsh: Afon Efyrnwy) is a river in Powys, Wales, and Shropshire, England, a tributary of the River Severn. It is best known for Lake Vyrnwy, the reservoir created by damming its upper course in the 1880s to supply water to Liverpool — the first great masonry dam project of its kind in Britain.

Course

The Vyrnwy gathers several mountain streams around the Berwyn and Cambrian foothills of western Powys. Its upper valley was flooded between 1881 and 1889 by Liverpool Corporation to form Lake Vyrnwy (Llyn Efyrnwy), a reservoir about 8 km long retained by a cyclopean masonry dam 44 m high across the valley, designed by George Frederick Deacon and Thomas Hawksley; water first reached Liverpool in 1892 via a 77-mile aqueduct. The drowned village of Llanwddyn was rebuilt on the shore below the dam — a cause of lasting controversy in Wales, comparable to later campaigns over Tryweryn.

Below the dam the river flows east through Meifod and Llansantffraid-ym-Mechain, crosses into Shropshire, and joins the Severn near Melverley. In its lowland reach it forms part of the England–Wales border and is prone to flooding of the surrounding farmland.

Ecology and recreation

The moorland around Lake Vyrnwy is a major RSPB reserve and Site of Special Scientific Interest, managed for hen harriers, peregrines and black grouse, while the lake and river below are fished for trout and grayling. The straining tower standing in the lake is a well-known landmark.
