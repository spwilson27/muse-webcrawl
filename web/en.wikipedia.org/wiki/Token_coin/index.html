Token coin
DefinitionPrivately issued coin-like money without legal-tender status
Period of greatest use17th–19th centuries (Britain)

In numismatics, token coins or trade tokens are coin-like objects used instead of coins. The field of token coins is part of exonumia, and token coins are token money. Their denomination is shown on the token, but they have no legal tender status, being privately minted and generally redeemable only by the issuer. They function as money based on the faith people have in the issuer.

History

Tokens have appeared whenever official small change was scarce. In England, lead tokens circulated from the reign of Elizabeth I, but the classic era began in the mid-17th century, when a shortage of regal copper coinage during and after the Civil War led thousands of tradesmen, towns, and innkeepers to issue their own farthing and halfpenny tokens between about 1648 and 1672, when Charles II's regal copper coinage and a proclamation suppressed them.

The pattern repeated during the Industrial Revolution. From 1787, the Anglesey mines of Thomas Williams issued the famous "Druid" penny tokens, beginning the great series of provincial tokens (the "Conder tokens") struck by industrialists, merchants, and banks until official regal copper coinage resumed in 1797 and tokens were banned in 1817. Silver tokens were issued during the Napoleonic-era shortage of 1811–1812.

Other uses

Tokens were also produced for specific purposes: transport tokens for buses and subways (such as the New York City subway token), telephone tokens, arcade and casino tokens, pub and store tokens, and the "hard-times tokens" of the United States in the 1830s–40s and Civil War tokens of 1861–64. In company towns, employers paid wages in tokens ("truck" or scrip) redeemable only at the company store, a practice restricted by Truck Acts in Britain.
