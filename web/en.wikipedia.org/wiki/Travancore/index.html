Travancore (Malayalam: Tiruvitāṅkūr) was an Indian kingdom and later a princely state of British India, occupying much of the present-day Indian state of Kerala and the southernmost part of Tamil Nadu. Emerging from the small Venad kingdom, it was unified and greatly expanded by Maharaja Marthanda Varma in the eighteenth century.

History

Marthanda Varma (reigned 1729–1758) defeated the Dutch East India Company at the Battle of Colachel in 1741 — a landmark defeat of a European power by an Asian ruler — and annexed neighbouring chiefdoms. In 1750 he dedicated the kingdom to the deity Padmanabha (Vishnu), whose temple in Thiruvananthapuram remains emblematic of the state; subsequent rulers styled themselves servants of the god. Travancore entered subsidiary alliance with the British and was ruled in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries by reforming maharajas including Swathi Thirunal, a noted patron and composer of Carnatic music, and Chithira Thirunal Balarama Varma.

Society and legacy

Travancore was noted for comparatively high literacy and early advances in public education and health, alongside a rigid caste order that reform movements contested; the Temple Entry Proclamation of 1936 opened Hindu temples in the state to all castes. After Indian independence, Travancore merged with Cochin in 1949 to form Travancore-Cochin, which became part of Kerala in 1956.
