Scramjet

Schematic of a scramjet: air remains supersonic through the combustor

A scramjet (supersonic combustion ramjet) is a variant of a ramjet air-breathing jet engine in which combustion takes place in supersonic airflow. Like a ramjet, it relies on high vehicle speed to compress incoming air before combustion, rather than using a rotating compressor — but unlike a ramjet, it does not slow the airflow to subsonic speeds. This allows a scramjet to operate efficiently at extremely high speeds, theoretically up to about Mach 12–24, although demonstrated flight remains below Mach 10.

Principles

Air entering the scramjet intake is compressed by shock waves generated by the vehicle's forebody and the intake geometry. Fuel — typically hydrogen or a hydrocarbon — is injected into the still-supersonic stream, where it must mix and burn within milliseconds. Keeping the flow supersonic avoids the extreme temperatures and dissociation losses that occur when hypersonic air is slowed to subsonic speeds, but makes ignition and flame-holding ("like lighting a match in a hurricane") the central engineering challenge. Because a scramjet has no thrust at rest, the vehicle must first be accelerated by another means, usually a rocket or a combined-cycle engine.

History

The concept was developed from the 1950s onward in the United States, Britain, France, and the Soviet Union. Key milestones include the University of Queensland's HyShot experiments (2002), NASA's hydrogen-fuelled X-43A, which reached Mach 6.8 and then Mach 9.6 in 2004 — the fastest air-breathing flights ever achieved — and the hydrocarbon-fuelled Boeing X-51 Waverider, which flew at Mach 5.1 for over three minutes in 2013. India (HSTDV), China, Russia, and Australia have also tested scramjet demonstrators.

Applications

Proposed applications include hypersonic cruise missiles — scramjet-powered weapons such as the Russian Zircon and the American HAWC/Air-Launched Rapid Response concepts have driven intense development — and reusable space launch vehicles, where air-breathing propulsion could reduce the oxidiser that must be carried. Practical obstacles include thermal management, materials, guidance at hypersonic speed, and the narrow flight corridor in which scramjets work.
