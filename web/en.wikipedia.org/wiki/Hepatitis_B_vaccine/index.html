Hepatitis B vaccine

The hepatitis B vaccine is a vaccine that prevents infection with the hepatitis B virus (HBV). It was the first vaccine developed by recombinant DNA technology and is regarded as the first "anti-cancer vaccine", because preventing chronic HBV infection prevents hepatocellular carcinoma. The World Health Organization recommends a dose within 24 hours of birth, followed by two or three further doses.

History

The vaccine was developed from the work of Baruch Blumberg, who discovered the Australia antigen (hepatitis B surface antigen, HBsAg), work for which he shared the 1976 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The first vaccine, licensed in 1981, was prepared from HBsAg purified from the plasma of chronic carriers. Recombinant vaccines produced in yeast replaced the plasma-derived product in the late 1980s.

Medical uses

The vaccine is given by injection into muscle, typically in a series of two to four doses. It is recommended for all infants, for healthcare workers, and for people at increased risk of exposure, including those with diabetes, chronic liver disease, or household contact with carriers. Protection develops in more than 95% of healthy recipients after the primary series.

Effectiveness and safety

The vaccine is on the WHO List of Essential Medicines. Side effects are usually mild and include soreness at the injection site and low-grade fever. Countries with universal infant immunisation have seen dramatic declines in childhood HBV carriage and liver cancer incidence.
