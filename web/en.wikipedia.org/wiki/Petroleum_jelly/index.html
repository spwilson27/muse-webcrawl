Petroleum jelly

Petroleum jelly, petrolatum, white petrolatum, soft paraffin, or multi-hydrocarbon (CAS number 8009-03-8) is a semi-solid mixture of hydrocarbons, originally promoted as a topical ointment for its healing properties. It was patented and marketed by Robert Chesebrough in the 1870s under the name Vaseline, which has become a genericized trademark in many countries. Petroleum jelly is widely used as a skin protectant, moisturizer, and lubricant, and in countless household and industrial applications.

History

In 1859, oil workers in Titusville, Pennsylvania noticed a waxy residue ("rod wax") clogging their rig pumps, which they used on cuts and burns. Chemist Robert Augustus Chesebrough refined the residue by vacuum distillation and filtration through bone char, patented the process in 1872, and trademarked the name Vaseline — said to combine the German Wasser (water) and Greek elaion (olive oil). Chesebrough famously promoted the product by burning his own skin and treating the wounds in public demonstrations.[1]

Properties and uses

Petrolatum is hydrophobic and occlusive: spread on skin it forms a barrier that reduces transepidermal water loss, which is why dermatologists recommend it for wound care, eczema, and chapped skin. Well-refined white petrolatum is regarded as non-comedogenic and is an FDA-recognized skin protectant. Beyond medicine it serves as a lubricant, rust preventive, leather conditioner, and in cosmetics, candle-making, and the protection of electrical terminals. Because it is a by-product of petroleum refining, crude or poorly refined grades can contain polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, so cosmetic- and pharmaceutical-grade material is highly purified.
