Continental slope

The continental slope is the slope between the outer edge of the continental shelf and the deep ocean floor. It marks the seaward boundary of the continent proper: beyond it lie the continental rise and the abyssal plain. The slope begins at the shelf break, typically at a depth of about 100–200 metres, and descends to depths of roughly 3,000 metres or more.

Characteristics

  • Gradient: averaging about 4 degrees — much steeper than the shelf (about 0.1 degrees) but gentle compared with mountain slopes on land.
  • Width: on average about 20 km, ranging from a few kilometres to over 100 km.
  • Relief: the slope is incised by submarine canyons, some rivalling the Grand Canyon in scale, cut by turbidity currents and mass wasting.
  • Sediment: fine muds predominate, transported from the shelf and reworked by slumps, slides and contour currents; at its base, accumulated sediment forms the gentler continental rise.

Formation

The slope's position reflects the edge of continental crust and the long-term balance between sediment deposition, faulting and erosion. Along passive margins it is built outward by sediment; along active margins it may coincide with a subduction trench and be much steeper and narrower.

Legal significance

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, the continental slope region matters to coastal states' claims to an extended continental shelf: Article 76 uses the "foot of the continental slope" as a reference line from which the outer limits of the shelf may be measured.

Ecology

The slope supports distinctive benthic communities and deep-water fisheries, cold-water corals and methane seep ecosystems, and is a zone of active study for deep-sea mining and carbon storage.
