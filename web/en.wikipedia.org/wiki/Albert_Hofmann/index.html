Albert Hofmann (11 January 1906 – 29 April 2008) was a Swiss chemist known best for being the first known person to synthesize, ingest, and learn of the psychedelic effects of lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD). Hofmann's team also isolated, named and synthesized the principal compounds of psilocybin mushrooms. He authored more than 100 scientific articles and numerous books, including LSD: My Problem Child.

Early life and career

Hofmann was born in Baden, Switzerland, and studied chemistry at the University of Zürich, completing his doctorate in 1929 on the chemical structure of chitin. In 1929 he joined Sandoz Laboratories in Basel, where he worked on the medicinal chemistry of plants and fungi, including the ergot alkaloids — research that led him first to synthesize LSD-25 in 1938 while investigating circulatory stimulants derived from lysergic acid.

Discovery of LSD

The compound was shelved for five years. On 16 April 1943, Hofmann accidentally absorbed a small quantity through his fingertips and experienced unusual perceptual effects. Three days later, on 19 April 1943 — a date celebrated as "Bicycle Day" — he deliberately ingested 250 micrograms and rode home by bicycle while experiencing the first intentional LSD trip. Sandoz marketed the drug as Delysid for psychiatric research from 1947. Hofmann took the substance seriously as a tool for psychiatry and mysticism, and was disappointed by its prohibition in the late 1960s; in later life he advocated renewed scientific research into psychedelics.

Later work

Hofmann isolated psilocybin and psilocin from Psilocybe mexicana in 1958, identified the active principles of morning glory seeds (ergine), and helped synthesise important pharmaceuticals including methylergometrine. A lifelong lover of nature, he wrote about the spiritual dimensions of the natural world in books such as Insight/Outlook. In 2007 he was ranked first in a Daily Telegraph list of the 100 greatest living geniuses. He died of a heart attack at his home in Switzerland at the age of 102.
