Xuanxue

Xuanxue (玄學, literally "dark learning" or "mysterious learning"), sometimes called Neo-Daoism in Western scholarship, was a school of Chinese philosophy that flourished during the Wei and Jin dynasties (3rd–4th centuries CE), a period of political fragmentation after the fall of the Han dynasty. Xuanxue scholars reinterpreted the classics of Daoism — the Daodejing, Zhuangzi, and the I Ching (the "Three Mysteries", sanxuan) — alongside Confucian texts, developing sophisticated metaphysics centred on the concepts of non-being (wu) and being (you).

Main currents

He Yan and Wang Bi (226–249) founded the movement, arguing that the sage Confucius — who, they claimed, embodied non-being without speaking of it — surpassed Laozi. Wang Bi's commentaries on the Daodejing and I Ching became canonical. A second current, represented by the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove and especially Xiang Xiu and Guo Xiang, stressed ziran (things being "so of themselves") and produced the definitive commentary on the Zhuangzi. Pei Wei later championed the value of being (you) against what he saw as the movement's neglect of practical affairs.

The associated practice of qingtan ("pure conversation") — witty, metaphysical salon debate — became a hallmark of aristocratic culture, recorded in the anecdote collection A New Account of the Tales of the World (Shishuo Xinyu). Later critics blamed Xuanxue's otherworldliness for the political collapse of the Western Jin, while modern scholars see it as one of the most original moments in Chinese metaphysics and a bridge by which Buddhist ideas were first interpreted in China.
