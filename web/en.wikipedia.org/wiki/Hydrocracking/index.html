Hydrocracking

Hydrocracking is a catalytic petroleum refining process in which heavy, long-chain hydrocarbon feedstocks — typically vacuum gas oil and other heavy fractions — are broken into lighter, more valuable products such as diesel, jet fuel, kerosene, and liquefied petroleum gas in the presence of hydrogen at high pressure and elevated temperature. Unlike fluid catalytic cracking, hydrocracking saturates aromatic and olefinic compounds as it cracks them and simultaneously removes sulfur, nitrogen, and oxygen impurities, so its products are exceptionally clean and stable.

Process

In a typical two-stage hydrocracker, pre-treated feed is mixed with hydrogen and passed over a bifunctional catalyst that combines a hydrogenation component (metals such as nickel, molybdenum, tungsten, cobalt, or noble metals) with an acidic cracking component (amorphous silica-alumina or zeolite Y). Conditions are severe — roughly 260–480 °C and 35–200 bar of hydrogen pressure. The first stage emphasizes hydrotreating (desulfurization and denitrogenation), and the second stage performs most of the cracking. Unconverted oil from the fractionator may be recycled to extinction or drawn off as high-quality lubricant base stock.

Products and uses

Hydrocracking is prized for its flexibility: by adjusting catalyst and severity, a refinery can swing output between naphtha (for gasoline or petrochemicals) and middle distillates. Hydrocracked diesel has a high cetane number and very low sulfur, meeting ultra-low-sulfur fuel specifications, and hydrocracked jet fuel has excellent low-temperature and smoke-point properties. The process also converts low-value heavy residues and even some renewable feeds (vegetable oils, in co-processing) into premium fuels. Its main drawbacks are high capital cost and large hydrogen consumption, usually supplied by steam reforming of natural gas.

History

Hydrocracking descends from the Bergius coal-hydrogenation process developed in Germany in the early 20th century and from I.G. Farben's work on destructive hydrogenation. Modern petroleum hydrocracking was commercialized in the late 1950s and 1960s, notably by Chevron (Isocracking) and UOP, and spread worldwide as demand shifted toward diesel and jet fuel and as environmental rules tightened limits on sulfur and aromatics.
