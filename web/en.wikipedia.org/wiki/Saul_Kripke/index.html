Saul Aaron Kripke (November 13, 1940 – September 15, 2022) was an American philosopher and logician, widely regarded as one of the most influential philosophers of the second half of the 20th century. He made fundamental contributions to modal logic, the philosophy of language and metaphysics while still a teenager and young scholar.

Early work in logic

A child prodigy, Kripke published his completeness theorem for modal logic at 17 and while an undergraduate at Harvard developed the possible-worlds semantics — now called Kripke semantics — that gave modal and intuitionistic logic a rigorous model theory. He also contributed to recursion theory and set theory.

Naming and Necessity

His 1970 Princeton lectures, published as Naming and Necessity (1972/1980), transformed philosophy of language and metaphysics. Kripke argued that proper names are rigid designators — they refer to the same object in every possible world — and that their reference is fixed by a causal-historical chain rather than by descriptions. He defended the existence of necessary truths known a posteriori, such as "water is H2O", and necessary a priori contingencies, reshaping debates about identity and the mind–body problem.

Later work

Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language (1982) presented the influential "Kripkenstein" sceptical paradox about meaning. He taught at Rockefeller University and Princeton, and from 2003 at the CUNY Graduate Center, where the Saul Kripke Center preserves his unpublished manuscripts and lectures.
