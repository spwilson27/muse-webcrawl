Fujiwara no Michinaga (藤原 道長, 966 – 3 January 1028) was a Japanese statesman of the Heian period. The fourth or fifth son of Fujiwara no Kaneie, he rose to become the most powerful figure at the imperial court, and his career represents the peak of the Fujiwara clan's domination of Japanese politics through the regency system and marriage ties to the imperial family.

Life and career

Michinaga was born in Kyoto in 966. His father Kaneie served as regent (sesshō and kampaku), and after Kaneie's death in 990 a succession struggle followed among his sons. Michinaga's elder brothers Michitaka and Michikane each briefly held the regency, but both died young, and by 995 Michinaga had emerged as head of the Fujiwara clan and dominant figure at court.

Although he never formally took the titles of sesshō or kampaku for most of his career, Michinaga exercised power as the emperor's father-in-law and grandfather. He married his daughters to emperors and imperial princes: his daughter Shōshi became consort to Emperor Ichijō and mother of Emperor Go-Ichijō, and other daughters were married to Emperor Sanjō and to crown princes. Through this strategy, three of his daughters became empresses and he was grandfather to two emperors.

The height of power

A famous anecdote records that at a banquet Michinaga composed the poem "Kono yo wo ba / waga yo to zo omou / mochizuki no / kaketaru koto mo / nashi to omoeba" ("This world, I think, is indeed my world; like the full moon, I lack nothing"), expressing his confidence that nothing in the world was beyond his grasp. The poem is preserved in his diary, the Mido Kampaku ki, one of the earliest surviving diaries by a Japanese courtier.

Michinaga sponsored great building projects, most notably the Hōjō-ji temple in Kyoto, whose golden hall and pagoda symbolised Fujiwara splendour. He was also a patron of literature; the court of his daughter Shōshi included Murasaki Shikibu, author of The Tale of Genji, and the rival court of Empress Teishi included Sei Shōnagon, author of The Pillow Book.

Later life and death

In 1016 Michinaga's son Yorimichi became regent for the young Emperor Go-Ichijō. Michinaga took Buddhist vows in 1019, though he continued to exert influence from retirement. He died on 3 January 1028. His descendants continued to hold the regencies for much of the eleventh century.

Legacy

Michinaga is generally regarded as the archetype of the Fujiwara regent and one of the most powerful non-imperial figures in Japanese history. His diary and the court literature produced under his patronage remain primary sources for the culture of the mid-Heian court.
