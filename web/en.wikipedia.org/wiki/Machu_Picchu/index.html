Machu Picchu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Machu Picchu
Location: Cusco Region, Peru — 2,430 m in the Andes
Built: c. 1450, under the Inca emperor Pachacuti
Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site (1983)
[Image: Machu Picchu, the Inca citadel, beneath Huayna Picchu at dawn]

Machu Picchu, the Inca citadel, beneath Huayna Picchu at dawn

Machu Picchu is a fifteenth-century Inca citadel in the Eastern Cordillera of southern Peru, on a mountain ridge 2,430 metres above sea level above the Urubamba River. It is the most familiar icon of the Inca Empire and one of the New Seven Wonders of the World (chosen in a 2007 global poll).

Built around 1450, most likely as a royal estate of the emperor Pachacuti, Machu Picchu unites more than 200 structures — temples (of the Sun, of the Three Windows), the Intihuatana ritual stone, terraced fields, fountains and storehouses — in the Inca's mortarless ashlar masonry, fitted to the ridge as if grown from it. It was never found by the Spanish and so never looted; abandoned within a century, it remained known locally while the outside world "discovered" it through Hiram Bingham's expeditions from 1911, sponsored by Yale and the National Geographic Society.

Tourism pressure — thousands of visitors a day in peak season — has forced Peru into timed tickets, circuits and caps to protect the stone. Reaching it by the Inca Trail, ending at the Sun Gate at dawn, remains one of the world's defining journeys; the artefacts Bingham took to Yale were returned to Peru in 2011–12 after a long dispute.
