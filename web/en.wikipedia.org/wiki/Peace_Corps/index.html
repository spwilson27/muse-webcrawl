The Peace Corps is an independent agency of the United States government that sends American volunteers abroad to work with host communities on locally driven development projects in education, health, agriculture, environment, youth development and community economic development. Created at the height of the Cold War as an emblem of American idealism, it has sent more than 240,000 volunteers to over 140 countries since 1961.

Founding

The idea drew on earlier proposals by Senator Hubert Humphrey and Congressman Henry Reuss, and on President John F. Kennedy's impromptu 2 a.m. speech at the University of Michigan in October 1960, when the candidate challenged students to serve their country in the cause of peace by living and working in developing nations. Kennedy established the Peace Corps by executive order on 1 March 1961; Congress made it permanent with the Peace Corps Act that September. His brother-in-law Sargent Shriver, the first director, built the agency at breakneck speed: the first volunteers — teachers for Ghana and Tanganyika — arrived in the field in August 1961.

Mission and goals

The agency's mission is "to promote world peace and friendship" through three statutory goals: (1) helping the people of interested countries meet their need for trained men and women; (2) promoting a better understanding of Americans on the part of the peoples served; and (3) promoting a better understanding of other peoples on the part of Americans. Volunteers serve 27 months — about three months of language and technical training followed by two years living at the level of their host communities, receiving a modest living allowance and a readjustment payment on completion.

History since 1961

Numbers peaked in 1966 at about 15,500 volunteers. The agency survived budget cuts and periodic accusations of naïveté or of serving US foreign-policy ends; it was made fully independent in 1981. Programmes expanded to Eastern Europe after the Cold War and to China (as "US-China Friendship Volunteers") in 1993. The September 11 attacks prompted renewed interest in service, and the agency's health and safety practices were repeatedly reformed, notably after the 2011 publicity over its response to sexual assaults on volunteers, leading to the Kate Puzey Volunteer Protection Act. In March 2020, for the first time in its history, the Peace Corps evacuated all ~7,000 volunteers worldwide because of the COVID-19 pandemic; volunteers began returning to posts in 2022.

Criticism and assessment

Assessments credit the Corps with durable goodwill toward the United States, deep language and area expertise among returned volunteers (many of whom enter diplomacy, development and academia), and modest but real local benefits, while critics question the development impact of short-term, often unskilled placements and the costs per volunteer. Its cultural footprint is large: the Peace Corps experience helped define a generation's ideal of public service and remains a touchstone of American "soft power".
