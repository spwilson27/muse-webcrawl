The violin is a wooden string instrument in the violin family, the smallest and thus highest-pitched member in regular use. It has four strings tuned in perfect fifths (G, D, A, E) and is typically played by drawing a bow across the strings or by plucking them (pizzicato). The violin is central to Western classical music and appears in many other genres, from folk and country (where it is often called a fiddle) to jazz and rock.

A violin with its bow; the design has changed little since the 16th century.

History

The violin emerged in northern Italy in the early 16th century, likely in Brescia and Cremona, adapting features of the rebec, fiddle, and lira da braccio. The workshops of Andrea Amati, the Guarneri family, and Antonio Stradivari in Cremona defined its form; Stradivari and Guarneri del Gesù instruments of the late 17th and early 18th centuries remain the most prized ever made, fetching tens of millions of dollars. In the 19th century, the stronger Tourte-model bow and modifications for greater volume adapted the instrument to larger concert halls.

Construction and acoustics

A violin consists of a hollow body of spruce (top) and maple (back, ribs, neck) with f-shaped sound holes, a bridge transmitting string vibration to the body, an internal sound post and bass bar, and a fingerboard of ebony. String vibration is radiated chiefly by the body's resonant modes; Helmholtz motion of the bowed string produces its characteristic rich spectrum.

Performance and repertoire

Violinists form the largest section of the modern orchestra, divided into first and second violins. The solo repertoire includes landmarks such as Bach's Sonatas and Partitas, Paganini's 24 Caprices—Paganini's virtuosity reshaped technique—and the concertos of Beethoven, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, and Sibelius. Famous violinists include Jascha Heifetz, Yehudi Menuhin, Itzhak Perlman, Anne-Sophie Mutter, and Hilary Hahn.
