The IBM Personal Computer (model 5150, commonly known as the IBM PC) is the first microcomputer released in the IBM PC model line and the basis for the IBM PC compatible de facto standard. Released on August 12, 1981, it was created by a team of engineers under Don Estridge at IBM's Entry Systems Division in Boca Raton, Florida. Its open architecture, use of off-the-shelf components, and rapid adoption by business made it the dominant personal computer platform of the 1980s and gave the generic term "PC" its modern meaning.

Development

IBM, then dominant in mainframes, was late to the microcomputer market created by the Altair 8800, Apple II, and TRS-80. Given an unusually free hand and a one-year deadline, the Boca Raton team adopted an open design: an Intel 8088 processor, a published bus with expansion slots, off-the-shelf parts from outside suppliers, and technical reference manuals published for third-party developers. Needing an operating system quickly, IBM licensed 86-DOS from the small company Microsoft, releasing it as PC DOS; Microsoft retained the right to sell its own version, MS-DOS, to other manufacturers — a decision with enormous long-term consequences.

Hardware

The original PC used an Intel 8088 running at 4.77 MHz, with 16 KB of RAM in the base model (expandable to 640 KB on later system boards and cards), one or two 5.25-inch floppy drives, and five 8-bit ISA expansion slots. It could be used with a television or with IBM's monochrome (MDA) or color (CGA) monitors. The keyboard — the famous Model F — was widely praised for its feel. Cassette storage was supported but rarely used. A hard disk arrived with the IBM PC XT (1983), and the PC AT (1984) introduced the 16-bit Intel 80286 and the bus later called ISA.

Impact and clones

The PC was an immediate commercial success, legitimizing personal computing for large corporations and spawning a vast ecosystem of software — VisiCalc, then Lotus 1-2-3 (a "killer app" that drove hardware sales), dBASE, and WordPerfect. Because the design was open and its BIOS could be legally reverse-engineered by clean-room methods (pioneered by Compaq in 1982–83), competitors produced IBM PC compatibles; by the late 1980s these clones, running MS-DOS, dominated the market and IBM progressively lost control of the standard it had created. Its 1987 PS/2 line attempted to reassert control with the proprietary Micro Channel bus but failed to displace the open standard. IBM sold its personal computer division to Lenovo in 2005.

Legacy

The IBM PC's architecture — x86 processors, expansion buses evolving into PCI and PCI Express, and BIOS/UEFI firmware — underlies the vast majority of desktop and laptop computers sold since. The term "PC" shifted from a product name to the name of the whole compatible platform, and the machine's design decisions (including the 640 KB conventional memory limit) shaped software development for more than a decade.
