Twilight Zone accident

Image placeholder: Twilight Zone accident

The Twilight Zone accident was a fatal accident that occurred during the filming of Twilight Zone: The Movie at Indian Dunes, near Santa Clarita, California, in the early hours of 23 July 1982. A Bell UH-1 Iroquois helicopter crashed after being damaged by pyrotechnic explosions, killing actor Vic Morrow and two child actors, Myca Dinh Le (aged 7) and Renee Shin-Yi Chen (aged 6), who were on the ground.

The accident

The scene, directed by John Landis, depicted Morrow's character carrying two children across a river in Vietnam while fleeing a pursuing helicopter. During a take shortly after 2:20 a.m., mortar-effect pyrotechnics detonated close beneath the low-flying helicopter, delaminating its tail rotor. The aircraft spun out of control and crashed into the river where Morrow and the children were standing, striking all three. The six people aboard the helicopter were injured but survived.

Aftermath

Investigations revealed that the children's presence on the set violated California child-labour laws: they had been hired illegally, paid under the table and were working at night without permits, in a scene contrary to regulations on explosives near children. Landis, associate producer George Folsey Jr., production manager Dan Allingham, special effects coordinator Paul Stewart and helicopter pilot Dorcey Wingo were charged with involuntary manslaughter and child endangerment. After a lengthy trial in 1986–87, all were acquitted. Civil lawsuits by the families were settled out of court.

Impact

The accident remains one of the most notorious in film history. It led the studios and guilds to tighten safety enforcement, expand the role of safety officers, formalise bulletins on sets, and reduce the use of live explosives and low-flying aircraft near performers. The footage of the crash was impounded and has never been officially released; the film was completed and released in 1983 without the scene. The tragedy also permanently affected the reputations of those involved and is frequently cited in discussions of directors' responsibility for set safety.
