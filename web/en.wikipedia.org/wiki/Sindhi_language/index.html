Sindhi (سنڌي) is an Indo-Aryan language spoken by the Sindhi people, principally in the Pakistani province of Sindh and in parts of India, where large Sindhi Hindu communities settled after the Partition of 1947. It has roughly 30 million speakers. Sindhi is notable among Indo-Aryan languages for its large consonant inventory, which includes a full series of implosive consonants in addition to the usual aspirated and unaspirated stops.

History

Sindhi developed in the lower Indus valley from local Prakrits, with early literature in verse traditions associated with Sufi and Bhakti poets. The language's golden age of poetry came in the eighteenth century with Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, whose Shah Jo Risalo remains its most beloved work. Under British rule in the nineteenth century, a standard written form was fixed, based on the dialect of Hyderabad (Sindh).

Writing systems

In Pakistan, Sindhi is written in an extended Perso-Arabic script adopted under British administration in 1853; the alphabet has 52 letters, with special characters for the implosive consonants. In India, Sindhi is written both in this script and in Devanagari, and historically it was also written in merchant scripts such as Khudabadi and other Landa varieties by Hindu traders. Sindhi is an official language of Sindh province, used in provincial government and education, and is one of the 22 scheduled languages of India, where it is unusual in having no home state.

Dialects and features

Major dialects include Vicholi (the central, standard variety), Lari, Lasi, Thari, Kachhi (toward Kutch) and Siroli. Sindhi phonology includes four implosives (ɓ, ɗ, ʄ, ɠ), retroflex consonants, and nasalised vowels. Its vocabulary draws on Sanskrit-derived stock with substantial borrowings from Arabic and Persian, reflecting the region's history.
