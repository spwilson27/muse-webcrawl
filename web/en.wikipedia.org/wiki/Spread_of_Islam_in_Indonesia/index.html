Spread of Islam in Indonesia

The spread of Islam in Indonesia began in the western parts of the Indonesian archipelago in the late 13th century and proceeded gradually over several centuries, largely through trade, until it became the dominant religion in Java and Sumatra by the end of the 16th century. Today Indonesia is the world's most populous Muslim-majority country, with roughly 87 percent of its population identifying as Muslim, most of them Sunni.

Early history

Evidence of Muslim traders in the archipelago dates to the 8th and 9th centuries, when Arab and Persian merchants traveling between the Middle East and China stopped at ports in Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. The tombstone of Fatimah bint Maimun in Leran, East Java, dated to 1082 CE, is among the earliest Islamic inscriptions found in Java, though it likely marks the grave of a foreign Muslim rather than evidence of a local Muslim community.

The first indigenous Muslim kingdom in the archipelago was the Samudera Pasai Sultanate in northern Sumatra, established in the late 13th century. The traveler Marco Polo, passing through Perlak in 1292, noted that its inhabitants had converted to Islam through contact with Saracen merchants. Ibn Battuta, who visited Samudera Pasai in 1345–46, described a functioning Shafi'i sultanate.

Trade networks

Islam spread principally along maritime trade routes. Muslim merchants from Gujarat, Bengal, the Coromandel Coast, Arabia, Persia, and later China carried both goods and religion to the busy port cities of the archipelago, including Malacca, Aceh, Demak, Gresik, and Makassar. Conversion was often pragmatic as well as spiritual: local rulers who embraced Islam gained access to a wider commercial network and to alliances with Muslim trading partners, while the faith's emphasis on literacy and its legal framework appealed to port merchants.

The Wali Sanga

In Javanese tradition, the spread of Islam on Java is credited to the Wali Sanga, the "Nine Saints," a group of revered missionaries active in the 15th and 16th centuries. Figures such as Sunan Kalijaga are remembered for blending Islamic teaching with local culture, using wayang shadow puppetry and gamelan music as vehicles for religious instruction. Their tombs remain important pilgrimage sites.

Sultanates and consolidation

From the 16th century onward, powerful sultanates — including Aceh, Demak, Banten, Mataram, Ternate, and Tidore — consolidated Islam across the archipelago. Aceh became a major center of Islamic scholarship in Southeast Asia, sometimes called the "veranda of Mecca." Islam's expansion continued into eastern Indonesia, reaching Sulawesi and parts of the Malukus, though Bali remained predominantly Hindu.

Colonial period and modern era

During Dutch colonial rule, Islam became a focus of Indonesian identity and anti-colonial organization; groups such as Sarekat Islam (founded 1912) were among the first mass nationalist movements. In the 20th century, the reformist Muhammadiyah (1912) and the traditionalist Nahdlatul Ulama (1926) emerged as the two largest Muslim organizations, shaping the distinctly Indonesian character of the religion, often described as tolerant and syncretic.
