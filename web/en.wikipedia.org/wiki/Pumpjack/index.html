Pumpjack


A pumpjack in operation in Texas.

A pumpjack is the overground drive for a reciprocating piston pump in an oil well. It is used to mechanically lift liquid out of the well if there is not enough bottom hole pressure for the liquid to flow all the way to the surface. The arrangement is often used for onshore wells. Pumpjacks are common in oil-rich areas in the United States and Canada and are also known as a nodding donkey, pumping unit, horsehead pump, beam pump, or sucker rod pump.

Mechanism

A prime mover—usually an electric motor or gas engine—drives a gearbox that rotates a crank and counterweights. The crank moves a walking beam pivoted on a central bearing (the Samson post); one end of the beam carries the curved "horse head," which via steel cables (the bridle) raises and lowers a polished rod connected to a long string of sucker rods running down the well. The downhole pump at the bottom of the string has valves that lift oil toward the surface on each stroke. The horse head's curved shape keeps the bridle's pull vertical so the polished rod is not bent.

Use

Pumpjacks typically produce between roughly 5 and 40 litres of liquid per stroke, with each stroke taking a few seconds. Well production can range from a few barrels to several hundred barrels of fluid per day, usually with a high fraction of water in mature fields. Pumpjacks are iconic symbols of oil-producing regions such as West Texas, Oklahoma, and Alberta.
