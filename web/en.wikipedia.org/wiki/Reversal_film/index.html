Reversal film

In photography, reversal film or slide film is a type of photographic film that produces a positive image on a transparent base. The developed film is cut and mounted into individual slides, which are typically projected onto a screen using a slide projector. Reversal film is also used in motion picture production and was historically the standard medium for color news photography and magazine reproduction.

Processing

Unlike negative film, reversal film is processed so that the image formed is positive rather than negative. In the widely used E-6 process, the film is first developed in a black-and-white developer, then exposed to light (or a chemical fogging agent), then developed in a color developer that forms dyes in the previously unexposed silver halide, after which the silver is bleached away, leaving a positive dye image. Famous reversal film brands included Kodachrome (1935–2010, processed in the complex K-14 process), Ektachrome, Fujichrome Velvia and Provia, and Agfachrome.

Characteristics

Reversal films were prized for their vivid color saturation, fine grain, and sharpness, and were the preferred medium of professional photographers whose work was destined for print reproduction, since editors could view the exact final image on a light table. They have a narrower exposure latitude than negative film, so exposure must be precise. Although digital photography displaced most slide film use after the 2000s, reversal films continue to be manufactured in smaller quantities for enthusiasts and artists; Ektachrome was reintroduced by Kodak in 2018.
