Great Seal of the Realm

Obverse and reverse of a Great Seal of the United Kingdom.

The Great Seal of the Realm is a seal that is used to symbolise the sovereign's approval of important state documents in the United Kingdom. A new seal is struck for each monarch, and the old seal is ceremonially defaced. The seal is kept by the Lord High Chancellor, who is formally Keeper of the Great Seal, and is affixed to documents such as royal proclamations, writs, letters patent, treaties and the commissions appointing senior officials and judges.

Design and use

The modern Great Seal is double-sided, typically about 6 inches (150 mm) in diameter and cast in silver. The obverse shows the sovereign enthroned in coronation robes; the reverse shows the sovereign on horseback. It is impressed into a wax or wafer pendant attached to the document by cords or ribbons. Scotland has its own Great Seal, used since the Union and kept by the Keeper of the Great Seal of Scotland — an office held ex officio by the First Minister — while Wales acquired a separate seal in 2011.

History

Edward the Confessor is credited with introducing a great seal for England in the 11th century, and its design — the king enthroned on one side and mounted on the other — was established by the reign of William the Conqueror and his sons. A celebrated incident occurred in 1642, when Charles I carried the seal with him to York, an act Parliament treated as tantamount to taking the government itself; in 1646 Parliament ordered a new seal. Thefts of the seal, such as the 1784 burglary of the Lord Chancellor's house, required new seals to be struck. Since 1877 the work of sealing has been carried out under the Crown Office, and the number of documents sealed each year runs to several hundred.
