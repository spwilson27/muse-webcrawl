The Camp David Accords were a pair of political agreements signed by Egyptian President Anwar Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin on 17 September 1978, following twelve days of secret negotiations at Camp David, the country retreat of the President of the United States in Maryland. The two framework agreements were negotiated and signed with the mediation of United States President Jimmy Carter, and were named after the location of the negotiations.

The agreements led directly to the 1979 Egypt–Israel peace treaty, the first peace treaty between Israel and any of its Arab neighbours. Begin and Sadat shared the 1978 Nobel Peace Prize for their achievement, while the accords remained controversial in the Arab world, where they were seen as a separate peace that abandoned the Palestinians.

Background

Egypt and Israel had fought four wars since 1948, most recently the Yom Kippur War of 1973, after which the Sinai Peninsula remained under Israeli occupation. In November 1977, Sadat made a dramatic visit to Jerusalem and addressed the Knesset, breaking the Arab consensus against direct negotiation with Israel. Subsequent bilateral talks stalled, however, and in the summer of 1978 President Carter invited both leaders to Camp David to break the deadlock, wagering his own prestige on intensive personal diplomacy.

The negotiations

From 5 to 17 September 1978, Carter shuttled between the Egyptian and Israeli delegations, who at times refused to meet face to face. The talks nearly collapsed several times: Begin resisted withdrawing Israeli settlements and forces from Sinai, while Sadat insisted on linking any settlement to progress for the Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza. The American team produced successive single negotiating texts, and Carter threatened to end the summit and blame the recalcitrant party. On the final day, agreement was reached on two frameworks.

The frameworks

A Framework for Peace in the Middle East

The first framework dealt with the West Bank and Gaza Strip. It called for a five-year transitional period of Palestinian self-government, during which the inhabitants of the territories would elect a self-governing authority; Israeli forces would withdraw to specified security locations; and Egypt, Israel, Jordan and the Palestinian representatives would negotiate the final status of the territories. It invoked UN Security Council Resolution 242, with its formula of land for peace, as the basis for settlement. This framework was never implemented in its intended form.

A Framework for the Conclusion of a Peace Treaty between Egypt and Israel

The second framework provided the blueprint for a peace treaty: full Israeli withdrawal from the Sinai Peninsula in stages over three years; the establishment of normal relations between Egypt and Israel, including diplomatic recognition, trade and free passage through the Suez Canal and other waterways; and demilitarised zones and international forces in Sinai. It was implemented almost exactly as written.

Consequences

The Egypt–Israel peace treaty was signed on the White House lawn on 26 March 1979. Israel completed its withdrawal from Sinai in April 1982, dismantling settlements such as Yamit. Egypt recovered its territory and has maintained the peace ever since — a "cold peace," but one that has survived the assassination of Sadat in 1981 (in part by Islamists enraged by the treaty), several Gaza wars and regional upheavals.

The Arab League condemned the accords; Egypt was suspended from the League (and its headquarters moved from Cairo to Tunis) until 1989. Within Israel, the accords were supported by the opposition Labour Party and ratified by the Knesset despite opposition from Begin's right flank, which split his party. The Palestinian autonomy provisions produced talks that eventually deadlocked, a failure often cited by critics, though the framework's concepts fed into the Oslo process of the 1990s.
