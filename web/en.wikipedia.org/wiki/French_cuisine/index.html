French cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A classic French bistro dish with sauce and seasonal vegetables]

French cuisine is the cooking traditions and practices of France, renowned for their refinement and influence on Western cooking. It was codified in the 17th–19th centuries by chefs such as La Varenne, Carême and Auguste Escoffier, whose Haute cuisine system of sauces, stocks and brigade organisation became the template for professional kitchens worldwide.

In 2010 the "gastronomic meal of the French" was inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list. Signature products include the Croissant and baguette, hundreds of Cheeses, foie gras, coq au vin, bouillabaisse and crème brûlée, and wines from Bordeaux, Burgundy and Champagne.

History

French cooking emerged from medieval court cuisine, transformed by Italian influence in the 16th century and by the restaurant culture that followed the Revolution, when unemployed chefs of the aristocracy opened public dining rooms in Paris. The 19th century saw Carême's grande cuisine and then Escoffier's streamlined classical system in Le Guide Culinaire (1903). The Michelin Guide, published since 1900, became the arbiter of excellence.

Nouvelle cuisine and after

In the 1970s nouvelle cuisine — associated with Paul Bocuse, Michel Guérard and the critics Gault and Millau — reacted against heavy classical cooking with lighter sauces, shorter cooking and artistic plating. Modern French cooking blends classical technique with global ingredients and, increasingly, vegetables and sustainability.

Regional cooking

Burgundy gives boeuf bourguignon and escargots; Provence, ratatouille and bouillabaisse; Normandy, apples, cream and Camembert; Brittany, crêpes and salted butter; Alsace, choucroute and tarte flambée. Each region guards its appellations of wine and cheese fiercely.
