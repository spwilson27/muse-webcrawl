Eye tracking is the process of measuring either the point of gaze (where one is looking) or the motion of an eye relative to the head. An eye tracker is a device for measuring eye positions and eye movement. Eye tracking has been used extensively in research on the visual system, in psychology, psycholinguistics, marketing, human–computer interaction, and clinical diagnosis.

Methods

The most widely used method is video-oculography: one or more cameras image the eye, usually illuminated by near-infrared light, and track the pupil centre together with the corneal reflection (Purkinje image) to compute the gaze direction — typically after a short calibration procedure. Older methods include electro-oculography, measuring skin potentials around the eye, and the scleral search coil, in which a contact lens bearing a coil moves in a magnetic field; the latter offers the highest precision and was long the gold standard in oculomotor research. Modern trackers sample at 60 Hz to over 1,000 Hz with accuracy around half a degree of visual angle.

Data and metrics

Raw gaze samples are parsed into fixations, saccades, and smooth pursuit. Common analyses include scanpaths, heat maps of fixation density, areas of interest, dwell time, and pupillometry — changes in pupil diameter used as an index of cognitive load or arousal.

Applications

Applications include usability testing of websites and products, reading and language research, advertising studies, gaze-controlled interfaces and assistive communication for people with severe motor disabilities, pilot and driver monitoring, and foveated rendering in virtual-reality headsets, which renders in high detail only where the user is looking.

Privacy

Because gaze data can reveal attention, cognitive state, and potentially health or identity information, it is increasingly treated as sensitive biometric data and is covered by privacy regulation such as the GDPR and Illinois BIPA.
