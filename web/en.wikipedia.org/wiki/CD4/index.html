CD4

In molecular biology, CD4 (cluster of differentiation 4) is a glycoprotein that serves as a co-receptor for the T-cell receptor (TCR). CD4 is found on the surface of immune cells such as helper T cells, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. It was discovered in the late 1970s and was originally known as leu-3 and T4 (after the OKT4 monoclonal antibody that reacted with it) before being named CD4 in 1984.

Function

CD4 is a co-receptor of the T-cell receptor (TCR) and assists the latter in communicating with an antigen-presenting cell. Using its intracellular domain, CD4 amplifies the signal generated by the TCR by recruiting the kinase Lck, which is essential for activating many molecular components of the signalling cascade of an activated T cell. CD4 also interacts directly with MHC class II molecules on the surface of the antigen-presenting cell via its extracellular domain.

Role in HIV infection

CD4 is the primary cell-surface receptor used by the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) to enter its host cells, binding the viral envelope protein gp120 together with a chemokine co-receptor such as CCR5 or CXCR4. Progressive destruction of CD4+ T cells is the hallmark of HIV disease; the CD4 count (cells per microlitre of blood) is a key clinical measure, and a count below 200 cells/µL is one criterion for an AIDS diagnosis.

Structure

Like many cell surface receptors/markers, CD4 is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, with four immunoglobulin variable-like extracellular domains (D1–D4), a transmembrane domain and a short cytoplasmic tail.
