Allende meteorite

The Allende meteorite is the largest carbonaceous chondrite ever found on Earth. It fell in the early hours of 8 February 1969 near the village of Pueblito de Allende in Chihuahua, Mexico, scattering tonnes of material over a large strewn field; about two tonnes were recovered, and samples were distributed to laboratories worldwide just months before the first Apollo Moon samples arrived.

Composition

Allende is classified as a CV3 carbonaceous chondrite. It is rich in calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs) — white, irregular objects that are among the oldest materials known to have formed in the Solar System, dated to about 4.567 billion years ago. The meteorite also contains chondrules, presolar grains, and a carbon-rich matrix with organic compounds including amino acids.

Scientific importance

Because of its abundance and primitive composition, Allende became the best-studied meteorite in history and a standard reference for the chemistry of the early Solar System. Isotopic anomalies in its inclusions provided evidence about nucleosynthesis in stars that predated the Sun and about the processes of planetary formation.
