Cold reading

Cold reading is a set of techniques used by mentalists, psychics, fortune-tellers and mediums to imply that the reader knows much more about a person than they actually do. Without prior knowledge, a skilled cold-reader can quickly obtain a great deal of information by analysing the person's body language, age, clothing, appearance and manner of speech, and by carefully phrasing questions and statements so that the subject supplies most of the details.

Techniques

Common methods include fishing — offering vague statements and watching for reactions to narrow them down; Barnum statements, general descriptions that most people accept as personally meaningful; the rainbow ruse, which credits the subject with both a trait and its opposite; and fine flattery. Readers also exploit statistical probabilities, such as common names, common causes of death and typical life events. When a statement misses, the reader reframes it so the subject searches for a connection elsewhere, a process sometimes called "pushing" the statement.

Psychology

The effectiveness of cold reading depends heavily on the subject's willingness to cooperate. People tend to remember hits, forget misses, and interpret ambiguous statements as accurate — a pattern related to the Barnum effect and subjective validation. Subjects often disclose significant information through verbal and non-verbal feedback without realising it.

Hot reading

By contrast, hot reading uses information gathered beforehand — from research, accomplices or surreptitious listening — presented as if obtained supernaturally. Skeptics such as Ian Rowland have published detailed expositions of cold reading to demonstrate that apparently paranormal readings require no special powers.
