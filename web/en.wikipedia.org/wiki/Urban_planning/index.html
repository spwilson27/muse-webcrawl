Urban planning

Urban planning (also called town planning, city planning, or regional planning in some contexts) is a technical and political process concerned with the development and design of land use and the built environment, including air, water, and the infrastructure passing into and out of urban areas, such as transportation, communications, and distribution networks. It guides the orderly growth of settlements, balances competing demands on land, and seeks to shape places that are economically viable, socially equitable, and environmentally sustainable.

History

Planned cities are ancient: the grid of Mohenjo-daro, Hippodamian grids in Greece, and Roman colonial towns all applied deliberate layouts. Renaissance ideal cities, Haussmann's renovation of Paris (1853–1870), and the response to industrial-era slums laid modern foundations. Ebenezer Howard's Garden City movement (1898) and the first town planning legislation (Britain, 1909) institutionalized the profession. Twentieth-century modernism — Le Corbusier's Radiant City and CIAM's functional zoning — produced tower blocks, expressways, and separated land uses, a legacy powerfully criticized by Jane Jacobs in The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961), which championed mixed use, density, and street-level vitality. Later movements include New Urbanism, transit-oriented development, and smart growth.

Practice and tools

Planning operates through statutory plans (comprehensive plans, local plans), development control, and instruments such as zoning ordinances, subdivision regulations, building codes, and environmental impact assessment. Contemporary practice emphasizes public participation, strategic and spatial planning at the regional scale, heritage conservation, and increasingly climate adaptation, resilience, and data-driven "smart city" methods. In the United States the profession is represented by the American Planning Association and certified by the AICP; in the UK by the Royal Town Planning Institute.

Theories

Planning theory debates the planner's role: the rational-comprehensive model, incrementalism (Lindblom), advocacy planning (Davidoff), communicative and collaborative planning (Healey, Forester), and radical planning. Recurring tensions include growth versus equity, market-led regeneration versus public control, and technocratic expertise versus democratic participation.
