Paleo-Indians

Paleo-Indians (or Paleoamericans) were the first peoples to enter and colonise the Americas during the final glacial episodes of the late Pleistocene. Migrating from Siberia across Beringia — the land bridge that joined Asia to North America during the last Ice Age — their descendants ultimately spread the length of both continents. The Paleo-Indian period, defined by distinctive fluted stone projectile points and a hunting economy that included now-extinct megafauna, conventionally ends around 8000 BCE with the transition to the Archaic period, though dates vary by region.

Migration and settlement

Genetic and archaeological evidence indicates that the ancestors of Native Americans diverged from Siberian populations more than 20,000 years ago, spent millennia in or near Beringia, and moved south as the great Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets retreated — probably both along a Pacific coastal route and through an interior ice-free corridor. Securely dated early sites include Monte Verde in Chile (c. 14,500 years ago), Page-Ladson in Florida and Paisley Caves in Oregon; claimed evidence of a much earlier human presence, such as the White Sands footprints in New Mexico (dated to roughly 21,000–23,000 years ago), continues to be debated.

Cultures

The Clovis culture (c. 13,000 years ago), named for finds near Clovis, New Mexico, produced the first widely distributed fluted points and was long treated as the earliest American culture — a view overturned by pre-Clovis sites. The later Folsom culture of the Great Plains specialised in hunting giant bison (Bison antiquus), while the diverse Plano cultures continued big-game hunting into the early Holocene. Paleo-Indian groups lived in small, mobile bands, exploiting a wide range of plants and animals in addition to mammoths, mastodons and other megafauna.

Megafauna extinctions

The disappearance of most large American mammals around the end of the Pleistocene coincides with both rapid climate change (the Younger Dryas interval) and human expansion. The relative weight of hunting — the "overkill" hypothesis associated with Paul Martin — climate and other causes remains one of archaeology's enduring debates.
