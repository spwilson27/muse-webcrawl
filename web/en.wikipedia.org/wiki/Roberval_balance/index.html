Roberval balance

InventorGilles Personne de Roberval
Year1669 (presented to the French Academy of Sciences)
TypeEqual-arm pan balance with parallelogram linkage

The Roberval balance is a weighing balance invented by the French mathematician Gilles Personne de Roberval (1602–1675) and presented to the French Academy of Sciences in 1669. Unlike a simple beam balance, its pans sit above the beam and remain level regardless of where an object is placed on them, because a parallelogram linkage keeps the pan supports vertical at all times.

Mechanism

In a Roberval balance, the central vertical column carries a horizontal beam pivoting at its center. Two vertical rods hang (or stand) from the beam's ends, and a second lower horizontal bar links the bottoms of the rods, forming a parallelogram. The pans are mounted on top of the vertical rods. As the beam tips, the parallelogram deforms but the rods — and hence the pans — stay upright and parallel to the column. Consequently the torque produced by a load depends only on its weight, not on its position on the pan, which is not true of a simple steelyard or beam balance.

The "Roberval enigma"

The device long puzzled observers because, naively, it seems a load placed anywhere on a pan should unbalance the parallelogram; contemporaries called this behaviour the "Roberval enigma." The resolution lies in the geometry of the linkage and the fact that the system has a single degree of freedom: virtual-work analysis shows equilibrium depends only on the weights, with positional terms cancelling. Some apparent paradoxes arise only in idealized frictionless models.

Use

Roberval balances became the standard shopkeeper's counter scale in France and elsewhere through the 19th and 20th centuries, typically made of cast iron or brass with loose pans, and remain common in markets and kitchens. The Roberval linkage principle also underlies many modern mechanical and platform scales, where load position on a platform must not affect the reading.
