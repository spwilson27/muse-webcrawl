Hückel's rule

In organic chemistry, Hückel's rule predicts that a planar, cyclic, conjugated molecule will have aromatic properties if it has 4n + 2 π electrons, where n is a non-negative integer (n = 0, 1, 2, ...). The rule was formulated by German physicist and physical chemist Erich Hückel in 1931 as part of his molecular orbital treatment of benzene and other cyclic conjugated systems, now known as Hückel molecular orbital theory.

Criteria for aromaticity

For Hückel's rule to apply, a molecule must be:

  • Cyclic — a ring of atoms.
  • Planar — so that adjacent p orbitals can overlap.
  • Fully conjugated — every atom in the ring contributes a p orbital.
  • Possessing 4n + 2 π electrons — 2, 6, 10, 14, ... electrons in the π system.

Examples

Benzene, with 6 π electrons (n = 1), is the archetypal example. Other Hückel systems include the cyclopentadienyl anion (6 π electrons), the cycloheptatrienyl (tropylium) cation (6 π electrons), the cyclopropenyl cation (2 π electrons, n = 0), naphthalene (10 π electrons), and the aromatic heterocycles pyrrole, furan, thiophene, and pyridine. Planar cyclic conjugated systems with 4n π electrons are instead antiaromatic and unusually unstable, as illustrated by cyclobutadiene and the cyclopentadienyl cation.

Refinements and extensions

Later work extended the concept beyond simple monocycles: Frost circles (Frost–Musulin diagrams) provide a graphical mnemonic for the π energy levels of monocyclic systems; Baird's rule describes aromaticity in the lowest excited triplet state (where 4n systems are aromatic); and Möbius aromaticity applies to twisted ring systems. Large rings may escape antiaromaticity by puckering out of planarity — cyclooctatetraene, with 8 π electrons, adopts a non-planar tub shape and behaves as an ordinary polyene.
