Hardiman

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hardiman was the first attempt to build a practical powered exoskeleton, developed by General Electric in the late 1960s in collaboration with the United States military. The machine was a full-body, hydraulically powered frame intended to allow a single operator to lift loads of up to 1,500 pounds (680 kg) as if they weighed only a few pounds.

Development

Work began in 1965 under a joint Army–Navy contract, with Ralph Mosher, designer of earlier GE "walking truck" and handyman manipulator projects, leading the concept. Hardiman I was a complex system of hydraulic and electronic servomechanisms: the operator's arms and legs were strapped into an articulated exoskeleton whose motions were amplified by powerful actuators.

Problems and cancellation

The prototype, completed around 1970, never worked as a complete system. Only one arm was made functional—it could lift 750 pounds—but the full-body machine was dangerously unstable, and attempts to make it walk produced violent, uncontrolled movements. Weighing some 1,700 pounds (770 kg) itself, the suit was too heavy, too slow and too unsafe for practical use. GE abandoned the project in the early 1970s.

Legacy

Despite its failure, Hardiman is remembered as the pioneering ancestor of modern powered exoskeletons such as those developed for military load-carrying, industrial assistance and medical rehabilitation. It demonstrated both the promise of human amplification and the difficulty of intuitive, stable control—problems that took decades of advances in computing and actuators to address.
