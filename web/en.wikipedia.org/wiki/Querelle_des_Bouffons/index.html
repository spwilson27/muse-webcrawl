The Querelle des Bouffons ("Quarrel of the Comic Actors"), sometimes known in English as the War of the Buffoons, was a pamphlet war and cultural controversy that erupted in Paris in 1752–1754 over the relative merits of French and Italian opera. It pitted defenders of the French operatic tradition — associated with the court of Louis XV and the works of Lully and Rameau — against champions of Italian opera buffa, among them leading figures of the Enlightenment such as Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Denis Diderot and Friedrich Melchior, Baron von Grimm.

Italian buffoni performing in Paris, whose success ignited the quarrel.

Background

Italian comic opera had been heard in Paris before, but it was the arrival in 1752 of an Italian troupe directed by Eustachio Bambini that lit the fuse. On 1 August 1752 the troupe performed Giovanni Battista Pergolesi's intermezzo La serva padrona at the Académie Royale de Musique (the Paris Opéra). The piece's naturalness, tunefulness and wit delighted a large part of the audience — and scandalized another part, which saw in its success an attack on the solemn French tragédie lyrique, with its mythological plots, elaborate stage machinery, choruses and dances.

The two camps

The dispute mapped onto the topography of the opera house itself. Supporters of French opera gathered beneath the king's box, the coin du roi ("king's corner"), while the partisans of Italian music occupied the coin de la reine ("queen's corner") beneath the box of Queen Marie Leszczyńska. The "queen's corner" drew in the philosophes of the Encyclopédie, who treated the quarrel as part of a broader campaign against courtly convention and in favour of nature, sentiment and simplicity in art.

The pamphlet war

Dozens of pamphlets, letters and satires poured from the presses. Grimm opened the campaign with Le petit prophète de Boehmischbroda (1753), a droll satire in which a Bohemian musician dreams of the quarrel from heaven. The most consequential contribution was Rousseau's Lettre sur la musique française (November 1753), which argued that the French language — lacking accent, sonority and clear prosody — was intrinsically unsuited to music, concluding that "the French have no music and can have none." Rameau, France's leading composer and theorist, replied in defence of French opera and of his own harmonic theories, and the exchange between the two men helped poison relations between Rousseau and the Parisian musical establishment for years. Diderot, d'Alembert and others also weighed in on the Italian side, while court musicians and pamphleteers answered for the French tradition.

Outcome and legacy

The quarrel burnt itself out in 1754 when the Italian troupe left Paris. Neither side won a decisive victory: French opera retained its institutional dominance, and La serva padrona was not adopted into the Opéra's repertory. Yet the ideas aired in the pamphlets proved durable. The taste for simplicity, natural declamation and tuneful melody encouraged the flourishing of French opéra comique in the following decades, and Rousseau himself composed a successful French opera in the Italianate manner, Le devin du village (1752). The Querelle is often read as a rehearsal, in miniature, for the larger cultural and political conflicts of late Enlightenment France.
