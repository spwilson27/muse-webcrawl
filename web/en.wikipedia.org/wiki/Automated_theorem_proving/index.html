Automated theorem proving (also known as ATP or automated deduction) is a subfield of automated reasoning and mathematical logic dealing with proving mathematical theorems by computer programs. Automated reasoning over mathematical proof was a major impetus for the development of computer science.

History

Early landmarks included the Logic Theorist (1956) of Newell, Simon and Shaw, which proved theorems from Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica, and J. Alan Robinson's resolution principle (1965), which gave provers a single, powerful inference rule. The Argonne school's Otter prover assisted in solving open problems in algebra and logic; in 1996 William McCune's EQP prover settled the Robbins conjecture, a problem that had resisted human mathematicians for decades.

Techniques

First-order provers typically work by refutation: the negation of the goal is converted to clause normal form and a contradiction is derived using resolution or the superposition calculus with unification. Other approaches include semantic tableaux, sequent calculi, rewriting, and reduction to decidable fragments — satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) solvers combine SAT solving with decision procedures for arithmetic, arrays and bit vectors. First-order validity is semi-decidable, so provers cannot guarantee termination on non-theorems; heuristics for search control are therefore central to performance.

Applications

Automated provers are used in software and hardware verification, program synthesis, mathematics formalisation (in cooperation with interactive proof assistants), and AI planning. The annual CADE ATP System Competition (CASC) benchmarks provers on the TPTP problem library.
