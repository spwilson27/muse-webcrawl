Epoch (astronomy)

Epoch (astronomy)
Current standardJ2000.0 (1 January 2000, 12:00 TT)
Previous standardB1950.0
Applies toCelestial coordinates, orbital elements, star catalogues

In astronomy, an epoch is a moment in time used as a reference point for some time-varying astronomical quantity, such as the celestial coordinates or elliptical orbital elements of an astronomical body. Because such quantities change — through precession, nutation, proper motion, and orbital perturbation — a measurement is only meaningful when the instant to which it refers is specified.

Use

Star catalogues and atlases give positions for a standard epoch. The current standard is J2000.0, corresponding to 12:00 Terrestrial Time on 1 January 2000, which replaced B1950.0 (based on Besselian years). The "J" indicates a Julian epoch. Positions must be converted between epochs — and to the epoch of date of an observation — by applying precession, nutation, and the object's proper motion. Orbital elements of planets, asteroids, and comets are likewise quoted for a stated epoch, and ephemerides propagate them to other dates.

Equinox and epoch

Epoch is closely related to, but distinct from, equinox: catalogues specify both the reference frame (equator and equinox) and the epoch of the coordinates, since stars move measurably over decades. High-precision work uses the International Celestial Reference System, effectively fixing the frame so that only epoch (via proper motion) remains to be tracked for individual stars.
