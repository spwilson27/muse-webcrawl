Proteinogenic amino acids are amino acids that are incorporated biosynthetically into proteins during translation. The word "proteinogenic" means "protein creating". Throughout known life there are 22 genetically encoded (proteinogenic) amino acids: the 20 specified by the universal genetic code, plus selenocysteine, encoded by the codon UGA in the presence of a special recognition sequence, and pyrrolysine, encoded by UAG in certain archaea and bacteria.

The standard twenty

The twenty standard amino acids, grouped by side-chain character, are:

  • Nonpolar: alanine (A), valine (V), leucine (L), isoleucine (I), methionine (M), proline (P), phenylalanine (F), tryptophan (W), glycine (G).
  • Polar uncharged: serine (S), threonine (T), cysteine (C), asparagine (N), glutamine (Q), tyrosine (Y).
  • Positively charged: lysine (K), arginine (R), histidine (H).
  • Negatively charged: aspartate (D), glutamate (E).

Of these, nine are essential in human nutrition — histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine — because the human body cannot synthesize them.

Selenocysteine and pyrrolysine

Selenocysteine, sometimes called the 21st amino acid, is found in all domains of life in enzymes such as glutathione peroxidases; it is inserted by recoding a UGA stop codon guided by a SECIS element. Pyrrolysine, the 22nd, occurs in methylamine methyltransferases of methanogenic archaea and some bacteria, inserted at a UAG codon with the help of a PYLIS element.

Amino acids in other roles

Many amino acids occur in proteins only after post-translational modification (e.g. hydroxyproline, phosphoserine) and are not directly genetically encoded. Hundreds of other amino acids exist in nature with non-protein functions, such as citrulline and ornithine in the urea cycle.
