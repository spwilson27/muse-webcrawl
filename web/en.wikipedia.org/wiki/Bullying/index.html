Bullying is the use of force, coercion, hurtful teasing, or threat to abuse, aggressively dominate, or intimidate another person. The behaviour is often repeated and habitual. Researcher Dan Olweus, a pioneer in the field, defined bullying by three key components: it is aggressive behaviour or intentional harm-doing, it is carried out repeatedly over time, and it occurs within an interpersonal relationship characterised by an imbalance of power.

Types

Bullying may be classified by its form:

  • Physical bullying — hitting, pushing, tripping, or damaging property.
  • Verbal bullying — name-calling, insults, teasing, and threats.
  • Relational (social) bullying — spreading rumours, social exclusion, and manipulating friendships.
  • Cyberbullying — bullying through digital devices and online platforms, including harassing messages and impersonation.

Contexts include school bullying, workplace bullying (including mobbing), bullying in prisons and the military, and hazing in organizations.

Effects

Victims of bullying are at increased risk of depression, anxiety, low self-esteem, academic difficulties, physical health problems, and suicidal ideation. Those who bully others also face elevated risks of later antisocial behaviour and mental-health problems, and bystanders may experience distress as well. Effects can persist into adulthood.

Prevention and intervention

Whole-school programs — most famously the Olweus Bullying Prevention Program developed in Norway — combine school-wide rules, classroom discussion, individual intervention, and parental involvement, and have been shown to reduce bullying substantially. Many jurisdictions have enacted anti-bullying legislation requiring schools to adopt policies against bullying and harassment. Workplace bullying is addressed in some countries by occupational-safety and anti-discrimination laws.
