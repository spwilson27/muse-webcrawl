Common carp

Common carp
Scientific name: Cyprinus carpio
Family: Cyprinidae
Order: Cypriniformes
Native range: Europe and Asia
The common carp (Cyprinus carpio) is one of the most widely introduced freshwater fish.

The common carp (Cyprinus carpio) is a widespread freshwater fish of eutrophic waters in lakes and large rivers in Europe and Asia. The native wild populations are considered vulnerable to extinction by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), but the species has also been domesticated and introduced worldwide, and is often considered a destructive invasive species.

Description

Although tolerant of most conditions, common carp prefer large bodies of slow or standing water and soft, vegetative sediments. They typically grow to 40–80 cm and 15 kg, though exceptional specimens exceed 40 kg. Carp have a deep body, large scales, two barbels at the corners of the mouth, and a long dorsal fin.

Domestication and koi

Carp have been raised as food fish in ponds since Roman times and were selectively bred in China and Japan. Ornamental domesticated carp coloured for personal enjoyment are known as koi. In Central and Eastern Europe, carp is a traditional part of Christmas Eve meals.

As an invasive species

Introduced carp are a major pest in Australia, North America and elsewhere. Their bottom-feeding stirs up sediment, increases turbidity and damages aquatic vegetation, degrading habitat for native fish and waterfowl. Control programmes include commercial harvesting, exclusion screens, and research into biological controls such as cyprinid herpesvirus 3.
