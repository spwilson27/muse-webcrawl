Exploration of Australia

The exploration of Australia spans the arrival of Aboriginal peoples at least 65,000 years ago, the first European landfalls in the early 17th century, the charting of the continent's coastline by 1803, and the crossing of its arid interior in the 19th century.

European maritime exploration

The Dutch East India Company ship Duyfken, under Willem Janszoon, made the first documented European landing in 1606 on Cape York Peninsula; Dirk Hartog landed on the west coast in 1616, and Abel Tasman charted Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania) and parts of the mainland in 1642–44. In 1770 James Cook charted the east coast and claimed it for Britain at Possession Island. Matthew Flinders circumnavigated the continent in 1801–03, proving it was a single landmass and popularising the name "Australia".

Inland exploration

After Gregory Blaxland, William Lawson and William Wentworth crossed the Blue Mountains in 1813, explorers pushed inland along the rivers: Charles Sturt traced the Murray–Darling system, Edward John Eyre crossed the Nullarbor fringe, and Ludwig Leichhardt disappeared in 1848 attempting an east–west crossing. The Burke and Wills expedition of 1860–61 made the first south–north crossing but its leaders perished at Cooper Creek. John McDouall Stuart's successful crossings (1860–62) opened the route for the Overland Telegraph. Later expeditions by Ernest Giles, Peter Warburton and John Forrest crossed the western deserts.

Recognition

Exploration depended heavily on Aboriginal guides, trackers and knowledge; many "discoveries" were of places long known and named by Indigenous custodians. The journeys also mapped resources for pastoral expansion, with profound consequences for Aboriginal peoples.
