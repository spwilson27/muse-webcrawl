Racial integration

Racial integration, or simply integration, is the goal of bringing different races together in a society where members of each group enjoy equal access to institutions, public spaces, housing, education and employment. It is the opposite of racial segregation, the enforced separation of racial groups. In the United States the term is closely associated with the civil rights movement and efforts to desegregate schools, the military, public transport and other facilities after the era of Jim Crow laws.

History in the United States

Formal integration advanced through a series of legal and political milestones. In 1948 President Harry S. Truman ordered the desegregation of the armed forces. The Supreme Court's 1954 decision in Brown v. Board of Education declared state laws establishing separate public schools for Black and white students unconstitutional, overturning the "separate but equal" doctrine of Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). Implementation was often resisted, as in the Little Rock Crisis of 1957, and was reinforced by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Desegregation versus integration

Scholars distinguish desegregation — the legal removal of barriers — from integration, which implies the actual mixing of groups and the sharing of power and resources. Court-ordered busing in the 1970s attempted to achieve integrated schools, but white flight to suburbs and later court decisions weakened these efforts, and many school systems have resegregated.

Other contexts

Integration has also been a goal in post-apartheid South Africa, in Northern Ireland, and in debates about immigrant communities in Europe, where it overlaps with questions of cultural assimilation and multiculturalism.
