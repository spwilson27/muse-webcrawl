Nicolas Boileau-Despréaux

Nicolas Boileau-Despréaux (1636–1711), generally called Boileau, was a French poet, satirist and critic whose influence on French letters earned him the title of legislator of Parnassus. A friend of Molière, Racine and La Fontaine, he was appointed historiographer to Louis XIV alongside Racine and elected to the Académie française in 1684.

Works

Boileau first made his reputation with his Satires (1666 onwards), polished verse attacks on bad taste and pretentious contemporaries. His mock-heroic poem Le Lutrin and his translation of Longinus's treatise On the Sublime (1674) consolidated his authority. His masterpiece is L'Art poétique (1674), a didactic poem in four cantos, modelled on Horace's Ars Poetica, which set out the doctrine of French classicism: clarity, reason, truth to nature, respect for the unities, and careful versification. Its maxims — such as "what is well conceived is clearly stated" — became proverbial.

Influence

Boileau's rules shaped French drama and poetry for a century and, through Alexander Pope's Essay on Criticism, English neoclassicism as well. The Romantics later revolted against his authority, but his verse remains a monument of classical French style.
