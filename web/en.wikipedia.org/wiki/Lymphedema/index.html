Lymphedema

Lymphedema
Specialty: Vascular medicine
Symptoms: Swelling of arms or legs, heaviness, recurrent infections
Causes: Lymph node removal, radiation, filariasis, inherited defects

Lymphedema is a condition of localized swelling caused by a compromised lymphatic system, in which excess protein-rich fluid (lymph) accumulates in the interstitial tissues. It most commonly affects an arm or leg, but can also involve the trunk, head, or neck.

Causes

Primary lymphedema results from developmental abnormalities of the lymphatics and may be congenital (Milroy disease) or appear later in life. Secondary lymphedema is far more common: worldwide its leading cause is lymphatic filariasis, a parasitic infection; in developed countries the most common cause is cancer treatment, particularly lymph node dissection and radiation therapy for breast cancer.

Signs and stages

Early swelling may be soft and pitting; over time the tissue becomes firm and fibrotic (Stemmer's sign — inability to pinch the skin at the base of the second toe — is a classic finding). Stages range from stage 0 (latent) to stage 3 (lymphostatic elephantiasis). Complications include cellulitis, lymphangitis, and, rarely, lymphangiosarcoma.

Management

There is no cure, but complete decongestive therapy — manual lymphatic drainage, compression bandaging and garments, exercise, and skin care — is the standard of care. Surgical options in selected cases include lymphaticovenular anastomosis, vascularized lymph node transfer, and suction-assisted lipectomy.
