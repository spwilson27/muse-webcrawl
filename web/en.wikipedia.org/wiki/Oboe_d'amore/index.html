The oboe d'amore (Italian for "oboe of love") is a double-reed woodwind instrument in the oboe family. Slightly larger than the regular oboe, it sounds a minor third lower, in A, and has a characteristic bulbous bell (called a Liebesfuß, "love foot") that gives it a softer, more veiled tone than the brighter standard oboe.

History

The instrument was developed in the early 18th century, probably by makers in Leipzig, and was used extensively by Johann Sebastian Bach, who wrote many obbligato parts for it in his cantatas, as well as in the original version of the Magnificat and the Mass in B minor. Georg Philipp Telemann and Christoph Graupner also wrote for it. It fell out of use in the later 18th century as orchestral tastes changed.

Revival

The oboe d'amore was revived in the late 19th and 20th centuries. Richard Strauss used it in the Sinfonia Domestica, and Maurice Ravel scored a prominent solo for it in Boléro. Modern makers such as F. Lorée and Marigaux produce instruments for today's period and modern orchestras, and Bach's parts are now routinely played on it again rather than adapted for cor anglais.

Construction

Like the oboe, it is made of grenadilla or other dense wood (historically also boxwood or fruitwood), with a conical bore. Its bell is globular rather than flared, and the reed is mounted on a short, slightly curved metal crook or bocal. It is a transposing instrument in A: music is written a minor third higher than it sounds.
