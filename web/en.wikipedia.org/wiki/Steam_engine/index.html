Steam engine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A preserved stationary steam engine with a large flywheel in a mill engine house]
A mill steam engine.

A steam engine is a heat engine that performs mechanical work using steam as its working fluid. Steam engines powered the Industrial Revolution: they drove factories, pumps, locomotives and ships. Early atmospheric engines (Newcomen, 1712) were greatly improved by James Watt from the 1770s, and steam remained a dominant prime mover until the early 20th century, when steam turbines, internal combustion engines and electric motors displaced it.

How it works

Water boiled in a boiler produces high-pressure steam that pushes a piston in a cylinder; connecting rods and cranks convert the reciprocating motion into rotation. Watt's separate condenser, double-acting cylinders and governor made engines far more efficient. Compound and triple-expansion engines reused steam in successive cylinders.

Impact

Steam freed factories from water-power sites, enabled railways and steamships, and transformed mining with reliable pumping. The science of thermodynamics grew from studying steam-engine efficiency (Carnot, 1824).
