One-shot film

Also calledOne-take film; "oner"
TechniqueSingle continuous take, real or simulated
Notable examplesRussian Ark, Victoria, Birdman, 1917

A one-shot film (also called a one-take film) is a feature film shot in a single continuous take, or edited to appear as if it were. The technique eliminates visible editing, unfolding the story in real time and requiring extensive choreography of actors, camera movement, lighting, and set design.

History

Directors experimented with very long takes throughout cinema history — Alfred Hitchcock's Rope (1948) was staged to look like a single take, constrained by the roughly ten-minute capacity of film magazines. True feature-length single takes only became practical with lightweight digital cameras and high-capacity recording media in the 2000s.

True single-take films

Alexander Sokurov's Russian Ark (2002) was the landmark true one-shot feature: a single 96-minute Steadicam shot through the Hermitage Museum involving some 2,000 actors, recorded uncompressed to a hard disk after three failed attempts. Sebastian Schipper's Victoria (2015), a Berlin crime thriller, was filmed in one genuine 138-minute take in the early morning hours, on the third attempt. Other true one-take features include Timecode (2000, four simultaneous takes shown in split screen) and the Norwegian film Blind Spot (2018).

Simulated one-shot films

Many prominent "one-shot" films are constructed from many takes joined by hidden cuts, often masked by whip pans, darkness, or digital stitching. Alejandro G. Iñárritu's Birdman (2014) and Sam Mendes's war film 1917 (2019) are leading examples; both won the Academy Award for Best Cinematography. The British film Boiling Point (2021), set in a restaurant kitchen, is a genuine single take, unlike the long-take television episodes frequently shot to simulate one.

Technique and challenges

Because a single mistake can ruin an entire take, one-shot productions rehearse like theatre, with marks, cueing systems, and backup plans. Camera operators favour gimbals and Steadicams; focus, exposure, and sound must adapt continuously as the camera moves between interiors and exteriors. Directors cite the format's unbroken tension and immersion — the audience shares the characters' real-time experience — as its chief artistic rewards.
