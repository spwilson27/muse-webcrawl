Golden Bull of 1222

The Golden Bull of 1222 was a golden bull, or edict, issued by King Andrew II of Hungary. The law established the rights of the Hungarian nobility, including the right to disobey the king when he acted contrary to law (the jus resistendi, or right of resistance). The Golden Bull is often compared to England's Magna Carta of 1215.

Background

Andrew II's lavish donations of royal estates to his followers, his costly foreign campaigns, and resentment among the royal servants (servientes regis) and lesser nobles over the growing power of the barons forced the king to concede the charter at Székesfehérvár in 1222. The document took its name from its golden seal.

Provisions

The Bull exempted nobles and church property from taxation, forbade the imprisonment of nobles without trial, limited the king's ability to grant offices and estates to foreigners, and confirmed that the Diet would meet annually at Székesfehérvár. Nobles were obliged to serve in defensive wars but not in campaigns abroad unless paid. The final clause granted the bishops, barons and nobles the right to resist the king if he violated the Bull's provisions — a right retained until 1687.

Legacy

Andrew confirmed the Bull in 1231. Its principles shaped Hungarian constitutional development for centuries and were invoked repeatedly by the nobility against royal absolutism.
