Algal biofuel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Algal biofuel is an alternative to liquid fossil fuels that uses algae as a source of energy-rich oils. Microalgae accumulate lipids that can be converted into biodiesel, while other species produce carbohydrates convertible to bioethanol or biogas. Because many algae grow far faster than land crops and can be cultivated on non-arable land with saline or wastewater, algal fuels have attracted research interest since the United States Department of Energy's Aquatic Species Program (1978–1996).

Production

Algae are grown either in open raceway ponds or in closed photobioreactors. After harvesting — a technically difficult step because the cells are small and dilute — the biomass is dewatered and the oils extracted, then refined into fuels such as renewable diesel, gasoline or jet fuel. Some processes instead use hydrothermal liquefaction of whole algae into biocrude, or fermentation of sugars released from the cells.

Advantages and challenges

Potential advantages include high areal productivity, use of carbon dioxide from industrial flue gas, no direct competition with food crops, and treatment of wastewater. However, the costs of cultivation, harvesting and dewatering have so far kept algal fuels far more expensive than petroleum, and many ventures founded in the 2000s and 2010s failed or pivoted to higher-value products such as nutritional supplements and cosmetics. Research continues into strain engineering and process integration.
