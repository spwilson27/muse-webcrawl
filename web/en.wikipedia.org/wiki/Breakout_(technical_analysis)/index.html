Breakout (technical analysis)

In technical analysis of financial markets, a breakout is a movement of a security's price through an identified level of support or resistance, usually accompanied by increased trading volume. A breakout above resistance is interpreted as a bullish signal that the price may continue rising; a breakout below support — sometimes called a breakdown — is interpreted as bearish. Breakout trading is one of the most widely used strategies in chart-based trading, resting on the idea that once a barrier that has contained price is decisively breached, accumulated supply or demand imbalance propels price in the direction of the break.

Price consolidates below resistance, then breaks out on rising volume

Characteristics

Breakouts commonly occur from chart patterns such as triangles, rectangles (trading ranges), flags, head-and-shoulders formations, and horizontal channels. Technicians look for confirmation in the form of a close beyond the level rather than an intraday spike, a surge in volume on the breaking bar, and sometimes a successful retest in which price returns to the broken level — now acting as support after resistance, or vice versa — before continuing. The projected objective is often estimated by adding the height of the preceding pattern to the breakout point (the "measured move").

False breakouts

A false breakout (or failed breakout, bull trap, or bear trap, depending on direction) occurs when price moves beyond a level but quickly reverses back into the prior range, trapping traders who entered on the break. False breakouts are common because support and resistance levels are watched by many participants and because stop-loss orders clustered beyond obvious levels can be triggered by brief excursions ("stop runs"). Traders manage the risk with confirmation rules, smaller position sizes, and stops placed back inside the pattern.

Breakout strategies

Systematic trend-following systems are built largely on breakouts: the classic Donchian channel system used by the Turtle Traders of the 1980s entered positions when price exceeded the highest high (or lowest low) of the previous 20 or 55 days. Momentum research in finance has found that time-series momentum — a close relative of breakout trading — has been profitable on average across many markets and long samples, though academic opinion remains divided on whether chart patterns in general have predictive power beyond momentum effects.
