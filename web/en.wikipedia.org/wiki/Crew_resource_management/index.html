Crew resource management

Crew resource management (CRM) is a set of training procedures and practices for settings in which human error can have devastating effects — originally the flight deck of an aircraft — designed to improve communication, decision-making, leadership, teamwork and situational awareness so that all available resources (information, equipment and people) are used to promote safety. CRM teaches crew members, especially junior ones, to question authority appropriately, and captains to solicit and act on input, replacing a steep cockpit authority gradient with assertive but respectful communication.

Origins

Investigations of accidents in the 1970s found that many crashes resulted not from technical failure but from breakdowns in communication and leadership. In the Tenerife airport disaster of 1977, two Boeing 747s collided, killing 583 people, after a first officer's challenge to a captain's take-off decision went unheeded. In 1978 United Airlines Flight 173 crashed in Portland, Oregon, when the crew fixated on a landing-gear problem while the fuel ran out, the flight engineer failing to press his concerns. In response, a 1979 NASA workshop coined the term (originally "cockpit resource management"), and United Airlines launched the industry's first comprehensive CRM programme in 1981.

Content and generations

CRM curricula address communication, situational awareness, problem-solving and decision-making, teamwork, leadership and followership, workload and stress management, and the recognition of error chains (formalised in error-management and threat-and-error-management models). Training evolved from individual psychology and managerial-style seminars toward integrated, recurrent simulator-based practice (line-oriented flight training) assessed with behavioural markers, and it was extended to cabin crew, dispatchers and maintenance personnel. Regulators such as the FAA and EASA require CRM training for commercial aircrew.

Beyond aviation

The approach has been adopted in maritime transport (bridge and engine-room resource management), railways, offshore energy, firefighting, the military and medicine, where analogues such as anaesthesia crisis resource management and team-training programmes (often derived from aviation CRM) target the communication failures implicated in surgical and emergency-care errors.
