Wood-burning stove

A wood-burning stove (or wood burner or log burner in the UK) is a heating or cooking appliance capable of burning wood fuel, typically logs, and wood-derived biomass fuel. Generally the appliance consists of a solid metal (usually cast iron or steel) closed fire chamber, a firebrick base and an adjustable air control.

Operation

The stove is connected to a chimney or flue by ventilating stove pipe. Hot gases rise by convection, drawing fresh air into the firebox. Modern stoves use secondary combustion or catalytic converters to burn off gases and particles that would otherwise escape as smoke, raising efficiency to 70–80 percent or more, compared with about 20 percent for an open fireplace.

Air pollution

Wood smoke contains fine particulate matter (PM2.5), carbon monoxide and volatile organic compounds. Because of health concerns, many jurisdictions regulate stove emissions; in the United States the EPA certifies stoves under tightening standards, and some towns restrict burning during air-quality alerts.

History

Enclosed stoves developed from open hearths; Benjamin Franklin's 1741 "Pennsylvania fireplace" and the cast-iron box stoves of the 19th century made wood heating far more efficient. Wood stoves remain common for home heating in rural areas worldwide.
