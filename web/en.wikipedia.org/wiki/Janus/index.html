Janus

In ancient Roman religion and myth, Janus (Latin: Ianus) is the god of beginnings, gates, transitions, time, duality, doorways, passages, frames and endings. He is usually depicted as having two faces, one looking to the future and the other to the past — an image captured on Roman coins and still familiar from the "Janus-faced" metaphor for two-sidedness. The month of January (Ianuarius) is named for him.

Nature and functions

Janus was one of the most ancient and distinctly Roman of gods, with no direct equivalent in Greek mythology. He presided over the abstract and concrete beginnings of everything: the beginning of the day, month and year, of sacred and profane undertakings, of journeys, births, marriages and deaths, and the passage between barbarism and civilisation, war and peace. Prayers in Roman ritual conventionally invoked Janus first, before even Jupiter, because he was the doorkeeper who gave access to the other gods. In the Carmen Saliare he was honoured as "god of gods".

Temple of Janus Geminus

His most famous shrine stood in the Roman Forum: the Temple of Janus Geminus, a bronze structure with doors at both ends, traditionally founded by King Numa Pompilius. Its doors were ceremonially open in wartime and closed when Rome was at peace throughout its dominions — a rare event: the gates are said to have been closed only a handful of times in Roman history, under Numa, after the First Punic War, and three times under Augustus.

Myth and later influence

Myths make Janus a primordial king of Latium who welcomed the exiled Saturn, received the gift of knowing past and future, and ruled a golden age. Ovid's Fasti opens with an interview with the god explaining his dual nature. His two-faced image became a lasting emblem of transition and ambiguity, giving English the words "January" and "janitor" (doorkeeper), and the adjective "Janus-faced", used both for honest two-sided awareness of past and future and, pejoratively, for deceitful duplicity.
