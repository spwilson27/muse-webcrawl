Checks and balances

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Checks and balances are a system of mutual controls among the separate branches of government, designed so that no single branch can become too powerful. Closely associated with the separation of powers doctrine, the idea is that each branch is given defined powers to limit the others: ambition, in Madison's phrase, is made to counteract ambition.

In the United States Constitution, the President may veto legislation but Congress may override the veto by two-thirds of both houses; the Senate must consent to appointments and treaties; the courts may declare acts of either branch unconstitutional through judicial review; and Congress may impeach and remove the President or judges. Montesquieu's The Spirit of Law (1748) supplied the theory, though the American framers deliberately blended as well as separated powers so that each branch would depend partly on the others.

Origins

Ancient precedents include the mixed constitution of the Roman Republic, where consuls, Senate and assemblies each held elements of power. Polybius praised this balance as the secret of Roman stability. Early modern writers, notably Locke and Montesquieu, developed the modern doctrine that liberty is preserved when legislative, executive and judicial powers are not united in the same hands.

Operation and criticism

Supporters argue that checks and balances prevent tyranny and force deliberation; critics note that they can produce gridlock, blur accountability, and — in presidential systems facing a hostile legislature — contribute to crises that parliamentary systems resolve by a change of government. Juan Linz's critique of presidentialism drew heavily on this problem. The system works differently where powers are fused, as in Westminster systems, where accountability runs mainly through Parliament and elections rather than inter-branch vetoes.
