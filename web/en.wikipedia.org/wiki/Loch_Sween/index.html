Loch Sween (Scottish Gaelic: Loch Suathain or Loch Suibhne) is a sea loch on the west coast of Argyll and Bute, Scotland, on the Sound of Jura. It cuts into the Knapdale peninsula and extends roughly 13 kilometres inland from its mouth near the village of Tayvallich to the head of the loch near Bellanoch, where it meets the Crinan Canal basin area.

Geography

The loch is notable for its intricate shape, with a series of side arms or "inner lochs" branching off the main channel, including Caol Scotnish, Loch na Cille and the Linnhe Loch — small, sheltered basins that support rich marine habitats such as maerl beds and native oyster reefs. The island of Danna lies at the loch's mouth, connected to the mainland by a causeway, and Ulva Islands and the MacCormaig Isles sit near its entrance.

History

Castle Sween, on the eastern shore, is one of the oldest stone castles in Scotland, built in the 12th century by Suibhne (Sven), ancestor of the MacSweens. The area is rich in early medieval archaeology, including carved stones at Kilmory Knap Chapel and platforms and rock carvings associated with the kingdom of Dál Riata. The loch and its shores later came under the Campbells and were a centre of fishing and coastal trade.

Natural history and protection

Loch Sween was designated Scotland's first community-led Marine Protected Area campaign area and in 2014 became part of the national MPA network (Loch Sunart to the Sound of Jura MPA). Its waters support flame shell beds, maerl, burrowed mud with sea pens, and harbour porpoises. Ashore, the Taynish National Nature Reserve at the western entrance is one of Scotland's finest surviving Atlantic oakwoods, famous for its butterflies, including the marsh fritillary, and for beavers reintroduced to the nearby Knapdale forest in the Scottish Beaver Trial (2009–2014).
