RegionEastern Europe (Pripyat–middle Dnieper region, debated)

Proto-Slavic is the unattested, reconstructed proto-language of all the Slavic languages. It represents the Slavic speech of roughly the late 1st millennium BCE through the first centuries CE, and it gradually differentiated into the modern East Slavic, West Slavic and South Slavic languages as Slavic speakers expanded across central and eastern Europe during the early medieval period.

Development

Proto-Slavic developed from Proto-Balto-Slavic, sharing innovations with Baltic such as Winter's law and a common stock of vocabulary. Its history is conventionally divided into early, middle (Common Slavic) and late stages. Because Slavic remained remarkably uniform for a long time, the closely related late stage — Common Slavic — can be reconstructed in unusual detail; the earliest written Slavic, Old Church Slavonic of the 9th century, still stood close to it.

Phonology

Key features include the three palatalizations of velars (giving č, ž, š and later c, dz, s in different environments), the loss of closed syllables with simplification of consonant clusters, nasal vowels *ę and *ǫ (preserved today only in Polish and Kashubian), the ultra-short vowels called yers (*ь, *ъ) whose later loss shaped all Slavic phonologies, and a pitch-accent system whose reflexes survive in Serbo-Croatian and Slovene. Liquid diphthongs were resolved by metathesis in South and East Slavic (compare Russian gorod, Polish gród).

Grammar

Proto-Slavic nouns inflected for seven cases, three genders and three numbers (singular, dual, plural). The verb system centred on the contrast of perfective and imperfective aspect, developed fully in Slavic, with aorist and imperfect past tenses retained longest in South Slavic (Bulgarian and Macedonian), where a definite article also developed postpositively.

Vocabulary

Reconstructed vocabulary reflects a temperate-forest farming society: words for ploughing, grain, beekeeping, kinship and early social organization, plus early loanwords from Germanic (e.g. *xlěbъ "bread", *kъnędzь "prince") and Iranian languages.
