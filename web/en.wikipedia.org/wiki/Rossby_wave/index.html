Rossby wave

Rossby waves, also known as planetary waves, are a type of inertial wave naturally occurring in rotating fluids. They were first identified by Carl-Gustaf Arvid Rossby in 1939. Rossby waves exist in the Earth's atmosphere and oceans, and are important in meteorology and climatology: the large meanders of the jet stream and the pattern of high- and low-pressure systems are manifestations of atmospheric Rossby waves.

Mechanism

Rossby waves arise because the Coriolis parameter varies with latitude (the beta effect). When a parcel of air or water is displaced from its equilibrium latitude, conservation of potential vorticity causes it to oscillate, and the oscillation propagates as a wave. Atmospheric Rossby waves generally propagate westward relative to the mean flow, although their absolute motion depends on the strength of the westerly winds.

Significance

Rossby waves shape day-to-day weather in mid-latitudes and can become stationary, producing persistent extremes such as heat waves, cold spells and blocking patterns. Oceanic Rossby waves cross ocean basins over years and play a role in climate variability, including the adjustment of the ocean during El Niño events.
