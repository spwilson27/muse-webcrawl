Lost-wax casting

Lost-wax casting (also called investment casting, precision casting, or cire perdue in French) is the process by which a duplicate metal sculpture (often silver, gold, brass or bronze) is cast from an original sculpture. Intricate works can be achieved by this method.

Process

  1. A model is made in wax (hollow for large works, over a clay core), capturing every detail of the original.
  2. Wax rods (sprues) are attached to channel the metal and vent gases.
  3. The model is coated ("invested") in layers of heat-resistant material — traditionally clay or plaster mixed with sand — forming a mould.
  4. The mould is heated; the wax melts and runs out ("lost wax"), leaving a cavity.
  5. Molten metal is poured in. After cooling, the mould is broken away.
  6. The casting is cleaned, sprues are removed, and the surface is chased and patinated.

Because the mould is destroyed to release the casting, each traditional lost-wax cast is unique, though rubber moulds allow modern editions.

History

The technique is over 5,000 years old: early examples include objects from the Indus Valley civilisation and the Chalcolithic Nahal Mishmar hoard (c. 3700 BC) from the Levant. It was used for the bronzes of ancient Greece and Rome, the Benin Bronzes of Nigeria, pre-Columbian goldwork in South America, and Renaissance masterpieces such as Benvenuto Cellini's Perseus with the Head of Medusa (1554) and the bronze doors and statuary of many cultures.

Modern uses

Industrial investment casting produces precision components for aerospace, dentistry and jewellery, using ceramic shell moulds and alloys from stainless steel to superalloys.
