Just transition

Just transition
OriginNorth American labour movement, 1970s–1990s (Tony Mazzocchi)
Key adoptionILO guidelines (2015); Paris Agreement preamble (2015)
FocusWorkers' and communities' rights in decarbonisation

Just transition is a framework developed by the trade union movement and adopted by governments and international bodies to guide the shift to a low-carbon, environmentally sustainable economy in a way that is fair to workers and communities whose livelihoods depend on fossil fuels and other high-emission industries. Its core principle is that those who have the least responsibility for climate change — and those whose jobs are displaced by climate policy — should not bear its costs.

Origins

The idea grew out of the North American labour and environmental-justice movements of the 1970s. Oil, Chemical and Atomic Workers union leader Tony Mazzocchi campaigned for a "Superfund for workers" — guaranteed support and retraining for those displaced by the closure of toxic or polluting industries — arguing that workers should not have to choose between their jobs and their health or environment.

Trade union federations, notably the International Trade Union Confederation (ITUC) under Sharan Burrow, promoted the concept internationally in the 2000s; the slogan "there are no jobs on a dead planet" became associated with the campaign.

International frameworks

  • The International Labour Organization adopted Guidelines for a just transition in 2015.
  • The preamble of the 2015 Paris Agreement takes into account "the imperatives of a just transition of the workforce and the creation of decent work and quality jobs".
  • The Just Transition Mechanism of the European Union, part of the European Green Deal, provides tens of billions of euros to coal-dependent regions.
  • South Africa's Just Energy Transition Partnership (announced at COP26 in 2021) became a template for international climate finance packages.

Content and debate

Typical just-transition policies include retraining and reskilling, income support and pension protection, investment in affected regions, social dialogue with unions, and community regeneration. The concept has also been broadened to address energy poverty, Indigenous rights, gender, and the global South. Critics debate whether the term stretches too widely, whether funding matches rhetoric, and whether — as some argue — it can be used to delay decarbonisation.
