Halo effect

Halo effect
Type: Cognitive bias
First described: Edward Thorndike (1920)
Reverse form: Horn effect

The halo effect is a cognitive bias in which an observer's overall impression of a person, company, brand or product in one area positively influences their feelings and judgments in other, unrelated areas. The term was coined by psychologist Edward Thorndike in 1920 from his studies of military officers' ratings of their soldiers.

Evidence and applications

Physically attractive people are, for example, more likely to be judged intelligent, kind and competent — the "what is beautiful is good" stereotype. The halo effect influences performance appraisals, jury decisions, education, marketing and brand extension. Its negative counterpart, where one bad trait colours the whole judgment, is called the horn effect or devil effect.

Mitigation

Structured interviews, independent ratings of separate traits, and awareness of the bias are used to reduce halo errors in evaluation.
