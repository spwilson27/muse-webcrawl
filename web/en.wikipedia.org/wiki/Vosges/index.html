The Vosges (French: [voʒ]; German: Vogesen) are a range of low mountains in eastern France, running roughly north–south along the west bank of the Rhine, opposite Germany's Black Forest, from which they are separated by the Rhine Rift Valley. They extend about 120 kilometres (75 miles) from the Belfort Gap in the south to the Saverne Pass in the north.

Geography

The Vosges are an ancient Hercynian massif, uplifted and re-shaped when the Rhine graben subsided in the Tertiary era. The highest summits, the ballons — so called for their rounded, dome-like profiles — are in the southern, crystalline part of the range: the Grand Ballon reaches 1,424 metres, followed by the Storkenkopf, Hohneck, and Ballon d'Alsace. Northward the range lowers into the sandstone Northern Vosges. The eastern slopes fall steeply to the Alsace wine-growing plain, while the western side descends gently into Lorraine. Dense forests of fir, spruce, and beech cover much of the range; the ridge line is dotted with glacial lakes such as Gérardmer and Longemer, and high pastures (chaumes) traditionally grazed in summer.

History and culture

The main crest long formed the linguistic and political boundary between French-speaking Lorraine and German-speaking Alsace. The mountains were heavily fought over in the First World War, notably at Hartmannswillerkopf (the Vieil Armand), whose battlefield is now a national memorial. The range is protected in part by the Ballons des Vosges Regional Natural Park (south) and the Northern Vosges Regional Natural Park, the latter a UNESCO biosphere reserve twinned across the German border with the Palatinate Forest. The Route des Crêtes, built as a military road in 1914–1918, now serves tourism along the ridge.
