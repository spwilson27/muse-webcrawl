Lumbricus terrestris is a large, reddish worm species widely distributed in Europe and now common as an introduced species in North America and elsewhere. It is known in Britain as the common earthworm or lob worm, and in North America — where it emerges at night — as the nightcrawler. Adults typically reach 20–25 cm in length. The species is anecic: it builds permanent vertical burrows that may extend 2 metres deep, from which it emerges at night to drag surface leaf litter into the burrow to feed.

Biology

Like other earthworms, L. terrestris is a simultaneous hermaphrodite, exchanging sperm with a partner and later depositing fertilised eggs in a cocoon secreted by the clitellum (saddle). It breathes through its moist skin, has a closed circulatory system with five pairs of aortic arches ("hearts"), and moves by waves of muscular contraction braced by bristle-like chaetae. Individuals may live for several years in captivity, though predation is heavy in the wild. Burrowing aerates the soil and middens of casts mark burrow entrances; Charles Darwin's final book, The Formation of Vegetable Mould Through the Action of Worms (1881), quantified the species' power to turn over and enrich soil.

Ecological role and invasion

In its native European range the species is a valued decomposer and food source for birds and mammals. In North America, where European earthworms were absent from formerly glaciated forests, introduced L. terrestris is an ecosystem engineer with mixed effects: beneficial in gardens and farmland, but in northern hardwood forests it consumes the leaf-litter layer that native plants, invertebrates, and ground-nesting birds depend on, altering nutrient cycling. It is also sold widely as fishing bait, a major pathway of spread.
