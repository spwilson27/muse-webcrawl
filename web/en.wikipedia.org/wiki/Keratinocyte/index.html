Keratinocyte

Cultured human keratinocytes

Keratinocytes are the principal cell type of the epidermis, the outermost layer of the skin, constituting about 90% of its cells. They produce the fibrous protein keratin and, as they mature and migrate upward, form the tough, water-resistant barrier that protects the body against microorganisms, ultraviolet radiation, heat and water loss.

Life cycle

Keratinocytes originate from stem cells in the deepest epidermal layer, the stratum basale. As new cells are produced, older keratinocytes are pushed upward through the stratum spinosum and stratum granulosum, progressively flattening, accumulating keratin filaments and lipid granules, and losing their nuclei — a process called keratinisation or cornification. In the outermost stratum corneum the dead, flattened cells (corneocytes) are embedded in a lipid matrix before being shed from the surface. The full journey from basal layer to shedding takes roughly four weeks in healthy skin.

Functions

Beyond forming the physical barrier, keratinocytes are immunologically active: they produce cytokines and antimicrobial peptides, respond to injury by initiating wound healing and re-epithelialisation, and synthesise vitamin D in response to sunlight. They interact closely with the epidermis's other residents — melanocytes (pigment), Langerhans cells (immune surveillance) and Merkel cells (touch).

Clinical relevance

Disorders of keratinocyte growth underlie psoriasis, in which accelerated turnover produces scaly plaques; keratinocyte cancers — basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma — are the most common malignancies in humans. Cultured keratinocyte sheets have been used since the 1980s as grafts for extensive burns, and keratinocyte biology is a major model system in cell and stem-cell research.
