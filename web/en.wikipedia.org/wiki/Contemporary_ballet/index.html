Contemporary ballet

Contemporary ballet is a genre of dance that incorporates elements of both classical ballet and modern dance. It allows greater freedom of movement than classical ballet permits, including use of the upper body, floor work, turned-in legs and bare feet, while retaining ballet technique — including pointe work — as its foundation. The genre emerged in the later 20th century as choreographers sought to extend the classical vocabulary.

Development

The seeds of contemporary ballet lie in the neoclassical ballet of George Balanchine, who stripped away sets, costumes and story in works such as Apollo (1928) and emphasised speed and musicality. Choreographers including William Forsythe, Jiří Kylián, Mats Ek and Twyla Tharp pushed further: Forsythe deconstructed classical positions and improvisation structures; Kylián blended ballet with modern and folk influences at Nederlands Dans Theater; Tharp mixed ballet with jazz and popular music. Companies dedicated to the idiom, such as Alonzo King's LINES Ballet and Complexions Contemporary Ballet, made it a distinct category alongside classical and modern companies.

Characteristics

Contemporary ballet choreography often features extreme extensions, off-balance partnering, asymmetry, and a mix of pointe shoes, soft shoes and bare feet within the same work. Music ranges from classical scores to electronic and popular music. Because the term is a hybrid, boundaries are contested: some works labelled contemporary ballet lean heavily towards modern dance, while others remain close to neoclassicism. Critics and companies use the term loosely to describe new choreographic work made on ballet-trained dancers that is not in the 19th-century classical style.
