Isotope fractionation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Isotope fractionation is the partial separation of isotopes of an element during physical or chemical processes, producing substances whose isotopic ratios differ from the starting material. Because isotopes of the same element behave almost identically in chemistry, differences in mass cause slight differences in reaction rates, diffusion, phase changes and biological uptake — effects exploited in geochemistry, climate science, ecology and forensic analysis.

Types

Equilibrium fractionation

Equilibrium fractionation arises from differences in the zero-point energies of molecules containing different isotopes: heavier isotopes concentrate preferentially in compounds with stronger, stiffer bonds. It is strongest at low temperatures and diminishes with heating.

Kinetic fractionation

Kinetic fractionation occurs when lighter isotopes react or diffuse faster than heavier ones, as in evaporation (light isotopes evaporate more readily), diffusion through porous media, or enzymatic reactions. Rayleigh distillation describes the progressive change in the isotopic composition of a reservoir as a product is removed.

Mass-independent fractionation

Most fractionation depends on mass differences, but some reactions (notably certain photochemical reactions of oxygen and sulfur) produce mass-independent signatures that do not scale with mass, providing tracers of atmospheric processes and of the early Earth's oxygen-free atmosphere.

Applications

Isotopic ratios (e.g. 18O/16O, 13C/12C, 15N/14N, D/H) are the basis of palaeoclimate records from ice cores and marine sediments, radiocarbon dating, food-web and migration studies, doping control, and the detection of adulterated food and of greenhouse-gas sources. In medicine, deuterium and carbon-13 tracers are used in diagnostic tests.
