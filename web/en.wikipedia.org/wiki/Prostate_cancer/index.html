Prostate cancer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Prostate cancer is cancer of the prostate, the walnut-sized gland below the bladder in men that contributes fluid to semen. It is among the commonest cancers in men, especially older men, and often grows so slowly that many die with it rather than of it — a fact that shapes its management and screening debates.

Detection and dilemmas

Testing uses the blood marker PSA (prostate-specific antigen) and MRI, with diagnosis by biopsy. PSA screening detects many slow-growing cancers that would never cause harm, leading to overtreatment; active surveillance — monitoring with tests and biopsies — is now standard for many low-risk cases. Symptoms, when they occur, often come from benign prostate enlargement rather than cancer; advanced disease typically spreads to bones.

Treatment

Options range from surveillance to surgery (radical prostatectomy), Radiation therapy, and hormone (androgen deprivation) therapy, since prostate cancer growth is driven by testosterone. Newer hormone drugs, chemotherapy and targeted radiation extend life in metastatic disease. Risk rises with age, family history, African ancestry and inherited BRCA mutations.
