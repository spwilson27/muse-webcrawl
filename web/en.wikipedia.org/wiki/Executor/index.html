An executor carries out the terms of a deceased person's will.

An executor is a person named in a will to carry out its provisions after the testator's death. The executor's duties include applying for probate, identifying and safeguarding the deceased's assets, paying debts, taxes, and funeral expenses, and distributing the remaining estate to the beneficiaries according to the will. Where a person dies without a will (intestate), or without a named executor able to act, a court appoints an administrator to perform the equivalent role; both are classed as personal representatives.

Duties and powers

Executors act in a fiduciary capacity and owe duties of loyalty and care to the estate and its beneficiaries. Typical tasks include notifying banks and agencies of the death, collecting income due, valuing and sometimes selling property, preparing estate accounts, and defending the will if it is contested. An executor may be a family member, a friend, or a professional such as a solicitor or bank; executors are generally entitled to reimbursement of expenses and, if the will so provides or the court allows, a commission or fee. A female executor was traditionally termed an executrix, though the neutral term is now usual.

Liability and removal

Because executors personally represent the estate, they can be liable for losses caused by maladministration ("devastavit"), for example distributing assets before paying creditors or taxes. Beneficiaries may apply to the court to compel an accounting or, in serious cases, to have an executor removed and replaced. Executors may also renounce the role before accepting it ("renunciation of probate"), after which the next named executor or an administrator takes over.
