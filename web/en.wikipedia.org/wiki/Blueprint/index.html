Blueprint

A cyanotype blueprint with white lines on a blue field

A blueprint is a reproduction of a technical drawing or engineering drawing using a contact print process on light-sensitive sheets. Introduced by Sir John Herschel in 1842, the process — cyanotype — allowed rapid and accurate production of an unlimited number of copies of architectural and engineering plans. The result was a negative image: white lines on a deep blue background, which gave the process its name.

Process

The drawing, made on translucent paper or film, was placed over paper coated with a mixture of ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide and exposed to ultraviolet light. Exposed areas turned blue through a photochemical reaction forming Prussian blue pigment, while the shadowed lines remained soluble and were washed away with water, leaving white lines.

Use and decline

Blueprints were the standard means of copying large-format plans in architecture and engineering for more than a century. From the mid-twentieth century they were displaced first by diazo (whiteprint) copies and later by large-format photocopiers, plotters, and digital CAD files. The word "blueprint" survives metaphorically for any detailed plan, scheme, or model for a project.
