Legal citation

Legal citation is the practice of crediting and referring to legal documents and resources, such as constitutions, statutes, reported cases, decisions, books and treatises. Briefs and legal memoranda usually contain many citations, in forms intended to provide information about where the writer found the cited material and how the material supports the writer's argument.

Case citations

A citation to a judicial decision typically identifies the volume, reporter and page where the decision begins, the court and year, and a pinpoint page for a specific proposition — for example, Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954). Neutral or public-domain citations, which refer to the court's own numbering rather than a printed reporter, are used by a growing number of jurisdictions.

Style guides

In the United States, the dominant style guide is The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation, published by the law reviews of Harvard, Yale, Columbia and the University of Pennsylvania. The ALWD Guide to Legal Citation is an alternative. In the United Kingdom, the Oxford Standard for the Citation of Legal Authorities (OSCOLA) is widely used.

Purpose

Citations allow readers to locate and verify authorities, signal the weight of an authority (binding or persuasive), and reflect the system of precedent on which common-law reasoning depends.
