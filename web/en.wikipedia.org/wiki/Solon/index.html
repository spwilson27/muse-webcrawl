Solon

Solon (Greek: Σόλων; c. 630 – c. 560 BC) was an Athenian statesman, lawmaker, political philosopher, and poet. He is credited with having laid the foundations for Athenian democracy. Solon's efforts to legislate against political, economic and moral decline resulted in a set of reforms known as the Solonian Constitution, which included the cancellation of debts (the seisachtheia, or "shaking off of burdens"), the abolition of debt slavery, and the restructuring of Athenian political institutions along wealth-based lines. He was counted among the Seven Sages of Greece.

Life

Solon was born into an aristocratic family, the son of Execestides, and claimed descent from the last kings of Athens. According to Plutarch, he worked as a merchant in his youth, travelling widely and acquiring knowledge of the wider Greek world. He first came to prominence through his poetry, including elegies urging the Athenians to recover the island of Salamis from Megara.

Archonship and reforms

In 594/3 BC Solon was elected archon, with extraordinary powers to mediate between the rich landowners and the impoverished peasantry. Athens was in crisis: many small farmers had fallen into debt, had lost their land, and had even been sold into slavery. Solon's reforms, collectively the seisachtheia, cancelled existing debts, freed those enslaved for debt, and forbade loans secured on the person.

Politically, Solon divided citizens into four property classes — the pentakosiomedimnoi, hippeis, zeugitai and thetes — and distributed political rights according to wealth rather than birth. He created the Council of Four Hundred and enhanced the role of the popular assembly (the ekklesia) and the people's court (the heliaia).

Travels and later life

After completing his reforms, Solon is said to have extracted an oath from the Athenians not to change his laws for ten years and then left Athens to travel — visiting Egypt, Cyprus and Lydia, where Herodotus places his famous encounter with King Croesus. He returned to find Athens falling under the tyranny of Peisistratus, which he opposed but could not prevent. He died around 560 BC.

Poetry

Surviving fragments of Solon's elegiac and iambic verse defend his reforms as a middle course between the demands of the rich and the poor, and articulate his ideal of eunomia (good order).
