Underground mining (hard rock)

Underground mining (hard rock) refers to various underground mining techniques used to excavate "hard" minerals, usually those containing metals, such as ore containing gold, silver, iron, copper, zinc, nickel, tin and lead. It also involves the same techniques used to excavate ores of gems and diamonds. Soft rock mining refers to the excavation of softer minerals, such as coal and longwall mining, or oil shale and other rocks.

Access

Access to an underground mine can be achieved through a decline (ramp), inclined vertical shaft or adit. Declines are commonly used where the orebody is relatively shallow, while vertical shafts are sunk for deeper deposits. Levels are horizontal workings driven along or towards the orebody, and stopes are the excavations from which ore is extracted.

Methods

Common hard rock underground methods include cut and fill stoping, sublevel stoping, room and pillar mining, shrinkage stoping, long hole stoping and vertical crater retreat (VCR). The choice of method depends on the size, shape, orientation and grade of the orebody, the strength of the surrounding rock, and economic factors. Mechanised mining uses jumbos for drilling, load-haul-dump (LHD) vehicles for mucking, and trucks or conveyors to haul ore to the surface.

Ventilation and ground support

Ventilation is critical to supply fresh air, remove blasting fumes and diesel exhaust, and control heat and dust. Ground support — rock bolts, mesh, shotcrete and cable bolts — is installed to stabilise excavations and protect workers from falls of ground.
