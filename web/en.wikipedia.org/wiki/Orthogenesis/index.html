Orthogenesis

Orthogenesis, also known as orthogenetic evolution, progressive evolution, evolutionary progress, or progressionism, is an obsolete biological hypothesis that organisms have an innate tendency to evolve in a definite direction towards some goal (teleology) due to some internal mechanism or "driving force". According to the hypothesis, the largest-scale trends in evolution have an absolute goal such as increasing biological complexity. Prominent historical figures who favoured the theory include Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, Pierre Teilhard de Chardin and Henri Bergson.

Orthogenesis depicted evolution as proceeding along straight, goal-directed lines.

History

The term was coined by Wilhelm Haacke in 1893 and popularised by Theodor Eimer, who applied it to trends such as colouration in butterflies. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when the mechanism of natural selection was in doubt (the "eclipse of Darwinism"), orthogenesis was a serious alternative for many palaeontologists and biologists. Fossil sequences seemed to show straight-line trends — the increasing size and reduced side toes of horses, the ever-larger antlers of the "Irish elk", and the coiling of ammonites — which orthogeneticists read as internally driven momentum that could continue past the point of usefulness and even cause extinction ("racial senescence").

Decline

The modern evolutionary synthesis of the 1930s and 1940s, uniting Mendelian genetics with selection, left no room for an internal drive: George Gaylord Simpson showed that supposed straight-line fossil trends, when examined in full, were branching and contingent, driven by selection and chance. Orthogenesis was largely abandoned by the 1950s, and the term now functions mainly in the history and philosophy of biology. Modern biology recognises long-term trends but explains them through selection, developmental constraint, genetic drift and species sorting rather than intrinsic direction toward a goal.
