Poiesis

Poiesis (Ancient Greek: ποίησις) is a term in ancient Greek philosophy meaning "making", "creation", or "production" — literally, the bringing of something into being that did not exist before. It is the root of the modern word "poetry". In Greek thought, poiesis described any activity in which a person brings something into existence, from the work of the craftsman and the artist to, in Plato's Symposium, procreation and every form of creative production.

In Plato and Aristotle

In the Symposium, the priestess Diotima explains to Socrates that poiesis is broad: every cause that makes something pass from non-being into being is poiesis, so that all craftsmanship qualifies, though usage reserves the name "poet" for those concerned with music and metre. Aristotle, especially in the Poetics and the Nicomachean Ethics, distinguished poiesis from praxis (action or doing). Poiesis has its end outside itself — the product made — whereas praxis has its end in itself, in acting well. Both were in turn distinguished from theoria, contemplation.

Heidegger and later thought

Martin Heidegger revived the term in his philosophy of technology, notably in the essay "The Question Concerning Technology" (1954). For Heidegger, poiesis is a "bringing-forth" (Greek: aletheia, unconcealment) — a revealing in which something comes into presence, as a flower blooms or a silversmith brings forth a chalice. He contrasted this poetic bringing-forth with the "challenging-forth" (Herausfordern) of modern technology, which sets upon nature as standing-reserve. The concept has since been taken up in aesthetics, art therapy (as in the work of Stephen K. Levine), and theories of creative practice, where poiesis denotes the formative, world-disclosing power of making.
