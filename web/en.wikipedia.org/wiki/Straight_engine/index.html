Straight engine

A straight engine (or inline engine) is a piston engine where all of the cylinders are positioned in a row along the crankshaft, in a bank, as opposed to a flat engine, which has cylinders in a horizontal arrangement, or a V engine, which has cylinders in two banks at an angle to each other.

A typical straight-six (inline-six) engine.

Design

A straight engine is considerably easier to build than an otherwise equivalent horizontally opposed or V engine, because both the cylinder bank and crankshaft can be milled from a single metal casting, and it requires fewer cylinder heads and camshafts. Straight engines also tend to be narrower than equivalent flat engines, although they can be taller, especially with overhead camshafts, and engines with more cylinders can be quite long.

Configurations

  • Straight-two — two cylinders; common in motorcycles and small machinery.
  • Straight-three — increasingly common in small cars for fuel economy.
  • Straight-four — the most common configuration in modern cars.
  • Straight-five — used by manufacturers such as Audi and Volvo.
  • Straight-six — inherently balanced and smooth; once common, revived by several manufacturers.
  • Straight-eight — used in luxury and performance cars in the early 20th century.

The straight-six is prized for its inherent primary and secondary balance, which makes it run very smoothly without balance shafts.
