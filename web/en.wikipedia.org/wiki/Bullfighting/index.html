Bullfighting is a physical contest that involves a bullfighter attempting to subdue, immobilise, or kill a bull, usually according to a set of rules, guidelines, or cultural expectations. In its best-known Spanish form, the corrida de toros, a matador faces a fighting bull bred for aggression in a bullring, performing a ritualised sequence of movements with cape and sword before a crowd. Variants exist in Portugal, southern France, and several Latin American countries.

The Spanish corrida

A Spanish-style bullfight is divided into three acts, or tercios, announced by trumpet. In the first, the matador observes the bull's behaviour while picadors on armoured horses weaken its neck muscles with lances. In the second, banderilleros plant barbed darts (banderillas) in the bull's shoulders. In the third, the faena, the matador works alone with a small red cloth (muleta) and a sword, performing passes intended to display artistry and control before killing the bull with a thrust between the shoulder blades. The presiding official may award the matador one or both of the bull's ears — and exceptionally the tail — for an outstanding performance; very rarely, an exceptionally brave bull is pardoned (indultado) and returned to stud.

History

Bull-related spectacles in Iberia date to antiquity, and bullfighting on foot developed in Spain in the 18th century, when Francisco Romero of Ronda is credited with introducing the muleta and the practice of facing the bull on foot. The ritualised modern corrida was standardised in the 19th and 20th centuries by figures such as Juan Belmonte and Manolete, whose deaths in the ring reinforced its mythology. Ernest Hemingway's Death in the Afternoon (1932) introduced the spectacle to a wide English-speaking readership, treating it as tragic art.

Controversy and decline

Bullfighting is fiercely contested. Supporters defend it as an art form and cultural heritage — Spain has protected it by law as part of the national cultural patrimony — and note that fighting bulls live free-range lives far longer than beef cattle. Opponents condemn it as cruelty to animals and a public spectacle of killing. Attendance in Spain has declined for decades, particularly among the young; Catalonia banned the practice in 2010, though Spain's Constitutional Court overturned the ban in 2016, and several Latin American jurisdictions have restricted or abolished corridas. Portuguese bullfighting differs in that the bull is not killed in the ring.
