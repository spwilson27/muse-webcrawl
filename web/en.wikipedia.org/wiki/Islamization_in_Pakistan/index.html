Islamization in Pakistan

Islamization in Pakistan refers to the process by which Islamic law, institutions and symbolism have been incorporated into the state and society of Pakistan. The term is most closely associated with the regime of General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq (1977–1988), who pursued a systematic programme of Islamization after seizing power in a military coup, but its roots lie in Pakistan's founding ideology and the constitutional debates of the 1950s and 1970s.

Background

Pakistan was created in 1947 as a homeland for the Muslims of British India under the "two-nation theory". The 1949 Objectives Resolution declared that sovereignty belongs to Allah and that the state would enable Muslims to order their lives according to the Qur'an and Sunnah; it later became a substantive part of the constitution. In 1974, under Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, parliament declared the Ahmadiyya community non-Muslim, alcohol was banned in 1977, and the weekend was changed in moves partly intended to appease religious parties.

Zia-ul-Haq's programme

Zia, who executed Bhutto in 1979, made Islamization the central legitimising theme of his rule — the policy known as Nizam-e-Mustafa ("the system of the Prophet"). Key measures included:

The Hudood Ordinances (1979) introduced Islamic punishments for offences including theft, adultery (zina) and alcohol consumption, including amputation and stoning in principle. The Zina Ordinance in particular was criticised for conflating rape with adultery and for its effects on women. Amendments to the blasphemy laws in the 1980s made derogatory remarks about the Prophet Muhammad punishable by death (section 295-C of the Pakistan Penal Code). A Federal Shariat Court (1980) was created to review laws for repugnancy to Islam, and the Council of Islamic Ideology gained prominence. Zakat and ushr ordinances (1980) introduced state collection of Islamic alms tax and an agricultural levy, while attempts were made to promote interest-free (riba-free) banking. Education curricula and the media were reoriented toward Islamic content, and the state's patronage of madrassas expanded, a policy accelerated by the Afghan jihad of the 1980s, when Zia's government, with US and Saudi support, armed Afghan mujahideen against the Soviet Union.

Legacy

Later governments retained most of the framework: the Eighth Amendment entrenched Zia-era changes, women's protection legislation in 2006 partially reformed the Hudood regime, and the blasphemy laws remain politically untouchable, with accusations of blasphemy repeatedly sparking mob violence. Critics hold Islamization responsible for sectarian militancy, discrimination against minorities and the empowerment of religious parties; supporters argue it reflects the will of Pakistan's Muslim majority and the country's raison d'être.
