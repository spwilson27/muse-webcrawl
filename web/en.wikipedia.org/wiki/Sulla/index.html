Sulla

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Born: 138 BC, Rome
Died: 78 BC (aged 59–60), Puteoli
Offices: Consul (88, 80 BC); Dictator (82–79 BC)
Known for: March on Rome; proscriptions; constitutional reforms

Lucius Cornelius Sulla Felix (138–78 BC), commonly known as Sulla, was a Roman general and statesman who won the first full-scale civil war in Roman history and seized power as dictator. His marches on Rome and his bloody proscriptions broke long-standing republican norms and set precedents that later strongmen, including Julius Caesar, would follow.

Early career

Born into an impoverished branch of the patrician Cornelii, Sulla rose through military talent and good fortune. Serving under Gaius Marius in the Jugurthine War, he negotiated the capture of Jugurtha in 105 BC, an episode that sowed rivalry between the two men. He distinguished himself in the Social War (91–88 BC) and was elected consul for 88 BC, receiving the command against Mithridates VI of Pontus.

The march on Rome and civil war

When the tribune Sulpicius transferred the eastern command to Marius, Sulla led his legions on Rome — the first time a Roman general had done so. He then departed for the First Mithridatic War, defeating Mithridates and imposing the Peace of Dardanus. Meanwhile the Marian faction retook Rome; on his return in 83 BC Sulla fought a second civil war, winning the decisive Battle of the Colline Gate in November 82 BC.

Dictatorship and proscriptions

Appointed dictator "for the making of laws and the settling of the constitution", Sulla published proscription lists offering rewards for the killing of his enemies; thousands of senators, equestrians, and others were murdered and their property confiscated. His reforms strengthened the Senate, curtailed the powers of the tribunes, and reorganised the courts. Having reshaped the state, Sulla astonished contemporaries by resigning the dictatorship in 79 BC and retiring to his estate at Puteoli, where he died the following year.

Legacy

Sulla's career demonstrated that a Roman army could be loyal to its commander rather than the republic, and his settlement largely unravelled within a decade. His memoirs, now lost, were an important source for later historians such as Plutarch.
