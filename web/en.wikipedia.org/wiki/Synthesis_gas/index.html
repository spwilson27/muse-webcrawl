Synthesis gas

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Synthesis gas, or syngas, is a mixture of hydrogen and carbon monoxide, in various ratios. The gas often contains some carbon dioxide and methane. It is principally used for producing ammonia or methanol, which are themselves used in the production of synthetic petrol and other chemicals. Syngas is combustible and can be used as a fuel; historically it was used as a replacement for gasoline, when gasoline supply has been limited.

The name comes from its use as an intermediate in creating synthetic natural gas and for producing ammonia or methanol. Syngas is usually a product of gasification and the main application is electricity generation. It is also a key intermediate in the gas-to-liquids production of hydrocarbons.

Production

The chemical composition of syngas varies based on the raw materials and the processes. Syngas produced by coal gasification generally is a mixture of 30 to 60% carbon monoxide, 25 to 30% hydrogen, 5 to 15% carbon dioxide, and up to 5% methane. Steam reforming of natural gas, CH4 + H2O ⇌ CO + 3 H2, gives a hydrogen-rich gas well suited to ammonia and hydrogen manufacture, while partial oxidation of hydrocarbons yields a more CO-rich product. The water–gas shift reaction, CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2, is used to adjust the H2:CO ratio for downstream processes.

Applications

Syngas is the feedstock for the Haber–Bosch ammonia synthesis (after shift conversion and CO2 removal), for methanol synthesis over copper–zinc oxide catalysts, and for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis of liquid hydrocarbons. It is also used in the production of synthetic natural gas, in direct reduced iron steelmaking, and increasingly as an intermediate in biomass and waste conversion, where gasification of diverse feedstocks produces a gas that must be cleaned of tars and contaminants before use.

History

Gas mixtures rich in hydrogen and carbon monoxide were produced from coal in the nineteenth century as "town gas" for lighting and heating, and water gas served as an industrial fuel gas. The modern concept of syngas as a chemical intermediate dates from the development of ammonia and methanol synthesis in the early twentieth century and the Fischer–Tropsch process in the 1920s.
