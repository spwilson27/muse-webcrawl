In folklore, a mermaid is an aquatic creature with the head and upper body of a female human and the tail of a fish. Mermaids appear in the folklore of many cultures worldwide, including Europe, Latin America, Asia and Africa. The male equivalent is a merman; the collective term is merfolk.

Characteristics and folklore

Sometimes mermaids are associated with perilous events such as floods, storms, shipwrecks and drownings; in other traditions they are benevolent or beneficent, bestowing boons or falling in love with humans. Like the sirens of Greek mythology, mermaids are sometimes depicted as luring sailors to their deaths with enchanting music and song. In Hans Christian Andersen's fairy tale "The Little Mermaid" (1837), a mermaid gives up her life in the sea in pursuit of a human soul; the story inspired the Copenhagen statue and Disney's 1989 animated film.

Origins and analogues

The earliest mermaid-like figures may include the Assyrian goddess Atargatis and the Greek Tritons and Nereids. Across cultures, related beings include the Slavic rusalka, the Japanese ningyo, the West African water spirit Mami Wata, and the Irish merrow. Sightings reported by sailors, including Christopher Columbus's report in 1493 of "female forms" in the Caribbean, are now generally attributed to manatees and other sirenians — the order Sirenia takes its name from the sirens.

Hoaxes

Famous fabricated mermaids include the "Fiji mermaid" exhibited by P. T. Barnum in 1842, a composite of a monkey's torso and a fish's tail, and specimens made in Japan and the Balkans from stitched animal parts. The Discovery Channel mockumentary Mermaids: The Body Found (2012) prompted the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration to issue a statement that no evidence of aquatic humanoids has ever been found.
