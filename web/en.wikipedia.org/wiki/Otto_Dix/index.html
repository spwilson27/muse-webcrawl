Wilhelm Heinrich Otto Dix (2 December 1891 – 25 July 1969) was a German painter and printmaker, noted for his ruthless and harshly realistic depictions of German society during the Weimar Republic and of the brutality of war. Together with George Grosz, he is widely considered one of the most important artists of the Neue Sachlichkeit (New Objectivity).

War experience and early work

Dix volunteered for the German Army in 1914 and served as a machine-gunner on both the Western and Eastern fronts, an experience that haunted his art for the rest of his life. After the war he studied in Dresden and Düsseldorf, moved through Expressionism and Dada, and produced searing images of crippled veterans, prostitutes and murder scenes, including The Skat Players (1920) and the fifty-print cycle The War (1924).

Mature work

In the later 1920s Dix became the portraitist of Weimar Germany—works such as the Portrait of the Journalist Sylvia von Harden (1926) combined Old Master technique with unsparing social observation. His monumental triptych The War (1929–1932), now in Dresden, juxtaposes soldiers marching to battle, a corpse-strewn battlefield and the artist himself as a rescuer, in the format of a religious altarpiece.

Nazi era and later life

The Nazis dismissed Dix from his Dresden teaching post in 1933 and condemned his work as "degenerate art"; hundreds of his paintings were confiscated and some burned. He withdrew to internal exile at Lake Constance, painting landscapes, and in 1945 was conscripted into the Volkssturm and taken prisoner by the French. After the war he received honours in both East and West Germany and divided his time between Dresden and the west. He died in Singen in 1969.
