Liturgy of the Hours

The Liturgy of the Hours (Latin: Liturgia Horarum), also known as the Divine Office (Latin: Officium Divinum) or the Work of God (Opus Dei), is the daily prayer of the Catholic Church, comprising a cycle of psalms, hymns, scripture readings and intercessions to be prayed at fixed hours throughout the day. Together with the Mass it constitutes the official public prayer life of the Church, sanctifying the hours of each day and the whole course of the liturgical year.

Priests and deacons are obliged to pray the Liturgy of the Hours daily; religious communities celebrate it in common, and lay people are encouraged to join in it as the prayer of the whole Church.

Structure

Each hour typically consists of an opening versicle ("O God, come to my assistance; O Lord, make haste to help me"), a hymn, psalmody drawn on a four-week cycle through the Psalter, a short reading from Scripture, a responsory, one of the Gospel canticles (Benedictus at Lauds, Magnificat at Vespers, Nunc dimittis at Compline), intercessions, the Lord's Prayer and a concluding prayer.

History

The practice developed from the Jewish pattern of prayer at set hours and the early Church's morning and evening prayer. St Benedict's Rule (c. 530) gave the monastic office its classic shape, with its famous phrase opus Dei — "the work of God" — to which nothing was to be preferred. The Roman breviary standardised the office for secular clergy; Pius V issued an official breviary in 1568. Following the Second Vatican Council, Paul VI promulgated a thoroughly revised Liturgy of the Hours in 1970–1971, restoring Lauds and Vespers as the "hinge" hours of the day and arranging the psalter over four weeks.

In other churches

Parallel traditions exist in Eastern Christianity (the cycle of Vespers, Compline, Matins and the Hours) and in Anglicanism, where the Book of Common Prayer distilled the offices into Morning Prayer and Evening Prayer (Mattins and Evensong), still sung daily in cathedrals.
