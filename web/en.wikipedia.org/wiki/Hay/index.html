Hay

Hay is grass, legumes, or other herbaceous plants that have been cut and dried to be stored for use as animal fodder, either for large grazing animals raised as livestock, such as cattle, horses, goats, and sheep, or for smaller domesticated animals such as rabbits and guinea pigs. Pigs can eat hay, but do not digest it as efficiently as herbivores do.

Production

Hay production involves cutting forage plants — commonly grasses such as timothy, alfalfa (lucerne), and clover — and allowing them to dry in the field, a process called curing. Traditionally this was done by hand with scythes and hay rakes; modern production uses mowers, tedders, and balers that compress the dried material into round or rectangular bales for storage. Hay must be dried sufficiently (typically below about 15–20% moisture) to prevent mould growth and spontaneous combustion in storage.

Composition and quality

Good hay is judged by its leafiness, colour, smell, and the maturity of the plants at cutting. Legume hays such as alfalfa are richer in protein and calcium than grass hays. Hay that is cut late, rained on, or stored damp loses nutritional value and may develop dangerous moulds.

History

The storage of dried forage for winter feeding has been essential to animal husbandry in temperate climates since antiquity. The hay meadow, managed by annual cutting rather than grazing, created distinctive semi-natural grassland habitats. Mechanisation in the 19th and 20th centuries — the mower, the horse-drawn rake, and the baler — transformed haymaking from one of the most labour-intensive tasks of the farming year.
