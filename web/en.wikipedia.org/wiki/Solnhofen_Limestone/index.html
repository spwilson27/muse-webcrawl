Solnhofen Plattenkalk

TypeSedimentary rock (lithographic limestone)
AgeLate Jurassic (Tithonian), ~150 million years
LocationBavaria, Germany (Altmühl valley)

The Solnhofen Limestone (German: Solnhofener Plattenkalk) is a Jurassic Konservat-Lagerstätte in Bavaria, Germany, that preserves a rare assemblage of fossilised organisms, including highly detailed imprints of soft-tissued organisms such as sea jellies. The most famous fossil from the Solnhofen is the early bird Archaeopteryx. Composed of very fine-grained limestone that formed in lagoons, the rock preserves fossils in extraordinary detail and has been quarried for centuries, first for roofing tiles and later as lithographic stone.

Geology

During the Late Jurassic, around 150 million years ago, the region lay at the edge of the Tethys Ocean in a warm, shallow archipelago. Sponge–algal reefs grew around quiet lagoons whose bottom waters were hypersaline and depleted in oxygen. Organisms that died or were swept into the lagoons sank to the bottom, where the absence of scavengers and currents allowed them to be buried intact in fine carbonate mud. The resulting micritic limestone splits into thin, even slabs, many of which bear perfectly preserved fossils.

Fossils

More than 600 species have been identified from the Solnhofen deposits, including ammonites, belemnites, crustaceans, fish, marine reptiles, pterosaurs and the celebrated Archaeopteryx, of which about a dozen skeletal specimens are known, all from the Solnhofen area. Land animals and plants are rare and were washed in from nearby islands covered in scrubby vegetation.

Quarrying and lithography

The stone's fine, even texture made it ideal for lithographic printing after Alois Senefelder invented the process in the late 18th century. Solnhofen stone remained the standard lithographic material well into the 19th century; today the quarries supply mainly tile and building stone, and fossils continue to be found by quarry workers and collectors.
