A false awakening is a vivid and convincing dream about awakening from sleep, while the dreamer in fact remains asleep. After a false awakening, subjects often dream they are performing daily morning routines such as showering, making breakfast, or leaving for work. False awakenings may be followed by real awakenings, or may continue into another dream, a lucid dream, or sleep paralysis.

The concept was identified by British philosopher and psychical researcher Celia Green in her 1968 book Lucid Dreams, based on correspondence with dreamers who reported such experiences. She distinguished false awakenings from ordinary dreams by their unusual realism: the dreamt bedroom is typically the dreamer's actual room, reproduced in convincing detail.

Types

Green and later researchers described two broad types. In Type 1 false awakenings, the dreamer "wakes" into a realistic mundane scene and proceeds with ordinary activities; nothing seems amiss until some detail or the eventual real awakening reveals the deception. In Type 2, the atmosphere is charged or ominous: the dreamer senses that something is wrong, with an eerie stillness or a feeling of menace, and the episode may shade into sleep paralysis with hallucinated presences.

Loops

False awakenings can occur in loops: the dreamer appears to wake, begins the morning routine, then "wakes" again, sometimes repeatedly, each time believing the awakening is real. Such loops have been reported particularly by lucid dreamers, whose attempts to stabilise or exit a lucid dream can collapse into another simulated awakening.

Explanation

False awakenings occur during or at the end of REM sleep and are thought to arise as the brain begins generating the expectancy of waking — simulating the most familiar environment, the bedroom, as consciousness partially returns while dreaming persists. They are more commonly reported during morning sleep, daytime naps, and periods of sleep disruption. Reality testing techniques taught to lucid dreamers — such as reading text twice or checking a clock — are commonly recommended to detect them.

In culture

The experience appears in literature and film as a device for nested unreality, for example in stories in which a character repeatedly "wakes" into new dreams, and it has been linked speculatively to philosophical thought experiments about the certainty of one's own waking state.
