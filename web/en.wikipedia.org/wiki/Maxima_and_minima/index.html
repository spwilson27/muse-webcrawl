Maxima and minima

In mathematical analysis, the maxima and minima (singular: maximum and minimum) of a function are respectively the largest and smallest values the function takes, either within a given neighbourhood (local or relative extrema) or over the entire domain (global or absolute extrema). Together they are known as extrema (singular: extremum). Pierre de Fermat was one of the first mathematicians to formulate a general method (adequality) for finding maxima and minima.

Definitions

A function f defined on a set X has a global maximum at x* if f(x*) ≥ f(x) for all x in X; it has a local maximum at x* if the inequality holds for all x in some neighbourhood of x*. The definitions for minima reverse the inequalities. A point at which a local extremum occurs is called an extremal point.

Finding extrema

For a differentiable function of one variable, Fermat's theorem states that local extrema in the interior of the domain occur at critical points where the derivative is zero or undefined. The first derivative test and second derivative test help classify such points: if the second derivative at a critical point is positive, the point is a local minimum; if negative, a local maximum. Functions of several variables use partial derivatives, the gradient and the Hessian matrix. On a closed bounded interval, extrema may also occur at the endpoints; by the extreme value theorem, a continuous function on such an interval always attains a global maximum and minimum.

Applications

Finding maxima and minima — optimisation — is fundamental throughout mathematics, physics, economics and engineering: minimising cost or energy, maximising profit, likelihood or entropy, and locating equilibrium states. Non-smooth and discrete settings lead to methods such as linear programming and combinatorial optimisation.
