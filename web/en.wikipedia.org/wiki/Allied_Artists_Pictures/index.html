Allied Artists Pictures Corporation was an American film production and distribution company. It grew out of Monogram Pictures, a leading "Poverty Row" B-movie studio: in 1946 Monogram's executives created Allied Artists as a subsidiary for higher-budget productions, and in 1953 the whole company adopted the Allied Artists name, though inexpensive genre pictures remained its staple until bankruptcy in 1979.

History

Monogram, founded in 1931, produced westerns, Bowery Boys comedies and Charlie Chan and Cisco Kid series pictures. The Allied Artists brand was launched to signal prestige: early successes included It Happened on Fifth Avenue (1947). In the 1950s, under president Steve Broidy, the renamed studio mixed B-pictures such as Invasion of the Body Snatchers (1956) and Roger Corman's early films with prestige projects from directors including John Huston (Moulin Rouge, 1952), William Wyler (Friendly Persuasion, 1956) and Billy Wilder (Love in the Afternoon, 1957).

Later years

Television production and distribution sustained the company in the 1960s, and it scored late theatrical successes distributing Cabaret (1972) and releasing Papillon (1973) and The Man Who Would Be King (1975). Heavy losses on other ventures followed; Allied Artists filed for bankruptcy in 1979, and its library passed through several owners, with much of the post-1938 catalogue eventually held by major studio libraries.
