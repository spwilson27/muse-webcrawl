Dominion theology

Dominion theology, also known as dominionism, is a group of Christian political ideologies that seek to institute a nation governed by Christians and based on their understandings of biblical law. The ideologies draw on the "dominion mandate" of Genesis 1:28, in which God grants humankind dominion over the earth. Extents of rule and ways of achieving governing authority vary significantly between adherents.

Origins and forms

Dominion theology is principally a phenomenon of conservative evangelical and fundamentalist Christianity in the United States. Its most systematic form is Christian Reconstructionism, developed by R. J. Rushdoony and others from the 1960s, which advocates theonomy — the application of Old Testament law to modern society. A softer, charismatic strand emerged from the Latter Rain movement and figures such as C. Peter Wagner; the associated Seven Mountain Mandate teaches that Christians should take influence over seven spheres of society — religion, family, education, government, media, arts and entertainment, and business — in order to transform nations. The New Apostolic Reformation is frequently linked to this strand.

Beliefs

Most dominionists hold postmillennial eschatology: the belief that Christ will return after Christians have established God's kingdom on earth. They typically regard secular government, public education and pluralist democracy with suspicion, and many seek to reshape law and culture along biblical lines, though they differ sharply on whether this should occur through democratic politics, cultural influence or legal reconstruction. The term "dominionist" is rarely a self-description; many so labelled reject it, and scholars debate how widely the label applies.

Criticism

Critics, including other Christians, argue that dominion theology conflicts with religious freedom and the separation of church and state, and that its harder forms would impose a theocratic order. Defenders of the label's targets respond that ordinary Christian political engagement — voting, advocacy and holding office — is being unfairly conflated with theocracy.
