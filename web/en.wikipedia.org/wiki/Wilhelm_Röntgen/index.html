Wilhelm Röntgen

Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen (27 March 1845 – 10 February 1923) was a German mechanist and physicist who produced and detected electromagnetic radiation in a wavelength range known as X-rays. His discovery, made in November 1895, revolutionised medicine and physics; in 1901 he received the first Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

Discovery of X-rays

On 8 November 1895, while experimenting with cathode rays from a Crookes tube covered in black cardboard, Röntgen noticed that a nearby screen coated with barium platinocyanide fluoresced even though no visible light reached it. He concluded that the tube emitted a new, invisible, highly penetrating kind of ray, which he called "X" for unknown. Over the following weeks he investigated the rays systematically, and on 22 December 1895 he produced the famous radiograph of his wife Anna Bertha's hand, showing the bones and her ring.

His paper "On a New Kind of Rays" was published within weeks and spread around the world with extraordinary speed; within a year X-rays were in clinical use. Röntgen refused to patent his discovery, believing it should benefit humanity freely, and donated his Nobel Prize money to his university.

Later life

Röntgen spent most of his career at Würzburg and later Munich, working on the properties of crystals, gases, and liquids with characteristic precision. He died of intestinal cancer in Munich in 1923. The element roentgenium (atomic number 111) and the unit of radiation exposure, the roentgen, are named in his honour; X-rays are still called "Röntgen rays" in German and many other languages.
