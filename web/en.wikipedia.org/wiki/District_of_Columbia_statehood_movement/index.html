The District of Columbia statehood movement is a political movement that advocates making the District of Columbia a U.S. state, to provide the residents of the federal district with voting representation in Congress and full self-government. The District's roughly 700,000 residents — more than the population of Wyoming or Vermont — pay federal income taxes and serve in the military but elect only a non-voting delegate to the House of Representatives and no senators.

Background

The Constitution gives Congress exclusive legislative authority over the federal district. Residents gained the right to vote for President only with the Twenty-third Amendment (1961) and an elected mayor and council under the Home Rule Act of 1973, though Congress retains authority to review and overturn local laws and budgets. Critics of the status quo invoke the revolutionary-era slogan "taxation without representation", which appears on D.C. license plates.

Recent efforts

In a 2016 referendum, over 85% of D.C. voters supported statehood. The Washington, D.C. Admission Act (H.R. 51), which would admit the residential areas as the "Washington, Douglass Commonwealth" (honouring Frederick Douglass) while retaining a small federal district around the Capitol and White House, passed the House of Representatives in 2020 and again in 2021 but did not advance in the Senate. Opponents argue that the Constitution requires amendment for D.C. statehood or favour retrocession to Maryland; supporters respond that Congress may admit new states by ordinary legislation.
