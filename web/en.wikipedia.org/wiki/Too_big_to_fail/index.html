Too big to fail
Concept: Institutions whose failure would be catastrophic
Popularised: 1980s; 2008 financial crisis
Responses: Dodd–Frank Act, Basel III, resolution regimes

"Too big to fail" (TBTF) is a theory in banking and finance that certain corporations, particularly financial institutions, are so large and interconnected that their failure would be disastrous to the wider economy, and therefore governments should support them when they face potential failure.

History

The term was popularised by US Congressman Stewart McKinney in 1984 hearings on the rescue of Continental Illinois. It became central in the 2008 financial crisis, when the US government bailed out firms such as AIG and large banks, while Lehman Brothers was allowed to fail in September 2008 — triggering a global panic that strengthened the TBTF debate.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue TBTF creates moral hazard: implicitly guaranteed firms borrow more cheaply and take greater risks. Reforms include higher capital requirements under Basel III, stress tests, living wills, orderly liquidation authority under the 2010 Dodd–Frank Act, and designations of systemically important financial institutions (SIFIs).
