Slenderness ratio

In architecture and structural engineering, a slenderness ratio is the ratio between the length of a column and its least cross-sectional dimension (or radius of gyration). It measures a column's susceptibility to buckling: the higher the ratio, the more likely the member is to fail by elastic instability rather than by crushing of the material.

Formally, the slenderness ratio is often written λ = KL/r, where L is the unsupported length, K is the effective-length factor accounting for end conditions, and r is the least radius of gyration of the cross-section. Building codes classify columns as "short" or "long (slender)" using limiting values of λ; Euler's formula for critical buckling load applies to slender columns, while empirical parabolic formulas (such as the Johnson formula) are used for intermediate slenderness.

Other uses

The term is also used in other contexts: in building design, for the ratio of a skyscraper's height to its width (Manhattan's modern "pencil towers" exceed ratios of 1:20); in ballistics and fluid mechanics for elongated bodies; and in biology for body proportions of animals and plant stems.
