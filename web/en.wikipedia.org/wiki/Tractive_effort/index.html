Tractive effort

Tractive effort (also tractive force) is the force generated by a vehicle's engine or motor at the driving wheels, used to pull or propel loads. The term is most commonly applied to locomotives, where it measures the pulling force exerted at the wheel rims, and hence the weight of train the locomotive can start and haul.

Railway use

For a steam locomotive, a common nominal formula estimates tractive effort from boiler pressure, cylinder dimensions and driving wheel diameter. Starting tractive effort is the maximum force available from rest, while continuous tractive effort is the force that can be sustained at speed, limited by the power of the locomotive: as speed rises, tractive effort falls, since power equals force multiplied by speed.

The usable tractive effort is capped by adhesion: the friction between the driving wheels and the rail. The ratio of weight on the driving wheels to starting tractive effort is the factor of adhesion, typically around 4 to 4.5 for steam locomotives; a lower value means the locomotive is prone to slipping.

Road vehicles

In road vehicles, tractive effort at the driven wheels must overcome rolling resistance, gradient resistance and aerodynamic drag. The concept underlies the design of tractors, where drawbar pull — tractive effort minus the force needed to move the tractor itself — determines working capacity.
