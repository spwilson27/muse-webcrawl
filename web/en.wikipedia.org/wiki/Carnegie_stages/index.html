The Carnegie stages are a standardized system for describing the development of the vertebrate embryo, used especially for the human embryo. The system divides the first roughly 60 days of development into 23 stages, defined not by chronological age or size but by the appearance of specific morphological structures, so that embryos of different ages and even different species can be compared directly.

History

The staging system grew out of the great embryo collection assembled at the Carnegie Institution of Washington's Department of Embryology in Baltimore, founded by Franklin P. Mall. George L. Streeter published the developmental "horizons" in the 1940s, later revised and formalized as Carnegie stages by Ronan O'Rahilly and Fabiola Müller in works published between 1973 and 1987. The reference specimens of the Carnegie Collection are now held by the National Museum of Health and Medicine in Washington, D.C.

The stages

Stage 1 begins with fertilization; cleavage and blastocyst formation occupy stages 2–3; implantation occurs at stages 4–5; gastrulation at stages 6–8; and the folding of the embryo and formation of the neural tube, somites, heart, and limb buds through stages 9–23. Stage 23, reached at about day 56–60, marks the end of the embryonic period proper, after which the developing human is conventionally called a fetus. Because stages are defined by structures—such as the number of somites or the appearance of the hand and foot plates—they remain useful even when the age of a specimen is unknown.
