QuestionWhether Puerto Rico was part of the United States for tariff purposes under the Uniformity Clause

Downes v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 244 (1901), was a landmark United States Supreme Court case and one of the Insular Cases. It concerned the constitutional status of territories acquired after the Spanish–American War, in particular Puerto Rico.


Downes v. Bidwell is a leading Insular Case.

Background and holding

A merchant, Samuel Downes, challenged duties imposed under the Foraker Act on oranges shipped from Puerto Rico to New York, arguing they violated the Constitution's Uniformity Clause because Puerto Rico was part of the United States. In a fractured 5–4 decision, the Court upheld the duties, reasoning that Puerto Rico was a territory "appurtenant and belonging to" the United States but not part of it for revenue purposes.

Doctrine

Justice Edward White's concurrence introduced the doctrine of territorial incorporation — distinguishing "incorporated" territories destined for statehood, to which the Constitution applies in full, from "unincorporated" territories, where only fundamental rights apply. The Insular Cases remain controversial and have been criticised by modern justices, but they still underpin the legal status of Puerto Rico, Guam and other U.S. territories.
