Thomas Wakley (11 July 1795 – 16 May 1862) was an English surgeon and social reformer, best known as the founder of The Lancet, one of the world's oldest and most influential medical journals, which he established in 1823. As editor he campaigned against nepotism and incompetence in the medical profession, exposed malpractice, and pushed for reform of medical education and licensing.

The Lancet

Wakley launched The Lancet in October 1823, publishing verbatim lecture notes from leading London surgeons — a practice the lecturers tried to stop through the courts. The journal's campaigns contributed to the passage of the Medical Act 1858, which created a register of qualified practitioners.

Politics and coronership

A radical, Wakley was elected MP for Finsbury in 1835 and served until 1852, advocating parliamentary reform, abolition of the Corn Laws, and reform of the Poor Law. As coroner for West Middlesex he reformed inquest procedures, insisted on medical evidence, and investigated deaths in workhouses and factories.

Legacy

Wakley died in Madeira in 1862. The Lancet continues to publish, and the Wakley Prize for essays is awarded in his memory.
