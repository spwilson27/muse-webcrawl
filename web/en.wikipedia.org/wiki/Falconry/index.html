Falconry is the hunting of wild animals in their natural state and habitat by means of a trained bird of prey. Small animals are hunted; squirrels and rabbits often fall prey to these birds. Two traditional terms are used to describe a person involved in falconry: a "falconer" flies a falcon, an "austringer" (Old French) flies a hawk or an eagle. In modern falconry, the red-tailed hawk (Buteo jamaicensis), Harris's hawk (Parabuteo unicinctus), and the peregrine falcon (Falco peregrinus) are some of the more commonly used birds of prey.

The practice of hunting with a conditioned falconry bird is also called "hawking" or "gamehawking", although the words "hawking" and "hawker" have become used so much to refer to petty traveling traders, that the terms "falconer" and "falconry" now apply to most use of trained birds of prey to catch game. However, many contemporary practitioners still use these words in their original meaning.

In early English falconry literature, the word "falcon" referred to a female peregrine falcon only, while the word "hawk" or "eyas" referred to a female hawk. A male hawk or falcon was referred to as a "tiercel" (sometimes spelled "tercel"), as it was roughly one-third (tierce) smaller than the female. A falcon which has been used in falconry for a year is termed an "intermewed" hawk.

History

Falconry is thought to have originated in Central Asia or the ancient Middle East, possibly as early as 4000 BCE, although the earliest firm evidence is later. A bas-relief found at Khorsabad, in the palace of Sargon II of Assyria (r. 722–705 BCE), is often cited as an early depiction. The art spread east to China and west through Persia into the Islamic world, where it flourished; the medieval Arabic treatise tradition produced detailed works on the care and training of hawks.

In Europe, falconry became a highly codified aristocratic pursuit during the Middle Ages. The Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II (1194–1250), an ardent falconer, wrote De Arte Venandi cum Avibus ("The Art of Hunting with Birds"), a landmark treatise based on his own observations. In England, the Boke of St Albans (1486) assigned particular birds to particular ranks of society, an elaborate hierarchy that has become famous, though its strictness is debated by historians.

Falconry declined in much of Europe with the spread of firearms in the 17th and 18th centuries, but it survived in the Arabian Peninsula — where hunting with sakers and peregrines remains culturally central — and was revived in Britain and North America in the 20th century. In 2010, falconry was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, initially for eleven countries and later expanded.

Birds used

Birds used in falconry include:

  • Peregrine falcon (Falco peregrinus) — famed for the high-speed stoop, used mainly for game birds in open country.
  • Gyrfalcon (Falco rusticolus) — the largest falcon, historically the bird of kings.
  • Saker falcon (Falco cherrug) — traditional in Arabia and Central Asia.
  • Northern goshawk (Accipiter gentilis) — a short-winged accipiter flown in wooded country at rabbits and pheasants.
  • Harris's hawk — unusual among raptors for its social hunting behaviour.
  • Red-tailed hawk — the commonest beginner's bird in North America.
  • Golden eagle — flown in Central Asia, famously by the Kazakh and Kyrgyz berkutchi, at foxes and even wolves.

Equipment and terminology

Traditional equipment includes the hood, which calms the bird; jesses, leather straps on the legs; bells and modern telemetry transmitters for locating a bird in flight; the lure, a padded dummy garnished with meat used to recall the bird; the heavy leather glove or gauntlet; and the mews, the housing where birds are kept. Falconry has given English a rich vocabulary: "mews", "haggard", "bate", "mantle", and phrases such as "under the thumb", "fed up", and "hoodwinked" are popularly traced to the mews, though some of these etymologies are folk traditions.

Regulation

Falconry is legally regulated in many countries. In the United States it is governed by federal and state law, with apprenticeship, general, and master classes of permit, administered in cooperation with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act framework. In the United Kingdom, falconry itself requires no licence to fly, but birds must be registered and close-ringed or microchipped under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 and CITES rules.
