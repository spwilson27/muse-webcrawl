Tide mill

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tide mill is a water mill driven by tidal rise and fall. A dam with a sluice is created across a suitable tidal inlet, or a section of river estuary is made into a reservoir. As the tide comes in, it enters the mill pond through a one-way gate, and this gate closes automatically when the tide begins to fall. When the tide is low enough, the stored water can be released to turn a water wheel.

History

Tide mills are among the oldest tide-powered devices, with possible Roman precedents and securely dated examples from early medieval Ireland: a mill at Nendrum Monastery has been dated to 787 AD. Tide mills spread around the coasts of Europe, especially in England, France, Spain and Portugal, and later on the Atlantic coast of North America. The Domesday Book records tide mills in England, and London's tidal mills on the Fleet and the Thames operated for centuries.

Numbers peaked in the 18th and 19th centuries — there were several hundred in Britain and around New England — before steam and later electric power displaced them. A few survive in working order, including Eling Tide Mill in Hampshire and Woodbridge Tide Mill in Suffolk.

Operation

The mill can work only for a few hours around low tide, when there is sufficient head difference between the pond and the sea, so millers' hours were dictated by the tidal cycle. Some mills used two ponds or larger wheels to extend the working period. The principle is related to modern tidal barrage power generation, of which the Rance Tidal Power Station in Brittany is the outstanding example.
