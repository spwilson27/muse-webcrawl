Guardian of Scotland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Guardians of Scotland were regents who governed the Kingdom of Scotland during periods when the monarch was a minor or absent, most notably during the interregna of 1286–1292 and 1296–1306 in the First War of Scottish Independence. The office was exercised collectively or by individuals acting in the name of the "community of the realm".

The first Guardians, 1286–1292

When Alexander III died in 1286, his heir was his three-year-old granddaughter Margaret, the Maid of Norway. Six guardians — two bishops, two earls, and two barons, representing the regions of Scotland — were elected by a parliament at Scone to govern in her name. Their seal depicted Saint Andrew and the royal arms with the inscription "the seal of Scotland appointed for the government of the kingdom". Margaret's death in 1290 triggered the succession crisis known as the Great Cause, judged by Edward I of England, who imposed John Balliol as king in 1292.

The Wars of Independence

After Edward deposed Balliol in 1296, resistance leaders governed as Guardians: William Wallace and Andrew Moray after their victory at Stirling Bridge (1297), and later Robert the Bruce and John Comyn jointly. The Guardians issued documents in the name of the absent king and the community of the realm — a striking assertion that authority resided in the kingdom itself. The guardianship continued in various hands until Bruce seized the throne in 1306.

Later use

Guardians were again appointed during the minority of David II and in the Second War of Independence, including Andrew Murray of Bothwell and Archibald Douglas. The concept of guardianship left a lasting mark on Scottish constitutional thought, later echoed in the Declaration of Arbroath (1320).
