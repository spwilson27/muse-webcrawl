Pressure flaking

Pressure flaking is a technique for shaping stone tools in which flakes are removed by applying steady pressure to the edge of a stone blank with a pointed tool, rather than by striking it. It allows fine, controlled shaping and was used to finish projectile points, knives, and other bifacial tools in many prehistoric cultures.

Technique

The knapper braces the blank and presses the tip of a tool — traditionally antler, bone, or hardwood, and today often a copper-tipped flaker — against its edge until a small flake detaches. Repeated removals thin and sharpen the edge and can produce regular serrations, notches, and invasive surface retouch.

Archaeological examples

Sophisticated pressure flaking appears in the Upper Paleolithic, notably in the finely worked laurel-leaf points of the Solutrean culture of France and Spain, and reaches high refinement in Neolithic Egyptian flint knives, Danish daggers, and the gunflints of early modern Europe. The Australian Aboriginal craftsman and the modern experimental knapper Ishi are also known for the technique.
