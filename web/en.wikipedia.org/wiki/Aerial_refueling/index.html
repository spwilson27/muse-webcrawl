Aerial refueling, also known as air refueling, in-flight refueling or air-to-air refueling, is the process of transferring aviation fuel from one aircraft (the tanker) to another (the receiver) during flight. It allows receiving aircraft to remain airborne longer, extend their range, and take off with greater payloads, since fuel can be topped up after departure.

Systems

Two principal systems are in use. In the flying boom system, used chiefly by the United States Air Force, a rigid telescoping boom is flown by a boom operator into a receptacle on the receiver, permitting high fuel transfer rates. In the probe-and-drogue system, used by the US Navy and most other air forces, the tanker trails a flexible hose with a stabilising drogue into which the receiver flies its fixed probe; this allows helicopters and multiple simultaneous receivers, at lower transfer rates.

History

Experiments date to the 1920s, including daring hose transfers between biplanes; the US Army Air Corps' Question Mark endurance flight of 1929 demonstrated practical refuelling. Britain's Flight Refuelling Limited developed the looped-hose system before and during the Second World War. Refuelling became operationally routine in the 1950s with jet aircraft, was decisive in long-range operations from the Vietnam War onward, and remains central to modern air power.
