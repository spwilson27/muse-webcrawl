Ethical egoism is the normative ethical position that moral agents ought to act in their own self-interest. It differs from psychological egoism, which claims that people can only act in their self-interest, and from rational egoism, which holds that acting in one's interest is rational. Ethical egoism does not require that agents harm others, nor does it forbid helping them: it holds that actions such as aiding others are right when, and because, they serve the agent's own interests, broadly understood.

Forms

Philosophers distinguish individual ethical egoism (I ought to pursue my interests, with no claim about others), personal ethical egoism, and universal ethical egoism — the doctrine that everyone ought to pursue their own interests. Henry Sidgwick discussed egoism as one of the "methods of ethics" in The Methods of Ethics (1874), noting its tension with utilitarianism in what he called the dualism of practical reason.

Ayn Rand

The most influential popular defence is that of Ayn Rand, who argued in The Virtue of Selfishness (1964) and her novels that rational self-interest is a virtue and that altruism — understood as self-sacrifice — is morally destructive. Rand's "rational egoism" nevertheless condemned whim-worship and predation, since harming others, she argued, is not in a rational person's long-term interest.

Arguments and objections

Supporters argue that egoism respects individual autonomy, that each person is best placed to know their own good, and that universal self-reliance could maximise overall welfare. Critics object that ethical egoism is self-defeating when universalised (the egoist cannot consistently will that others adopt it against the egoist's interests), that it cannot resolve conflicts of interest between agents, and that it yields counter-intuitive verdicts — it seems to license harming the innocent whenever doing so benefits the agent. James Rachels argued that egoism arbitrarily privileges one's own interests without a relevant difference from others'.

Related positions

Ethical egoism is often contrasted with utilitarianism, which counts everyone's interests equally, and with ethical altruism, which makes others' welfare the standard. Elements of egoistic reasoning also appear in contractarian theories, where moral rules are justified as being in each agent's interest to accept.
