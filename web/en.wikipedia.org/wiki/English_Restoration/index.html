The English Restoration, also known simply as the Restoration, began in 1660 when the English, Scottish and Irish monarchies were restored under the rule of Charles II. The term also describes the wider era of his reign and that of his brother James II, characterised by a revival of theatre, literature and court culture after the austerity of the Interregnum that followed the execution of Charles I in 1649.

Background

After the death of Oliver Cromwell in 1658 and the resignation of his son Richard in 1659, England descended into political instability. General George Monck marched south from Scotland, occupied London and arranged for elections to a Convention Parliament, which invited Charles II to return. Charles issued the Declaration of Breda, promising religious toleration and a general pardon, and entered London on 29 May 1660, his thirtieth birthday.

The Restoration settlement

The Convention and the subsequent Cavalier Parliament (1661–1679) restored the monarchy, the House of Lords and the Church of England. Regicides were tried and executed, and the bodies of Cromwell and others were posthumously exhumed and hanged. The Clarendon Code re-established Anglican supremacy and penalised Protestant dissenters, despite Charles's personal preference for toleration.

Culture

The period is famous for the reopening of the theatres closed under the Puritans, the appearance of actresses on the English stage for the first time, Restoration comedy (associated with writers such as William Wycherley and William Congreve), the poetry of John Dryden, and the founding of the Royal Society in 1660. The era was also marked by catastrophe: the Great Plague of 1665 and the Great Fire of London in 1666.
