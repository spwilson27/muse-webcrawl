Fly fishing
Angling with a lightweight artificial "fly" cast by the weight of the fly line itself.

Fly fishing is an angling technique that uses a specialized, weighted fishing line (a "fly line") and a lightweight lure called an artificial fly, which imitates a small invertebrate — most often an aquatic insect — to attract and catch fish. The primary distinction from other casting methods is that the weight for casting comes from the line rather than the lure. Because of this, fly fishing relies on the fly casting stroke, in which the angler moves the fly rod back and forth to form a loop of line in the air.

Fly fishing can be done in fresh or saltwater. Techniques for freshwater fly fishing are distinguished by whether the fly is intended to float on the surface (a dry fly), sink below it (a wet fly or nymph), or imitate a larger prey item such as a baitfish (a streamer). The method is most often associated with trout and salmon, but is used for a wide range of species.

History

The earliest accounts of fishing with an artificial fly are Roman: Claudius Aelianus, writing around AD 200, described Macedonian anglers catching fish on the river Astraeus with hooks dressed in red wool and feathers to imitate flies. The first detailed English treatise is the Treatyse of Fysshynge wyth an Angle (1496), traditionally attributed to Dame Juliana Berners, which described twelve fly patterns. Izaak Walton's The Compleat Angler (1653), with Charles Cotton's later additions on fly fishing, helped popularize the art. In the nineteenth century, English chalk-stream anglers such as F. M. Halford codified the dry-fly method, while G. E. M. Skues championed fishing with subsurface nymphs. In North America the Catskills became a centre of fly design, and tackle evolved from split-cane (bamboo) rods to fibreglass and then graphite.

Equipment

  • Fly rod — a long, flexible rod, typically 7 to 11 feet, rated by the weight of line it is designed to cast
  • Fly reel — usually a simple large-arbour reel holding line and backing, with a drag system for playing fish
  • Fly line — a heavy, coated line, often weight-forward tapered, which provides the mass for casting
  • Leader and tippet — a tapered monofilament or fluorocarbon section connecting line to fly
  • Flies — hooks dressed with fur, feather, and synthetic materials to imitate insects, crustaceans, or baitfish
  • Waders and accessories — chest waders, nets, forceps, and fly boxes

Casting

Unlike spin casting, where the lure's weight pulls the line, in fly casting the angler casts the line itself. The basic cast accelerates the rod to a stop, sending a loop of line unrolling toward the target; successive false casts extend the line before the final presentation cast lays the fly on the water. Specialized casts include the roll cast (for confined spaces), the reach cast, the double haul (to increase line speed), and the spey casts used with two-handed rods for salmon and steelhead.

Techniques

Anglers "match the hatch" by choosing flies that imitate the insects hatching at the time. Dry-fly fishing targets fish rising to feed at the surface; nymph fishing drifts weighted imitations of larval insects near the bottom, often with a strike indicator; streamer fishing retrieves larger flies to imitate baitfish or leeches and provoke strikes from predatory fish. Stillwater techniques from boats or banks, and saltwater fly fishing for species such as bonefish, tarpon, and permit on tropical flats, are major branches of the sport. Tenkara, a Japanese method using a rod, line, and fly without a reel, has also gained a following.
