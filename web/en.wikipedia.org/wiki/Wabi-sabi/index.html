Wabi-sabi

In traditional Japanese aesthetics, wabi-sabi (侘び寂び) is a world view centred on the acceptance of transience and imperfection. It is sometimes described as one of beauty that is "imperfect, impermanent, and incomplete". Characteristics of wabi-sabi aesthetics include asymmetry, roughness, simplicity, economy, austerity, modesty, intimacy, and the appreciation of both natural objects and the forces of nature.

A rough, irregular tea bowl of the kind prized in the wabi aesthetic of chanoyu.

Origins

Wabi originally connoted the loneliness of living in nature, remote from society, and sabi meant "lean" or "withered"; both terms acquired positive aesthetic meanings through Zen Buddhism and the tea ceremony. Tea master Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591) is credited with developing the wabi style of tea, favouring humble Korean rice bowls and rough Japanese pottery over perfect Chinese ware.

Related concepts

The practice of kintsugi — repairing broken pottery with lacquer mixed with gold — is often cited as an expression of wabi-sabi values. The concept overlaps with the Buddhist teaching of the three marks of existence: impermanence (mujō), suffering (ku) and emptiness or absence of self-nature (kū).
