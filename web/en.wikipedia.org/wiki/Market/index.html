Market

[Image: shoppers and stallholders at a traditional street market]

A market is a composition of systems, institutions, procedures and social relations through which parties engage in exchange — buyers and sellers coming together so that goods, services, labour, assets or information can be traded, usually for Money. Markets may be physical places, such as a farmers' market or a stock exchange floor, or purely virtual, as in online marketplaces; what defines them is the mechanism by which prices are formed and trades matched.

Market structures

Economists classify markets by the degree of Competition. In perfect competition many small buyers and sellers trade a homogeneous product, none can influence price, and resources are allocated efficiently in theory. A Monopoly is a market with a single seller; an Oligopoly has a few dominant sellers; monopolistic competition has many sellers of differentiated products. Real markets lie between these poles, and structure shapes prices, output, innovation and firms' profits.

Market economies and market failure

In a market economy, most allocation decisions are decentralised through markets and the price mechanism, coordinated — in Adam Smith's famous metaphor — as if by an "invisible hand". Markets can fail: externalities such as pollution, public goods that are under-provided, information asymmetries and monopoly power are standard cases of market failure, used to justify taxes, regulation or public provision. Financial markets — for shares, bonds and currencies — apply the same exchange logic to assets rather than goods.
