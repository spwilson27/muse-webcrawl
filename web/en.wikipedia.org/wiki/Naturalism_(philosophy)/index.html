Naturalism (philosophy)

In philosophy, naturalism is the view that everything arises from natural properties and causes, and that supernatural or spiritual explanations are excluded or discounted. It typically holds that the methods of the natural sciences are the most reliable route to knowledge about reality. Naturalism is not a single doctrine but a family of ontological and methodological positions.

Ontological naturalism

Ontological (or metaphysical) naturalism holds that reality is exhausted by nature — that there is nothing beyond the natural world studied by science. It is closely associated with physicalism, the view that everything is physical or depends on the physical. Ontological naturalists generally deny the existence of gods, souls, and other supernatural entities, or treat belief in them as unwarranted.

Methodological naturalism

Methodological naturalism is the working principle that scientific inquiry should seek natural explanations regardless of the investigator's metaphysical commitments. It underlies modern scientific practice and the demarcation of science from pseudoscience; unlike ontological naturalism, it makes no claim about what ultimately exists.

History and variants

Naturalistic themes appear in ancient Greek atomism and in early Indian and Chinese thought. In the modern period, philosophers such as John Dewey, George Santayana and Ernest Nagel developed self-consciously naturalist philosophies in America in the early twentieth century. W. V. O. Quine's "naturalized epistemology" urged that philosophy be continuous with science. Liberal or "relaxed" naturalists argue that the natural world can include moral values, meanings and other phenomena not reducible to physics, while "hard" naturalists insist on stricter scientific reduction. Naturalism is also a common foundation for positions in ethics, philosophy of mind and philosophy of religion.

Criticism

Critics argue that naturalism may be self-undermining (if beliefs are merely causal products, why trust them?), that it struggles to account for consciousness, abstract objects such as numbers, and normativity, and that strict versions risk scientism.
