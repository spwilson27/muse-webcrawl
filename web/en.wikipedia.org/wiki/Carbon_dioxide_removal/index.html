Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) is a process in which carbon dioxide (CO2) is removed from the atmosphere and durably stored in geological, terrestrial or ocean reservoirs, or in long-lived products. It is also known as greenhouse gas removal or, especially in its technological forms, as negative emissions technologies (NETs). CDR is distinct from carbon capture and storage applied to fossil-fuel exhaust (which prevents emissions rather than removing past ones) and from solar radiation modification (which does not reduce CO2 at all).

Methods range from familiar practices — afforestation, reforestation and improved soil management — to engineered approaches such as direct air capture with storage (DACCS), bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), enhanced rock weathering and ocean alkalinity enhancement. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change concluded in its Sixth Assessment Report that deployment of CDR is "unavoidable" if net zero CO2 or greenhouse gas emissions are to be achieved, and virtually all modelled pathways limiting warming to 1.5 °C or 2 °C include substantial CDR.

Methods

  • Afforestation and reforestation: Growing new forests stores carbon in biomass and soils; the largest current CDR method, but storage can be reversed by fire, disease or later logging.
  • Soil carbon sequestration: Agricultural practices (cover crops, reduced tillage, biochar) that increase soil organic carbon.
  • Biochar: Pyrolysing biomass into a stable charcoal and adding it to soils, where much of its carbon persists for centuries.
  • BECCS: Growing biomass for energy, capturing the CO2 from combustion or fermentation and storing it geologically — combining energy production with removal.
  • Direct air capture (DACCS): Machines use chemical sorbents or solvents to extract CO2 directly from ambient air, followed by geological storage. Operating plants (e.g., Climeworks' Orca and Mammoth in Iceland) remain small — tens of thousands of tonnes per year — relative to gigatonne needs.
  • Enhanced weathering: Spreading crushed silicate rocks such as basalt on land or coasts, accelerating natural reactions that convert CO2 to dissolved bicarbonate.
  • Ocean-based methods: Ocean alkalinity enhancement, ocean fertilisation and artificial upwelling/downwelling; promising in scale but ecologically uncertain and difficult to verify.
  • Peatland and wetland restoration and blue carbon (mangroves, seagrasses, salt marshes).

Scale and scenarios

Total current CDR is estimated at about 2 billion tonnes of CO2 per year, almost all from conventional management on land; novel methods such as DACCS and BECCS contribute only a few million tonnes. IPCC pathways limiting warming to 1.5 °C typically deploy CDR in the range of roughly 5–15 Gt CO2 per year by mid-century, alongside — and not instead of — deep emissions cuts. Durability of storage varies from decades (soils, forests) to more than 10,000 years (geological storage of mineralised CO2).

Governance, markets and criticism

CDR has become a significant policy and market arena: the United States funds DAC hubs and offers the 45Q tax credit; the European Union is developing a certification framework for carbon removals; and a voluntary market in removal credits has grown, with large advance purchases by corporate buyers (such as Microsoft and the Frontier consortium). Critics raise concerns about "mitigation deterrence" — the risk that promised future removals excuse continued emissions — as well as land competition with food production (especially for BECCS and afforestation at scale), energy demands of direct air capture, permanence and verification difficulties, and environmental justice impacts. These debates have pushed guidance (e.g., the Oxford Principles for Net Zero Aligned Offsetting) favouring durable, geological storage for residual emissions.

Definitions

The IPCC defines CDR as "anthropogenic activities removing CO2 from the atmosphere and durably storing it in geological, terrestrial, or ocean reservoirs, or in products." It excludes natural uptake not directly caused by human activity and distinguishes CDR from emissions reduction and from carbon capture and utilisation where storage is short-lived.
