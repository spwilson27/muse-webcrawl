Earth's atmosphere

The atmosphere of Earth is the layer of gases, known collectively as air, retained by Earth's gravity that surrounds the planet and forms its planetary atmosphere. The atmosphere protects life on Earth by creating pressure allowing for liquid water to exist on the Earth's surface, absorbing ultraviolet radiation, warming the surface through heat retention (the greenhouse effect), and reducing temperature extremes between day and night.

Composition

By mole fraction, dry air contains 78.08% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen, 0.93% argon, 0.04% carbon dioxide, and trace amounts of other gases. Air also contains a variable amount of water vapor, on average around 1% at sea level. The composition of the atmosphere has changed substantially over geologic time: free oxygen first appeared in quantity during the Great Oxidation Event about 2.4 billion years ago, produced by photosynthetic cyanobacteria.

Structure

The atmosphere is divided into layers based on temperature:

  • Troposphere — from the surface to about 12 km; where nearly all weather occurs and temperature decreases with height.
  • Stratosphere — to about 50 km; contains the ozone layer, which absorbs ultraviolet radiation, and temperature increases with height.
  • Mesosphere — to about 85 km; the coldest layer, where most meteors burn up.
  • Thermosphere — to about 600 km; very low density, temperature increases strongly with height; auroras occur here.
  • Exosphere — the outermost layer, fading into space; light atoms can escape to space from this region.

The Kármán line, at 100 km altitude, is commonly used as the boundary between the atmosphere and outer space.

Pressure and density

Atmospheric pressure decreases exponentially with altitude; about half of the atmosphere's mass lies below 5.5 km. Standard sea-level pressure is 101.325 kPa (1 atm). The density of air at sea level is about 1.225 kg/m³.

Evolution and escape

Earth's early atmosphere, formed by outgassing from the interior and delivery by impacts, was rich in carbon dioxide and water vapor and contained almost no free oxygen. Over time, carbon dioxide was drawn down into carbonate rocks and the oceans, while photosynthesis oxygenated the air. Some light gases, especially hydrogen and helium, escape from the top of the atmosphere into space.
