Penicillin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Penicillin was the first widely used antibiotic and remains a cornerstone drug family. In 1928 Alexander Fleming noticed that a stray Penicillium mould on a staphylococcus culture plate had killed the bacteria around it, and named the mould's active substance penicillin. A decade later, Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, Norman Heatley and their Oxford team purified it, proved its power in 1940–41, and — with American industrial fermentation during the Second World War — turned it into the 'miracle drug' that made pneumonia, sepsis, syphilis and infected wounds survivable.

Penicillins work by blocking bacterial cell-wall synthesis, so growing bacteria burst. They are generally safe, though a minority of people have true penicillin allergy. Chemists later produced semi-synthetic variants — ampicillin, amoxicillin, flucloxacillin — with broader spectra or resistance to bacterial penicillinase enzymes.

Impact

Penicillin's arrival, alongside the other antibiotics it inspired, is credited with a significant share of the 20th century's gains in life expectancy and with making modern surgery, transplantation and chemotherapy practicable. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the 1945 Nobel Prize. Fleming himself warned in his Nobel lecture that under-dosing would breed resistant bacteria — a prescient summary of the resistance era.

Development

The wartime scale-up — deep-tank fermentation, the hunt for higher-yielding strains (including one from a Peoria cantaloupe) — is a landmark of industrial microbiology, and mass production made penicillin cheap worldwide by the late 1940s.
