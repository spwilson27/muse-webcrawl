Sir William Fothergill Cooke (4 May 1806 – 25 June 1879) was an English inventor. Together with Charles Wheatstone he developed the Cooke and Wheatstone telegraph, the first practical electric telegraph system to enter commercial service, and co-founded the Electric Telegraph Company, the world's first telegraph company.

Early life

Cooke was born in Ealing, the son of a surgeon. After education at Durham and the University of Edinburgh he served in the British army in India. While studying anatomy in Heidelberg in 1836 he saw a demonstration of an experimental needle telegraph and immediately grasped its commercial potential for railway signalling and communications.

The Cooke and Wheatstone telegraph

Returning to England, Cooke sought technical help and was introduced to Charles Wheatstone, professor at King's College London. Their partnership patented a five-needle telegraph in June 1837; a simplified two-needle and later single-needle version followed. The system was installed on the Great Western Railway between Paddington and West Drayton in 1839, and famously helped secure the arrest of the murderer John Tawell in 1845 after his description was telegraphed from Slough to Paddington. Relations between Cooke and Wheatstone soured into a long priority dispute, resolved partly by arbitration, and both men were later recognised as joint inventors.

Business career and honours

In 1846 Cooke helped found the Electric Telegraph Company, which built a national network and brought the telegraph into everyday public use. Financially less successful in later ventures, he received a civil list pension, and was knighted in 1869. He died at Farnham in 1879.
