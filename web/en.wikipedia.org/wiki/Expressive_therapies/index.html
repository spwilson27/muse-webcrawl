Expressive therapies

The expressive therapies are the use of the creative arts as a form of therapy, including the distinct disciplines of art therapy, dance/movement therapy, drama therapy, music therapy, poetry therapy, and psychodrama. Unlike traditional art expression, the process of creation is emphasized rather than the final product. The expressive therapies are based on the assumption that people can heal through the various forms of creative expression.

History

The roots of the expressive therapies lie in the early 20th century, when artists and psychiatrists began to notice the therapeutic value of patients' artwork. Art therapy emerged as a distinct discipline in the 1940s through figures such as Adrian Hill and Margaret Naumburg. Dance therapy, associated with Marian Chace, and psychodrama, founded by Jacob L. Moreno, developed in parallel. Professional associations and training programs were established from the 1960s onward, and the field has since expanded to include intermodal approaches that combine several art forms within a single session.

Modalities

  • Art therapy — uses drawing, painting, and sculpture.
  • Music therapy — uses listening, improvisation, and songwriting.
  • Dance/movement therapy — uses movement and dance to integrate emotional, cognitive, and physical aspects of the individual.
  • Drama therapy and psychodrama — use role play, storytelling, and enactment.
  • Poetry and bibliotherapy — use writing and literature.

Applications

Expressive therapies are used with children, trauma survivors, people with developmental disabilities, patients in palliative care, and individuals for whom verbal communication is difficult. Research evidence varies by modality and condition; the therapies are often used alongside conventional psychotherapy and medical treatment.
