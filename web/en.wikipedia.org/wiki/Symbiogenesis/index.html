Symbiogenesis

Symbiogenesis (from Greek symbiosis + genesis), or endosymbiotic theory, is the leading evolutionary theory of the origin of eukaryotic cells from prokaryotic organisms. The theory holds that mitochondria and plastids (such as chloroplasts), and possibly other organelles, originated as free-living bacteria that were taken up by another cell as endosymbionts.

History and evidence

The idea was proposed by Konstantin Mereschkowski in 1905–1910 (who coined the term symbiogenesis), elaborated by Ivan Wallin for mitochondria in the 1920s, and revived and championed by Lynn Margulis in her 1967 paper and the 1970 book Origin of Eukaryotic Cells. Evidence includes the organelles' own circular DNA, bacterial-type (70S) ribosomes, double membranes, and reproduction by binary fission. Primary endosymbiosis of an alphaproteobacterium gave rise to mitochondria in the common ancestor of eukaryotes; a later cyanobacterial endosymbiosis gave plastids to the ancestors of plants and algae, and secondary and tertiary endosymbioses spread plastids across many protist lineages. Horizontal endosymbiotic gene transfer has moved most symbiont genes to the host nucleus.
