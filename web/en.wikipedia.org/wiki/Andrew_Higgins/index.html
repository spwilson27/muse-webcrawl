Andrew Jackson Higgins (28 August 1886 – 1 August 1952) was an American boatbuilder and industrialist whose New Orleans company, Higgins Industries, designed and mass-produced the LCVP (Landing Craft, Vehicle, Personnel) — the "Higgins boat" — used in every major Allied amphibious landing of the Second World War. Dwight D. Eisenhower later called him "the man who won the war for us," crediting the shallow-draft, ramp-bowed landing craft with making the strategy of open-beach invasions possible.

Early career

Higgins built workboats for the Louisiana lumber, oil, and trapping trades. His "Eureka" boat, with a spoon bow and a propeller recessed in a semi-tunnel, could run in inches of water, ride up onto mud banks, and back off again — qualities that attracted the US Marine Corps, which needed a landing craft for troops and vehicles. After years of competing designs, a 1941 test showed Higgins's boat outperforming the Navy Bureau of Ships' own model.

Second World War production

Adapting the Eureka design with a steel bow ramp — an idea refined after the Marines saw the Japanese Daihatsu landing craft in China — Higgins produced the LCVP, capable of carrying 36 troops or a jeep-sized vehicle at 9 knots and beaching them directly. Higgins Industries grew from a small boatyard into a complex of plants employing some 20,000 workers in New Orleans, notable for an integrated workforce unusual in the segregated South, and built more than 23,000 boats of all types, including PT boats and landing craft variants. At peak production the company turned out boats at a rate unmatched in shipbuilding history.

Legacy

The National WWII Museum in New Orleans displays a Higgins boat built to the original specifications; the Andrew Higgins Drive in the city bears his name. His inclusive hiring and seven-day production drives made him a symbol of the American home-front mobilisation.
