Christoph Willibald (Ritter von) Gluck (2 July 1714 – 15 November 1787) was a German composer of Italian and French opera in the early Classical period. He was a central figure in the reform of operatic drama, seeking to subordinate music to poetry and to eliminate the abuses that had crept into opera seria, in works such as Orfeo ed Euridice (1762), Alceste (1767), and Iphigénie en Tauride (1779).

Life

Gluck was born in Erasbach in the Upper Palatinate and grew up in Bohemia, where his father was a forester. He studied music in Prague and then in Milan, where he was a pupil of Giovanni Battista Sammartini and composed his first operas in the Italian style. After travels that took him to London, where he met Handel, he settled in Vienna, becoming Kapellmeister at the imperial court.

The operatic reform

Working with the librettists Ranieri de' Calzabigi and others, Gluck set out his reform principles in the famous preface to Alceste: music should serve the drama, overtures should prepare the audience for the action, the chorus should participate meaningfully, and virtuoso display should not interrupt the story. Orfeo ed Euridice, with its simplicity and noble expressiveness — including the celebrated aria "Che farò senza Euridice" — became the model of the new style.

In Paris during the 1770s, Gluck's French operas, including Iphigénie en Aulide, Armide, and Iphigénie en Tauride, provoked the celebrated quarrel between the Gluckists and the supporters of Niccolò Piccinni, the so-called Querelle des Gluckistes et des Piccinnistes.

Legacy

Gluck's reforms profoundly influenced later opera, including the work of Mozart, Berlioz, and Wagner, who conducted and admired Iphigénie en Aulide. His mature operas remain in the repertory, and his ballet music, such as the Dance of the Blessed Spirits, is widely performed.
