Andrew Jackson

Andrew Jackson (March 15, 1767 – June 8, 1845) was the seventh president of the United States, serving from 1829 to 1837. A frontier lawyer, planter, slaveholder and soldier from Tennessee, he rose to national fame as the general who defeated the British at the Battle of New Orleans in the War of 1812 and crushed the Red Stick Creeks at Horseshoe Bend. His presidency founded the modern Democratic Party, expanded the power and popular character of the presidency — he was the first president to claim to embody the people's will directly — and was marked by the Indian Removal Act and the forced displacement of Native nations (the Trail of Tears), the destruction of the Second Bank of the United States in the "Bank War", the Nullification Crisis with South Carolina, and the payment of the entire national debt. "Jacksonian democracy" — broader white male suffrage, spoils-system patronage, suspicion of banks and concentrated wealth — defined an era; historians continue to argue over a legacy that joins the democratisation of American politics to the dispossession of Native Americans and the defence of slavery.

Early life and military career

Jackson was born on the Carolina frontier to Scots-Irish immigrant parents; his father died before he was born, and the Revolutionary War destroyed the rest of his family: at thirteen he served as a courier, was captured, and was slashed by a British officer's sword for refusing to clean his boots (he carried the scars, and a hatred of Britain, for life); smallpox in prison nearly killed him, and his mother died nursing American prisoners. Orphaned and nearly penniless, he read law, was admitted to the bar in 1787, and rode west to Nashville, where he prospered as a lawyer, land speculator, planter (building the Hermitage plantation, worked by enslaved people) and duellist — in an 1806 duel he killed Charles Dickinson, taking a bullet beside his heart that could never be removed.

Appointed major general of Tennessee militia, Jackson in the Creek War of 1813–14 — sparked by the Red Stick attack on Fort Mims — destroyed the Red Sticks at Horseshoe Bend (March 1814) and imposed the Treaty of Fort Jackson, seizing some 23 million acres of Creek and allied land. In the War of 1812's final campaign he defended the Gulf Coast, seized Spanish Pensacola, and on 8 January 1815 won New Orleans, the victory that made him America's foremost popular hero. In 1818 he invaded Spanish Florida in the First Seminole War, executing two British subjects and provoking a cabinet crisis; in 1821 he became Florida's first American military governor after its purchase.

Path to the presidency

Jackson led the popular and electoral vote in the election of 1824 but lost the presidency in the House of Representatives to John Quincy Adams — Jacksonians cried a "corrupt bargain" when Henry Clay became secretary of state — and spent four years organising the coalition that won decisively in 1828, in one of the dirtiest campaigns in American history; his wife Rachel died weeks after the election, of a heart attack Jackson blamed on the slanders against her. His inauguration in 1829, when a mob of supporters invaded the White House reception, announced the arrival of the people's president.

Presidency (1829–1837)

Jackson wielded the veto more than all his predecessors combined, replaced officeholders with loyalists on an unprecedented scale ("rotation in office"), and governed as the self-declared tribune of the majority. His first term was rocked by the Peggy Eaton affair and Calhoun's fall. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 authorised the exchange of Native lands in the East for territory in the West; its implementation — resisted by the Cherokee all the way to the Supreme Court, which ruled in their favour to no avail — culminated after his presidency in the forced Cherokee removal of 1838–39, the Trail of Tears, in which thousands died. In 1832 he vetoed the recharter of the Bank of the United States, withdrew federal deposits to favoured "pet banks", and destroyed the Bank — cheered by his supporters as a blow against monopoly, blamed by opponents (and later historians, in part) for the speculative boom that ended in the Panic of 1837. When South Carolina nullified the federal tariff and threatened secession, Jackson issued his Nullification Proclamation, obtained a Force Bill, and stared the nullifiers down while accepting a compromise tariff — saving the Union, for a generation. In January 1835 the last of the national debt was paid off, the only such moment in American history; the same month he survived the first attempted assassination of a US president, beating off his assailant with his cane when both pistols misfired.

Later life and legacy

Jackson retired to the Hermitage in 1837, remained the Democratic Party's elder statesman — engineering the nomination and election of his protégé Van Buren and, in 1844, of James K. Polk, and urging the annexation of Texas — and died in June 1845. His memory was immediately contested: Whigs had named him "King Andrew I"; Democrats built their party's identity on him (the Jefferson– Jackson Day dinners endured for a century and a half); his portrait hangs in the modern Oval Office politics of both admirers and critics. For modern reassessors, Jackson the democratiser and Union- saver stands inseparably beside Jackson the slaveholder and architect of Indian removal — the central controversy of his historical reputation.
