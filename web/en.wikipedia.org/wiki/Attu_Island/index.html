Attu Island is the largest island of the Near Islands group of the Aleutian Islands of Alaska and the westernmost point of land of the United States relative to the rest of the country. Remote and treeless, it lies roughly 1,700 km west of the Alaska mainland and only about 335 km from the Russian Commander Islands. For most of its recorded history it was home to an Unangax̂ (Aleut) community; it is now uninhabited.

History

Attu was inhabited by Unangax̂ people for thousands of years before Russian fur traders arrived in the eighteenth century. By the twentieth century the village of Attu on Chichagof Harbor was a small community centered on fox trapping, fishing, and basket weaving — Attu baskets are prized examples of Aleut weaving.

On 7 June 1942, during the Second World War, Japanese troops occupied Attu (along with Kiska), the only battles fought on incorporated American soil in the war. The island's 45 residents were taken to Japan as prisoners, where about half died. In May 1943 U.S. forces retook the island in the Battle of Attu, one of the war's bloodiest amphibious landings in proportion to the forces engaged, ending with a mass Japanese banzai charge. The surviving Attu villagers were never permitted to return home and were resettled at Atka.

Later years

After the war the U.S. Coast Guard operated a LORAN navigation station at Massacre Bay, staffed by about twenty personnel; it was the island's last permanent settlement until the station closed in 2010, leaving Attu uninhabited. The island, part of the Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge, is noted among birdwatchers for rare Asian vagrant species blown across from Siberia, and its battlefield sites are preserved within a national historic area.
