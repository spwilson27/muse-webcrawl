Environmental, social, and corporate governance

Environmental, social, and corporate governance (ESG) is a set of considerations for evaluating companies and investments, including effects on the environment, effects on society, and the adequacy of a company's governance. ESG considerations emerged within the investing profession, and today investment firms rate the ESG performance of companies and incorporate the ratings into investment analysis.

Components

  • Environmental — criteria concerning a company's impact on the natural world, such as carbon emissions, energy use, waste and pollution, natural resource conservation, and climate risk.
  • Social — criteria concerning relationships with employees, suppliers, customers, and communities, including labour standards, diversity, human rights, and product safety.
  • Governance — criteria concerning leadership, executive pay, audits, internal controls, board composition, and shareholder rights.

History

ESG descends from earlier movements of socially responsible investing and corporate social responsibility. The modern term was popularised by the 2004 United Nations report Who Cares Wins, and the UN-backed Principles for Responsible Investment (PRI), launched in 2006, committed signatory investors to incorporating ESG factors into their decisions. During the 2010s and early 2020s, ESG-labelled assets grew rapidly, accompanied by a large industry of rating agencies and disclosure frameworks such as the Global Reporting Initiative, SASB, and the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD).

Criticism and debate

ESG has been criticised from several directions: for inconsistency and opacity in ratings, for enabling greenwashing, for diluting financial returns, and — particularly in the United States — as a vehicle for political objectives in corporate decision-making, prompting anti-ESG legislation in some states. Supporters argue that ESG factors capture long-term risks and opportunities material to investors.
