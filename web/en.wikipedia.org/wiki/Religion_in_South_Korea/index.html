Religion in South Korea

Religion in South Korea is characterised by the fact that a majority of South Koreans (56.1% as of the 2015 national census) have no formal affiliation with any religious organisation. Among those who do affiliate with a religion, the majority are Christians and Buddhists, with the Christian population having roughly doubled and the Buddhist population roughly halved in membership over the past two decades.

According to the 2015 census, 19.7% of the population was Protestant, 15.5% Buddhist, and 7.9% Catholic. A small percentage of South Koreans (0.8% in total) belong to other religions, including Won Buddhism, Confucianism, Cheondoism, Daesun Jinrihoe, Islam, Daejongism, Jeungsanism and Orthodox Christianity.

History

Historically, Korean religious life was shaped by Korean shamanism (Muism), Buddhism — introduced from China in the 4th century CE — and Confucianism, which became the state ideology of the Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) and largely displaced Buddhism from public life. Christianity arrived in the late 18th century through Korean envoys to Beijing and grew rapidly during the 20th century, particularly after the Korean War.

Buddhism

Buddhism was introduced to the Korean peninsula in 372 CE. Under Joseon it was suppressed in favour of Neo-Confucianism, but survived in mountain temples. Major orders include the Jogye Order, the largest Korean Buddhist denomination. Notable temples include Bulguksa and Haeinsa, the latter housing the Tripitaka Koreana woodblocks.

Christianity

Protestant missionaries arrived in the 1880s, and Christianity expanded dramatically after 1945. South Korea is home to some of the world's largest churches, including Yoido Full Gospel Church in Seoul. Catholicism, introduced earlier through self-evangelisation by Korean scholars, also grew significantly in the late 20th century.

Indigenous and other religions

Korean shamanism continues to be practised by shamans (mudang). New religions founded in Korea include Cheondoism, Won Buddhism and the Unification Church. The Muslim population, long small, has grown with immigration and labour migration.
