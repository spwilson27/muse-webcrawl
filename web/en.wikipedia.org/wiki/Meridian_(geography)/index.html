Overview

A meridian is an imaginary line on Earth's surface running from the North Pole to the South Pole, along which longitude is constant. Every meridian is half of a great circle; the other half is the meridian exactly 180° away, on the opposite side of the Earth.

Meridians converge at the poles, so the distance between two meridians one degree apart is greatest at the Equator — about 111 kilometres — and shrinks to zero at the poles. This is why degrees of longitude have no fixed length on the ground, unlike degrees of latitude.

Prime meridian

Longitude is counted east and west from an agreed zero line, the prime meridian. Since an international conference in 1884, the world's prime meridian has been the one passing through the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, London — the Greenwich meridian. Earlier maps used many local prime meridians, through Paris, Ferro, Cádiz and other observatories.

The meridian opposite Greenwich, at 180°, forms the basis of the International Date Line in the Pacific.
