Asymmetric information

In contract theory, mechanism design, and economics, an information asymmetry is a situation in which one party to a transaction has more or superior information than another. This happens in two general forms: adverse selection, where the less informed party lacks information before the transaction, and moral hazard, where one party cannot observe the other's actions afterwards.

The market for lemons

George Akerlof's 1970 paper "The Market for Lemons" showed how information asymmetry in the used-car market can drive good products out: sellers know whether their car is a "lemon", buyers do not, so buyers offer an average price, driving owners of good cars to withdraw. Akerlof, Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information.

Remedies

Mechanisms that reduce asymmetry include signalling (Spence's job-market model, where education signals ability), screening by the uninformed party, warranties and guarantees, reputation, third-party certification, and disclosure regulation such as financial reporting rules and prospectus requirements.
