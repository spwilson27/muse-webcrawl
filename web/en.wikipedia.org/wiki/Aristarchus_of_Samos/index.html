Aristarchus of Samos

Aristarchus of Samos (Ancient Greek: Ἀρίσταρχος ὁ Σάμιος; c. 310 – c. 230 BC) was an ancient Greek astronomer and mathematician who presented the first known heliocentric model of the universe, placing the Sun at the centre and the Earth in orbit around it. He was influenced by Philolaus of Croton but identified the "central fire" with the Sun. His astronomical ideas were rejected in favour of the geocentric theories of Aristotle and Ptolemy until they were revived nearly 1,800 years later by Nicolaus Copernicus.

Heliocentrism

The heliocentric hypothesis is known from Archimedes' The Sand Reckoner, which reports that Aristarchus proposed that the Sun and fixed stars remain unmoved while the Earth revolves around the Sun on the circumference of a circle, and that the sphere of the fixed stars is vastly larger than Earth's orbit — an early answer to the objection that no stellar parallax could be observed. Plutarch reports that Cleanthes thought Aristarchus should be indicted for impiety for "putting the hearth of the universe in motion".

On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon

Aristarchus's only surviving work, On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon, is a geocentric-era geometric treatise that computes the relative sizes of the Sun and Moon and their distances from Earth from observations of lunar phases, eclipses, and the angle between the Sun and Moon at half-moon. His measurement of that angle (87°, against the true 89.85°) made his solar distance far too small (about 19 times the Earth–Moon distance rather than about 389), but the method was mathematically sound, and his results implied the Sun was much larger than the Earth — plausibly the motivation for his heliocentrism.

Other work

Aristarchus is also credited by Vitruvius with inventing a hemispherical sundial (the scaphe), and Ptolemy records that he observed the summer solstice of 280 BC.

Legacy

The lunar crater Aristarchus is named after him, as is the asteroid 3999 Aristarchus. His heliocentric proposal is often cited as an example of an idea far ahead of its time.
