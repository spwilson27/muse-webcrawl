Physical therapy (PT), also known as physiotherapy, is a healthcare profession concerned with the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of movement disorders and physical impairments. Physical therapists use exercise, manual therapy, education and physical agents such as heat, cold and electrical stimulation to restore function, relieve pain and prevent disability.

History

Exercise, massage and manipulation were used therapeutically in ancient China, India, Greece and Rome. The modern profession emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries: Per Henrik Ling's Swedish gymnastics system, the founding of the Chartered Society of Physiotherapy in Britain (1894), and the rehabilitation of polio patients and wounded soldiers during and after the First World War drove professionalisation.

Practice

Assessment typically covers strength, range of motion, gait, balance, posture and function. Interventions include therapeutic exercise, joint mobilisation and manipulation, soft-tissue techniques, neuromuscular re-education, gait training, and modalities such as ultrasound and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS). Therapists work in hospitals, outpatient clinics, schools, sports settings and patients' homes.

Specialty areas

Recognised specialties include orthopaedic, sports, neurological (e.g. stroke, Parkinson's disease), cardiopulmonary, paediatric, geriatric, women's health and wound care physical therapy.

Education and regulation

In the United States the entry-level degree is the Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT), and practitioners are licensed by states. In many other countries, including the United Kingdom, Australia and Canada, physiotherapists qualify through bachelor's or master's degrees and register with a national regulator. Direct access — seeing a therapist without a physician referral — is permitted in many jurisdictions.
