Modal verb

English modal verbs express possibility, obligation, permission and ability

A modal verb is a type of auxiliary verb used to express modality — that is, possibility, necessity, obligation, permission, ability or likelihood. In English the central modal verbs are can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will and would, together with marginal members such as ought to, need and dare.

Properties in English

English modal verbs are grammatically defective: they have no infinitive, no participles, and no third-person singular -s form ("she can", not "she cans"). They are followed by the bare infinitive of the main verb, form questions by inversion, and are negated directly with not, without do-support. Historically, several derive from Old English "preterite-present" verbs.

Types of meaning

Linguists distinguish epistemic modality, concerning judgements about truth ("That must be the postman"), from deontic modality, concerning obligation and permission ("You must stop"), and dynamic modality, concerning ability ("She can swim"). The same verb often covers several types: must is both deontic and epistemic, and context resolves the reading.

In other languages

Many languages express modality with ordinary, fully inflected verbs rather than a special defective class; German, for instance, has modal verbs such as können and müssen that inflect more completely than their English cognates. Other languages use particles, affixes or adverbs for the same functions.
