Curvature

The curvature of a curve measures how sharply it bends at each point.

In mathematics, curvature is any of several strongly related concepts in geometry that intuitively measure the amount by which a curve deviates from being a straight line, or a surface deviates from being a plane. For curves, the canonical example is that of a circle, which has a curvature equal to the reciprocal of its radius. Smaller circles bend more sharply, and hence have higher curvature; larger circles bend more gently and have lower curvature.

The curvature of a differentiable curve is the rate of change of its tangent vector with respect to arc length. For a plane curve given parametrically, the signed curvature can be written in terms of first and second derivatives. Curvature is a central notion in differential geometry, appearing in the theory of surfaces through Gaussian curvature and mean curvature, and in general relativity through the Riemann curvature tensor, which describes the geometry of spacetime itself.

History

The concept of curvature was discussed informally by early geometers, but a precise definition emerged with the development of calculus in the seventeenth century. Newton and Leibniz considered the osculating circle — the circle that best approximates a curve at a point — and curvature was identified with the reciprocal of that circle's radius. Euler and later Gauss extended the idea to surfaces, with Gauss's Theorema Egregium showing that Gaussian curvature is an intrinsic property of a surface.

Curvature of plane curves

For a regular parametric plane curve, curvature is defined as the magnitude of the derivative of the unit tangent vector with respect to arc length. Intuitively, curvature measures how fast the direction of motion rotates as one travels along the curve. A straight line has zero curvature everywhere; a circle of radius R has constant curvature 1/R.

Curvature of surfaces

At each point of a smooth surface, the normal curvatures in different tangent directions vary between two extremes, the principal curvatures. Their product is the Gaussian curvature and their average is the mean curvature. Surfaces of positive Gaussian curvature, like spheres, curve away in the same direction along all directions, while saddle-shaped surfaces have negative Gaussian curvature.
