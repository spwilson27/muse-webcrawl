Marianne

Marianne wearing the Phrygian cap of liberty

Marianne (French: [maʁjan]) has been the national personification of the French Republic since the French Revolution, as a symbol of liberty, equality, fraternity and reason, and a portrayal of the Goddess of Liberty. Marianne is displayed in many places in France and holds a place of honour in town halls and law courts.

Origins

Marianne is said to have been created in 1792 by the poet Guillaume Lavabre, who wrote a popular song about a heroine named Marianne from Provence. The name combines two common names, Marie and Anne, and became attached to the allegorical figure of the Republic that appeared on Revolutionary seals and coinage. After the Bourbon Restoration the image was banned, but it returned with each republic.

Imagery

Marianne is traditionally depicted as a young woman wearing a Phrygian cap — the soft cap worn by freed slaves in antiquity and revived as a symbol of liberty — often bare-breasted or in classical robes, bearing the tricolour flag, a fasces, or a tablet of the Declaration of the Rights of Man. Eugène Delacroix's 1830 painting Liberty Leading the People is the most famous depiction. Each generation of official Marianne busts in town halls is modelled on a prominent French woman, including Brigitte Bardot (1969), Catherine Deneuve (1985), and Laetitia Casta (2000).

Legacy

Marianne appears on French postage stamps, coins, and the national logo adopted in 1999, and is the name of the French satirical weekly Marianne. She remains, alongside the Gallic rooster, France's most recognisable emblem.
