Adirondack Forty-Sixers

The Adirondack Forty-Sixers are an organization of hikers who have climbed all 46 of the traditionally recognized High Peaks of the Adirondack Mountains in northeastern New York. A person who completes the list becomes a "Forty-Sixer" and is assigned a membership number. The peaks were originally surveyed as all exceeding 4,000 feet (1,219 m) in elevation; later surveys showed that four of them (Blake Peak, Cliff Mountain, Nye Mountain, and Couchsachraga Peak) are actually below 4,000 feet, while MacNaughton Mountain, which exceeds 4,000 feet, was left off the original list. Out of tradition, the list of 46 has been retained unchanged.

History

The first Forty-Sixers were brothers Robert and George Marshall and their guide Herbert Clark, who completed the climbs between 1918 and 1925, guided by an early survey of the region and by the writings of early Adirondack surveyor Verplanck Colvin. Grace Hudowalski, who finished in 1937 as the ninth person overall and the first woman, became the club's longtime historian and correspondent, personally answering letters from aspiring members for decades; in 2014 the peak formerly known as East Dix was renamed Grace Peak in her honor. The club was formally organized in 1948 as the Forty-Sixers of Troy, and later became the Adirondack Forty-Sixers, Inc.

The peaks

The highest of the 46 is Mount Marcy at 5,344 feet (1,629 m), the highest point in New York State, followed by Algonquin Peak (5,114 ft) and Mount Haystack (4,960 ft). Other well-known summits on the list include Whiteface Mountain, Mount Colden, Giant Mountain, and the Great Range peaks such as Gothics, Basin, and Saddleback. Many of the peaks have no maintained trail and are reached by unmarked "herd paths," which adds to the challenge.

Membership and variations

More than 15,000 people have registered as Forty-Sixers. A winter variant — climbing all 46 peaks during the winter season — earns the designation "Winter Forty-Sixer." The organization promotes Leave No Trace ethics, trail stewardship, and correspondence traditions descended from Hudowalski's era: aspirants report their climbs to club correspondents as they progress. The Forty-Sixers also fund trail work and outdoor education programs in the High Peaks region.
