New Netherland (Dutch: Nieuw-Nederland) was a 17th-century Dutch colony in North America, claimed between 1614 and settled from 1624 until its capture by England in 1664. Administered by the Dutch West India Company, it encompassed the present-day states of New York and New Jersey and parts of Delaware, Connecticut, and Pennsylvania, extending from the Delmarva Peninsula to southern New England along the Hudson, Delaware, and Connecticut river valleys.

Origins

The Dutch claim rested on Henry Hudson's 1609 exploration of the Hudson River for the Dutch East India Company. Dutch traders established fur-trading posts, including Fort Orange (near present-day Albany) in 1624. The Dutch West India Company, chartered in 1621, sponsored permanent settlement; in 1626 Director-General Peter Minuit consolidated the settlers at the tip of Manhattan, founding the capital, New Amsterdam.

Society

The colony's economy centered on the fur trade with the Iroquois and Algonquian peoples. The patroonship system (1629) granted vast estates, such as Rensselaerswijck, to investors who settled fifty colonists. Settlement remained sparse — perhaps 9,000 people by 1664 — but remarkably diverse, with Dutch, Walloon, French, German, Scandinavian, English, African, and Jewish inhabitants. Enslaved Africans, first brought in the 1620s, built much of New Amsterdam. Relations with Native peoples alternated between trade and violence, notably in Kieft's War (1643–1645) and the Esopus Wars.

Directors-General

The colony was governed by directors-general including Cornelius Jacobsen May, Willem Verhulst, Peter Minuit, Wouter van Twiller, Willem Kieft, and — from 1647 to 1664 — Peter Stuyvesant, its most consequential leader.

English conquest and legacy

In 1664, during the Second Anglo-Dutch War era, an English expedition seized the colony; New Amsterdam was renamed New York and Fort Orange became Albany. The Dutch recaptured the territory in 1673 but surrendered it permanently in the Treaty of Westminster (1674), receiving Suriname in exchange. Dutch influence endures in American law, religious tolerance traditions, and countless place names, from Brooklyn and Harlem to the Hudson Valley's Dutch colonial architecture.
