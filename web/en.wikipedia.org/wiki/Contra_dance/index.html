Contra dance

Contra dance (also contradance, contra-dance) is a form of folk dancing made up of long lines of couples. It has mixed origins in English country dance, Scottish country dance, and French dance styles adopted in the 17th century. Sometimes described as New England folk dance or Appalachian folk dance, contra dances can be found around the world and are particularly popular in North America, where a caller teaches each dance before the music begins and prompts figures during it.

Formation and figures

Dancers form long lines of couples facing each other ("contra" lines), typically in "improper" or "proper" formation. Over the course of a dance each couple progresses up or down the line, dancing a repeated sequence of figures — such as the allemande, do-si-do, balance and swing, hey for four, and California twirl — with each neighbouring couple in turn. A dance lasts until every couple has danced with every other, often ten minutes or more.

Music

Contra dances are danced to live music, traditionally fiddle tunes — reels and jigs in 32-bar AABB form — played by bands combining fiddle, piano, guitar, banjo, mandolin and other instruments. The music's phrasing closely matches the figures; tempos are brisk, around 112–120 beats per minute.

History

The form descends from the French contredanse and the English longways dances published by John Playford from 1651. It flourished in 18th-century America and survived in rural New England when it fell from fashion elsewhere. A revival, associated with figures such as Ralph Page and later Dudley Laufman, spread the tradition nationally from the 1970s; today communities hold weekly dances in many U.S. and Canadian cities, and events such as dance weekends and the New England Folk Festival sustain the tradition.
