Vladimir K. Zworykin

Vladimir Kosma Zworykin (Russian: Влади́мир Козьми́ч Зворы́кин; 29 July 1888 – 29 July 1982) was a Russian-American inventor and engineer, and a pioneer of television technology. Working for Westinghouse and then RCA, he invented the two devices that made all-electronic television practical: the iconoscope, a camera tube, and the kinescope, a cathode-ray-tube receiver. He is often called, together with Philo Farnsworth and John Logie Baird, one of the "fathers of television".

Early life

Zworykin studied engineering at the St. Petersburg Institute of Technology, where he worked with Boris Rosing, an early researcher into cathode-ray-tube imaging, and also studied under Paul Langevin in Paris. After service in the First World War he emigrated to the United States in 1919, becoming a citizen in 1924, and joined Westinghouse in Pittsburgh.

Television inventions

In 1923 Zworykin filed a patent for an all-electronic television system centred on a camera tube — the future iconoscope — and in 1924 for the kinescope receiver. Westinghouse showed little interest after a crude 1925 demonstration. In 1929 he joined RCA under David Sarnoff, who funded the programme generously; by 1933 the iconoscope was sensitive enough for practical broadcasting, and RCA demonstrated electronic television at the 1939 New York World's Fair. A long patent battle with Philo Farnsworth ended with RCA licensing Farnsworth's image dissector patents — but Zworykin's charge-storage camera tube became the industry standard for two decades.

Later work

At RCA Laboratories in Princeton, which he directed as vice-president, Zworykin worked on electron microscopy, photoelectric cells, night-vision devices, and medical electronics. He received more than 120 patents, the IEEE Medal of Honor, and the National Medal of Science (1966). He died on his 94th birthday in 1982.
