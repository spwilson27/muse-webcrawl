Luminance is a photometric measure of the luminous intensity per unit area of light travelling in a given direction. It describes the amount of light that passes through, is emitted from, or is reflected from a particular area, and falls within a given solid angle. Brightness is the term for the subjective impression of the objective luminance measurement standard (CIE 1924). The SI unit of luminance is the candela per square metre (cd/m²), often called a "nit".

Definition

Luminance is defined by the derivative of luminous flux with respect to both projected area and solid angle: it measures how bright a surface will appear to an observer looking along the direction of travel. For a perfectly diffusing (Lambertian) surface, luminance is the same from all viewing angles. Unlike luminous intensity, luminance of an extended source does not change with distance, which is why distant objects do not appear dimmer per unit area (although their total flux at the eye falls).

Relation to other quantities

Luminance is the photometric counterpart of the radiometric quantity radiance, weighted by the luminosity function describing the human eye's sensitivity to different wavelengths. It is related to illuminance (the light falling on a surface, in lux) and to luminous intensity (in candelas). In video and colour science, the term luma (Y′) denotes a gamma-encoded weighted sum of RGB components used to carry the brightness information of an image, and it is often confused with true (relative) luminance.

Typical values

  • Sun at noon: ~1.6 × 10⁹ cd/m²
  • Incandescent lamp filament: ~10⁷ cd/m²
  • White paper in sunlight: ~30,000 cd/m²
  • Typical computer display: 50–300 cd/m²
  • Moonlit surface: ~0.01 cd/m²
