RISC

Placeholder image: a RISC microprocessor die.

In computer science, a reduced instruction set computer (RISC) is a computer architecture designed to simplify the individual instructions given to the computer to accomplish tasks. Compared to the instructions given to a complex instruction set computer (CISC), a RISC computer might require more instructions (more code) in order to accomplish a task because the individual instructions perform simpler operations. The goal is to offset this need to encode more instructions by making those instructions simple enough that each can be executed in a single clock cycle, allowing pipelining and high clock speeds.

The term RISC was coined by David Patterson of the University of California, Berkeley. Other important early RISC projects included the IBM 801, the Berkeley RISC-V predecessors RISC I and RISC II, and the Stanford MIPS project.

Characteristics

Common characteristics associated with RISC designs include a small, uniform set of instructions with simple addressing modes; a load–store architecture in which only explicit load and store instructions access memory while arithmetic operates on registers; a large number of general-purpose registers; fixed-length instruction encoding to simplify decoding; and an emphasis on compiler optimisation and pipelining rather than microcode.

History

Ideas later recognised as RISC appeared in designs such as the CDC 6600 of the 1960s. The modern RISC philosophy emerged in the late 1970s and early 1980s from the IBM 801 project led by John Cocke, the Berkeley RISC project, and Stanford's MIPS project. Commercial RISC processors followed, including ARM, MIPS, SPARC, PowerPC and DEC Alpha. The ARM architecture, originally developed by Acorn Computers, became dominant in mobile and embedded systems, and by the 2010s ARM-based chips were shipping in the billions per year.

RISC vs. CISC

The classic debate contrasted RISC with CISC designs such as the x86 family. In practice the distinction blurred: x86 processors internally translate instructions into RISC-like micro-operations, while successful RISC architectures added more complex instructions. Energy efficiency, rather than instruction count alone, became the decisive advantage of RISC designs in mobile computing, and high-performance RISC-derived processors such as Apple's M-series chips later matched or exceeded contemporary x86 designs in general-purpose performance.
