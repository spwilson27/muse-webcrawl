Timurid Empire

The Timurid Empire was a late medieval Turco-Mongol empire founded by the conqueror Timur (Tamerlane) and at its height, around 1405, stretching from western Anatolia and the Caucasus through Persia and Central Asia to the borders of western China and northern India. From his capital at Samarkand in Transoxiana, Timur — a Muslim of the Barlas clan who claimed the mantle of Genghis Khan — built by thirty-five years of almost continuous campaigning one of the largest contiguous empires in history, sacking Delhi (1398), Aleppo and Damascus (1400–01), and defeating the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at Ankara (1402), an event that threw the Ottoman state into an eleven-year interregnum. His empire, never an administered state in the bureaucratic sense, fragmented among his descendants after his death in 1405 on campaign against Ming China — but the Timurid successor courts of Samarkand and Herat then produced one of the great flowerings of Persianate art, architecture, astronomy and letters, the Timurid Renaissance, and a Timurid prince, Babur, carried the line's ambitions to India, where in 1526 he founded the Mughal Empire.

Timur's conquests

Timur (1336–1405) rose in the chaos of the Chagatai Khanate's collapse, took Samarkand in 1370, and spent the rest of his life in the field. He conquered Persia city by city (1386–87, and again, with massacres of legendary ferocity at Isfahan and elsewhere, in the 1390s), destroyed the power of the Golden Horde at the Kunduzcha (1391) and Terek (1395) rivers — a blow from which the Mongols of the steppe never recovered, and which inadvertently eased the rise of Moscow — invaded India in 1398, annihilating the Delhi Sultanate's army and sacking Delhi with enormous slaughter, then turned west: Syria fell in 1400–01, Baghdad was retaken and its population massacred, and at Ankara in July 1402 he captured Sultan Bayezid I, who died in captivity. Timur died at Otrar in February 1405, marching at the head of a vast army to conquer Ming China. His campaigns are reckoned among the most destructive in history — population losses across his conquests are estimated in the millions, and the pyramids of skulls he raised became his emblem — while the booty and the deported craftsmen of a dozen capitals were poured into Samarkand, whose Bibi-Khanym mosque and Gur-e Amir mausoleum (where Timur lies beneath the famous jade cenotaph) still proclaim his ambition.

The successors and the Timurid Renaissance

Of Timur's sons, Shah Rukh (r. 1409–1447), ruling from Herat, preserved the empire's eastern half in peace for four decades, fostering trade with Ming China and a brilliant court culture. His son Ulugh Beg, governor and then ruler at Samarkand, was the dynasty's prodigy: a working astronomer whose observatory and star tables (the Zij-i Sultani) were the most accurate produced before the telescope, and whose madrasa still fronts the Registan. After Shah Rukh's death the empire splintered among Timurid princes; under Sultan Husayn Bayqara at Herat (r. 1469–1506) came a final flowering — the poetry of Jami and of Mir Ali Shir Nava'i, who raised Chagatai Turkic to a literary language, and the miniatures of Behzad, the greatest painter of the Persian tradition. The capitals' art radiated outward, defining the classical style of Persianate culture from Ottoman Anatolia to Mughal India.

Fall and legacy

The end came from the steppe: the Uzbeks under Muhammad Shaybani took Samarkand (1500–01) and Herat (1507), extinguishing Timurid rule in Central Asia, while Safavid Persia rose in the west. But the dynasty's most consequential act lay ahead: Zahir-ud-din Muhammad Babur, a Timurid prince of Fergana driven from Samarkand three times, turned to Kabul and then to India, where at Panipat in 1526 he founded the empire the world knows as Mughal — Babur, author of the Baburnama, one of the great autobiographies of world literature, consciously styled himself Timur's heir. In Central Asia, Timur himself, suppressed in the Soviet period, was rehabilitated after 1991 as the founding hero of Uzbekistan, his statues replacing Marx in Tashkent's squares.
