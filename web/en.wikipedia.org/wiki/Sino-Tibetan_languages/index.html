Sino-Tibetan languages

The Sino-Tibetan languages, also called Trans-Himalayan in some scholarship, form a family of more than 400 languages spoken across East, South and Southeast Asia. By number of native speakers it is the second-largest language family in the world, after Indo-European, owing chiefly to the Sinitic languages (Chinese varieties), which include the world's most spoken first language, Mandarin. The other major branch is Tibeto-Burman, which includes Tibetan and Burmese.

Classification

The family is conventionally divided into two primary branches: Sinitic (Chinese) and Tibeto-Burman. The internal structure of Tibeto-Burman remains debated, with subgroups such as Tibetic, Lolo-Burmese, Karenic, Bodo-Garo and numerous smaller clusters in the Himalayas and Northeast India. Some scholars question whether Sinitic is coordinate with all the rest; the term "Trans-Himalayan" is sometimes used to avoid implying a settled binary split.

Characteristics

Many Sino-Tibetan languages are tonal, isolating or analytic, and predominantly monosyllabic in their roots, though the degree varies: Sinitic and Karenic are typically strictly analytic, while many Himalayan languages have complex verb agreement morphology. Word order varies, with Sinitic and Karenic being subject–verb–object and most Tibeto-Burman languages subject–object–verb.

History

The family's homeland is generally placed in northern China or the eastern Himalayan region, with diversification beginning perhaps 6,000 years ago alongside early farming populations of the Yellow River basin. Old Chinese is attested in oracle bone inscriptions from the late 2nd millennium BC, and Classical Tibetan and Old Burmese provide ancient records for Tibeto-Burman.
