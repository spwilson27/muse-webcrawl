Mixed economy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mixed economy is an economic system that combines elements of a market economy with elements of a planned economy, markets with state intervention, or private enterprise with public enterprise. Most contemporary economies are mixed in practice: markets allocate most goods, while states provide public goods, regulate externalities and market power, run social insurance, own some enterprises, and manage macroeconomic stabilisation.

Varieties

The mix varies by country and era: the post-war Western European settlement (nationalised utilities, welfare states) was more state-heavy than the US model; the Nordic countries combine open markets with very large public sectors; East Asian developmental states directed credit and industry while relying on export markets; China's "socialist market economy" pairs market mechanisms with pervasive party-state control. "Varieties of capitalism" scholarship (Hall and Soskice) distinguishes liberal from coordinated market economies by how firms finance, train and bargain — the institutional wiring behind the market/state ratio. Debates over privatisation (1980s–90s) and re-nationalisation or industrial policy (2020s) are movements along this spectrum rather than escapes from it.
