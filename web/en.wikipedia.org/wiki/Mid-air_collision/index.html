Mid-air collision

A mid-air collision is an accident in which two or more aircraft come into unplanned contact during flight. Owing to the relatively high speeds involved and the likelihood of subsequent impact with the ground or sea, mid-air collisions usually result in severe damage or the total loss of at least one of the aircraft involved, and they are almost always fatal to occupants of at least one aircraft.

Causes

Mid-air collisions typically result from errors by pilots or air traffic controllers, failures of communication, or flight in congested or uncontrolled airspace where separation depends on the "see and avoid" principle. Military training flights, airshows, and aerial refuelling operations carry particular risks.

Notable examples

  • 1956 Grand Canyon collision — TWA and United Air Lines airliners collided over the Grand Canyon, killing all 128 aboard both aircraft; the disaster spurred major reform of US air traffic control.
  • 1978 San Diego collision — PSA Flight 182 collided with a Cessna 172; 144 people died.
  • 1986 Cerritos collision — Aeroméxico Flight 498 collided with a Piper Archer over Los Angeles suburbs.
  • 1996 Charkhi Dadri collision — the deadliest mid-air collision, between a Saudi Arabian Airlines Boeing 747 and a Kazakhstan Airlines Il-76 near New Delhi, killing 349 people.
  • 2002 Überlingen collision — a Bashkirian Airlines Tu-154 and a DHL Boeing 757 collided over Germany, killing 71, mostly children.

Prevention

Prevention relies on air traffic control separation, transponder-based surveillance, and airborne collision avoidance systems such as TCAS, which instructs pilots to climb or descend when a conflict is detected. After Überlingen, procedures were clarified so that TCAS instructions take precedence over controller instructions.
