Non-covalent interaction

A non-covalent interaction is a type of chemical bond that, unlike a covalent bond, does not involve the sharing of electron pairs. Non-covalent interactions are individually weak but collectively crucial to the structure and function of biomolecules.

Types

Major classes include hydrogen bonds, ionic (electrostatic) interactions, van der Waals forces (including London dispersion), hydrophobic interactions, π-stacking between aromatic rings, halogen bonds and cation–π interactions. Typical strengths range from about 1 to 35 kJ/mol, far below covalent bonds.

Biological role

Protein folding, DNA double helix stability, enzyme–substrate recognition, antibody binding and molecular self-assembly all depend on the sum of many non-covalent contacts. Because they are reversible, these interactions allow dynamic processes such as signalling and allosteric regulation.

Supramolecular chemistry

Non-covalent synthesis is the basis of supramolecular chemistry and host–guest systems, recognised by the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Cram, Lehn and Pedersen.
