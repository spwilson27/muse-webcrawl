Benoit Mandelbrot

The Mandelbrot set, whose intricate boundary made fractals famous.

Benoit B. Mandelbrot (20 November 1924 – 14 October 2010) was a Polish-born French-American mathematician and polymath, widely known as the father of fractal geometry. Working much of his career at IBM, he developed a "theory of roughness" with applications in mathematics, physics, finance, geology and computer graphics, and popularised the concept of the fractal.

Early life and education

Mandelbrot was born in Warsaw to a Lithuanian Jewish family and emigrated with his family to Paris in 1936, escaping wartime persecution in France. He studied at the École Polytechnique under Gaston Julia and Paul Lévy, gained a master's degree in aeronautics from Caltech, and received his doctorate from the University of Paris in 1952. His uncle Szolem Mandelbrojt was a prominent mathematician of the Bourbaki circle, though Mandelbrot's own highly visual, intuition-driven style stood apart from that group's formalism.

Fractals

In 1975 Mandelbrot coined the word fractal (from the Latin fractus, "broken") to describe geometric shapes that are self-similar at every scale and often have non-integer dimension. His 1967 paper "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?" showed that the measured length of a coastline grows without bound as the measuring scale shrinks — the coastline paradox. His books Fractals: Form, Chance and Dimension (1977) and The Fractal Geometry of Nature (1982) argued that clouds, mountains, rivers and prices are better described by rough fractals than by smooth Euclidean geometry.

The Mandelbrot set, generated by iterating the simple rule z → z² + c, became the most famous mathematical image of the computer age, revealing endless detail at its boundary. Mandelbrot also contributed the Mandelbrot–Julia set theory, fractal models of financial markets later presented in The (Mis)behavior of Markets (2004), and early work on Zipf's law and information theory.

Career and honours

Mandelbrot spent 35 years at IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center, becoming an IBM Fellow, and later was Sterling Professor of Mathematical Sciences at Yale. Among his many honours were the Wolf Prize for Physics (1993), the Japan Prize (2003) and the Légion d'honneur. He died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 2010.
