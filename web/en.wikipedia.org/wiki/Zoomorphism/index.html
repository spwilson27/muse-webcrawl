Zoomorphism

Zoomorphism is the shaping of an object, artwork, deity or concept in animal form, or the attribution of animal characteristics to non-animal subjects. It is the inverse of anthropomorphism, which attributes human traits to animals or objects. Zoomorphic representation is among the oldest motifs in human art and religion, from Palaeolithic carvings to modern design.

Zoomorphic vessels and figures, shaped as animals, occur in cultures worldwide from prehistory onward.

In religion

Many religious traditions depict gods wholly or partly in animal form. Ancient Egyptian deities frequently combined animal heads with human bodies — Horus with a falcon's head, Anubis with a jackal's, Bastet as a cat — while some, such as the Apis bull, were worshipped as animals outright. In Hinduism, the elephant-headed Ganesha and the monkey god Hanuman are prominent zoomorphic or hybrid forms. Zoomorphic symbolism also appears in the Hebrew Bible and Christian art, where the four Evangelists are symbolised by a lion, an ox, an eagle and a man, and the Holy Spirit is depicted as a dove.

In art and language

Zoomorphic art includes animal-shaped vessels (rhytons), jewellery, weapons and architecture, from Scythian goldwork and Celtic ornament to the animal interlace of Viking art. In literature and everyday language, zoomorphism describes people in animal terms — calling someone a "snake", "wolf" or "lamb" — and underlies fables, heraldry and much fantasy fiction. The concept also appears in psychology and design, where products shaped like animals, from children's toys to kettles, exploit the appeal of zoomorphic form.
