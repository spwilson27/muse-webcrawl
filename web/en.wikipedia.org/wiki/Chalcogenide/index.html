Chalcogenide

A chalcogenide glass sample

A chalcogenide is a chemical compound consisting of at least one chalcogen anion and at least one more electropositive element. Although all group 16 elements of the periodic table are defined as chalcogens, the term chalcogenide is more commonly reserved for sulfides, selenides, and tellurides, rather than oxides.

Many metal ores exist as chalcogenides. Photoconductive chalcogenide materials have many practical applications, notably in xerography. Interest in these materials has also grown because of uses in phase-change memory and in infrared optics, where chalcogenide glasses transmit wavelengths blocked by ordinary silica glass.

Metal chalcogenides

Many chalcogenides are based on transition metals. Iron pyrite (FeS2) is a classic example, and molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) is the archetypal two-dimensional layered material. Some chalcogenides display unusual electronic properties, including superconductivity in compounds such as FeSe.

Chalcogenide glasses

Chalcogenide glasses are covalent glasses containing one or more chalcogens (sulfur, selenium, or tellurium) bonded to elements such as germanium, arsenic, or antimony. They are transparent in the infrared and can be drawn into optical fibers for sensing, imaging, and telecommunications.

Applications

Chalcogenides are used in phase-change memory (PCM), rewritable optical discs, solar cells (such as CdTe and CIGS), thermoelectric devices, and infrared lenses and windows. The alloy Ge2Sb2Te5 (GST) is widely used in phase-change storage because it switches rapidly and reversibly between amorphous and crystalline states.
