The history of Thailand spans from early Bronze Age settlements such as Ban Chiang to a succession of Tai kingdoms — Sukhothai, Ayutthaya, and the Chakri kingdom centred on Bangkok. Thailand (formerly Siam) is notable as the only Southeast Asian nation never formally colonised by a European power, though it ceded territory to Britain and France in the colonial era and was occupied by Japan during the Second World War.

Early history

The Chao Phraya basin saw the Mon Dvaravati culture flourish from the sixth century, bringing Theravada Buddhism; from the tenth century the Khmer Empire dominated much of the region. Tai peoples, migrating southward from the region of southern China and northern Vietnam, established principalities such as Chiang Mai (Lanna) and Sukhothai.

Sukhothai and Ayutthaya

In 1238 Si Inthrathit founded the Sukhothai Kingdom, considered the first Siamese state. King Ramkhamhaeng (r. 1279–1298) is credited with creating the Thai script and extending the kingdom's influence. Sukhothai declined and was absorbed by the rising kingdom of Ayutthaya, founded in 1350 by King Uthong (Ramathibodi I).

Ayutthaya grew into one of Asia's great trading cities, dealing with China, India, Persia, Japan, and Europe. It warred repeatedly with Burma; the Burmese captured it in 1569 and destroyed it utterly in April 1767 after a long siege. General Taksin regrouped the Siamese at Thonburi and reunified the country, but was overthrown in 1782 by Chao Phraya Chakri, who became Rama I and founded Bangkok as the capital.

The Bangkok era and modernisation

The early Chakri kings consolidated the kingdom. King Mongkut (Rama IV, r. 1851–1868) opened Siam to Western trade with the Bowring Treaty (1855). His son Chulalongkorn (Rama V, r. 1868–1910) abolished slavery, centralised administration, and preserved independence by playing Britain and France against each other, at the price of ceding Laos, Cambodia, and Malay territories.

The revolution of 1932 ended the absolute monarchy and established a constitutional monarchy. The country, renamed Thailand in 1939, allied with Japan in 1941 and afterwards with the United States during the Cold War, experiencing alternating military and civilian governments. The 1973 uprising brought a brief democratic period, followed by the 1976 Thammasat massacre and further coups. Since the 1990s Thailand has oscillated between elected governments — notably those of Thaksin Shinawatra and his allies — and military coups in 2006 and 2014.
