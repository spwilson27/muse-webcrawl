Cauchy–Riemann equations

The Cauchy–Riemann equations are a pair of partial differential equations which, together with continuity conditions, characterise holomorphic (complex differentiable) functions. For f(x + iy) = u(x, y) + i v(x, y), they read: ∂u/∂x = ∂v/∂y and ∂u/∂y = −∂v/∂x.

Interpretation

The equations say that the real and imaginary parts of a holomorphic function are harmonically conjugate: each satisfies Laplace's equation, and their level curves meet orthogonally. Geometrically they express that a complex-differentiable map is conformal — it preserves angles and locally acts as a rotation-dilation.

History

Versions of the equations appeared in work of d'Alembert on fluid flow in 1752 and were used by Euler; Cauchy employed them systematically from 1814, and Riemann made them foundational in his 1851 dissertation on complex function theory. See Cauchy's integral formula.
