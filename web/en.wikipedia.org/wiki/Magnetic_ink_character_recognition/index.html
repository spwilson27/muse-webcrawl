Magnetic ink character recognition

Magnetic ink character recognition code, known in short as MICR code, is a character recognition technology used mainly by the banking industry to streamline the processing and clearance of cheques and other documents. MICR encoding, called the MICR line, is at the bottom of cheques and other vouchers and typically includes the document-type indicator, bank code, bank account number, cheque number, cheque amount (usually added after a cheque is presented for payment), and a control indicator.

How it works

The characters are printed with a special ink containing magnetizable particles, usually iron oxide. A reader-sorter machine magnetizes the ink and then reads the distinctive magnetic waveform produced by each character as it passes a read head. Because recognition depends on the magnetic signal rather than the optical appearance, MICR characters remain readable even when stamped over, marked, or soiled.

Fonts

Two MICR fonts are in use worldwide. E-13B, developed in the 1950s under the auspices of the American Bankers Association, is used in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and other countries. CMC-7, developed in France by Bull, is used in much of Europe and South America. E-13B has a repertoire of 14 characters: the digits 0–9 and four control symbols (transit, amount, on-us, and dash).

History and decline

MICR was demonstrated to the American Bankers Association in 1956 and adopted as standard by 1959, with SRI International and General Electric among the key developers; the distinctive E-13B shapes were designed by the Stanford Research Institute team. Although electronic payments and image-based cheque truncation (such as the US Check 21 Act) have reduced the physical sorting of paper, MICR lines remain standard on cheques.
