Viktor Meyer (8 September 1848 – 8 August 1897) was a German chemist who made major contributions to organic and inorganic chemistry. He is best known for inventing the Victor Meyer apparatus for determining vapour densities (and hence molecular weights), for discovering thiophene, and for coining the term "stereochemistry".

Career

Meyer studied at Heidelberg under Robert Bunsen and Emil Erlenmeyer, receiving his doctorate at the age of nineteen. After a period with Adolf von Baeyer in Berlin, he held professorships at the Stuttgart Polytechnic, the University of Zürich, Göttingen, and finally Heidelberg, where he succeeded Bunsen in 1889.

Scientific work

In 1882, while lecturing on benzene derivatives, Meyer discovered thiophene as a contaminant responsible for a colour reaction previously attributed to benzene itself — a classic episode in chemical research. His group systematised the chemistry of nitro compounds and oximes, and he introduced the concept of steric hindrance to explain why some esterifications fail. His vapour-density method, published in 1878, became a standard laboratory technique.

Death

Overworked and suffering from nervous exhaustion and depression, Meyer took his own life in Heidelberg in 1897, at the height of his reputation.
