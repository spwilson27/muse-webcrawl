Methanol toxicity

Methanol toxicity (also called methanol poisoning) is poisoning from methanol, most commonly after drinking adulterated or improperly distilled alcoholic beverages, or ingesting products such as windshield washer fluid. Methanol itself is only mildly intoxicating; its danger comes from its metabolites. As little as 10 mL of pure methanol can cause permanent blindness, and around 30 mL can be fatal.

Mechanism

In the liver, alcohol dehydrogenase converts methanol to formaldehyde, which aldehyde dehydrogenase rapidly converts to formic acid. Formate accumulates because its further breakdown is slow in humans, causing a severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. Formate also inhibits mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase and is particularly toxic to the optic nerve, producing the characteristic visual disturbances — described as looking through a snowstorm — and potentially permanent blindness.

Symptoms and treatment

Symptoms are often delayed 12–24 hours after ingestion while metabolites accumulate. They include headache, dizziness, nausea, abdominal pain, visual loss, and in severe cases coma, seizures, and death.

Treatment aims to block methanol metabolism and remove it from the body. Antidotes include fomepizole, a competitive inhibitor of alcohol dehydrogenase, and ethanol, which competes for the same enzyme. Haemodialysis removes methanol and formate and corrects acidosis; sodium bicarbonate and folate therapy are also used.

Outbreaks

Mass methanol poisonings recur worldwide when counterfeit or moonshine alcohol is made with industrial methanol, sometimes killing dozens of people in a single incident.
