Geumgwan Gaya

Capital: Gimhae (present-day South Gyeongsang Province)
Existed: 42 – 532 CE (traditional dates)
First king: Suro of Geumgwan Gaya
Succeeded by: Silla

Geumgwan Gaya (Korean: 금관가야; "Golden Crown Gaya"), also known as Bon-Gaya, was the ruling city-state of the Gaya confederacy during the Three Kingdoms period of Korea, centred on present-day Gimhae in the lower Nakdong River valley. According to the foundation legend recorded in the Samguk Yusa, it was founded in 42 CE by King Suro, who descended from heaven in a golden egg, and its royal line continued until the state surrendered to Silla in 532 CE.

History

Geumgwan Gaya grew wealthy on its strategic position: fertile rice land in the Nakdong delta, abundant iron production, and a harbour that made it a hub of trade with the Chinese commanderies, the Wa polities of the Japanese archipelago, and the other Gaya states. Its port at Gimhae exported iron ingots — used almost as currency — throughout the region.

The early 5th century brought pressure from Goguryeo, whose campaigns in support of Silla broke Geumgwan Gaya's dominance of the confederacy, and leadership passed to Daegaya in the west. Weakened by Silla's expansion and internal division, Geumgwan Gaya was finally annexed by King Beopheung of Silla in 532. Its last king, Guhyeong, surrendered and was incorporated into Silla's aristocracy with his territory reorganised as Geumgwan-gun.

Legend of King Suro

The Samguk Yusa recounts that nine village chiefs, guided by a heavenly voice, watched six golden eggs descend from the sky; from them hatched six princes, the eldest of whom, Suro, became king of Geumgwan. His queen, Heo Hwang-ok, was said to have arrived by ship from the distant kingdom of Ayuta (identified in tradition with Ayodhya in India), bringing with her a pagoda and cargo of silk and jewels — a legend that has made the Gimhae Kim clan's royal tomb a site of Korean–Indian cultural exchange.

Archaeology

The royal tomb clusters at Daeseong-dong and the shell middens around Gimhae have yielded rich grave goods — iron armour, horse trappings, gold ornaments, and imported glass and pottery — confirming the state's wealth and maritime connections. The Gaya Tumuli, including those of Geumgwan Gaya, were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.
