Honour killing


Protest against honour killings

An honour killing (American English: honor killing) is a form of femicide in which a person, usually a woman or girl, is murdered by members of her family or community because the perpetrators believe she has brought dishonour or shame upon the family. Typical triggers cited by perpetrators include refusing an arranged marriage, being in a relationship disapproved of by the family, seeking a divorce, dressing in ways deemed inappropriate, or being a victim of sexual assault.

Prevalence

Honour killings occur in many parts of the world, including the Middle East, South Asia, and within immigrant communities elsewhere. The United Nations Population Fund has estimated that as many as 5,000 women are killed each year in honour-related crimes, though the true number is believed to be substantially higher because many cases go unreported or are disguised as accidents or suicides. They form part of the broader category of honour-based abuse, which also includes forced marriage and female genital mutilation.

Legal responses

Historically, some legal systems treated honour as a mitigating factor—so-called "crimes of passion" provisions or reduced penalties for honour killings. Over recent decades many countries have reformed such laws: Jordan, Pakistan (in 2016), and others have tightened penalties, and international bodies including the United Nations General Assembly have adopted resolutions calling for the elimination of crimes against women committed in the name of honour. The Council of Europe and the Istanbul Convention explicitly reject custom, tradition, or honour as justifications for violence.

Related practices

Related phenomena include dowry deaths in South Asia, where brides are killed over disputes about dowry payments, and "honour suicides," in which victims are pressured to take their own lives. Activists and scholars debate the role of culture and religion; major religious authorities have condemned the practice, and researchers generally attribute it to patriarchal social norms rather than religious doctrine.
