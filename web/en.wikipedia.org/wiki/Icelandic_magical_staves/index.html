Icelandic magical staves

Icelandic magical staves (Icelandic: galdrastafir) are sigils — symbols derived from runes and other characters — that were believed to have magical effects when drawn, carved, or worn. They are preserved mainly in Icelandic grimoires (galdrabækur) from the 17th century onward, reflecting a folk-magic tradition that combined runic lore, medieval European occultism, Christian prayer, and pre-Christian Norse elements.

Notable staves

  • Ægishjálmur (Helm of Awe) — a stave of eight spiked arms radiating from a center point, believed to confer invincibility and strike terror into enemies. A symbol worn between the eyes, it is mentioned in the Völsunga saga and is today the most popular of the staves as a tattoo and souvenir.
  • Vegvísir (Wayfinder) — an eight-armed stave said to prevent its bearer from losing his way, even in storms and when the route is unknown. It is attested in the 19th-century Huld manuscript and popularized internationally in recent decades.
  • Other staves include designs for specific purposes: to catch a thief, to win a wrestling match (glíma), to obtain a wish, to calm anger, to prevent drowning, or to win love.

Grimoires and practice

The staves were recorded in manuscripts such as the Galdrabók (c. 1600), which combines Icelandic material with continental influences. Their use commonly involved carving the sign on wood, metal, or skin, sometimes accompanied by a spoken charm or prayer. During the 17th-century Icelandic witch trials, possession of runes and staves was among the charges brought against accused sorcerers, most of whom were men.

Modern popularity

Today the staves appear widely in Icelandic popular culture, tourism, music (both Björk and members of the metal scene have worn them as tattoos), and neopagan practice, though scholars note that some popular attributions — such as Viking-age origins for the Vegvísir — are not historically supported.
