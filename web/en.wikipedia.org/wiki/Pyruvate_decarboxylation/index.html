Pyruvate decarboxylation


Pyruvate converted to acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2

Pyruvate decarboxylation or pyruvate oxidation, also known as the link reaction, is the conversion of pyruvate into acetyl-CoA by the enzyme complex pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. It is called the "link reaction" because it links glycolysis, which produces pyruvate in the cytosol, to the citric acid cycle, which consumes acetyl-CoA in the mitochondrial matrix.

Reaction

Pyruvate, a three-carbon molecule produced by glycolysis, is transported into the mitochondrion and oxidatively decarboxylated: one carbon is released as carbon dioxide, the remaining two-carbon acetyl group is attached to coenzyme A, and NAD+ is reduced to NADH. The overall transformation is:

pyruvate + NAD+ + CoA → acetyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+

Significance

The reaction is irreversible in animals and commits the carbon skeleton of glucose to oxidation or fat synthesis: acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to pyruvate, which is why fatty acids cannot be net-converted into glucose. Per molecule of glucose, two pyruvate molecules undergo the link reaction, yielding 2 acetyl-CoA, 2 NADH and 2 CO2. In yeast and some bacteria, a different enzyme, pyruvate decarboxylase, converts pyruvate to acetaldehyde during alcoholic fermentation.
