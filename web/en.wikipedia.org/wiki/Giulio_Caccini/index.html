Giulio Caccini

Born: October 8, 1551, Rome, Papal States

Died: Buried December 10, 1618, Florence

Occupation: Composer, singer, teacher

Known for: Le nuove musiche (1602), early opera

Giulio Caccini (October 8, 1551 – buried December 10, 1618) was an Italian composer, teacher, and singer of the late Renaissance and early Baroque periods. A leading member of the Florentine circle known as the Camerata, he was a pioneer of the new solo song with basso continuo accompaniment and one of the creators of opera.

Life

Caccini was born in Rome and as a boy sang in the Cappella Giulia at St Peter's. Taken to Florence in 1565, he entered the service of the Medici court, where his singing won him fame across Europe. He participated in the discussions and experiments of the Florentine Camerata around Count Giovanni de' Bardi, which sought to revive the sung drama of ancient Greece. He composed music for the celebrated intermedi of 1589 and remained a central figure in Florentine musical life until his death.

Works

Caccini's most important publication is Le nuove musiche (1602), a collection of monodies and solo madrigals whose preface is one of the key documents of early Baroque music, explaining the new expressive style of singing — recitative-like declamation with written-out ornamentation over a basso continuo — and introducing terms for ornaments such as the trillo and gruppo.

He set the libretto of Ottavio Rinuccini's Euridice — the earliest surviving opera, published in 1600–1601, though Jacopo Peri's setting was performed first — and completed his own Il rapimento di Cefalo (1600) for the Medici wedding festivities. His rivalry with Peri and with Emilio de' Cavalieri over priority in the invention of the new style was intense, and Caccini notoriously prevented his singers from performing music by others in the 1600 Euridice.

Legacy

Caccini helped establish monody as the foundation of Baroque vocal music and trained many singers, including his daughters Francesca Caccini — a noted composer in her own right — and Settimia Caccini. The famous "Ave Maria" long attributed to him was in fact composed by the Soviet musician Vladimir Vavilov in the 20th century.
