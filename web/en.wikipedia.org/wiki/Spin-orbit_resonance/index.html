Spin-orbit resonance

A spin-orbit resonance is an orbital resonance in which a body's rotation period and orbital period are related by a ratio of small integers, maintained by tidal interactions. It is the generalisation of tidal locking (the 1:1 resonance, in which the body always shows the same face to its primary) to other commensurabilities.

Mechanism

Tidal forces raised by the primary on an orbiting body dissipate energy and evolve its spin. For a body in a circular orbit, tides typically drive it to the synchronous 1:1 state. However, if the orbit is eccentric, the orbital angular velocity varies around the orbit while the spin remains roughly constant, and the torque averaged over an orbit can vanish at other ratios, such as 3:2. A permanent asymmetry in the body's shape (a triaxial figure) helps to lock it into such a resonance.

Examples

The classic example is Mercury, which rotates on its axis exactly three times for every two revolutions around the Sun (a 3:2 resonance), a fact established by radar observations in 1965 after the planet had long been assumed to be tidally locked 1:1. Its orbital eccentricity (0.206) makes the 3:2 state the stable endpoint. The Moon is in a 1:1 resonance with Earth, as are most large moons of the outer planets. Pluto and its moon Charon are doubly tidally locked: each keeps the same face toward the other. Higher resonances and chaotic rotation can occur for irregular bodies, as with Saturn's moon Hyperion, which tumbles chaotically. Cassini states describe the related equilibrium configurations of the spin axis orientation.
