River bed

A river bed or streambed is the bottom of a river or stream on which the water flows. It is the physical confine of the normal water course, lying below the banks on either side. River beds may be formed of bedrock, gravel, sand, silt, or clay, and their character strongly influences the ecology and hydraulics of the watercourse above them.

Formation and composition

The material composing a river bed is largely determined by the energy of the stream. In steep headwater reaches, where flow velocities are high, beds are typically armored with coarse cobbles and boulders, with finer material winnowed away. In lowland reaches, where gradients flatten and flow slows, beds consist of sand, silt, and mud deposited from suspension. Bed material is continually sorted by the flow: floods entrain and move sediment downstream, while quieter periods allow deposition.

Geologists distinguish between bedrock channels, where the bed is cut into solid rock, and alluvial channels, where the bed is formed in sediment previously deposited by the river itself. Most lowland rivers are alluvial and can shift their beds substantially over time through erosion and deposition.

Bedforms

Sandy river beds are rarely flat. The interaction between flowing water and mobile sediment produces a variety of bedforms, including ripples, dunes, and antidunes. These features migrate downstream as sediment is eroded from their upstream (stoss) faces and deposited on their steeper downstream (lee) faces. The size and shape of bedforms depend on flow depth, velocity, and grain size, and they are used by sedimentologists to interpret the flow conditions of ancient rivers preserved in the rock record.

Ecology

The river bed is a habitat in its own right. The zone of saturated sediment beneath and beside the channel, known as the hyporheic zone, hosts invertebrates, microorganisms, and the eggs of many fish species. Salmon and trout, for example, bury their eggs in gravel beds in nests called redds, where oxygenated water percolating through the gravel sustains the developing embryos. Benthic invertebrates such as mayfly and caddisfly larvae live on and within the bed and form the base of the river food web.

Human interaction

River beds have been extensively modified by people. Dredging deepens beds for navigation; gravel extraction removes bed material for construction aggregate; and dams trap sediment upstream, starving downstream beds and causing them to coarsen or incise. When a river is diverted or dries up, its exposed bed may persist as a dry channel, arroyo, or wadi.
