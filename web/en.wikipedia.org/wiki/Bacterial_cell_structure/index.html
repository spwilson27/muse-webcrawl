Bacterial cell structure

Bacterial cell

DomainBacteria
Typical size0.5–5.0 μm
StructuresCell wall, membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, nucleoid; optional flagella, pili, capsule, plasmids, endospores

Bacterial cell structure describes the organisation of the prokaryotic cell, which lacks a membrane-bound nucleus and most of the organelles found in eukaryotic cells. Despite their relative simplicity, bacterial cells possess a highly organised internal structure, and many bear specialised external appendages that allow them to move, attach to surfaces, and exchange genetic material.

Intracellular structures

Nucleoid

The genetic material of a bacterium is concentrated in an irregularly shaped region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid. It typically contains a single circular chromosome of double-stranded DNA, together with associated proteins (such as HU and IHF) and RNA. Unlike the eukaryotic nucleus, the nucleoid is not surrounded by a membrane.

Plasmids

Many bacteria carry plasmids: small, usually circular, extrachromosomal DNA molecules that replicate independently of the chromosome. Plasmids often carry genes conferring advantages such as antibiotic resistance, and they can be transferred between cells by conjugation.

Ribosomes

Bacterial ribosomes are 70S particles, composed of a 50S large subunit and a 30S small subunit. They are the sites of protein synthesis and are a major target of antibiotics, many of which exploit the structural differences between bacterial and eukaryotic ribosomes.

Inclusion bodies and storage granules

Bacteria store nutrients in cytoplasmic granules, including glycogen, poly-β-hydroxybutyrate (a lipid-like polymer), polyphosphate (volutin) granules, and sulphur granules in photosynthetic and chemolithotrophic sulphur bacteria. Gas vesicles in some aquatic bacteria provide buoyancy.

Endospores

Certain Gram-positive genera, notably Bacillus and Clostridium, form endospores — dormant, highly resistant structures produced within the cell in response to nutrient limitation. Endospores can survive boiling, desiccation, radiation and disinfectants, and may remain viable for very long periods.

Cell envelope

Plasma membrane

The cytoplasmic membrane is a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. It acts as a selective permeability barrier, generates the proton motive force, and houses the electron transport chain and ATP synthase in most bacteria.

Cell wall and Gram staining

Outside the membrane lies a rigid cell wall of peptidoglycan (murein), a mesh of sugar chains cross-linked by short peptides. In Gram-positive bacteria the wall is thick (with teichoic acids) and retains the crystal violet stain; in Gram-negative bacteria the peptidoglycan layer is thin and is surrounded by an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS), whose lipid A component acts as an endotoxin. The space between the membranes is the periplasm. Mycoplasma species lack a cell wall entirely.

Capsule and S-layer

Many bacteria are coated with a capsule or slime layer of polysaccharide (occasionally polypeptide), which protects against phagocytosis and desiccation and contributes to biofilm formation. Some species also possess a crystalline protein S-layer.

External structures

Flagella

Bacterial flagella are helical filaments of flagellin rotated by a membrane-embedded motor — the basal body — powered by the proton motive force. Depending on species, flagella may be monotrichous, lophotrichous, amphitrichous or peritrichous. Rotation produces swimming motility, directed by chemotaxis.

Pili and fimbriae

Fimbriae are short hair-like appendages used for attachment to surfaces and host tissues. Longer sex pili mediate conjugation, the transfer of plasmid DNA between cells.
