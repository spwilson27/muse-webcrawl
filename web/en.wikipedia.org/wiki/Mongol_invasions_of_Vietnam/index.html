Mongol invasions of Vietnam

The Mongol invasions of Vietnam were three major military campaigns launched by the Mongol Empire and the Yuan dynasty against the Vietnamese kingdoms of Đại Việt (under the Trần dynasty) and Champa in 1258, 1285 and 1287–88. Despite initial successes, including the temporary capture of the Đại Việt capital Thăng Long (Hanoi), all three invasions ultimately failed, and the Trần dynasty preserved Vietnamese independence.

First invasion (1258)

In 1258 a Mongol army under Uriyangkhadai invaded Đại Việt en route to attack the Southern Song from the south. The Vietnamese evacuated and then harassed the occupiers of Thăng Long; weakened by the tropical climate and disease, the Mongols withdrew. King Trần Thái Tông agreed to a tributary relationship, forestalling further conflict for a generation.

Second invasion (1285)

Kublai Khan, having founded the Yuan dynasty, demanded passage to attack Champa and then outright submission. In 1285 large Yuan forces under Prince Toghon invaded from the north while Sogetu advanced from Champa. The Trần court, led militarily by Prince Trần Hưng Đạo, abandoned the capital, avoided pitched battle, and starved the invaders with scorched-earth tactics. Yuan forces, ravaged by heat and disease and ambushed on their retreat, withdrew with heavy losses.

Third invasion (1287–88)

The final invasion of 1287–88 ended in the famous Battle of Bạch Đẻng (1288), in which Trần Hưng Đạo destroyed the Yuan fleet by planting iron-tipped stakes in the riverbed, exposed at low tide, replicating the victory of Ngô Quyền over the Chinese in 938. Đại Việt thereafter resumed nominal tribute, and the Yuan abandoned plans of conquest.

Legacy

The victories are central to Vietnamese national memory; Trần Hưng Đạo is venerated as a national hero, and the campaigns are a classic illustration of a smaller state defeating a great empire through guerrilla strategy, terrain and climate.
