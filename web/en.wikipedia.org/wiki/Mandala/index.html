Mandala

Mandala
OriginIndian religions (Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism)
Meaning"Circle" (Sanskrit: मण्डल)
UsesMeditation, ritual, consecration, spiritual instruction

A mandala (Sanskrit: मण्डल, romanized: maṇḍala, lit. "circle") is a geometric configuration of symbols used in the spiritual traditions of Indian religions, particularly Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism, as a tool for meditation, ritual and devotion. In the generic sense, a mandala is a diagram representing the universe, organised around a unifying centre, typically laid out as a square with four gates containing a circle with a central point. Practitioners use the mandala as a focus for attention, a map of sacred realms, and an aid to trance and meditation.

Hinduism

In Hindu ritual, a basic mandala form called a yantra — composed of squares, triangles and floral patterns — serves as a two-dimensional representation of a deity's abode and as a focus for worship. The most famous yantra, the Shri Yantra, consists of nine interlocking triangles. Mandalas also structure temple architecture and festival ground paintings across South Asia.

Buddhism

In Vajrayana (Tantric) Buddhism, mandalas are elaborate sacred diagrams depicting the palaces and retinues of particular buddhas and deities, used in initiation (abhisheka), visualisation practice and consecration. Famous examples include the Mandala of the Two Realms in Japanese Shingon Buddhism — the Womb Realm (Garbhadhatu) and Diamond Realm (Vajradhatu) mandalas — and the Kalachakra mandala. Tibetan Buddhist monks create intricate temporary mandalas from coloured sand; after days or weeks of work the sand mandala is ritually dismantled and the sand poured into a river, teaching the doctrine of impermanence.

Psychology

The Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung, who painted mandalas in his own notebooks and observed them in patients' art, interpreted the mandala as a universal archetype of the Self and of psychological wholeness. Mandala drawing and colouring are now also used in art therapy and as a popular meditative pastime.

Political and architectural usage

The term "mandala" has been adopted beyond religion: historians use it for a model of diffuse, centre-based political power in pre-modern Southeast Asia; mandala-like radial plans appear in architecture from stupas such as Borobudur to ideal-city schemes; and the word is used in international relations theory for circles of allied states.
