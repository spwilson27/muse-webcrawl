Sámi languages

The Sámi languages are a group of Uralic languages spoken by the Sámi people in Northern Europe, in parts of Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia's Kola Peninsula. They are commonly considered to form a dialect continuum or a group of closely related languages, conventionally divided into western and eastern branches, and most are endangered.

Sámi languages
Family: Uralic → Finnic/Sámi branch
Region: Sápmi (northern Norway, Sweden, Finland, Kola Peninsula)
Largest: Northern Sámi (~20,000 speakers)
Total speakers: roughly 30,000 across all varieties

Classification

The western group includes Southern Sámi, Ume Sámi, Pite Sámi, Lule Sámi, and Northern Sámi; the eastern group includes Inari Sámi, Skolt Sámi, Akkala Sámi (extinct), Kildin Sámi, and Ter Sámi. Neighboring varieties are often mutually intelligible, but distant ones are not. Northern Sámi, spoken mainly in Norway, accounts for the large majority of speakers and has the strongest institutional support.

Status and revitalization

Assimilation policies in Norway, Sweden, and Finland during the 19th and 20th centuries sharply reduced transmission of the languages. Since the late 20th century, Sámi parliaments, language acts, broadcasting, and schooling have supported revitalization; several languages nonetheless have only a few hundred speakers, and language nest preschools and standardization efforts aim to rebuild speaker communities. Nine Sámi languages have standardized written forms, using Latin-based alphabets (and Cyrillic for Kildin Sámi in Russia).

Features

Sámi languages share consonant gradation, rich case systems, and a three-way number distinction (singular, dual, plural) in pronouns and verbs. Northern Sámi has seven or eight cases and complex consonant alternations at morpheme boundaries.
