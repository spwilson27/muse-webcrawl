The Kwakwaka'wakw ("Kwak'wala-speaking peoples") are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast, centred on northern Vancouver Island and the adjacent mainland coast of what is now British Columbia, Canada. They speak Kwak'wala, a Northern Wakashan language, and were historically known in English-language sources as the "Kwakiutl", a name properly belonging to one of their constituent groups.

Society and culture

Kwakwaka'wakw society was organised into groups called 'na'mima, often translated as "clans" or extended family units, each holding hereditary crests, names, dances, songs and privileges that were displayed and validated at potlatches. Rank, inherited titles and ceremonial prerogatives were central to social life.

The Kwakwaka'wakw are renowned for their dramatic ceremonial art, including large carved house posts, masks (such as the Huxwhukw and transformation masks used in the winter ceremonial, the Tseyka), and the Hamatsa ceremony. The potlatch, banned by the Canadian government from 1885 to 1951, remained a cornerstone of cultural continuity.

History

European contact began in the late 18th century with the maritime fur trade. Fort Rupert was established by the Hudson's Bay Company in 1849. Colonisation, disease and assimilation policies drastically reduced the population in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The 1921 potlatch hosted by Dan Cranmer led to arrests and the confiscation of ceremonial regalia, much of which was later repatriated to the U'mista Cultural Centre in Alert Bay and the Nuyumbalees Cultural Centre at Cape Mudge.

Territory and communities

Major Kwakwaka'wakw communities include Alert Bay ('Namgis First Nation), Tsaxis (Kwagu'ł), and villages along the Broughton Archipelago and Knight and Kingcome inlets.
