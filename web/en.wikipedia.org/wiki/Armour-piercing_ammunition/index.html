Armour-piercing ammunition

Armour-piercing ammunition (AP) is a type of projectile designed to penetrate armour — such as the steel plating of armoured vehicles, body armour, or concrete — rather than to maximise damage to soft targets. It typically uses a hardened core made of steel, tungsten carbide, tungsten alloy, or depleted uranium.

Design

AP projectiles concentrate kinetic energy onto a small point. A hard, dense penetrator core is often enclosed in a softer jacket, and a pointed or capped nose helps prevent shattering and ricochet. Armour-piercing capped (APC) and armour-piercing capped ballistic cap (APCBC) shells were standard anti-tank ammunition during the Second World War.

Types

Modern types include armour-piercing discarding sabot (APDS) and its fin-stabilised successor APFSDS, in which a sub-calibre penetrator is fired at very high velocity and the sabot is discarded after leaving the barrel. Small-arms AP rounds use hardened steel or tungsten cores, such as the 5.56×45mm M995 and similar designs.

History

Armour-piercing shells were developed in the late 19th century for naval guns to defeat warship armour, and became central to tank warfare in the 20th century as armour thickness increased. The race between armour and penetrator continues in modern main battle tank design.
