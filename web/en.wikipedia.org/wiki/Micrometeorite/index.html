Micrometeorite

A micrometeorite is a micrometeoroid that has survived entry through the Earth's atmosphere and reached the surface. They differ from meteorites in being smaller, more abundant, and composed differently; most originate from collisions between asteroids and from comets shedding material into interplanetary space.

Ranging in size from about 50 micrometres to 2 millimetres, micrometeorites contribute the bulk of extraterrestrial material accreted by Earth each year — estimates range from roughly 30,000 to 40,000 tonnes annually, far exceeding the mass delivered by larger meteorites.

Entry into the atmosphere

Micrometeoroids enter the atmosphere at velocities between about 11 and 72 km/s. Because of their high surface-area-to-volume ratio, small particles decelerate high in the atmosphere and radiate away frictional heat efficiently, so many melt only partially or not at all. Particles that melt completely form tiny spherical droplets called cosmic spherules, which solidify during descent.

Collection

Micrometeorites are collected from deep-sea sediments, polar ice and snow (notably in Antarctica), and stratospheric sampling by high-altitude aircraft. Since 2016, researchers have shown that well-preserved micrometeorites can also be recovered from urban rooftops, where they can be identified by their magnetism, spherical shapes and surface textures.

Significance

Because micrometeorites deliver a continuous flux of extraterrestrial matter, they are studied for clues about the early Solar System, the delivery of water and organic compounds to the early Earth, and the composition of comets and asteroids that are otherwise difficult to sample. Unmelted micrometeorites closely resemble carbonaceous chondrites and may contain amino acids and other prebiotic molecules.
