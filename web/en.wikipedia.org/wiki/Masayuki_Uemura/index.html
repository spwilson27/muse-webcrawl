Masayuki Uemura

Masayuki Uemura (上村 雅之, Uemura Masayuki, 20 June 1943 – 6 December 2021) was a Japanese engineer and video game producer best known as the chief architect of Nintendo's Famicom (released outside Japan as the Nintendo Entertainment System) and Super Famicom (Super Nintendo Entertainment System) home video game consoles. His work at Nintendo helped define the hardware of the 8-bit and 16-bit eras of gaming.

Early life and education

Uemura was born in Tokyo in 1943. He graduated from the Chiba Institute of Technology, where he studied electronic engineering. After graduating, he worked at Sharp Corporation, where he was involved with solar cell technology and, notably, with the light-sensor technology used in Nintendo's light gun toys.

Career at Nintendo

Uemura joined Nintendo in 1972, recruited after his work with Sharp brought him into contact with Nintendo's toy division. He initially worked on Nintendo's beam gun (light gun) series, which used solar cell technology he had helped develop at Sharp.

In the early 1980s, Nintendo president Hiroshi Yamauchi tasked Uemura with designing a home video game console that would play interchangeable game cartridges. The result was the Family Computer, or Famicom, released in Japan in July 1983. Built around a MOS Technology 6502-derived processor, the Famicom was designed to be inexpensive to manufacture while offering performance competitive with arcade machines of the day.

Uemura went on to lead the design of the Super Famicom, released in Japan in 1990, which introduced enhanced graphics and audio capabilities, including the Mode 7 graphics mode and a Sony-designed sound chip. Together, the two consoles sold more than 110 million units worldwide.

Later life

Uemura retired from Nintendo in 2004. He later became a professor and director of the Center for Game Studies at Ritsumeikan University in Kyoto, where he researched the history and culture of video games. Uemura died on 6 December 2021 at the age of 78. His death was announced by Ritsumeikan University, and tributes from across the games industry recognised his foundational role in home console gaming.
