Tōgō Heihachirō (東郷 平八郎, 27 January 1848 – 30 May 1934) was a Japanese admiral of the fleet and one of Japan's greatest naval heroes. As Commander-in-Chief of the Combined Fleet during the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905, he led the Imperial Japanese Navy to a decisive victory over Russia's Baltic Fleet at the Battle of Tsushima, an engagement that transformed global naval strategy. Western journalists called him "the Nelson of the East".

Early life and career

Tōgō was born in Kagoshima, in the Satsuma Domain, into a samurai family. As a teenager he fought in the Anglo-Satsuma War of 1863 and on the shogunate's losing side's opposite — the imperial cause — during the Boshin War, serving aboard the paddle steamer Kasuga. After the Meiji Restoration he joined the new Imperial Japanese Navy and was sent to Britain in 1871, training at the Royal Naval College and circumnavigating the globe aboard HMS Worcester and other vessels. He returned to Japan in 1878.

During the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895) he commanded the cruiser Naniwa, sinking the Chinese transport Kowshing — an incident that caused a diplomatic controversy with Britain — and fighting at the Battle of the Yalu River.

Russo-Japanese War

Appointed commander of the Combined Fleet in 1903, Tōgō directed the surprise attack on Port Arthur in February 1904 and the blockade that followed. His crowning achievement came on 27–28 May 1905 at Tsushima, where his fleet annihilated the Russian Baltic Fleet after its 18,000-nautical-mile voyage: the Russians lost 21 ships sunk and some 4,400 men, against three Japanese torpedo boats and 117 dead. Before the battle, Tōgō hoisted the Z flag aboard his flagship Mikasa with the signal: "The fate of the Empire depends upon this battle. Let every man do his utmost" — consciously echoing Nelson at Trafalgar.

Later life

Tōgō became Chief of the Naval General Staff and was raised to the peerage as a marquis (kōshaku). From 1914 to 1924 he supervised the education of the young Prince Hirohito, the future Emperor Shōwa. He was given a state funeral upon his death in 1934, attended by naval representatives from around the world. His flagship Mikasa is preserved as a museum ship at Yokosuka, and Tōgō shrines in Tokyo and Tsushima venerate him as a Shinto kami.
