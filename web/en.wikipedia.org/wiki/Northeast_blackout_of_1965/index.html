Northeast blackout of 1965

The Northeast blackout of 1965 was a major disruption of the electric power supply on Tuesday, 9 November 1965, affecting parts of Ontario in Canada and the northeastern United States, including Connecticut, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, and Vermont. About 30 million people over an area of some 207,000 km² were left without electricity for up to 13 hours.

Manhattan during the blackout of 9 November 1965

Cause

The failure began at 5:16 p.m. EST at the Sir Adam Beck No. 2 hydroelectric generating station near Niagara Falls, Ontario, where a protective relay, set too low, tripped on a transmission line that was well within its capacity. Power surged onto parallel lines, overloading and tripping them in cascade; generation at Niagara was redirected, destabilising the interconnected grid of the Canada–United States Eastern Interconnection. Within about five minutes, cascading trips separated the system and left the northeast dark during the evening rush hour.

Effects

Some 800,000 people were trapped in the New York City Subway, commuter trains stopped, and traffic lights failed, though a nearly full moon provided some light. Most areas had power restored within hours, but parts of New York City remained blacked out for up to 13 hours. The event spurred major reforms in grid reliability, including the creation of the North American Electric Reliability Corporation's predecessor bodies and mandatory reliability standards. A persistent urban legend claimed a sharp rise in births nine months later; later studies found no significant baby boom. The blackout was echoed by later events such as the New York City blackout of 1977 and the Northeast blackout of 2003. See also Northeast blackout of 2003 and power outage.
