Vega is the brightest star in the northern constellation of Lyra, the fifth-brightest star in the night sky, and the second-brightest in the northern celestial hemisphere after Arcturus. Located only 25 light-years from the Sun, it is one of the most luminous stars in the Sun's neighbourhood. Vega was long used as the baseline for calibrating the photometric brightness scale, and was once defined to have an apparent magnitude of exactly zero in all bands.

Properties

Vega is a main-sequence star of spectral type A0, about twice the Sun's mass, fusing hydrogen in its core. It is a rapid rotator, spinning once in about 12.5 hours; centrifugal force has flattened it into an oblate spheroid, with an equatorial radius about 19% larger than its polar radius, and its poles are thousands of degrees hotter than its equator. Because we view Vega nearly pole-on, it appears brighter than a spherical star of the same type would. In 1983 the IRAS satellite found an excess of infrared radiation around Vega — the first evidence of a debris disk of dust around a main-sequence star, a possible sign of an accompanying planetary system, although no planet has been confirmed.

History and future pole star

The name Vega derives from the Arabic an-nasr al-wāqiʿ, "the falling eagle." Vega was the first star other than the Sun to be photographed (in 1850) and the first to have its spectrum recorded. Owing to the precession of Earth's axis, Vega was the pole star around 12,000 BCE and will be again around 13,727 CE. Together with Altair and Deneb, it forms the prominent Summer Triangle asterism. In Chinese tradition, Vega is the weaver girl Zhīnǚ of the Qixi festival legend.
