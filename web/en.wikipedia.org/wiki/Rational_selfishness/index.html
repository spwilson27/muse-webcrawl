Rational selfishness — or rational egoism in its broader form, and more specifically the doctrine of Objectivist ethics — is the ethical position, chiefly associated with philosopher and novelist Ayn Rand, that acting in one's own rationally considered long-term self-interest is moral, and that selfishness properly understood is a virtue.

Concept

Rand argued that the standard meaning of "selfishness" — thoughtless exploitation of others — conflates rational self-interest with predation. For her, a rational person's interests properly understood do not conflict with the interests of others: the rational egoist neither sacrifices himself to others nor others to himself, lives by production and trade rather than force or fraud, and treats his own life and happiness as his highest purpose.

Distinguishing features

Rational selfishness differs from hedonistic or "selfishness without a self" whim-worship, which Rand condemned equally with altruism. Its virtues include rationality, independence, integrity, honesty, justice, productiveness, and pride. In politics the doctrine supports individual rights and laissez-faire capitalism.

Criticism

Critics question whether a sharp distinction between rational self-interest and other-regarding obligation can be sustained, whether Rand's derivation of morality from the choice to live commits a naturalistic fallacy, and whether moral life can dispense with unchosen duties of benevolence; psychological egoists and altruists alike have contested her reading of human motivation.
