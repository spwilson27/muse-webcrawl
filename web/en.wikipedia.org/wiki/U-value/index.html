U-value

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The U-value, or thermal transmittance, measures the rate of transfer of heat through a structure (which can be a single material or a composite), divided by the difference in temperature across that structure. It is expressed in watts per square metre kelvin (W/m²K).

Interpretation

The better insulated a structure is, the lower its U-value. A well-insulated modern wall may have a U-value around 0.2 W/m²K, while a single-glazed window is typically around 4.8–5.8 W/m²K. Building regulations in many countries specify maximum U-values for walls, roofs, floors and windows in new construction.

Calculation

U is the reciprocal of the total thermal resistance R of the assembly: U = 1/Rtotal, where Rtotal is the sum of the resistances of each layer plus the internal and external surface resistances. Resistances of individual layers equal thickness divided by thermal conductivity (λ).

Measurement

U-values of existing buildings can be measured in situ with heat flux plates and temperature sensors, usually over at least one to two weeks, to check whether real performance matches design calculations.
