Henri-Louis Bergson (18 October 1859 – 4 January 1941) was a French philosopher who was influential in the traditions of analytic philosophy and continental philosophy, especially during the first half of the 20th century until the Second World War, but also after 1966 when Gilles Deleuze published Le Bergsonisme. Bergson is known for his influential arguments that processes of immediate experience and intuition are more significant than abstract rationalism and science for understanding reality. He is also known for challenging the philosophy of materialism and for his concept of duration (la durée) — time as lived, indivisible, and qualitative, as opposed to the spatialized, measurable time of physics.

He was awarded the 1927 Nobel Prize in Literature "in recognition of his rich and vitalizing ideas and the brilliant skill with which they have been presented". In 1930 France awarded him its highest honour, the Grand-Croix de la Légion d'honneur.

Life

Bergson was born in Paris to a Polish Jewish father, the composer Michał Bergson, and an English Jewish mother. He was educated at the Lycée Condorcet and the École Normale Supérieure, and taught philosophy in provincial lycées before returning to Paris; in 1900 he was appointed to a chair at the Collège de France, where his lectures became celebrated public events attended by crowds of students and fashionable society alike. His major works — Time and Free Will (1889), Matter and Memory (1896), Laughter (1900), and Creative Evolution (1907) — made him the most famous philosopher in the world at the height of his career. During the First World War he undertook diplomatic missions to Spain and the United States on behalf of the French government.

Philosophy

Duration

Bergson's doctoral thesis Time and Free Will (Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience) distinguished between time as measured by clocks — an artificial, spatialized succession of identical instants — and duration, the continuous, indivisible flow of conscious experience in which past and present interpenetrate. For Bergson, free will is recovered once we stop projecting duration into space.

Memory and the élan vital

In Matter and Memory he argued that the brain does not store representations so much as select and enact them, and that memory is not reducible to brain states. In Creative Evolution (1907) he developed the notion of the élan vital, a creative impetus driving evolution toward ever greater complexity and unpredictability, and contrasted the intellect, which freezes reality into static concepts, with intuition, which grasps duration directly. The book made him internationally famous and influenced writers, artists, and theologians; it also brought him into tension with the Catholic Church, several of his works being placed on the Index of Forbidden Books.

In 1922 Bergson debated Albert Einstein in Paris on the nature of time, an encounter that became emblematic of the divide between philosophy and physics; his Duration and Simultaneity (1922) argued that Einstein's relativity did not exhaust the meaning of time. His last major work, The Two Sources of Morality and Religion (1932), distinguished closed morality and static religion from the open morality and dynamic religion of the mystics. Bergson died of bronchitis in occupied Paris in January 1941, having refused special exemptions offered to him as a celebrated academic under the Vichy anti-Jewish laws and having registered as a Jew in solidarity with the persecuted.
