A Stirling engine is a heat engine that converts thermal energy into mechanical work by cyclic compression and expansion of a fixed mass of gas — the working fluid — at different temperatures, so that there is a net conversion of heat energy to mechanical work. It is an external combustion engine: the heat is supplied from outside the sealed cylinder, so almost any heat source can power it. The engine was invented by the Scottish clergyman Robert Stirling in 1816.

The Stirling cycle

The idealized Stirling cycle consists of four processes: isothermal expansion, in which the hot gas expands and does work; constant-volume cooling, in which the gas passes through a regenerator and deposits heat; isothermal compression at the cold temperature; and constant-volume heating, in which the gas reabsorbs the stored heat from the regenerator. The regenerator — a porous matrix that temporarily stores heat — is the key to the engine's high potential efficiency, which in theory can approach the Carnot limit for the same temperature bounds.

Configurations

Three main configurations exist. In an alpha engine, two pistons in separate cylinders — one hot, one cold — shuttle the gas between them. In a beta engine, a piston and a loosely fitting displacer share one cylinder. In a gamma engine, the displacer and piston are in separate cylinders connected by a passage. Free-piston variants, in which the displacer and piston are not mechanically linked, are used in some cryocoolers and space power systems.

History

Stirling's 1816 patent was motivated partly by safety, since steam boiler explosions were common. Nineteenth-century Stirling "hot air engines" saw modest use for pumping water and driving small machinery. Philips revived the engine in the 1930s–1950s for quiet generator sets, and NASA and automotive firms studied it extensively in the 1970s and 1980s. Dish-Stirling solar systems have achieved among the highest solar-to-electric conversion efficiencies recorded.

Applications

Stirling engines are used where quiet operation, fuel flexibility, or sealed reliability matters: air-independent propulsion in Swedish and other submarines, solar dish generators, combined heat and power units, and cryocoolers (run in reverse) for infrared sensors and superconducting equipment. Radioisotope Stirling generators have been developed for deep-space missions. Drawbacks include slow starting, large size for a given power, and sealing and materials challenges at high temperature.
