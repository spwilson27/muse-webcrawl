Production–possibility frontier

A production–possibility frontier (PPF), also called a production possibility curve or boundary, is a model in microeconomics showing the maximum combinations of two goods or services that an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently employed, given the available factors of production and technology.

Shape and interpretation

Points on the curve represent efficient production; points inside it represent inefficiency (resources unemployed or misallocated), and points outside are unattainable without growth in resources or technology. The curve is typically drawn bowed outward (concave to the origin) because resources are not equally suited to producing both goods, so the opportunity cost of one good in terms of the other rises as more of it is produced. This slope, the marginal rate of transformation, reflects relative scarcity.

Shifts

Economic growth, technological progress or an increase in the labour force shifts the frontier outward, making previously unattainable combinations possible. War, natural disaster or capital destruction can shift it inward.

Uses

The PPF illustrates core economic concepts — scarcity, choice, efficiency and opportunity cost — and is used to introduce the idea of comparative advantage in international trade theory.
