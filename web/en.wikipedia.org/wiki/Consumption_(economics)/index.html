Consumption (economics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Consumption is the use of goods and services by households. In macroeconomics, consumption is a component of aggregate demand and the largest single part of gross domestic product in most economies; in national accounts its household component is called personal consumption expenditure (in the United States) or household final consumption expenditure. It is distinguished from consumption expenditure, which also includes government purchases of goods and services consumed in the current period.

The consumption function

John Maynard Keynes proposed the consumption function, in which current consumption depends mainly on current disposable income, with a marginal propensity to consume between zero and one. Later theories refined this: Franco Modigliani's life-cycle hypothesis and Milton Friedman's permanent income hypothesis hold that households smooth consumption over time based on expected lifetime resources, so transitory income changes have small effects. Robert Hall's random-walk model extended consumption theory under rational expectations. Behavioural economics adds factors such as mental accounting, self-control problems, and habits. Aggregate consumption depends on income, wealth, interest rates, consumer confidence, credit conditions, and expectations.

Measurement and significance

In the United States, personal consumption expenditures are reported by the Bureau of Economic Analysis and account for roughly 68–70 percent of GDP, split among durable goods, nondurable goods, and services (the largest and growing category). Because consumption is comparatively stable, swings in it are watched as indicators of business-cycle turning points; Keynesian policy analysis often focuses on stimulating consumption to close output gaps.
