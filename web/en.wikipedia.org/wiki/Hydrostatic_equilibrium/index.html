Hydrostatic equilibrium

In fluid mechanics, hydrostatic equilibrium (or hydrostatic balance) is the condition of a fluid or plastic solid at rest, in which the net force on each element is zero because the upward pressure gradient force exactly balances the downward pull of gravity. It is a fundamental concept in astrophysics, geophysics, and atmospheric science, governing the structure of stars, planets, and atmospheres.

The hydrostatic equation

For a body in spherical symmetry, hydrostatic equilibrium is expressed by the equation

dP/dr = − G m(r) ρ(r) / r²

where P is pressure, r is radius, G is the gravitational constant, m(r) is the mass enclosed within radius r, and ρ is density. In a planetary atmosphere the equivalent form dP/dz = −ρg states that pressure decreases with altitude at a rate proportional to the local density and gravitational acceleration.

In astrophysics

Stars maintain hydrostatic equilibrium for most of their lives: the inward gravitational attraction of their mass is balanced by the outward pressure gradient produced by thermal pressure and, in dense remnants, degeneracy pressure. Failure of this balance drives pulsations, collapse (as in core-collapse supernovae), or expansion. The condition also appears in the definition of a dwarf planet: the International Astronomical Union requires a body to have sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape. Galaxy clusters, too, are approximately in hydrostatic equilibrium, allowing their total mass to be estimated from the temperature and density profile of the hot intracluster gas observed in X-rays — a method known to be biased by the "hydrostatic mass bias."

In geology and meteorology

The related concept of isostasy describes the hydrostatic balance of Earth's lithosphere floating on the denser asthenosphere. In meteorology, the hydrostatic assumption underpins primitive-equation weather models and the hypsometric equation relating the thickness of an atmospheric layer to its mean temperature.
