William Bradford (c. 1590 – 9 May 1657) was an English Puritan Separatist who sailed aboard the Mayflower in 1620 and became the long-serving governor of Plymouth Colony in what is now Massachusetts. His journal, Of Plymouth Plantation, written between 1630 and 1651, is the principal contemporary history of the colony's first decades and a classic of early American literature.

Early life

Bradford was born in Austerfield, Yorkshire, and orphaned young. As a teenager he joined the Separatist congregation at Scrooby, whose members rejected the Church of England. When the congregation fled persecution, Bradford followed them to Amsterdam and then to Leiden in the Netherlands, where he worked in the textile trade and came of age.

Plymouth Colony

In 1620 Bradford sailed with the Leiden congregation aboard the Mayflower. He was a signatory of the Mayflower Compact. After the death of the colony's first governor, John Carver, in the spring of 1621, Bradford was elected governor — a post to which he was re-elected nearly every year for the rest of his life, serving some thirty terms. He guided the colony through its fragile early years, maintained diplomatic relations with Massasoit and the Wampanoag, and recorded the first harvest thanksgiving feast shared by colonists and Native Americans.

Of Plymouth Plantation

Bradford's history narrates the Separatists' persecution in England, their exile in Leiden, the Atlantic crossing, and the colony's growth. The manuscript disappeared during the American Revolutionary War and was rediscovered in London in the 1850s; it was returned to Massachusetts in 1897. Bradford is also credited with the first use in print of the term "Pilgrims" for the colonists, drawn from the Epistle to the Hebrews.
