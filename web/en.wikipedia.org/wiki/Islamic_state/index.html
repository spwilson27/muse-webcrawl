Islamic state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An Islamic state is a state whose legal and political order is based on Islamic law — though the term's application varies enormously, from states whose constitutions declare Islam the state religion and Sharia a source of legislation (many in the Middle East and North Africa), through the Islamic Republic of Iran, where clerical guardianship (velayat-e faqih) is constitutionalised, and Saudi Arabia, whose Basic Law declares the Quran and Sunna its constitution, to the self-declared “Islamic State” of the armed group ISIS, whose claim to statehood and Islamic legitimacy was rejected by the overwhelming majority of Muslim scholars and states.

As a modern political concept, the Islamic state was theorised by twentieth-century Islamist thinkers responding to the abolition of the Ottoman caliphate (1924): Abul A'la Maududi's doctrine of divine sovereignty (hakimiyya) and an ideological state; Sayyid Qutb's radicalisation of it; and the Muslim Brotherhood's gradualist variants. In practice, states calling themselves Islamic differ in elections, pluralism, courts and rights, and scholars distinguish constitutional types (theocratic, hybrid, and nominal) while debating whether any single model follows from Islamic sources. The term should not be confused with Muslim-majority state (a demographic description) or with the historical caliphates as such.
