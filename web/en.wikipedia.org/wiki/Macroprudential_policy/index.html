Macroprudential policy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Macroprudential policy is a framework of financial regulation aimed at mitigating systemic risk in the financial system as a whole, as opposed to "microprudential" supervision of individual institutions. It gained prominence after the 2007–2008 financial crisis demonstrated that the soundness of individual banks did not guarantee the stability of the system, because of interconnectedness, common exposures, and procyclical lending.

Tools

Macroprudential instruments include the countercyclical capital buffer (extra bank capital built up in booms and released in busts), the leverage ratio, liquidity requirements such as the Liquidity Coverage Ratio, systemically important bank surcharges (G-SIB buffers), sectoral tools such as loan-to-value and debt-to-income limits on mortgages, stress testing, and large-exposure limits. In many jurisdictions responsibility sits with central banks or dedicated committees — e.g. the Financial Policy Committee at the Bank of England, the Financial Stability Oversight Council in the United States, and the European Systemic Risk Board in the EU — under the global coordination of the Financial Stability Board and the Basel III framework.
