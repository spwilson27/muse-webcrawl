Optical frequency comb

An optical frequency comb is a spectrum of laser light consisting of a series of discrete, evenly spaced frequency lines, resembling the teeth of a comb. Frequency combs are generated by mode-locked lasers producing a regular train of ultrashort pulses; in the frequency domain each pulse train corresponds to thousands of phase-coherent spectral lines separated by the pulse repetition rate.

Applications

Because the absolute frequency of every comb tooth can be derived from two radio-frequency measurements, frequency combs act as a bridge between optical frequencies and countable microwave frequencies. They underpin optical atomic clocks, precision spectroscopy, calibration of astronomical spectrographs for exoplanet searches, and tests of fundamental physics.

History

The technique was developed in the late 1990s, notably by Theodor W. Hänsch and John L. Hall, who shared part of the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physics for their contributions to laser-based precision spectroscopy, including the optical frequency comb technique.
