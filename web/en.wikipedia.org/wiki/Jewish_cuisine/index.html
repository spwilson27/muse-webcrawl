Jewish cuisine

A selection of traditional Jewish foods.

Jewish cuisine refers to the cooking traditions of the Jewish people in the diaspora. It has evolved over many centuries, shaped by Jewish dietary laws (kashrut), Jewish festivals and Shabbat traditions, and the local economies and cuisines of the countries in which Jewish communities lived. Distinct styles developed, notably Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and Mizrahi cooking, as well as the cuisine of Israeli Jews.

Dietary laws

The laws of kashrut govern which foods may be eaten and how they are prepared. Meat and dairy products may not be cooked or eaten together; only animals with cloven hooves that chew the cud, and fish with fins and scales, are permitted; pork and shellfish are forbidden. Bread and wine have particular religious significance, and special foods are associated with holidays such as Passover, when leavened bread is avoided and matzah is eaten instead.

Ashkenazi cuisine

Ashkenazi food, from the Jewish communities of Central and Eastern Europe, includes dishes such as gefilte fish, chicken soup with matzah balls, chopped liver, kugel, latkes, knishes, cholent (a slow-cooked Sabbath stew), and baked goods including bagels, challah, rugelach, and hamantaschen. Pickled and smoked fish, such as herring and lox, reflect the ingredients available in the communities' often poor circumstances.

Sephardi and Mizrahi cuisine

Sephardi cuisine, from Iberian and Mediterranean communities, and Mizrahi cuisine, from Middle Eastern and North African communities, make greater use of olive oil, rice, legumes, spices, salads, and stuffed vegetables — dishes such as bourekas, shakshuka, hummus, falafel, and sabich. Many of these foods were adopted into modern Israeli cuisine.
