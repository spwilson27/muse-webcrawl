James Harrington (3 January 1611 – 11 September 1677) was an English political theorist of classical republicanism. His masterpiece, The Commonwealth of Oceana (1656), offered an exposition of an ideal republican constitution, developing the principles of the separation of powers, bicameral deliberation, the secret ballot, rotation in office, and an "agrarian law" limiting the accumulation of land, all grounded in his central doctrine that political power follows the distribution of property.

Life

Harrington was born into a gentry family at Upton, Northamptonshire, studied at Trinity College, Oxford, and travelled in Europe, developing an admiration for the republics of Venice and the Netherlands. He returned to England and was for a time a gentleman attendant on Charles I during the king's captivity in 1647–49, to whom he was personally devoted despite his republican convictions. During the Commonwealth he wrote Oceana, dedicated to Oliver Cromwell, and promoted his ideas through the Rota Club, a debating society that met at Miles's coffee house in London in 1659–60. After the Restoration he was arrested in 1661 on suspicion of conspiracy, imprisoned for a period without trial, and his health — mental and physical — deteriorated until his death in 1677.

Oceana and Harrington's thought

Harrington's fundamental law was that "empire follows the balance of property": where one man owns most of the land, government is absolute monarchy; where a few own it, mixed monarchy or oligarchy; where the people own it, a commonwealth is possible. England's civil wars, he argued, resulted from the Tudor-era shift of land from crown and church to the gentry and people, which made the old monarchy unsustainable. Oceana proposed an equal agrarian (capping landed estates), a senate that debates and proposes while a popular assembly resolves — separating wisdom from decision — magistrates chosen by secret ballot (the "Venetian ballot"), and rotation of offices to prevent entrenchment.

Influence

Harrington influenced English "neo-Harringtonian" critics of court corruption, the American Founding Fathers (John Adams quoted him approvingly), and French thinkers. Modern scholarship, especially J. G. A. Pocock's edition and interpretation, restored him to the centre of the civic republican tradition and of the idea of mixed government.
