Güira

The güira ([ˈɡwiɾa]) is a percussion instrument from the Dominican Republic, used as an essential component of merengue, bachata and other Caribbean music. It is a metal sheet — typically stainless steel — formed into a cylinder or open-backed shape, covered with raised bumps or ridges, and played by scraping with a stiff metal brush or fork-like scraper (puya/gancho).

Description and playing

The güira is traditionally handmade by lathe-turning and hammering a sheet of metal into a textured, usually cylindrical body with a handle. The player holds it in one hand and drags the scraper's metal tines across its ridged surface with the other, producing a continuous, bright, hissing rhythm that interlocks with the tambora drum to form the rhythmic foundation of a merengue típico ensemble. Both long sweeping strokes and short accented chatters are used.

History and relation to the güiro

The güira developed in the Dominican Republic in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, in part — it is often said — because a period ban on drums encouraged musicians to turn to metal instruments. It is closely related to the güiro, a scraped gourd (or modern fiberglass) instrument found across Latin America and in fellow Caribbean styles; the güira's metal construction gives it a louder, sharper and more cutting sound suited to dance bands. Players such as Rafaelito Román helped establish the instrument's virtuoso role in merengue típico.
