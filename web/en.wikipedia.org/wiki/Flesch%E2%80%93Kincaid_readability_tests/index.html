Flesch–Kincaid readability tests

The Flesch–Kincaid readability tests are readability tests designed to indicate how difficult a passage in English is to understand. There are two tests: the Flesch Reading Ease and the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level. Both use the same core measures — total words, total sentences and total syllables — but weight them differently.

Flesch Reading Ease

Developed by Rudolf Flesch in the 1940s, the test gives a score between 0 and 100 (higher is easier to read). Scores of 90–100 are understood by an average 11-year-old; 60–70 by 13- to 15-year-olds; and 0–30 are best understood by university graduates. Reader's Digest scores around 65, while academic journals often score below 30.

Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level

In 1975, under contract to the US Navy, J. Peter Kincaid and colleagues re-derived Flesch's formula to output a US school grade level instead. A score of 8.0 means an eighth grader can understand the text. The test was applied to Navy technical manuals and became widespread in education, publishing and government; several US states require insurance policies to meet a maximum Flesch–Kincaid grade level.

Limitations

The formulas only measure surface features — sentence length and word length — and can be gamed or mislead: short sentences of jargon score "easy" while remaining hard to comprehend. They remain, however, the most widely embedded readability metrics in word processors and publishing workflows.
