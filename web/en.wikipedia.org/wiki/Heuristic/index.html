Heuristic
From Greekheurískō — "I find, discover"
FieldsPsychology, computer science, mathematics, design
NaturePractical rule of thumb; usually effective, not guaranteed optimal

A heuristic is any approach to problem solving that employs a practical method or rule of thumb that is not guaranteed to be optimal or correct, but is sufficient for reaching an immediate, approximate goal. Where an exhaustive search is impractical, heuristics trade accuracy, completeness or precision for speed. The word, from the Greek heurískō ("I find"), shares its root with Archimedes' cry "Eureka".

In psychology

In cognitive psychology, heuristics are mental shortcuts that ease the cognitive load of decision-making. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's heuristics-and-biases programme identified the representativeness, availability and anchoring heuristics, showing how they produce systematic biases in judgement. Later researchers such as Gerd Gigerenzer argued that "fast and frugal" heuristics can be ecologically rational — sometimes more accurate than complex models in real-world environments.

In computer science and mathematics

In algorithm design, a heuristic function estimates the cost of reaching a goal, guiding searches such as A* pathfinding; an admissible heuristic never overestimates, guaranteeing optimality of the search. Metaheuristic frameworks — simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, tabu search, ant colony optimisation — provide general strategies for hard optimisation and NP-hard problems where exact solutions are infeasible. George Pólya's How to Solve It (1945) codified mathematical heuristics: understand the problem, devise a plan, work backwards, consider a simpler case.

Other uses

In user-interface design, Nielsen's ten usability heuristics are standard rules for evaluating interfaces. In education, "heuristic" teaching encourages discovery learning; in law and rhetoric the term can describe arguments that persuade by plausibility rather than proof.
