Frocking

In the United States military, frocking is the practice of allowing a commissioned or non-commissioned officer who has been selected for promotion to wear the insignia of the higher grade before their official date of promotion — that is, before the promotion takes legal effect and the increased pay begins. The term is peculiar to the United States armed forces.

Practice and authority

A frocked officer assumes the title, uniform and responsibilities of the higher rank but continues to receive the pay and seniority of their substantive grade until the actual promotion date. Frocking is granted by written authority; for general and flag officers it typically requires approval at service-secretary level and is used so that an officer selected for a senior billet can take up the position with the visible authority of the rank it requires. The number of officers who may be frocked to senior grades is limited by law and service policy.

History

The term derives from the custom of a chaplain or senior officer placing the new rank insignia — historically associated with the frock coat uniform — on the promotee. The practice became widespread in the US Navy and Marine Corps, and analogous arrangements exist under names such as "acting rank" or "local rank" in other countries' forces, though the word "frocking" itself is American.
