Caesar (title)

[Image placeholder: A gold aureus bearing the portrait and name of a Roman Caesar]

Caesar (Latin: [ˈkae̯.sar]) is a title of imperial character. It derives from the cognomen of the Roman dictator Julius Caesar. The change from being a familial name to a title adopted by the Roman emperors can be traced to AD 68, following the fall of the Julio-Claudian dynasty.

From name to title

For the first two centuries of the Empire, "Caesar" remained part of the emperor's own nomenclature: Augustus had been adopted by Julius Caesar, and successive emperors adopted the name into their formal titulature. From the reign of Hadrian onward it became conventional for the emperor's designated heir to receive the title Caesar while the reigning emperor styled himself Augustus.

In Diocletian's Tetrarchy (from AD 293), the system was formalised: two senior emperors, the Augusti, each appointed a junior colleague and heir, a Caesar. The title survived in the Byzantine Empire — Greek Καισαρ — as a high court dignity, usually conferred on close imperial relatives and later on foreign princes.

Legacy in later languages

The title gave rise to the words for "emperor" in many languages: German Kaiser, Russian Tsar (via Old Church Slavonic cěsarĭ), and cognates across Europe including Dutch keizer, Danish kejser, Turkish kayser and Arabic qayṣar. In this way the family name of one Roman became the generic word for supreme ruler for two thousand years.
