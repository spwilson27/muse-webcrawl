Siege of Leningrad
Date: 8 September 1941 – 27 January 1944
Location: Leningrad, USSR
Belligerents: Germany, Finland vs Soviet Union
Result: Soviet victory; siege lifted

The Siege of Leningrad was a prolonged military blockade of the city of Leningrad (now Saint Petersburg) by Nazi Germany and Finland during the Second World War. Lasting from 8 September 1941 to 27 January 1944 — 872 days — it was one of the longest and most destructive sieges in history, causing the deaths of an estimated 800,000 to over one million civilians, most from starvation.

The blockade

German Army Group North cut the last land connection to the city in September 1941. Hitler ordered that Leningrad be starved into submission and razed rather than captured. Finnish forces held positions to the north. The only supply route was across Lake Ladoga — by boat in summer and over the ice in winter, the famed "Road of Life", which brought in food and evacuated hundreds of thousands of civilians.

Life in the city

During the terrible winter of 1941–42, bread rations fell as low as 125 grams per day for dependents, and hundreds of thousands died of hunger and cold. Despite this, factories continued producing weapons, and cultural life persisted: Dmitri Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony, dedicated to the city, was performed in the besieged Philharmonic Hall in August 1942.

Lifting the siege

Operation Iskra in January 1943 opened a narrow land corridor, and a full Soviet offensive in January 1944 finally drove the Germans from the city's outskirts, ending the siege on 27 January 1944.

Legacy

Leningrad was named a Hero City of the Soviet Union. Memorials including the Piskarevskoye Memorial Cemetery, where hundreds of thousands of victims lie in mass graves, commemorate the siege; historians and courts have since characterised the starvation policy as an act of genocide.
