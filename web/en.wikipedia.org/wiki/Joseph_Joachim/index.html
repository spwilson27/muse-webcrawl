Joseph Joachim

Joseph Joachim (28 June 1831 – 15 August 1907) was a Hungarian violinist, conductor, composer, and teacher, regarded as one of the greatest violinists of the nineteenth century. A child prodigy who performed with Mendelssohn as a boy, he became the close friend and trusted musical adviser of Johannes Brahms, and the standard-bearer of a classical, anti-virtuosic ideal of violin playing.

Life

Joachim was born in Köpcsény (Kittsee), near Pressburg, in the Kingdom of Hungary. He studied in Vienna and at the Leipzig Conservatory, where at twelve he played Mendelssohn's Violin Concerto under the composer, and made a sensational London debut in 1844 playing Beethoven's Violin Concerto, again under Mendelssohn. After a period as concertmaster at Weimar under Liszt — whose aesthetic direction he came to resist — he moved to Hanover as royal concertmaster, and in 1868 to Berlin, where he founded and directed the Hochschule für Musik and led the Joachim Quartet, one of the most celebrated string quartets in Europe. He died in Berlin in 1907; in 1903 he had made a small number of gramophone recordings that remain precious documents of nineteenth-century playing.

Relationship with Brahms and musical legacy

Joachim introduced the young Brahms to Robert and Clara Schumann in 1853, and the two men remained lifelong collaborators, though the friendship cooled for a time over Brahms's part in Joachim's divorce case. Brahms dedicated his Violin Concerto (1878) to Joachim, who advised extensively on the solo part and wrote its familiar cadenza; Joachim likewise championed the concertos of Beethoven, Mendelssohn, and Bruch, edited the Bach solo works, and taught a generation of violinists, including Leopold Auer, in a spirit summed up by his motto, "Frei aber einsam" — "free but lonely" — which Brahms echoed in his F-A-E Sonata, a joint composition with Joachim and Schumann.
