Expressive therapy

Creative media such as paint, clay, music and movement are used in expressive therapy

Expressive therapy, also known as expressive arts therapy or creative arts therapy, is the use of the creative arts as a form of psychotherapy. It draws on visual art, music, dance and movement, drama, poetry and other art forms, on the principle that creative expression can foster self-awareness, emotional regulation, communication and healing — particularly where verbal expression alone is limited or difficult.

Modalities

Expressive therapy encompasses several established disciplines, each with its own training and professional bodies: art therapy, music therapy, dance/movement therapy, drama therapy and psychodrama, poetry therapy and play therapy. Intermodal expressive arts therapy, developed by figures such as Paolo Knill and Shaun McNiff, deliberately moves between art forms within a session, holding that different media illuminate different aspects of a client's experience.

Practice and settings

Practitioners work in hospitals, schools, mental-health clinics, hospices and community settings, with populations including children, trauma survivors, people with dementia and those in palliative care. The emphasis is on the process of making rather than the aesthetic quality of the product; the therapist may invite reflection on the artwork or performance as a route into feelings that are hard to articulate directly. Expressive therapies are frequently used as adjuncts to verbal psychotherapy and other clinical care.

History

The constituent therapies emerged in the mid-twentieth century in Britain and the United States, when artists working in psychiatric hospitals began to document the therapeutic value of patients' creative work. Professional associations and accredited training programmes followed from the 1960s onwards, and the umbrella field of expressive arts therapy consolidated in the 1970s with dedicated graduate programmes.
