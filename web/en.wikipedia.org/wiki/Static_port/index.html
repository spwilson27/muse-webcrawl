Static port

A static port is a point on the surface of an aircraft, spacecraft or other vehicle that samples the ambient — "static" — air pressure of the atmosphere undisturbed by the vehicle's motion. It is the reference input of the pitot-static system, which pairs static pressure with the dynamic pressure measured by a pitot tube to drive the aircraft's altimeter, airspeed indicator and vertical speed indicator.

Design and placement

Static ports are usually small holes or a perforated plate mounted flush with the skin, sited where the airflow is smooth and the local pressure closely matches free-stream static pressure across the flight envelope. Airliners typically have several ports on each side of the forward fuselage connected to separate air data computers, with cross-checking to detect a blocked or erroneous source. Because even slight protrusion or ice distorts the reading, ports are heated, carefully finished, and placarded against painting over.

Errors and accidents

Position error — the difference between measured and true static pressure — varies with speed, angle of attack and configuration and is calibrated out or corrected by the air data computer. Blockage is more dangerous: tape left over a port after washing contributed to the crash of Aeroperú Flight 603 in 1996, whose crew received contradictory altitude and speed warnings at night over the Pacific; a wasp nest blocked a pitot tube on Birgenair Flight 301 the same year. Such accidents underline why pre-flight inspection of ports and tubes is a checklist item, and why modern aircraft monitor multiple independent sources.

Alternative static sources

Many aircraft provide an alternate static source inside the cabin or cockpit for use if the external ports block; cabin pressure lags and differs slightly from outside pressure, so instruments fed from it read with known, tabulated errors (typically a slightly high altitude and airspeed) listed in the flight manual.
