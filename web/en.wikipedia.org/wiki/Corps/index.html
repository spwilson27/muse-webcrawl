Corps

A corps (pronounced “core”, plural corps pronounced “cores”) is a large military formation, typically comprising two or more divisions and numbering between roughly 20,000 and 50,000 soldiers, commanded by a lieutenant general. The word comes from the French corps d'armée, literally “body of the army”. In NATO symbology a corps is denoted by three X's.

History

The corps system originated in Revolutionary and Napoleonic France. In 1800, French armies began grouping divisions into semi-permanent formations, and Napoleon organized the Grande Armée into corps d'armée from 1804–1805, each a combined-arms miniature army with infantry, cavalry, artillery, and support troops under a marshal or senior general. The system allowed corps to march separately but concentrate for battle, and it was adopted by other European armies during the 19th century.

Modern usage

In the United States Army, corps such as I Corps, III Corps, V Corps, and XVIII Airborne Corps serve as the highest tactical headquarters, coordinating several divisions. Many armies also use “corps” to denote a branch or service community — for example a signal corps, medical corps, or corps of engineers — rather than a formation. The term is also used outside the military for organized bodies such as a diplomatic corps or a corps de ballet.
