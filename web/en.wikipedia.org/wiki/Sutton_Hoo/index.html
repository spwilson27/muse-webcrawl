Sutton Hoo

Sutton Hoo
Location: Suffolk, England
Period: early 7th century AD
Excavated: 1938–39, 1960s, 1980s
Finds held: British Museum

Sutton Hoo is the site of two early medieval cemeteries near Woodbridge, Suffolk, England, dating to the 6th–7th centuries. Its ship burial, excavated in 1939, is one of the greatest archaeological finds in England.

The ship burial

Under Mound 1 lay a 27-metre ship whose timber had rotted, leaving rivets and a ghost outline in the sand. The burial chamber held a helmet (reconstructed from fragments, with a face-mask design), a great gold buckle, garnet shoulder clasps, a sword, shield, lyre, the Byzantine silver Anastasius dish, and Merovingian gold coins. No body survived the acidic soil.

Identity

The burial is most often attributed to Rædwald, king of East Anglia (died c. 624), though Cenwalh and others have been proposed. The finds illuminate Anglo-Saxon kingship and links to Sweden and the Mediterranean, and are often read alongside Beowulf.

Excavation was prompted by landowner Edith Pretty; archaeologist Basil Brown made the first discoveries. The treasure was ruled property of Pretty, who donated it to the British Museum. The story is dramatised in the film The Dig (2021).
