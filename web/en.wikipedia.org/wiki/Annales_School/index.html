Annales school

The Annales school (French: École des Annales) is a group of historians associated with a style of historiography developed by French historians in the 20th century to stress long-term social history. It is named after its scholarly journal Annales d'histoire économique et sociale, founded in 1929 by Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre.

Approach

The Annales historians rejected the traditional emphasis on politics, diplomacy and great individuals, instead pursuing a "total history" drawing on geography, economics, demography, sociology and anthropology. Fernand Braudel distinguished three layers of historical time: the almost-motionless longue durée of structures and environment, medium-term conjunctures, and the surface foam of events (événements).

Generations

The first generation of Bloch and Febvre was followed after 1945 by Braudel's institutional consolidation around the VIe Section of the École pratique des hautes études (later EHESS) and masterpieces such as The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949). A third generation, including Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie and Jacques Le Goff, turned toward the history of mentalités and historical anthropology; a later "critical turn" re-engaged with politics and narrative. The school profoundly influenced social history worldwide, including the British journal Past & Present and world-systems analysis.
