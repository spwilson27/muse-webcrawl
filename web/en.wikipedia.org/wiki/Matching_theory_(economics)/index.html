Matching theory (economics)

Matching theory, in economics, is a mathematical framework describing the formation of mutually beneficial relationships over time, used to study how agents on two sides of a market are paired together — workers and firms, students and schools, kidney donors and patients, or men and women in marriage markets. It is part of market design.

Two-sided matching and stability

The field began with the 1962 paper "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage" by David Gale and Lloyd Shapley, which introduced the concept of a stable matching: a pairing in which no two unmatched agents would both prefer to be matched with each other over their assigned partners. Gale and Shapley proved that a stable matching always exists and can be found by the deferred acceptance algorithm, in which one side proposes and the other provisionally accepts or rejects.

Building on this foundation, Alvin Roth and others applied the theory to real markets, including the assignment of medical residents to hospitals (the National Resident Matching Program), school choice systems in New York and Boston, and kidney exchange programmes in which incompatible donor–patient pairs swap organs. Roth and Shapley shared the 2012 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for "the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design".

Search and matching in labour economics

A second strand of matching theory models the frictions of search: unemployed workers and vacant jobs are matched through a matching function, in which meetings between the two sides occur at a rate depending on their numbers. The Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides (DMP) model of search and matching became the standard framework for analysing unemployment, job creation, and wage bargaining, and its developers received the 2010 Nobel Memorial Prize.
