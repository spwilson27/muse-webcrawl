Mortgage loan

A mortgage loan or simply mortgage (/ˈmɔːrɡɪdʒ/) is a loan used either by purchasers of real property to raise funds to buy real estate, or by existing property owners to raise funds for any purpose while putting a lien on the property being mortgaged. The loan is "secured" by the borrower's property through a process known as mortgage origination. This means that a legal mechanism is put into place which allows the lender to take possession and sell the secured property (foreclosure or repossession) to pay off the loan in the event the borrower defaults on the loan or otherwise fails to abide by its terms.

Basics

Borrowers typically repay the loan with interest in regular instalments over a term of 15 to 30 years. The main components of the cost are principal and interest, often bundled with property taxes and homeowner's insurance in the monthly payment. The interest rate may be fixed for the whole term (fixed-rate mortgage) or may vary with a market index (adjustable-rate mortgage, ARM). The ratio of the loan to the property's appraised value — the loan-to-value ratio — influences the rate offered and whether mortgage insurance is required.

Types

  • Fixed-rate mortgage — interest rate and payment are constant over the term.
  • Adjustable-rate mortgage — rate resets periodically after an initial fixed period.
  • Interest-only mortgage — only interest is paid for a period, leaving the principal unchanged.
  • Government-insured loans — in the US, FHA loans and VA loans carry federal backing, permitting lower down payments.
  • Reverse mortgage — allows older homeowners to draw on home equity without monthly payments.

Application and underwriting

Lenders evaluate income, employment, assets, debts, and credit history, and order an appraisal of the property. In the United States, many loans are sold to government-sponsored enterprises Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac or securitised. Origination practices that loosened standards in the mid-2000s — including subprime lending to borrowers with poor credit — contributed to the subprime mortgage crisis and the global financial crisis of 2007–2008, prompting reforms such as ability-to-repay rules.
