Bradford Hill criteria

The Bradford Hill criteria, also known as Hill's criteria for causation, are a group of nine principles proposed by the English epidemiologist Sir Austin Bradford Hill in a 1965 presidential address to the Royal Society of Medicine. They provide a framework for assessing whether an observed association between an exposure and a disease is likely to be causal.

The nine criteria

Strength — a strong association is more likely causal than a weak one. Consistency — the association is observed repeatedly, by different investigators in different populations. Specificity — the exposure is associated with a specific disease and site. Temporality — the exposure precedes the disease; Hill regarded this as the one indispensable condition. Biological gradient — a dose–response relationship exists. Plausibility — the association fits current biological knowledge. Coherence — the interpretation does not conflict with the natural history and biology of the disease. Experiment — removing the exposure reduces the disease. Analogy — similar exposures are known to cause similar effects.

Context and use

Hill developed the criteria while working with Richard Doll on the studies linking cigarette smoking with lung cancer, research summarised in the 1964 United States Surgeon General's report. He stressed that the criteria were "viewpoints" rather than a checklist: none, except temporality, is necessary, and none is sufficient to prove causation. Nevertheless, they became foundational in epidemiology and are routinely invoked in occupational health, environmental regulation and litigation over alleged toxic harms.

Criticism

Modern epidemiologists note that specificity is often wrong (smoking causes many diseases), that weak associations can be causal, and that the criteria lack a formal statistical basis. They are best used, in Hill's own spirit, as aids to judgement rather than mechanical rules.
