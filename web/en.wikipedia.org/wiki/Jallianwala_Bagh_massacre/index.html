The Jallianwala Bagh massacre, also known as the Amritsar massacre, took place on 13 April 1919, when troops of the British Indian Army, under the command of Brigadier General Reginald Dyer, opened fire on a large crowd of unarmed Indian civilians gathered in the Jallianwala Bagh public garden in Amritsar, Punjab. The crowd, many of whom had come to the city for the Baisakhi festival and others to protest the arrest of independence leaders Saifuddin Kitchlew and Satyapal, was fired upon for about ten minutes until the troops' ammunition was nearly exhausted.

Background

The massacre occurred amid unrest following the First World War, the repressive Rowlatt Act extending wartime emergency measures, and the imposition of martial law in Punjab. Dyer had banned public assemblies, but the order was not widely communicated. On the morning of 13 April he marched a force of about ninety soldiers to the Bagh, a walled enclosure with only narrow entrances, and ordered fire without warning.

Casualties and aftermath

The official British inquiry, the Hunter Committee, reported 379 dead; Indian estimates, including those of the Indian National Congress's own inquiry, put the death toll as high as 1,000 or more, with around 1,200 wounded. Many also died in stampedes or by jumping into the well inside the garden. Dyer told the inquiry he intended to produce a "moral effect" across Punjab and would have used machine guns had the lanes admitted them. He was removed from command and censured, but was never prosecuted and received a substantial testimonial fund raised by supporters in Britain.

Legacy

The massacre marked a turning point in India's independence movement, shattering faith in British justice for many Indians; the poet Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest. A memorial and museum now stand at the site near the Golden Temple, and the bullet-scarred walls have been preserved. In 1940, Udham Singh assassinated Michael O'Dwyer, the former Lieutenant Governor of Punjab who had endorsed Dyer's actions.
