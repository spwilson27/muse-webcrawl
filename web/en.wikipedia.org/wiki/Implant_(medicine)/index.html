Implant (medicine)

An implant is a medical device manufactured to replace a missing biological structure, support a damaged biological structure, or enhance an existing biological structure. Implants are man-made devices, in contrast to a transplant, which is a transplanted biomedical tissue. The surface of implants that contact the body may be made of a biomedical material such as titanium, silicone, or apatite, depending on what is most functional.

Applications

Sensory and neurological

Cochlear implants convert sound into electrical stimulation of the auditory nerve for people with profound sensorineural hearing loss; retinal implants attempt to restore some vision. Deep brain stimulators are implanted for Parkinson's disease and other movement disorders, and pacemakers and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators regulate heart rhythm.

Orthopaedic

Joint replacements (hip, knee, shoulder), fracture fixation plates, screws, pins and intramedullary rods are among the most common implants. Materials such as titanium alloys and cobalt-chromium are chosen for strength and biocompatibility; osseointegration—the direct bonding of bone to the implant surface—is the basis of modern dental implants and many orthopaedic devices.

Other implants

Examples include intraocular lenses after cataract surgery, contraceptive implants placed under the skin of the arm, insulin pumps, breast implants, surgical mesh, stents, and implantable drug-delivery devices.

Risks and regulation

Implantation carries risks of infection—including biofilm formation on the device surface, which is highly resistant to antibiotics—foreign-body reaction, mechanical failure, wear debris, and the need for revision surgery. Devices are regulated by authorities such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Union under the Medical Device Regulation, with classification by risk; high-risk devices require substantial clinical evidence before marketing.
