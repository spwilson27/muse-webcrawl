The walrus (Odobenus rosmarus) is a large pinniped marine mammal with a discontinuous distribution around the North Pole in the Arctic Ocean and subarctic seas. It is the only living species in the family Odobenidae. Adult walruses are easily recognised by their prominent tusks, whiskered muzzle (mystacial vibrissae), and great bulk: males can weigh over 1,200 kg (2,600 lb).

Subspecies

Two subspecies are widely recognised: the Atlantic walrus (O. r. rosmarus) of the Canadian Arctic and Greenland, and the Pacific walrus (O. r. divergens) of the Bering and Chukchi Seas. A third, the Laptev walrus, is sometimes proposed but is now generally grouped with the Pacific subspecies.

Anatomy and behaviour

Both sexes grow long ivory tusks — elongated canine teeth that grow throughout life — used for hauling out onto ice, defence, and social display. Walruses feed mainly on benthic molluscs such as clams, which they locate with their sensitive whiskers and extract from the shell by suction. They are highly social, hauling out in herds on ice floes and beaches.

Relationship with humans

Walruses have been hunted for centuries by Arctic indigenous peoples for meat, blubber, skin, and ivory, and were heavily exploited commercially in the 18th and 19th centuries. They are now protected in much of their range, but climate change and the loss of summer sea ice threaten the Pacific population in particular. The species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN.
