Tonometry is the procedure eye care professionals perform to determine the intraocular pressure (IOP), the fluid pressure inside the eye. It is an important test in the evaluation of patients at risk from glaucoma. Most tonometers measure pressure in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).

Methods

Applanation tonometry

In applanation tonometry, the force required to flatten a standard area of the cornea is measured, and IOP is inferred from that force. The Goldmann applanation tonometer, mounted on a slit lamp, is considered the clinical reference standard. A fluorescein-stained tear film and a split-image prism are used to identify the endpoint.

Indentation tonometry

The Schiøtz tonometer measures the depth of indentation produced in the cornea by a plunger of known weight. It is largely of historical interest today.

Non-contact tonometry

Non-contact ("air puff") tonometers flatten the cornea with a calibrated puff of air and detect the moment of applanation optically. They are widely used for screening because nothing touches the eye and no anaesthetic drops are required.

Other methods

  • Perkins tonometer – a hand-held Goldmann-type device usable on supine patients and children.
  • Tono-Pen – a portable electronic applanation device.
  • Rebound tonometry (iCare) – a tiny probe bounces off the cornea; no anaesthetic is needed.
  • Dynamic contour tonometry (Pascal) – uses a contour-matching tip intended to be less dependent on corneal properties.
  • Palpation – estimating pressure by fingertip feel through the closed eyelid; crude and only distinguishes very high pressures.

Factors affecting measurement

Central corneal thickness, corneal curvature and biomechanical properties influence readings: thick corneas tend to overestimate IOP with Goldmann tonometry, thin corneas to underestimate it. Measurements vary during the day (diurnal variation), so serial measurements are often taken.

Clinical significance

Normal IOP is generally considered to be 10–21 mmHg. Raised IOP is the major modifiable risk factor for glaucoma, although glaucoma can occur at statistically normal pressures (normal-tension glaucoma) and many people with raised pressure never develop it (ocular hypertension).
