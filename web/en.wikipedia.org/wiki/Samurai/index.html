Samurai

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A suit of samurai armour (ō-yoroi style) with horned helmet and silk lacing]

The samurai were the hereditary military nobility and officer caste of medieval and early-modern Japan, rising from provincial warriors of the Heian period to become Japan's ruling class from the Kamakura shogunate (1185) until their abolition in the Meiji Restoration (1868). Bound by codes later systematised as bushidō, samurai served daimyo lords in exchange for land or rice stipends, fought with the paired swords (katana and wakizashi) that were their badge of rank, and in peacetime served as administrators and scholars. Their image — loyal, disciplined, ready for death — was largely codified in the peaceful Edo period and exported worldwide after their disappearance.

Rise and rule

Warrior bands (bushi) emerged as the imperial court delegated provincial security; the Taira and Minamoto clans' wars ended with Minamoto no Yoritomo's shogunate at Kamakura (1185/1192). Samurai government (bakufu) thereafter shared — and usually overshadowed — imperial authority. The Sengoku period (1467–1600) of warring daimyo made and unmade samurai fortunes; Toyotomi Hideyoshi's sword hunt (1588) and the Tokugawa settlement froze the class into a hereditary caste of some 5–7 per cent of the population.

Abolition and myth

Under Tokugawa peace, samurai became stipended bureaucrats, many impoverished. The Meiji government abolished their privileges in stages (1868–1876), provoking the Satsuma Rebellion (1877), the samurai's last stand, crushed by a conscript army. Bushidō as a systematic ethic was then popularised for a modern nation (Nitobe Inazō's Bushido: The Soul of Japan, 1899). Samurai aesthetics — swords, armour, seppuku, the lone swordsman — remain central to Japanese and global popular culture.
