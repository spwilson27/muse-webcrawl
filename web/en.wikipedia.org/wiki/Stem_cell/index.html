Stem cell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stem cells are cells with two special properties: they can divide to renew themselves over long periods, and they can differentiate into specialised cell types. Embryonic stem cells (from the early embryo) are pluripotent — able to form any cell of the body — while adult (tissue) stem cells, such as blood-forming stem cells in bone marrow, maintain and repair their tissue of residence.

In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka showed that ordinary adult cells can be reprogrammed back to pluripotency with four transcription factors — induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells) — earning the 2012 Nobel Prize and giving medicine patient-matched cells without embryos.

Medicine

Bone-marrow (haematopoietic stem cell) transplantation is the long-established stem-cell therapy, curing leukaemias and immune disorders — including as the vehicle for CRISPR gene therapies. Most other 'stem cell' offerings, especially in private clinics, remain unproven; regulators warn against them. Regenerative medicine trials target Parkinson's disease (dopamine neurons), retinal disease, diabetes (insulin-producing islets) and heart failure.

Biology

Stem cells sit in niches that meter out division: the gut lining renews in days, skin in weeks, while the brain's neurogenesis in adults is restricted and still charted. Cancer can be viewed partly as stem-cell biology gone wrong — the cancer stem cell hypothesis.
