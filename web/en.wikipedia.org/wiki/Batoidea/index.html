Batoidea is a superorder of cartilaginous fishes commonly known as rays. They and their close relatives, the sharks, comprise the subclass Elasmobranchii. Rays are the largest group of cartilaginous fishes, with well over 600 species in 26 families. They are distinguished by their flattened bodies, enlarged pectoral fins fused to the head, and gill slits on their ventral (under) surfaces.

A spotted eagle ray, a member of the Myliobatiformes.

Anatomy and behaviour

Most batoids are bottom-dwellers that feed on molluscs, crustaceans and small fish, crushing prey with flattened, pavement-like teeth; the plankton-feeding manta and devil rays are an exception, swimming in open water and filter-feeding. Many species are viviparous, giving birth to live young. Defence mechanisms include the venomous tail spines of stingrays, the electric organs of torpedo rays (capable of delivering a substantial shock), and the rostral saw of sawfishes, which are now critically endangered.

Evolution and classification

Batoids evolved from shark-like ancestors by the Jurassic period, adapting to life on the seabed. Molecular studies suggest they are the sister group to sharks within the elasmobranchs. The largest ray, the giant oceanic manta ray (Mobula birostris), can reach 7 metres across; the smallest are electric rays barely 10 cm long.

Conservation

Rays are highly vulnerable to overfishing because of their slow growth and low fecundity; many species are threatened, and sawfishes are among the most endangered of all marine fishes.
