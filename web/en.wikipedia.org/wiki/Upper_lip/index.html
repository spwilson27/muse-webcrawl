Upper lip

The upper lip (Latin: labrum superius) is the fleshy fold of tissue forming the upper margin of the mouth in humans and many other mammals. Together with the lower lip, it surrounds the oral opening, assists in articulation of speech, facilitates eating and drinking, and plays a central role in facial expression and non-verbal communication.

Anatomy

The external surface of the upper lip is skin bearing hair follicles and sweat glands; at the vermilion border it transitions to the red, semi-mucous membrane of the lip proper, which lacks hair and salivary glands on its outer surface. A shallow vertical groove, the philtrum, runs down the centre of the upper lip from the nasal septum to the vermilion border, where the slight central projection is called the tubercle. The orbicularis oris muscle forms the bulk of the lip, with elevators including the levator labii superioris raising it. Blood is supplied by the superior labial artery, a branch of the facial artery, and sensation is carried by the infraorbital branch of the trigeminal nerve (CN V2).

Development

The upper lip forms during the fourth to seventh weeks of embryonic development from the fusion of the medial nasal prominences with the maxillary prominences. Failure of complete fusion results in cleft lip, one of the most common congenital craniofacial anomalies, occurring in roughly 1 in 700 live births worldwide and typically repaired surgically in infancy.

Clinical significance

The upper lip is a common site for herpes simplex labialis ("cold sores"), basal cell carcinoma on its cutaneous portion, and traumatic lacerations. The expression "stiff upper lip" derives from the suppression of visible emotion associated with the lip's musculature. In dentistry and orthodontics, the relationship of the upper lip to the incisors is assessed in smile analysis and treatment planning.
