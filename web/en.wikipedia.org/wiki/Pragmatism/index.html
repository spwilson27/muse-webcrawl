Pragmatism


Illustration placeholder: Peirce and James, founders of the pragmatic tradition

Pragmatism is a philosophical tradition that began in the United States around 1870. Its origins are often attributed to the philosophers Charles Sanders Peirce, William James, and John Dewey. Pragmatism is commonly summarised by the pragmatic maxim formulated by Peirce: "Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object."

Pragmatists hold that most philosophical topics — the nature of knowledge, language, concepts, meaning, belief, and science — are best understood in terms of their practical uses and successes. For pragmatism, thought is an instrument for prediction, problem-solving, and action, and the truth of an idea is bound up with its practical consequences rather than with correspondence to a mind-independent reality pictured in advance.

Origins

The movement grew out of discussions in the informal "Metaphysical Club" in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in the early 1870s, which included Peirce, James, Chauncey Wright, and the future Supreme Court justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. Peirce's papers "The Fixation of Belief" (1877) and "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), published in Popular Science Monthly, are regarded as the founding documents. James credited Peirce in an 1898 lecture, launching the term into wider circulation, and expanded the doctrine in his 1907 book Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking.

Core ideas

The pragmatic maxim

Peirce's maxim is a rule for clarifying concepts: the meaning of an idea consists in the conceivable practical effects of its object. If two supposedly different ideas have all their conceivable effects in common, there is no real difference between them.

Truth and fallibilism

Peirce understood truth as the opinion that inquiry, carried far enough, would ultimately converge upon; he combined this with a thoroughgoing fallibilism — any current belief may be wrong. James held a looser view, treating true ideas as those that "work" in experience and can be verified. Dewey developed these themes into instrumentalism, a naturalistic account of inquiry as problem-solving continuous with science.

Later developments

Dewey applied pragmatism to education, democracy, ethics, and aesthetics, while George Herbert Mead extended it into social psychology. In the later 20th century, W. V. O. Quine, Wilfrid Sellars, and Richard Rorty revived pragmatic themes within analytic philosophy; Rorty's neopragmatism, set out in Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature (1979), recast philosophy as conversation rather than representation. Susan Haack and Hilary Putnam have defended more classical forms of the tradition.

Criticism

British philosophers such as Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore objected that pragmatism conflates truth with utility and cannot distinguish between beliefs that work and beliefs that are true. Defenders reply that pragmatism offers a theory of inquiry, not a crude reduction of truth to whatever happens to please.
