Degrees of freedom (physics and chemistry)

In physics and chemistry, a degree of freedom is an independent physical parameter in the formal description of the state of a physical system. The set of all states of a system is known as the system's phase space, and the degrees of freedom are the dimensions of that space. For a particle, degrees of freedom may correspond to the coordinates needed to specify its position; for molecules and thermodynamic systems, the term commonly counts the independent ways in which the system can store energy.

Molecular degrees of freedom

A molecule of N atoms has 3N total degrees of freedom. These divide into:

  • Translational — 3, for motion of the centre of mass along three axes.
  • Rotational — 3 for a non-linear molecule, 2 for a linear molecule (rotation about the molecular axis being physically insignificant).
  • Vibrational — the remainder: 3N − 6 for a non-linear molecule and 3N − 5 for a linear molecule, counting the normal modes of vibration.

Thus a monatomic gas particle has 3 degrees of freedom; a diatomic molecule such as N2 or O2 has 3 translational, 2 rotational, and 1 vibrational mode (though the vibrational mode may not be excited at room temperature).

Equipartition of energy

By the equipartition theorem of classical statistical mechanics, each quadratic degree of freedom contributes an average energy of ½kBT at temperature T, counting both kinetic and (for vibrations) potential terms. The degrees of freedom therefore determine the heat capacity of gases: monatomic ideal gases have molar heat capacity CV = (3/2)R, diatomic gases about (5/2)R at ordinary temperatures when vibration is frozen out, increasing toward (7/2)R when vibration becomes active. The failure of classical equipartition at low temperatures — "frozen" degrees of freedom — was an important motivation for quantum mechanics.

Thermodynamic usage

In thermodynamics, the term is also used in the sense of Gibbs's phase rule, F = C − P + 2, where the number of degrees of freedom F is the number of intensive variables (such as temperature and pressure) that can be varied independently without changing the number of phases P in equilibrium for a system of C components.
