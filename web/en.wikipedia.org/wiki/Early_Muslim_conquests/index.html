Early Muslim conquests

The early Muslim conquests, also known as the Arab conquests, were a series of campaigns initiated in the 7th century by the first caliphs of Islam. Beginning under the Rashidun Caliphate after the death of the prophet Muhammad in 632 CE and continuing under the Umayyad Caliphate, they created one of the largest empires in history, stretching from the Iberian Peninsula in the west to Central Asia and the Indus Valley in the east.

Background

Arabia was unified under Islam during the Ridda Wars under the first caliph, Abu Bakr. The two great powers of the region, the Byzantine (Eastern Roman) Empire and the Sasanian Persian Empire, had been weakened by decades of mutual warfare, plague and internal strife, leaving Syria, Iraq and Egypt exposed.

The Rashidun conquests

Under the caliphs Abu Bakr and Umar, Muslim armies conquered Syria after victories such as the Battle of Yarmouk (636), overran Iraq and Persia after Qadisiyya (636) and Nahavand (642), and took Egypt between 639 and 642. Commanders such as Khalid ibn al-Walid, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas and Amr ibn al-As won fame in these campaigns. By 651 the Sasanian Empire had ceased to exist and its last shah, Yazdegerd III, was dead.

The Umayyad expansion

After the civil wars of the mid-7th century, the Umayyad Caliphate (661–750) resumed expansion: North Africa was subdued, and in 711 armies crossed into Iberia, destroying the Visigothic kingdom and conquering most of the peninsula as al-Andalus. In the east, Muslim forces reached Transoxiana and crossed the Indus into Sindh. Two great sieges of Constantinople (674–678 and 717–718) failed, and the advance into western Europe was checked in Francia, traditionally at the Battle of Tours in 732.

Consequences

The conquests permanently reshaped the Middle East, North Africa and beyond, spreading Arabic and Islam across a vast region, though conversion was gradual and took centuries. The conquered territories were administered through garrison cities such as Kufa, Basra and Fustat, and the empire's revenues and institutions were organised under the caliphs in Medina and later Damascus.
