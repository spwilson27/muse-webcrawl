October Crisis
Date5 October – 28 December 1970
LocationMontreal, Quebec, Canada
PerpetratorsFront de libération du Québec (FLQ)
Key eventsKidnappings of James Cross and Pierre Laporte; murder of Laporte; invocation of the War Measures Act

The October Crisis (French: Crise d'Octobre) was a crisis in the Canadian province of Quebec in October 1970, when members of the Front de libération du Québec (FLQ), a separatist militant group, kidnapped British trade commissioner James Cross and Quebec labour minister Pierre Laporte. In response, Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau invoked the War Measures Act — its only peacetime use in Canadian history — and federal troops were deployed in Quebec and Ottawa. Laporte was murdered, Cross was released after two months, and the crisis profoundly shaped Canadian debates over civil liberties and Quebec nationalism.

Background

The FLQ had carried out bombings in Quebec since 1963 in pursuit of an independent, socialist Quebec. During the Quiet Revolution, Quebec nationalism was rising, and in 1970 the FLQ sought to escalate its campaign and provoke a popular uprising.

Kidnappings

On 5 October 1970, FLQ members kidnapped James Richard Cross from his Montreal home, demanding the release of imprisoned FLQ members, $500,000 in gold, and the broadcast of the FLQ manifesto — which was read on Radio-Canada. On 10 October a second cell kidnapped Pierre Laporte, Quebec's minister of labour, while he played football with his nephew. Laporte's body was found in the trunk of a car on 17 October; he had been strangled.

War Measures Act

On 16 October, at the request of the Quebec government and the City of Montreal, Trudeau proclaimed the War Measures Act, outlawing the FLQ and permitting arrest and detention without charge. Nearly 500 people were arrested; most were released without charges. When asked how far he would go, Trudeau gave his famous "Just watch me" reply. Thousands of troops guarded public buildings and patrolled Montreal streets.

Resolution

In early December police located the cell holding Cross; he was released on 3 December after 62 days in exchange for his kidnappers' safe passage to Cuba. The cell that murdered Laporte was captured in late December. Members Paul Rose and Francis Simard were convicted of murder; others received lesser sentences and exile.

Aftermath and legacy

Public support for the FLQ collapsed; the separatist cause continued democratically through the Parti Québécois. The use of the War Measures Act remains controversial and led to its replacement in 1988 by the more constrained Emergencies Act. The crisis remains a defining episode in modern Canadian history.
