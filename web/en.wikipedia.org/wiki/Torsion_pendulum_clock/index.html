Torsion pendulum clock

A torsion pendulum clock, commonly called an anniversary clock or 400-day clock, is a mechanical clock that keeps time with a torsion pendulum: a weighted wheel or decorative balls suspended from a thin wire or ribbon that twists back and forth, rotating rather than swinging.

Operation

The pendulum typically oscillates with a period of several seconds, far slower than a swinging pendulum, so the clock consumes very little energy: a single winding can run it for 400 days or more — traditionally a year, hence "anniversary clock", wound on an anniversary each year. The escapement gives the pendulum a small impulse each cycle via a fork engaging a pin on the suspension. Robert Leslie patented an early version in the United States in 1825; Anton Harder's 1880s German patents led to commercial production by firms such as Jahresuhren-Fabrik (JUF), Schatz and Kundo.

Characteristics

Most examples sit under a glass dome, with three or four rotating balls as the pendulum. They are notoriously sensitive to levelling and to temperature changes in the suspension wire, and are valued chiefly as ornaments rather than precision timekeepers.
