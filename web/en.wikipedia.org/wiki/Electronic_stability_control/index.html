Electronic stability control

Electronic stability control (ESC), also referred to as electronic stability program (ESP) or dynamic stability control (DSC), is a computerized technology that improves a vehicle's stability by detecting and reducing loss of traction (skidding). When ESC detects a loss of steering control, it automatically applies braking to individual wheels to help steer the vehicle where the driver intends to go, and may also reduce engine power.

Operation

ESC continuously monitors the steering wheel angle, wheel speeds, yaw rate, and lateral acceleration. By comparing the driver's intended direction with the vehicle's actual rotation, the system detects the onset of understeer (the front wheels lose grip and the car runs wide) or oversteer (the rear wheels lose grip and the car rotates). It then brakes a strategically chosen wheel—for example, the outer front wheel in oversteer—to generate a corrective yaw moment, often before the driver notices the skid.

History and mandates

Systems were developed in the late 1980s and early 1990s by suppliers such as Bosch, which introduced ESP on the Mercedes-Benz S-Class in 1995. Studies by insurance and safety bodies found ESC highly effective, reducing fatal single-vehicle crashes by roughly a third to a half. It became mandatory on new cars in the United States for model year 2012, in the European Union from 2014, and in many other markets.

Names

Manufacturers market the technology under many names, including ESP (Mercedes-Benz, Volkswagen Group), DSC (BMW, Jaguar), VSA (Honda), StabiliTrak (General Motors), and Vehicle Stability Control (Toyota).
