Crystal twinning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Twinned quartz crystals grown together in a symmetrical relationship.

Crystal twinning occurs when two or more separate crystals grow together in a specific symmetrical relationship, sharing some of the same crystal lattice points in a regular way. The resulting intergrowth is called a twin crystal. The orientation relationship between the parts is described by a twin law, which specifies a symmetry operation — a reflection across a plane, a rotation about an axis, or an inversion through a centre — that is not part of the symmetry of the individual crystal but relates the two individuals.

Definitions

In a contact twin, the two individuals meet along a single composition plane, as in the swallowtail twins of gypsum. In a penetration twin, the individuals interpenetrate each other, as in the cross-shaped staurolite twins and the cubic twins of fluorite. Repeated twinning on parallel planes produces polysynthetic twins, visible as fine parallel striations, famously in plagioclase feldspar, where the albite twin law is a diagnostic identification feature.

Formation

Twins form in three main ways:

  • Growth twins arise during crystallization, when an atom attaches in a "mistaken" twinned position on a growing face and the error propagates.
  • Transformation twins appear when a crystal cools through a phase transition into a lower-symmetry form, as in quartz passing from its high-temperature to low-temperature structure.
  • Deformation (mechanical) twins form when stress shears part of a crystal into the twin orientation, an important deformation mechanism in metals such as magnesium and tin — the audible "cry" of bent tin is caused by twinning.

Examples and significance

Many minerals have characteristic twin laws used in their identification: spinel twins on octahedra, Japan-law twins of quartz, Carlsbad and Manebach twins of orthoclase feldspar, and iron-cross twins of pyrite. In metallurgy, twin boundaries affect strength and ductility, and annealing twins in copper and austenitic steels are a standard feature of microstructure. Twinning can also complicate the determination of crystal structures by X-ray diffraction, where twinned specimens may mimic higher symmetry.
