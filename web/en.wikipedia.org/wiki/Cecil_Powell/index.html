Powell at Bristol.

Cecil Frank Powell (5 December 1903 – 9 August 1969) was a British physicist and Nobel laureate in Physics for his development of the photographic method of studying nuclear processes and for the resulting discovery of the pion (π-meson), the heavy subatomic particle whose existence Hideki Yukawa had predicted in 1935.

At Bristol, Powell perfected nuclear emulsions — thick photographic plates in which charged particles leave developable tracks — turning them into a precision instrument (with Giuseppe Occhialini and Hugh Muirhead and the young César Lattes). Emulsions exposed on the Pic du Midi (1946–47) and in the Bolivian Andes showed the π → μ decay chain: a heavier meson decaying to the lighter (muon), ending the decade-old confusion between the two. Powell's Bristol group, and the emulsion technique flown on balloons worldwide, mapped cosmic-ray physics in the years before accelerators took over; he later became a leading figure in European collaboration — a founding advocate of CERN — and in the Pugwash movement for disarmament.

Emulsion method

The technique's virtues were continuity (every second of exposure recorded), resolution (micron-scale tracks measured under microscopes by teams of scanners) and cheapness. Its images — star-like disintegrations and curling tracks — set the visual style of particle physics before the Bubble chamber era, and emulsion detectors still serve in neutrino physics (OPERA's tau-neutrino appearance measurement).
