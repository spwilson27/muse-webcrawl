Rule against perpetuities

The rule against perpetuities is a rule in the Anglo-American common law of property that prevents people from using legal instruments (usually a deed or a will) to exert control over the ownership of private property for a span of time beyond that permitted by law. It is notorious among law students and practitioners for its complexity.

Statement of the rule

The classic formulation, by John Chipman Gray, is:

No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than twenty-one years after some life in being at the creation of the interest.

Under the common-law rule, a contingent future interest is void from the outset if there is any possibility, however remote, that it might vest more than 21 years after the death of the last relevant life in being at the time the interest was created. Fertility is presumed regardless of age (the "fertile octogenarian"), and possibilities are judged at creation, not by what actually happens.

History

The rule developed in the 17th century, with its roots in the Duke of Norfolk's Case (1682), in which the House of Lords upheld a shifting executory interest but signalled that perpetuities — attempts to tie up land indefinitely — would not be tolerated. The period of a life in being plus 21 years crystallised over the following century.

Reform

Many jurisdictions have reformed the rule by statute: "wait-and-see" statutes judge validity by actual events rather than possibilities, cy-près doctrines allow courts to reform offending interests, and the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities substitutes a flat 90-year permissible period. Some U.S. states have effectively abolished the rule for trusts, to attract perpetual "dynasty trust" business.
