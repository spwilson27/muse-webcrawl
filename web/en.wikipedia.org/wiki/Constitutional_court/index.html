Constitutional court

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A constitutional court is a high court that deals primarily with constitutional law. Its main authority is to rule on whether laws that are challenged are in fact unconstitutional — that is, whether they conflict with constitutionally established rules, rights and freedoms. The model originates with the Austrian Constitutional Court of 1920, designed with Hans Kelsen's influence, and spread after 1945 (Italy, Germany) and again after 1989 across Central and Eastern Europe, Latin America, Africa and Asia.

Design

Constitutional courts stand apart from the ordinary judiciary (the concentrated model, contrasted with diffuse review by all courts as in the US). Access routes include referrals from courts in pending cases (concrete review), abstract review at the request of political actors (governments, legislators, ombudsmen), and in several systems individual constitutional complaints (Germany's Verfassungsbeschwerde, Spain's amparo), which make such courts major venues for rights litigation. Judges are typically appointed by multiple organs for long, non-renewable terms to disperse political control — though appointment battles in several countries (including controversies over Poland's Tribunal after 2015 and Hungary's court after 2010) show the model's vulnerability to court-curbing and packing.
