Oedipus (Greek: Οἰδίπους, "swollen foot") was a mythical king of Thebes in Greek mythology, whose story of unwitting parricide and incest became one of the most famous tragedies of the ancient world, dramatised by Sophocles in Oedipus Rex and Oedipus at Colonus. Doomed by prophecy before his birth, Oedipus killed his father Laius and married his mother Jocasta despite all efforts to avoid the oracle, and blinded himself when the truth came to light.

The myth

An oracle warned Laius, king of Thebes, that his son would kill him. When Jocasta bore a boy, Laius pierced the infant's ankles and had him exposed on Mount Cithaeron. A shepherd rescued the child, who was adopted by King Polybus and Queen Merope of Corinth and named Oedipus for his swollen feet. Grown to manhood, Oedipus heard the prophecy that he would kill his father and marry his mother; believing Polybus and Merope to be his parents, he fled Corinth.

At a place where three roads met, Oedipus quarrelled with an older man in a chariot and killed him and his attendants — the stranger was Laius. Travelling on to Thebes, Oedipus found the city terrorised by the Sphinx.

The riddle of the Sphinx

The Sphinx posed her riddle to all travellers: what creature walks on four feet in the morning, two at noon and three in the evening? Oedipus answered: man, who crawls as a baby, walks upright in maturity and leans on a staff in old age. Defeated, the Sphinx destroyed herself, and the grateful Thebans made Oedipus king; he married the widowed queen Jocasta, fulfilling the second half of the prophecy unknowingly. They had four children: Polynices, Eteocles, Antigone and Ismene.

Discovery and downfall

Years later a plague struck Thebes, and the oracle declared the city polluted by the unpunished murderer of Laius. Oedipus's relentless investigation — in Sophocles' dramatisation, a model of dramatic irony — led through the blind seer Tiresias and a surviving shepherd to the truth of his birth and deeds. Jocasta hanged herself; Oedipus, finding her body, blinded himself with her brooches and begged to be exiled.

Oedipus at Colonus and death

Accompanied by Antigone, the blind Oedipus wandered in exile until he reached Colonus, near Athens, where a grove of the Furies and Theseus's protection received him. There, in Sophocles' Oedipus at Colonus, he died mysteriously, his hidden grave becoming a blessing and protection for Athens.

Sources and interpretation

The story appears in the Odyssey, in lost epics (the Oedipodea), and in tragedies by Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides; Seneca wrote a Roman version. Aristotle held Oedipus Rex the finest tragedy. In the 20th century Freud named the Oedipus complex after the myth, and Lévi-Strauss used the tale as an exemplar of structural myth analysis.
