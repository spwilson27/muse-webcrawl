Digital photography uses cameras containing arrays of electronic photodetectors — image sensors — to capture images focused by a lens, as opposed to exposure on photographic film. The captured images are digitized and stored as computer files, ready for further digital processing, viewing, electronic publishing or printing. Digital photography is one of the major technological shifts in the history of the medium: it eliminated the cost and delay of film development, allowed instant review and deletion, and, combined with camera phones and the internet, transformed photography into a ubiquitous everyday activity.

Technology

At the heart of a digital camera is a sensor — historically a CCD and now almost universally a CMOS device — covered with a mosaic of colour filters (usually the Bayer pattern) so that each photosite records one colour; the full-colour image is reconstructed by demosaicing. Sensors are described by their physical size (full-frame, APS-C, Micro Four Thirds, and the small sensors of phones) and their resolution in megapixels. Images are stored as compressed JPEG or HEIF files or as minimally processed RAW files preserving the sensor data for later editing. Image processing increasingly takes place in the camera itself: modern phones use computational photography techniques — multi-frame stacking, HDR merging, night modes and depth estimation — to overcome the physical limits of small optics.

History

The enabling inventions were the CCD (1969, Willard Boyle and George E. Smith) and the CMOS sensor. In 1975 Kodak engineer Steven Sasson built the first self-contained digital camera: it weighed 3.6 kg, recorded a 0.01-megapixel black-and-white image onto a cassette tape in 23 seconds. Commercial digital cameras emerged around 1990–91 (the Dycam Model 1 and the Kodak DCS-100), consumer models spread in the later 1990s, and digital sales overtook film cameras in the early 2000s; Kodak itself, unable to adapt, entered bankruptcy in 2012. Camera phones, beginning with the Sharp J-SH04 in 2000 and exploding with the smartphone era after 2007, made the dedicated camera a specialist tool while raising annual photo counts into the trillions.

Impact

Instant feedback shortened the learning curve for photographers and changed professional workflows in journalism, medicine, science and commerce. The ease of sharing reshaped visual culture and social media, while raising questions about manipulation, provenance and the sheer volume of images; conversely, film photography has survived as a niche artistic practice.
