List of extinct animals

Placeholder image: the dodo, thylacine, and passenger pigeon are emblematic of human-caused extinction

This is a list of extinct animals — species and subspecies that have died out, with particular emphasis on extinctions in the Holocene era and the period of human influence. Extinction is a natural part of evolution, and the fossil record preserves countless species that vanished long before humans appeared; however, the rate of extinction has risen sharply in recent centuries due to habitat destruction, overhunting, introduced species, pollution, and climate change. The IUCN Red List catalogs hundreds of animal species documented as extinct since the year 1500, and the true total including invertebrates is far larger. The following are among the best-known examples, grouped by region and type.

Birds

  • Dodo (Raphus cucullatus) — flightless pigeon of Mauritius; last widely accepted sighting 1662; extinct by the late 17th century through hunting and introduced predators.
  • Passenger pigeon (Ectopistes migratorius) — once among the most abundant birds in North America, with flocks numbering in the billions; the last individual, Martha, died in the Cincinnati Zoo in 1914.
  • Great auk (Pinguinus impennis) — large flightless seabird of the North Atlantic; last two confirmed individuals killed on Eldey, Iceland, in 1852.
  • Moa (order Dinornithiformes) — giant flightless birds of New Zealand, hunted to extinction by Māori settlers by about the 15th century.
  • Elephant bird (family Aepyornithidae) — enormous ratites of Madagascar, extinct within roughly the last millennium.
  • Carolina parakeet (Conuropsis carolinensis) — the only parrot species native to the eastern United States; last captive bird died in 1918.
  • Ivory-billed woodpecker (Campephilus principalis) — declared extinct by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 2021 after decades without a confirmed sighting.

Mammals

  • Thylacine (Thylacinus cynocephalus) — marsupial predator of Tasmania; the last known individual died in Hobart Zoo in 1936.
  • Quagga (Equus quagga quagga) — South African zebra subspecies, extinct in the wild by the 1870s; last captive died in Amsterdam in 1883.
  • Steller's sea cow (Hydrodamalis gigas) — giant sirenian of the Bering Sea, discovered by science in 1741 and hunted to extinction by about 1768.
  • Woolly mammoth (Mammuthus primigenius) — mainland populations vanished around 10,000 years ago; the last island population on Wrangel Island survived until roughly 4,000 years ago.
  • Aurochs (Bos primigenius) — wild ancestor of domestic cattle; the last died in Poland's Jaktorów Forest in 1627.
  • Atlas bear, Bali tiger, Javan tiger, and Caspian tiger — regional subspecies eliminated by hunting and habitat loss in the 19th and 20th centuries.
  • Baiji (Lipotes vexillifer) — Yangtze river dolphin, declared functionally extinct after a 2006 survey found none.

Reptiles, amphibians, and fish

  • Golden toad (Incilius periglenes) — Costa Rican frog last seen in 1989; its disappearance is linked to climate-driven disease.
  • Pinta Island tortoise — subspecies of Galápagos tortoise whose last member, Lonesome George, died in 2012.
  • Chinese paddlefish (Psephurus gladius) — one of the world's largest freshwater fish, declared extinct in 2022.
  • Round Island burrowing boa and numerous island reptiles lost to introduced goats, rats, and pigs.

Invertebrates

  • Rocky Mountain locust (Melanoplus spretus) — once formed the largest swarms ever recorded in North America; extinct by the early 1900s.
  • Xerces blue (Glaucopsyche xerces) — butterfly of the San Francisco dunes, extinct by the 1940s; the first American butterfly known to be lost to urban development.
  • Hundreds of land snail species of Hawaii and Pacific islands, devastated by the introduced rosy wolf snail.

Prehistoric extinctions

Beyond the human era, the "Big Five" mass extinctions eliminated large fractions of marine and terrestrial life, including the non-avian dinosaurs in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago, and most marine species in the Permian–Triassic event 252 million years ago. Late Pleistocene megafauna losses — mammoths, saber-toothed cats, giant ground sloths — coincided with both climate change and human expansion.
