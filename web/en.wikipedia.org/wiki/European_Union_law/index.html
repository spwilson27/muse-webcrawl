European Union law is a system of rules operating within the member states of the European Union. Since the founding of the European Coal and Steel Community following World War II, the EU has developed the aim to develop a single market, an area of freedom, security and justice, and to promote peace and the rule of law. EU law is unique among international legal orders in that it is a supranational system: in areas where member states have conferred competences on the Union, EU law takes precedence over conflicting national law.

History

The origins of EU law lie in the Treaty of Paris (1951), which created the European Coal and Steel Community, and the Treaties of Rome (1957), which founded the European Economic Community and Euratom. Successive treaties — including the Single European Act (1986), the Maastricht Treaty (1992), the Treaty of Amsterdam (1997), the Treaty of Nice (2001), and the Treaty of Lisbon (2007) — progressively expanded the Union's competences and reformed its institutions.

Sources of law

The primary law of the Union consists of the treaties and the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union. Secondary law is made by the Union's institutions and includes regulations, which are directly applicable in all member states; directives, which bind member states as to the result to be achieved but leave the choice of form and methods to national authorities; and decisions, which are binding on those to whom they are addressed. Recommendations and opinions have no binding force.

Principles

The Court of Justice has developed foundational principles through its case law. In Van Gend en Loos (1963) the Court held that EU law can create rights for individuals that national courts must protect (direct effect). In Costa v ENEL (1964) it held that EU law takes precedence over national law (primacy or supremacy). Other key principles include subsidiarity, proportionality, conferral, and sincere cooperation.

Institutions

Legislative power is shared mainly between the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union, generally acting on proposals from the European Commission. The Court of Justice of the European Union, based in Luxembourg, ensures that EU law is interpreted and applied uniformly, in part through the preliminary reference procedure, by which national courts may ask it to rule on questions of interpretation.

Areas of law

EU law covers a wide range of fields, including the internal market and the four freedoms (goods, services, capital, and persons), competition law, agriculture, transport, environmental protection, consumer protection, employment law, and the area of freedom, security and justice, encompassing asylum, immigration, and judicial cooperation.
