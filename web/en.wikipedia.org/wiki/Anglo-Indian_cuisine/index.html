Anglo-Indian cuisine is the culinary tradition that developed during the British Raj through the interaction of British tastes with Indian ingredients and cooking techniques, and which became the food culture of the Anglo-Indian community. It blended the roasts, puddings and soups of Britain with Indian spices and methods, and in turn exported dishes — some of them transformed — back to Britain.

Development

British officials and soldiers of the East India Company employed Indian cooks (khansamas) who adapted familiar dishes to local produce: curries were standardised for British palates, chutneys and pickles accompanied roasts, and rice-based dishes emerged for breakfast and tiffin. Cookbooks for the colonial household, most famously The Indian Cookery Book (1880) and Flora Annie Steel and Grace Gardiner's The Complete Indian Housekeeper and Cook (1888), codified the repertoire.

Characteristic dishes

Signature dishes include kedgeree (from khichdi), a breakfast of rice, smoked fish and egg; mulligatawny soup (from Tamil milagu tannir, "pepper water"); railway mutton curry, served on long train journeys; country captain chicken; jalfrezi (from stir-frying leftovers with chillies); pish-pash (a soft rice-and-meat dish for invalids and children); and Christmas specialities such as kulkuls, guava cheese and plum cake in Anglo-Indian homes in Kolkata, Chennai and Bangalore. Bottled curry powders and pastes marketed in Britain were a direct legacy of this cuisine.

Legacy

After 1947 much of the Anglo-Indian community emigrated to Britain, Australia and Canada, carrying the cuisine with them; in India it survives in clubs, railway refreshment rooms and community cookbooks, and has been documented by writers such as Jennifer Brennan and Bridget White.
