Puuc

Puuc is the name of a region in the western part of the Mexican state of Yucatán, and of the distinctive Maya architectural style that flourished there during the Terminal Classic period (roughly AD 750–1000). The word puuc means "hill" or "range of hills" in Yucatec Maya, referring to the low range of hills in the region.

Architecture

Puuc architecture is characterized by smooth lower walls of plain limestone blocks, with upper façades richly decorated with stone mosaics. Motifs include geometric latticework, columns, and masks of the rain god Chaac, whose long-nosed masks often project from building corners. Buildings were typically constructed with a concrete core faced with thin veneer stones.

Sites

The best-known Puuc sites include Uxmal, Kabah, Sayil, Labná and Xlapak. Uxmal's Nunnery Quadrangle and Pyramid of the Magician are celebrated examples of the style. Because the region lacks surface water, Puuc cities depended on chultunes, underground cisterns, to store rainwater.

Decline

Most Puuc centres were abandoned by around AD 1000, during the broader Maya collapse of the Terminal Classic, though some occupation continued into the Postclassic period.
