Energy policy of the United States

The energy policy of the United States is determined by federal, state and local entities, and addresses issues of energy production, distribution, consumption and energy security. It has historically been shaped by a series of federal statutes, from the promotion of domestic oil and nuclear power in the mid-twentieth century, through the crisis-driven legislation of the 1970s, to twenty-first-century measures promoting efficiency, renewable energy and emissions reductions. The principal federal agency is the Department of Energy, created in 1977.

US policy balances fossil fuel production with a growing renewable sector.

History

For much of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries federal involvement was limited to land and water management and wartime controls. The Atomic Energy Act of 1946 and its 1954 successor launched civilian nuclear power. The 1973 oil embargo and the 1979 energy crisis transformed policy: President Nixon announced "Project Independence", Congress created the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (1975), enacted the first Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards, and in 1977 President Carter established the Department of Energy. The National Energy Act of 1978 promoted conservation and coal use while deregulating natural gas.

Modern legislation

The Energy Policy Act of 1992 encouraged alternative fuels and wholesale electricity competition; the Energy Policy Act of 2005 provided tax incentives for conventional and renewable production and mandated renewable fuel blending. The Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007 raised CAFE targets and created the Renewable Fuel Standard. The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 directed tens of billions of dollars to clean energy and grid modernisation. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 was the largest climate and energy investment in US history, providing roughly $369 billion in clean-energy tax credits and incentives, alongside continued support for domestic oil and gas leasing.

Production and security

The shale revolution — hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling — made the United States the world's largest producer of oil (from 2018) and natural gas, and a net total energy exporter in 2019 for the first time since the 1950s. Energy security policy nonetheless retains the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, the world's largest emergency crude stockpile, authorised to hold over 700 million barrels in Gulf Coast salt caverns.

Climate and environment

Federal climate policy has oscillated between administrations: the United States joined the Paris Agreement in 2016, withdrew in 2020, rejoined in 2021, and announced a further withdrawal in 2025. The Environmental Protection Agency regulates greenhouse gases under the Clean Air Act following the Supreme Court's 2007 decision in Massachusetts v. EPA, while many states operate their own renewable portfolio standards and cap-and-trade programmes such as California's and the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative.
