The galli (singular gallus) were the eunuch priests of the Phrygian goddess Cybele and her consort Attis. Their self-castration re-enacted the myth of Attis, who in a frenzy of grief or madness mutilated himself beneath a pine tree; those who wished to enter Cybele's priesthood imitated his act and thereafter lived in female dress and appearance.

Cult practice

The cult of the Magna Mater was officially received at Rome in 204 BC during the Second Punic War, when her sacred stone was brought from Pessinus. For two centuries Roman citizens were barred from the priesthood, which remained the province of foreign galli; the respectable, citizen archigallus who supervised the cult held a separate, non-eunuch office. The galli were famous in Roman streets for their processions: with long hair, white or coloured feminine clothing, jewellery and heavy percussion — tympana, cymbals and the Phrygian flute — they danced themselves into ecstasy and collected alms from bystanders.

The March festival

The climax of the galli's year was the Dies sanguinis ("Day of Blood") on 24 March, within the March festival of Cybele and Attis (Megalesia cycle). In mourning for Attis the priests flagellated themselves and gashed their arms, sprinkling the altars and the sacred pine with their blood; initiates who chose to join the priesthood performed their castration on this day in their religious frenzy. The festival continued with the Hilaria, a day of rejoicing for Attis's symbolic renewal.

Reception

Roman writers generally regarded the galli with a mixture of fascination and contempt, treating their effeminacy and self-mutilation as un-Roman, yet the cult persisted and was patronised into the fourth century AD. Catullus' poem 63, the Attis, is the most famous literary treatment of the myth and the galli's frenzy. The word gallus was said by ancient authors to derive from the river Gallus in Phrygia, whose waters supposedly drove drinkers mad.
