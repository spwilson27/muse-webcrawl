Superabundant number

In mathematics, a superabundant number is a natural number n whose sum-of-divisors ratio is greater than that of every smaller number. Writing σ(n) for the sum-of-divisors function, n is superabundant if

σ(n)/n > σ(m)/m   for all m < n.

The concept was introduced by Leonidas Alaoglu and Paul Erdős in 1944. The first superabundant numbers are 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 120, 180, 240, 360, 720, 840, 1260, 1680, 2520, 5040, … (sequence A004394 in the OEIS).

Properties

Every superabundant number is an abundant number and indeed a highly composite-like number: superabundant numbers are built from small primes with non-increasing exponents. Alaoglu and Erdős showed that a superabundant number must be divisible by all primes up to some bound, and studied the maximal order of log σ(n)/log n in connection with the distribution of primes.

Superabundant numbers are closely tied to the Riemann hypothesis. Robin's criterion states that the Riemann hypothesis is true if and only if σ(n) < eγ n log log n for all n > 5040, where γ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Because any counterexample must in effect maximise σ(n)/n, the search concentrates on superabundant (and the closely related colossally abundant) numbers. Guy Robin proved the criterion in 1984, building on work of Ramanujan on highly composite numbers.

Related concepts

  • Colossally abundant numbers — maximise σ(n)/n1+ε for some ε > 0; every colossally abundant number is superabundant.
  • Highly abundant numbers — record-setters for σ(n) itself rather than the ratio.
  • Perfect numbers — satisfy σ(n)/n = 2 exactly.
