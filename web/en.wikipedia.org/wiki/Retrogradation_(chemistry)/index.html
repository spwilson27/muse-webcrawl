Retrogradation (chemistry)

Retrogradation is a reaction that takes place when the amylose and amylopectin chains in cooked, gelatinized starch realign themselves as the cooked starch cools. During gelatinization, starch granules swell and amylose leaches out into the surrounding water. When the gel cools, the polymer chains re-associate into ordered, partially crystalline structures, expelling water in the process (syneresis).

Effects

Retrogradation is responsible for the staling of bread and other baked goods: as starch recrystallizes, the crumb becomes firm and dry-tasting even though little moisture has been lost overall. The process is fastest at refrigerator temperatures and can be partially reversed by reheating, which is why stale bread refreshes when toasted or warmed. Retrogradation also increases the proportion of resistant starch, which resists digestion in the small intestine and is of nutritional interest.

Amylose retrogrades rapidly, within hours, whereas the branched amylopectin retrogrades more slowly over days. Food manufacturers retard retrogradation with emulsifiers, enzymes, and modified starches, and waxy starches low in amylose retrograde less readily.
