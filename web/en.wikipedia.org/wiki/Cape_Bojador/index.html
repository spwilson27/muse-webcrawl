Cape Bojador (Portuguese: Cabo Bojador; Arabic: رأس بوجادور) is a headland on the west coast of Africa, in present-day Western Sahara, at approximately 26°07′ north latitude. For medieval European sailors it marked the feared limit of the navigable world, and its rounding in 1434 by the Portuguese mariner Gil Eanes was a landmark in the Age of Discovery.

History

Medieval maps and sailors' lore depicted the waters beyond Cape Bojador as a realm of boiling seas, contrary winds, treacherous shoals and mythical monsters. The cape's name is generally derived from the Arabic Abu Khatar, "father of danger". Strong southward currents, persistent fog and shallow, shifting sandbanks made return voyages against the prevailing winds appear impossible.

Under the sponsorship of Prince Henry the Navigator, Portuguese expeditions made repeated attempts to pass the cape in the 1420s and early 1430s. In 1434 Gil Eanes, a squire of Henry's household, finally rounded Bojador by standing well out to sea and returning on the offshore winds — the technique later systematised as the volta do mar. The breakthrough opened the way for the progressive Portuguese exploration of the African coast that culminated in the rounding of the Cape of Good Hope in 1488 and the sea route to India in 1498.

Geography

The cape lies on a low, sandy stretch of the Saharan coast south of the Canary Islands. A modern town of the same name, Boujdour, has grown up nearby and is administered by Morocco as part of the disputed territory of Western Sahara. A lighthouse marks the headland.
