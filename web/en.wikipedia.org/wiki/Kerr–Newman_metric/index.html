Kerr–Newman metric

The Kerr–Newman metric is the most general asymptotically flat, stationary solution of the Einstein–Maxwell equations in general relativity, describing the geometry in the region surrounding a charged, rotating mass.

History

Ezra Newman developed the solution in 1965, generalising Roy Kerr's 1963 metric for uncharged rotating masses and the Reissner–Nordström metric for charged non-rotating masses. A Kerr–Newman black hole is completely characterised by three parameters: mass M, angular momentum J and electric charge Q.

Properties

The metric possesses inner and outer event horizons and an ergosphere; for sufficiently large charge or spin the horizons disappear, which would expose a ring singularity — a possibility excluded, if Roger Penrose's cosmic censorship hypothesis holds, for physically realistic objects. Because astrophysical black holes are expected to discharge quickly, the Kerr metric is usually the relevant case in practice.
