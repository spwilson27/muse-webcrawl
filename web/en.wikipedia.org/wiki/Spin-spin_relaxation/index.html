Spin-spin relaxation

In nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), spin-spin relaxation is the mechanism by which the transverse component of the magnetization vector decays exponentially towards its equilibrium value of zero. It is also called transverse relaxation, or T2 relaxation. The characteristic time constant of this decay is called the spin-spin relaxation time, denoted T2.

After excitation by a radio-frequency pulse, the nuclear spins of a sample precess in phase, producing a detectable signal. Because each spin experiences a slightly different local magnetic field—owing to dipole-dipole interactions with neighbouring spins, chemical shielding and other local effects—the spins gradually lose phase coherence ("dephasing" or "fanning out"). The net transverse magnetization therefore decays, typically exponentially, even though no energy is transferred to the surrounding lattice. This distinguishes T2 relaxation from spin-lattice (T1) relaxation, which involves the recovery of longitudinal magnetization through energy exchange with the environment.

Measurement and T2*

The decay observed in a free induction decay is usually faster than the intrinsic T2 because inhomogeneities of the main magnetic field add a further dephasing contribution. The resulting effective relaxation time is denoted T2* ("T2-star"), related by 1/T2* = 1/T2 + 1/T2,inhom. A spin echo sequence, in which a 180° refocusing pulse reverses the dephasing caused by static field inhomogeneity, allows the true T2 to be measured; the Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) sequence of repeated echoes is the standard measurement method.

Significance

T2 values vary strongly between tissues and with molecular mobility: mobile liquids such as cerebrospinal fluid have long T2 values (seconds), while solids and macromolecule-bound water have very short T2 values (microseconds to milliseconds). This contrast underlies T2-weighted MRI, in which differences in transverse relaxation between tissues generate much of the image contrast used in clinical diagnosis. In NMR spectroscopy, T2 determines the natural linewidth of resonances, since the linewidth at half height is approximately 1/(πT2).
