In algebraic topology, the Betti numbers are used to distinguish topological spaces based on the connectivity of their n-dimensional homology groups. They are among the most basic topological invariants: roughly speaking, the kth Betti number bk counts the number of k-dimensional "holes" in a space. The concept is named after the Italian mathematician Enrico Betti, who introduced related ideas in the 1870s, and was developed further by Henri Poincaré in his foundational work on topology in the 1890s.

Definition

For a topological space X, the kth Betti number bk(X) is defined as the rank of the kth homology group Hk(X), that is, the dimension of the vector space Hk(X; Q) of homology with rational coefficients. When the homology groups are finitely generated, this is the number of free generators, ignoring torsion.

Intuitively:

  • b0 is the number of connected components of the space.
  • b1 is the number of one-dimensional holes, such as the loop through the hole of a torus.
  • b2 is the number of two-dimensional voids or cavities enclosed by the space.

Examples

For a sphere S2, the Betti numbers are b0 = 1, b1 = 0, b2 = 1. For a torus, b0 = 1, b1 = 2 (the meridian and longitude loops), and b2 = 1. A figure-eight (wedge of two circles) has b1 = 2.

Properties

The Betti numbers are related to the Euler characteristic χ by the alternating sum χ = Σ (−1)k bk. The generating function of the Betti numbers is the Poincaré polynomial P(t) = Σ bk tk. For closed orientable surfaces, Poincaré duality implies the symmetry bk = bn−k. Betti numbers are computable for simplicial complexes and are widely used in topological data analysis, for example in persistent homology.
