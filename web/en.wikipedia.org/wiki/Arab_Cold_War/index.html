Arab Cold War

The Arab Cold War pitted republican, socialist states against conservative monarchies.

The Arab Cold War (Arabic: الحرب العربية الباردة, al-ḥarb al-ʿarabiyya al-bārida) was a period of political rivalry in the Arab world lasting roughly from the 1950s to the 1970s, overlapping with and distinct from the global Cold War between the United States and the Soviet Union. The term was coined by American political scientist Malcolm Kerr in his 1965 book The Arab Cold War.

The rivals

On one side stood the revolutionary republics — above all Gamal Abdel Nasser's Egypt, together with Syria, Iraq (after 1958), Algeria, and later Libya — promoting Arab nationalism, Arab socialism, and unity. On the other stood the conservative monarchies, principally Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and Morocco, supported by the Hashemite Kingdom of Iraq until its overthrow in 1958. The rivalry was conducted through propaganda radio broadcasts such as Egypt's "Voice of the Arabs", subversion, coups, and proxy conflicts, most bloodily in the North Yemen Civil War (1962–1970), where Egypt backed the republicans and Saudi Arabia backed the royalists.

Decline

The Arab states' defeat in the Six-Day War of 1967 discredited Nasser and Nasserism, shifting the centre of gravity in Arab politics toward the oil-rich conservative states. The Arab Cold War is usually considered to have ended around 1970 with Nasser's death, though some historians extend it to the Iranian Revolution of 1979.
