Beaker culture

The Beaker culture, also known as the Bell Beaker culture, was an archaeological culture of the late Neolithic and early Bronze Age (roughly 2900–1800 BC) that spread across much of western and central Europe. It is named after its characteristic bell-shaped drinking vessels, decorated with bands of impressed or incised ornament.

Characteristics

Bell Beaker assemblages typically include the distinctive pottery beakers, copper daggers and awls, stone wrist-guards, barbed-and-tanged flint arrowheads, and V-perforated buttons. Burials were usually single inhumations under round barrows, with grave goods differing by sex — weapons and archery equipment with men, and ornaments such as gold hair rings and amber beads with women. The beakers themselves are often interpreted as drinking vessels, possibly for beer or mead, and the culture is associated with some of the earliest gold and copper metallurgy in several regions.

Origins and spread

The earliest Bell Beakers appear in the Tagus estuary region of Portugal around 2900–2800 BC, while a related tradition developed in the Lower Rhine. From these centres the phenomenon spread widely — to Iberia, France, Britain and Ireland, central Europe, and parts of Italy and North Africa. Scholars have long debated whether this spread reflects migration, exchange, or the diffusion of an ideology or "Beaker package" of practices.

Genetic evidence

Ancient DNA studies published from 2015 onward showed that the spread of the Beaker phenomenon into Britain and central Europe was accompanied by a large-scale migration of people carrying steppe-related ancestry, replacing much of the earlier Neolithic population in Britain within a few centuries. In Iberia, by contrast, the earliest Beakers show little steppe ancestry, suggesting that the phenomenon spread there initially through contact rather than migration.
