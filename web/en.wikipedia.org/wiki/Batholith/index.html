A batholith (from Greek bathos, depth + lithos, rock) is a large mass of intrusive igneous rock (also called plutonic rock), larger than 100 square kilometres (40 sq mi) in area, that forms from cooled magma deep in the Earth's crust. Batholiths are almost always made mostly of felsic or intermediate rock types, such as granite, quartz monzonite, or diorite.

Granitic peaks of the Sierra Nevada Batholith, California.

Formation

Although at first sight batholiths may appear uniform, they are in fact structures with complex histories and compositions, typically assembled over tens of millions of years from many separate pulses of magma (plutons) that rose through subduction-zone crust and crystallised at depth. The magma is thought to make room for itself by a combination of stoping, assimilation of country rock, and tectonic displacement. After uplift and erosion remove the overlying rock, the batholith is exposed at the surface, often forming the core of a mountain range.

Examples

Famous batholiths include the Sierra Nevada Batholith of California (which forms Yosemite's granite domes, including Half Dome and El Capitan), the Coast Plutonic Complex of British Columbia and Alaska — among the largest in the world — the Andean batholiths of Peru and Chile, and the Cornubian batholith beneath Cornwall and Dartmoor in England.
