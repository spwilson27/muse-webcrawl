Stealth technology

The Northrop Grumman B-2 Spirit stealth bomber.

Stealth technology, also termed low-observable technology, is a sub-discipline of military tactics and passive and active electronic countermeasures that encompasses a range of methods used to make personnel, aircraft, ships, submarines, missiles, satellites, and ground vehicles less visible — ideally invisible — to radar, infrared, sonar, and other detection methods. It corresponds to military camouflage for these parts of the electromagnetic spectrum (multi-spectral camouflage).

Principles

Radar stealth centers on reducing the radar cross-section (RCS) of a vehicle. Two main approaches are used together:

  • Shaping — designing surfaces so that radar energy is deflected away from the emitter rather than reflected back to it. Flat, angled facets (as on the F-117 Nighthawk) or carefully blended curves (as on the B-2 Spirit) scatter returns in controlled directions; edges are aligned so residual reflections concentrate in a few narrow "spikes."
  • Radar-absorbent material (RAM) — coatings and structures, such as iron-ball paints and ferrite tiles, that absorb radar energy and convert it to heat.

Stealth also addresses other signatures: buried or shielded engines, cooled exhausts, and serrated edges reduce infrared signature; quiet propellers, rafted machinery, and anechoic tiles reduce sonar signature on submarines; and visual camouflage and emissions control complete the low-observable suite.

History

During the Second World War, Germany experimented with radar-absorbent coatings on U-boat snorkels, and the Horten Ho 229 flying wing has often been cited (with debate) as an early low-observable design. The decisive theoretical breakthrough came from Soviet physicist Pyotr Ufimtsev, whose 1964 work on the diffraction of electromagnetic waves provided the mathematics — published openly and translated by the U.S. Air Force — that Lockheed's Skunk Works used, with early computer codes such as ECHO 1, to design the Have Blue demonstrator and the F-117, revealed publicly in 1988. The B-2 Spirit bomber (1989) applied curved, blended shaping enabled by more powerful computation.

Modern fifth-generation fighters such as the F-22 Raptor and F-35 Lightning II integrate shaping, materials, internal weapons carriage, and sensor fusion. Stealth naval vessels — Sweden's Visby-class corvettes and the U.S. Zumwalt-class destroyers — use faceted hulls and composite materials. Low-frequency (VHF) radars, multistatic radar, and infrared search-and-track systems are the principal counter-stealth technologies, as demonstrated when a Yugoslav S-125 battery shot down an F-117 in 1999.
