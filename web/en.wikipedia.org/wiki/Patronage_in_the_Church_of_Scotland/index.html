Patronage in the Church of Scotland

Patronage in the Church of Scotland was the right of a patron — typically a landowner (heritor), the Crown, a burgh or a university — to present a minister to a vacant parish charge. The dispute over lay patronage was one of the most divisive issues in Scottish church history between the Reformation and the 19th century, causing repeated secessions.

History

Patronage existed before the Reformation of 1560. The Church's Second Book of Discipline rejected it, and Parliament abolished it in 1649, restored it at the Restoration in 1662, abolished it again in 1690 after the Revolution, and controversially restored it by the Patronage Act of 1712 under Queen Anne — widely resented in Scotland as a breach of the Union settlement.

Controversy and secessions

Where patrons imposed unpopular ministers on congregations, resistance followed. The First Secession of 1733 under Ebenezer Erskine, the Relief Church of 1761, and above all the Disruption of 1843 — when some 450 ministers left to form the Free Church of Scotland over patronage and state interference — all stemmed substantially from this issue. Patronage in the Church of Scotland was finally abolished by Parliament in 1874, leaving congregations to elect their own ministers.
