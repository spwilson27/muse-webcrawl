Terrine

A terrine (French pronunciation: [tɛʁin]) is a French forcemeat loaf similar to a pâté, made with more coarsely chopped ingredients and cooked in a covered pottery mould — also called a terrine — in a bain-marie. Terrines are usually served cold or at room temperature, sliced thickly, and may be made of meat, game, fish, seafood, or vegetables.

Preparation

A terrine mixture typically combines ground or chopped meat (often pork, veal, or game) with fat, seasonings, and sometimes liver, bound and pressed into a lidded earthenware dish, frequently lined with bacon or caul fat. The dish is cooked slowly in a water bath, then weighted and chilled so that the loaf firms and its flavours meld. Classic examples include terrine de campagne (a rustic country-style pork terrine), game terrines, and layered vegetable terrines.

Terrine and pâté

The word "terrine" originally referred only to the vessel, which gives the dish its name. In modern usage, terrines are distinguished from pâtés by their coarser texture and by being moulded and served from the dish; a pâté is typically smoother and, in the case of pâté en croûte, baked in a pastry crust. Terrines are a staple of charcuterie and of the French hors d'oeuvre course.
