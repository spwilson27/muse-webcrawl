Nakaya diagram

The Nakaya diagram is a chart that relates the morphology (habit) of growing snow crystals to the temperature and water-vapour supersaturation of the environment in which they grow. It is named after the Japanese physicist Ukichiro Nakaya, who in the 1930s became the first person to grow artificial snow crystals in the laboratory and systematically mapped how their shapes depended on growth conditions.

Background

Working at Hokkaido University, Nakaya built a cold chamber in which he could suspend a rabbit hair in cooled, moist air and nucleate ice crystals on it. By varying the temperature and humidity he produced crystals of many different forms and, in 1936, created the first artificial snowflakes. His results were published in his 1954 book Snow Crystals: Natural and Artificial.

The diagram

The diagram plots crystal habit against temperature (roughly 0 °C down to about −40 °C) and supersaturation. Its principal findings are that:

  • Near 0 °C to about −3 °C, crystals grow as thin hexagonal plates.
  • From about −3 °C to −8 °C, they grow as needles and hollow columns.
  • From about −8 °C to −12 °C, plates again predominate.
  • From about −12 °C to −16 °C — most famously near −15 °C at high supersaturation — crystals grow as the elaborate branched stellar dendrites associated with the classic snowflake.
  • At still lower temperatures, columns and small plates return.

At any given temperature, higher supersaturation produces more complex, more branched forms, while lower supersaturation yields simpler, faceted prisms. The alternation of habit with temperature is attributed to temperature-dependent changes in the surface structure and growth rates of the basal and prism faces of ice.

Significance

Nakaya famously called snow crystals "letters sent from heaven", because their shapes encode a record of the temperature and humidity conditions through which they fell. The Nakaya diagram remains a standard reference in cloud physics and atmospheric science, and later researchers — notably Kobayashi, Magono and Lee, and Bailey and Hallett — refined and extended it with more detailed morphology charts.
