Signalling theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The peacock's tail is a classic example of a costly signal of fitness.

Within evolutionary biology, signalling theory is a body of theoretical work examining communication between individuals, both within species and across species. The central question is when organisms with conflicting interests, such as in sexual selection, should be expected to provide honest signals rather than cheating. Mathematical models describe how signalling can be evolutionarily stable.

The theory grew from Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle (1975), which proposed that reliable signals must be costly to the signaller — so costly that a lower-quality individual could not afford to fake them. Alan Grafen later provided formal game-theoretic support, showing that signalling costs can stabilize honesty at equilibrium.

In economics

In economics, signalling theory originated with Michael Spence's job-market signalling model (1973), in which education serves as a signal of a worker's ability because it is less costly for high-ability workers to obtain. Signalling models are used in contract theory, finance (e.g., dividend signalling), and marketing.

Honest signalling and costs

Signals may be indices (unfakable because physically linked to quality), handicaps (honest because of differential costs), or conventional signals (maintained by social enforcement). The type of cost required — wasted at equilibrium, paid only by cheaters, or opportunity costs — has been debated (e.g., Maynard Smith's distinction, and Lachmann, Számadó and Bergstrom's models).

Applications

Examples studied include the peacock's train in mate choice, stotting by gazelles signalling fitness to predators, alarm calls, begging calls of nestling birds, costly religious rituals as signals of group commitment, and conspicuous consumption in human societies.
