The Azusa Street Revival was a historic series of revival meetings that took place in Los Angeles, California, beginning in April 1906. Led by African-American preacher William J. Seymour, the meetings were characterised by speaking in tongues, dramatic worship services, and claims of miraculous healings. The revival is considered the catalyst for the worldwide spread of Pentecostalism in the 20th century.

Background

Seymour had studied under Charles Fox Parham in Houston, Texas, absorbing Parham's doctrine that speaking in tongues was the "Bible evidence" of baptism in the Holy Spirit, though as a Black man he was forced by segregation to listen from outside the classroom door. Invited to preach in Los Angeles in early 1906, Seymour was locked out of the Nazarene church where he was to speak after preaching this doctrine; prayer meetings continued in a home on Bonnie Brae Street, where on 9 April 1906 participants began speaking in tongues.

The meetings

The growing crowds moved to a derelict former African Methodist Episcopal church building at 312 Azusa Street, previously used as a stable and warehouse. Services ran almost continuously, often three times a day, seven days a week, for roughly three years at their peak. Worship was spontaneous, with no fixed programme; participants of all races, classes and ages mixed in an era of strict segregation — a feature that drew both wonder and hostile, often mocking, press coverage. The mission published a newspaper, The Apostolic Faith, which carried news of the revival worldwide free of charge, with circulation reaching tens of thousands.

Legacy

Visitors carried the Pentecostal message across the United States and to dozens of countries, seeding missions and denominations that grew into a global movement numbering hundreds of millions of adherents today. Seymour's influence within organised Pentecostalism later waned amid doctrinal disputes and the reassertion of racial divisions, but historians regard the Azusa Street Revival as the principal point of origin of modern Pentecostalism and a landmark in American religious history.
