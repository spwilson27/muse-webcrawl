Super recogniser

A super recogniser is a person with exceptionally strong face recognition ability — able to remember and identify faces, often from brief or poor-quality encounters, far above the population average. The term was coined in 2009 by Harvard and University College London researchers (Richard Russell, Brad Duchaine, and Ken Nakayama) in a study of prosopagnosia that identified individuals at the opposite, superior end of the face-recognition spectrum. An estimated 1–2% of the population may qualify.

Research

Super recognisers score highly on standardized tests such as the Cambridge Face Memory Test and the Glasgow Face Matching Test, recognize faces seen only briefly years earlier, and remain accurate even with disguised, aged, or low-resolution images. The ability appears partly heritable and distinct from general intelligence or general visual memory. It is considered the mirror image of developmental prosopagnosia (face blindness).

Applications

Police forces have recruited super recognisers to review CCTV footage and identify suspects and persons of interest. London's Metropolitan Police formed a super recogniser unit after officers identified an exceptional number of suspects from images taken during the 2011 England riots; the approach was used in investigations including the Skripal poisoning case in 2018, where officers helped track suspects through hours of surveillance footage. Border agencies and event-security firms also employ super recognisers, though researchers caution that even exceptional performers are not infallible, and their evidence is best combined with other identification methods.
