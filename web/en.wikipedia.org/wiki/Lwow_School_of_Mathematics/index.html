Lwow School of Mathematics

The Lwów School of Mathematics was a group of mathematicians who worked in the interwar period in Lwów, Poland (now Lviv, Ukraine), centred on the University of Lwów and the Lwów Polytechnic. It was one of the schools of the celebrated Polish School of Mathematics.

Members and the Scottish Café

Leading figures included Stefan Banach, Hugo Steinhaus, Stanisław Ulam, Stanisław Mazur, Juliusz Schauder, Mark Kac, and Antoni Łomnicki. The group famously gathered at the Scottish Café (Kawiarnia Szkocka), where they discussed problems for hours; notable problems and their solutions were recorded in the legendary Scottish Book, a notebook kept at the café, with prizes such as a live goose offered for solutions.

Contributions

The school made fundamental contributions to functional analysis (Banach spaces), set theory, topology, measure theory, and probability. Their journal, Studia Mathematica, founded in 1929 by Banach and Steinhaus, became a leading outlet for functional analysis. The school was destroyed by the Second World War: many members were murdered during the German occupation, including in the 1941 massacre of Lwów professors, while others, such as Ulam, emigrated and became prominent abroad.
