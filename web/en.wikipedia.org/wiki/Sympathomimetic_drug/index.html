Sympathomimetic drug

Sympathomimetic drugs (also known as adrenergic drugs) are stimulant compounds that mimic the effects of endogenous agonists of the sympathetic nervous system — the catecholamines epinephrine (adrenaline), norepinephrine (noradrenaline) and dopamine. They act by directly stimulating alpha- and beta-adrenergic receptors, by increasing the release or blocking the reuptake of catecholamines (indirect action), or both.

Uses

Epinephrine is the treatment of choice for anaphylaxis and is used in cardiac arrest and as an adjunct to local anaesthetics. Norepinephrine and phenylephrine are used as vasopressors in shock; dobutamine supports cardiac output in acute heart failure. Beta-2 selective agonists such as salbutamol (albuterol) and salmeterol relax bronchial smooth muscle in asthma and COPD. Pseudoephedrine and oxymetazoline are nasal decongestants. Amphetamines and related agents are used for ADHD and narcolepsy, while many sympathomimetics (cocaine, MDMA, methamphetamine) are drugs of misuse.

Adverse effects

Class effects include hypertension, tachycardia and arrhythmias, tremor, anxiety, insomnia, hyperglycaemia and, in excess, myocardial ischaemia. Interactions with monoamine oxidase inhibitors can precipitate hypertensive crisis.
