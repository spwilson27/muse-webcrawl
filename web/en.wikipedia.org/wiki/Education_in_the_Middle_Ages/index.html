Education in the Middle Ages was dominated in Western Europe by the Christian Church. After the collapse of Roman institutions in the West, monasteries and cathedrals preserved literacy and learning, teaching in Latin; from the 12th century, cathedral schools gave way to the first universities. Most formal education was directed to training clergy, though by the later Middle Ages grammar schools, guild apprenticeships and private tutors served growing lay demand.

Early medieval learning

Benedictine monasteries ran schools for their own novices and sometimes for extern pupils, copying manuscripts in scriptoria that preserved classical and patristic texts. Figures such as Bede in Northumbria and the scholars of the Carolingian Renaissance — promoted by Charlemagne's capitularies on education — raised standards of Latin learning. Girls' formal education was largely confined to convents, and lay literacy remained low.

The seven liberal arts

The standard curriculum inherited from late antiquity comprised the trivium — grammar, rhetoric and logic (dialectic) — followed by the quadrivium: arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy. Teaching centred on set texts and commentary, with disputation (structured debate) as the signature exercise of advanced study, producing the scholastic method.

Universities

From around 1088 (Bologna) and through the 12th and 13th centuries, permanent communities of masters and students — universitates — appeared at Bologna, Paris, Oxford, Cambridge, Salamanca and elsewhere, chartered by popes and rulers. Students began with a Master of Arts degree based on the liberal arts, then could proceed to the higher faculties of theology, law (canon and civil) and medicine. Lectures, disputations and examinations in Latin formed an international intellectual world; translations from Arabic and Greek, often made in Spain and Sicily, transformed the curriculum with Aristotle, Euclid, Galen and Averroes.

Late medieval expansion

The later Middle Ages saw grammar schools multiply (many endowed by guilds and chantries), vernacular literacy spread with paper, printing (from the 1450s) and commerce, and new humanist influences from Italy challenge the scholastic curriculum. Girls and women remained largely excluded from universities, though convent education and exceptional figures such as Christine de Pizan show the picture was not absolute.

Elsewhere

Beyond Latin Christendom, Byzantine education maintained classical Greek learning in state and church schools, and the Islamic world developed mosque schools and madrasas teaching law, theology, medicine and the sciences — institutions whose translations and commentaries fed back into European learning.
