Hart's inversor
InventorHarry Hart (1874)
TypeStraight line mechanism / inversor linkage
BarsSix (first inversor); five moving bars (second)
FunctionConverts rotary motion to exact straight-line motion

Hart's inversor is a mechanical linkage that converts rotary motion into exact straight-line motion without sliding guides. It was invented in 1874 by Harry Hart, who published two forms of the mechanism.

Geometry

The first inversor is based on an antiparallelogram linkage: four bars form a crossed quadrilateral, with two additional bars forming similar triangles whose vertices remain related by geometric inversion in a circle. Because the relevant points are always collinear with the base point and their distances multiply to a constant, the tracing point describes an exact straight line when another point is constrained to a circle. The second inversor, sometimes called Hart's W-frame, achieves the same result with fewer bars and a right-angle arrangement.

History and context

Hart's mechanism was one of several exact straight-line mechanisms discovered in the 19th century, following the earlier Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage of 1864. Unlike approximate mechanisms such as Watt's linkage, inversors produce mathematically exact straight lines, a problem of great interest in the age of steam engines.
