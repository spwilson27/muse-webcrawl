Taurobolium


Altar recording a taurobolium performed for the Magna Mater

In the Roman Empire, a taurobolium was a form of bull sacrifice connected with the worship of the Great Mother of the Gods (Magna Mater, or Cybele) and, in some instances, of Attis. The rite is attested mainly from the 2nd to the 4th centuries AD, and is known from hundreds of inscriptions, most of them from the western provinces of the empire.

The rite

In the most developed descriptions, a bull was slaughtered on a wooden platform or grating placed over a pit. The person undergoing initiation, dressed in special clothing, stood in the pit below and was showered with the bull's blood draining down through holes in the platform. The initiate was believed to emerge "reborn" (renatus) through the rite — the poet Prudentius gives a vivid, though hostile and possibly exaggerated, account of the ceremony in his Peristephanon.

Historians debate how far Prudentius's dramatic blood-bath reflects the usual practice. The much more numerous inscriptions typically record the act more soberly as the receipt of the bull's vires (blood or genitals) in a vessel and the dedication of an altar, and the rite may originally have been a more conventional sacrifice accompanied by consecration of the offering. The full blood-drenching form may have been an elaboration of the later period.

History

The earliest dated taurobolium inscription comes from Puteoli (modern Pozzuoli) in AD 134, where the rite was performed for Venus Caelestis rather than the Magna Mater. From the later 2nd century it became closely associated with the cult of the Magna Mater and Attis. Inscriptions record performances for the health of the emperor and the empire, for cities, and for private individuals, men and women alike. Some dedications claim the recipient was "reborn for eternity" (renatus in aeternum), a phrase which Christian writers found provocative.

The practice continued into the late 4th century, when a series of taurobolium altars was set up in the Phrygianum sanctuary on the Vatican Hill in Rome by pagan aristocrats — the last dated example belonging to AD 390, on the eve of the suppression of public pagan sacrifice under Theodosius I.

Related rites

The criobolium was the corresponding sacrifice of a ram (crios), frequently performed together with or as a cheaper alternative to the taurobolium. Both rites were sometimes repeated after an interval of years.
