Superinsulation

Superinsulation is an approach to building design, construction and retrofitting in which a building is insulated to a far higher level than conventional practice, combined with careful air sealing, so that heat loss is reduced to a minimum. A superinsulated house is intended to be heated predominantly by intrinsic heat sources — waste heat from appliances, body heat, and incidental solar gain — with very small amounts of backup heating.

History

The term was coined by Wayne Schick of the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign, whose team built the "Lo-Cal" house in 1976. The Saskatchewan Conservation House of 1977, funded by the government of Saskatchewan, Canada, demonstrated that a house in a severe cold climate could be heated almost entirely by internal gains. These projects influenced the later German Passivhaus standard developed by Wolfgang Feist and Bo Adamson.

Design features

Typical features include insulation levels of R-40 to R-60 in walls and R-60 or more in ceilings, meticulous airtightness (often below 0.6 air changes per hour at 50 Pa), high-performance windows, and balanced mechanical ventilation with heat recovery (HRV) to supply fresh air without losing the heat retained by the envelope.

Economics

Because the heating load becomes so small, a superinsulated design can eliminate conventional furnaces and ductwork, offsetting part of the extra insulation cost. Critics note that in mild climates the additional investment may have long payback periods.
