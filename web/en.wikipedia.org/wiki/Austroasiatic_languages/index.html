The Austroasiatic languages form a large language family spoken across Southeast Asia and eastern India, with around 168 languages and more than 100 million speakers. The two most widely spoken members are Vietnamese and Khmer (Cambodian), the national languages of Vietnam and Cambodia; other notable languages include Mon, Santali, and the Munda languages of India.

Classification

The family is traditionally divided into two great branches: the Mon–Khmer languages, spread from Vietnam and Cambodia through Thailand, Laos, Malaysia (the Aslian languages) and the Nicobar Islands, and the Munda languages of central and eastern India. Although Munda languages are typologically very different — agglutinative and with complex verb morphology, influenced by neighbouring Indo-Aryan and Dravidian languages — shared vocabulary establishes their genetic relationship with the rest of the family.

Characteristics

Most Mon–Khmer languages are analytic and sesquisyllabic, with a system of vowel "registers" (contrasts of phonation type such as breathy versus clear voice) that in some languages, notably Khmer and Mon, has replaced older consonant distinctions. Vietnamese developed tones under Chinese influence, an unusual feature within the family. Many Austroasiatic languages possess rich infixation — derivational affixes inserted inside roots — a hallmark of the family's morphology.

History

Comparative evidence and archaeology suggest the family's homeland lay in mainland Southeast Asia, with dispersal associated with early rice agriculture along the Mekong and Red River valleys. Khmer left the family's earliest extensive inscriptions, from the seventh century CE, while Mon inscriptions are nearly as old. A proposed "Austric" macrofamily linking Austroasiatic with Austronesian remains controversial.
