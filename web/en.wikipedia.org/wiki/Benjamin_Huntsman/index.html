Benjamin Huntsman

Born4 June 1704, Epworth, Lincolnshire, England
Died20 June 1776, Attercliffe, Sheffield, England
OccupationClockmaker, inventor, steel manufacturer

Benjamin Huntsman (4 June 1704 – 20 June 1776) was an English clockmaker, medical-instrument maker, and inventor who developed the crucible steel process, by which steel of a uniform, high quality could be produced in quantity for the first time. His work laid the foundation for Sheffield's dominance of the world steel and cutlery trades in the 19th century.

Early life and the search for better steel

Huntsman was born in Lincolnshire to German Quaker immigrant parents and set up as a clockmaker in Doncaster. Dissatisfied with the blister steel used for clock springs — its uneven carbon content made springs unreliable — he experimented for many years to find a way of refining steel to a uniform consistency.

The crucible process

Around 1740, having moved to Handsworth near Sheffield, Huntsman perfected his method: pieces of blister steel were packed into closed clay crucibles with a flux and heated in a coke-fired furnace until the steel fully melted. Impurities rose as slag, and the molten steel was cast into ingots of homogeneous composition. The process was kept as a trade secret for years; according to a well-known (if possibly embellished) story, a rival steelmaker named Walker obtained the secret by disguising himself as a beggar seeking warmth by the furnace.

Business and legacy

Sheffield's cutlers initially refused Huntsman's steel as too hard to work, so he exported to France; competition from French cutlery then forced the Sheffield trade to adopt it. Huntsman moved his works to Attercliffe in 1765. He declined election to the Royal Society, consistent with his Quaker principles. Crucible steelmaking remained the basis of Sheffield's quality-steel industry until the age of large-scale electric steelmaking.
