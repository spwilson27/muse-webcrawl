Sapir–Whorf hypothesis

The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, also called linguistic relativity, holds that the structure of a language influences or determines how its speakers perceive and think about the world. It is associated with the American linguists Edward Sapir and his student Benjamin Lee Whorf.

Versions

A "strong" version, linguistic determinism, claims language determines thought and that concepts not encoded in language cannot be thought; this is widely rejected. A "weak" version, that language influences habitual thought, is supported by experiments: for example, studies on colour vocabulary, spatial reference (such as absolute compass-based direction terms in Guugu Yimithirr), and number words.

Background

Whorf developed his ideas from studies of Native American languages, especially Hopi, arguing its grammar framed a different conception of time. Later scholarship questioned some of his Hopi claims, but renewed experimental work from the 1990s onward revived interest in moderate relativist effects. The hypothesis remains a central topic in linguistics, cognitive science and philosophy of language.
