Douglas Elton Fairbanks Sr. (born Douglas Elton Thomas Ullman; May 23, 1883 – December 12, 1939) was an American actor and filmmaker. One of the first and biggest stars of the silent era, he was best known for his swashbuckling adventure films, in which he performed his own athletic stunts with exuberant charm. In 1919 he co-founded the studio United Artists with Charlie Chaplin, D. W. Griffith, and his future wife Mary Pickford, and he was a founding member of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences, hosting the first Academy Awards ceremony in 1929.

Fairbanks defined the swashbuckler genre with films such as The Mark of Zorro and Robin Hood.

Career

Fairbanks began on Broadway before moving to films in 1915, quickly becoming popular in contemporary comedies playing the cheerful, all-American go-getter. His career peaked when he switched to costume adventures: The Mark of Zorro (1920), The Three Musketeers (1921), Robin Hood (1922) — among the most expensive films of its time — The Thief of Bagdad (1924), The Black Pirate (1926), and The Gaucho (1927). His marriage to Mary Pickford, "America's Sweetheart," made them Hollywood's first celebrity power couple; their Beverly Hills estate, Pickfair, hosted the era's most famous parties. The arrival of sound ended his dominance, and his final film was The Private Life of Don Juan (1934).

Legacy

Fairbanks died of a heart attack in 1939. His son, Douglas Fairbanks Jr., also became a leading actor. Fairbanks's acrobatic screen persona directly influenced later swashbucklers from Errol Flynn to the modern action hero.
