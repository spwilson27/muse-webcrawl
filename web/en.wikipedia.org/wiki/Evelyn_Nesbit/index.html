Evelyn Nesbit

Evelyn Nesbit (born Florence Evelyn Nesbit; December 25, 1884 or 1885 – January 17, 1967) was an American artists' model, chorus girl, and actress. She is best known for her years as a young woman in New York City, particularly her involvement in a love triangle that culminated in the murder of her former lover, architect Stanford White, by her husband, millionaire Harry Kendall Thaw, in 1906 — a sensational case dubbed "the Trial of the Century" by the press.

Evelyn Nesbit

Born: December 25, 1884/1885, Tarentum, Pennsylvania, U.S.

Died: January 17, 1967 (aged 82), Santa Monica, California, U.S.

Occupation: Model, actress

Spouses: Harry Kendall Thaw (m. 1905; div. 1915); Jack Clifford (m. 1916; div. 1933)

Early life and career

Nesbit was born near Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. After her father's death left the family impoverished, she moved with her mother and brother to New York City. Her striking beauty led to work as an artists' model from about age fourteen, sitting for leading artists and illustrators including Charles Dana Gibson, whose drawings helped make her a prototype of the "Gibson Girl". She then became a chorus girl on Broadway, appearing in productions such as Florodora and The Wild Rose.

Stanford White and Harry Thaw

As a teenager, Nesbit was drawn into the orbit of Stanford White, the celebrated architect of Madison Square Garden, who became her benefactor and lover. The relationship ended, and in 1905 she married Harry Kendall Thaw, the unstable heir to a Pittsburgh railroad and mining fortune, who was obsessed with White and convinced he had "ruined" Nesbit. On June 25, 1906, during a performance of the musical revue Mam'zelle Champagne at the rooftop theatre of Madison Square Garden, Thaw shot White dead in front of the audience.

The trials

Thaw's trials became a media sensation. Nesbit's testimony about her relationship with White was considered so scandalous that newspapers printed details only in sealed sections. The first trial ended in a hung jury; at the second, in 1908, Thaw was found not guilty by reason of insanity and committed to a mental asylum, from which he was released in 1915. Nesbit divorced him that year.

Later life

Nesbit returned to the stage and vaudeville, appeared in silent films, and later ran tea rooms and taught ceramics. She published two memoirs, The Story of My Life (1914) and Prodigal Days (1934). She had a son, Russell Thaw (later a noted aviator), born in 1910. In her final years she lived in California and worked as a technical adviser on the 1955 film The Girl in the Red Velvet Swing, which dramatised her story. She died in Santa Monica in 1967.

In popular culture

Nesbit is a central character in E. L. Doctorow's novel Ragtime (1975) and its film and musical adaptations, and her life has been the subject of numerous books and biographies.
