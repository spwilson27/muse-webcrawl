Quadrupel

Early modern gold multiples of the ducat, including quadruple-weight pieces.

A quadrupel (German, from Latin quadruplus, "fourfold") in numismatics is a coin struck at four times the weight — and usually four times the face value — of a standard denomination. Quadrupels were produced in a number of early modern European monetary systems, most often as multiples of the gold ducat, gulden, or pistole, and occasionally of silver denominations such as the thaler.

Usage

Multiple-weight coins were not ordinarily struck for everyday circulation. Like the related doppel (double) and portugalöser pieces, quadrupels served as presentation pieces, rewards, and stores of bullion value for wealthy merchants and princes. German states, the Austrian hereditary lands, and the free imperial cities issued gold quadrupels bearing the same types as their single-weight counterparts, and the term appears in mint ordinances and coin catalogues from the 16th to the 18th century. Spanish and Spanish-American mints likewise struck gold pieces of four escudos (the doblón), the functional equivalent of a quadrupel.

Terminology

In older catalogues, multiples are described systematically: a coin of double weight is a Doppel- piece, of triple weight a Trippel or triple, of fourfold weight a Quadrupel, and of fivefold a Quintupel. The terms are German because German numismatic scholarship classified the many large presentation strikings of the Holy Roman Empire.
