History of Poland

The history of Poland begins with the Piast dynasty in the 10th century and encompasses the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, once among Europe's largest states, the partitions that erased Poland from the map for 123 years, independence in 1918, devastation in the Second World War, communist rule, and the Solidarity movement that helped end the Cold War.

Piast and Jagiellonian Poland

Mieszko I, ruler of the Polans, adopted Christianity in 966; his son Bolesław the Brave became the first crowned king in 1025. After a period of fragmentation, Casimir the Great (r. 1333–1370) reunified and reformed the kingdom. The marriage of Queen Jadwiga to the Lithuanian grand duke Jogaila (1386) began a dynastic union; the combined forces defeated the Teutonic Order at Grunwald in 1410. The Union of Lublin (1569) created the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, a vast, religiously tolerant elective monarchy stretching from the Baltic toward the Black Sea — the age of Copernicus and a Polish golden age.

Decline and partitions

Weakened by wars, the liberum veto, and magnate oligarchy, the Commonwealth was partitioned by Prussia, Russia, and Austria in 1772, 1793, and 1795, despite reform efforts including the Constitution of 3 May 1791 — Europe's first modern written constitution. Poles rose repeatedly in uprisings (1794, 1830, 1863) and fought in Napoleon's armies.

Twentieth century

Poland regained independence in November 1918 under Józef Piłsudski and defended it against Soviet Russia in 1920. Invaded by Germany and the USSR in September 1939, Poland suffered catastrophic losses in the Second World War, including the murder of some three million Polish Jews in the Holocaust and the destruction of Warsaw after the 1944 uprising. Post-1945 Poland became a Soviet satellite, its borders shifted westward. The Solidarity trade union, founded in 1980 under Lech Wałęsa, forced semi-free elections in June 1989, precipitating the fall of communism across Eastern Europe. Poland joined NATO in 1999 and the European Union in 2004.
