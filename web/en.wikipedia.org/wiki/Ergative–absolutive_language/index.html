Ergative–absolutive language

Morphosyntactic alignment: in ergative languages the subject of an intransitive verb patterns with the object of a transitive verb.

An ergative–absolutive language, also called an ergative language, is a language in which the subject of an intransitive verb has the same grammatical marker as the direct object of a transitive verb, and a different marker from the subject of a transitive verb. This case-marking pattern is known as ergative–absolutive alignment.

The shared case is the absolutive case, while the case marking the transitive subject is the ergative case. This contrasts with nominative–accusative alignment, found in English and most European languages, where the intransitive subject and transitive subject share the nominative case and the transitive object takes the accusative.

Distribution

Ergative–absolutive alignment is found in languages on every inhabited continent. Well-known examples include Basque, Georgian and other Kartvelian languages, many Mayan languages, Inuit languages, and a large number of Australian Aboriginal languages such as Dyirbal and Warlpiri. Ergativity is especially common in the Caucasus, the Americas, and Australia.

Examples

In Basque, the intransitive subject and the transitive object take the absolutive case (unmarked), while the transitive subject takes the ergative suffix -k: Gizona etorri da ("The man has arrived") versus Gizonak mutila ikusi du ("The man saw the boy"), where gizonak carries the ergative marker.

Split ergativity

Many languages show split ergativity, exhibiting ergative alignment in some grammatical contexts and nominative–accusative alignment in others. The split may depend on tense or aspect (as in Hindi-Urdu and Georgian), on the person of the arguments (as in Dyirbal), or on the type of clause.
