Vacuum fluorescent display

A vacuum fluorescent display (VFD) is a display device once commonly used on consumer electronics equipment such as video cassette recorders, car radios and microwave ovens. A VFD operates on the principle of cathodoluminescence, roughly similar to a cathode ray tube but at much lower voltages. Unlike liquid crystal displays, a VFD emits a bright light with high contrast, and can support display elements of various colours.

Construction and operation

The device consists of a hot cathode (filaments), anodes (phosphor) and grids encased in a glass envelope under high vacuum. The cathode is an array of fine tungsten wires coated with alkaline earth metal oxides, heated so that they emit electrons. Control grids, biased positively or negatively, allow or repel the electron flow toward phosphor-coated anodes; where electrons strike the phosphor it fluoresces, typically in a blue-green colour. Unlike a typical vacuum tube, the cathode is not clearly visible.

Characteristics

VFDs offer wide viewing angles, high brightness visible in daylight, and operation over a broad temperature range, which made them popular in automotive dashboards and audio equipment. They are manufactured both as simple seven-segment numeric displays and as complex dot-matrix panels capable of rendering text and graphics. Their disadvantages include higher power consumption than an LCD, a bulky glass envelope, and gradual phosphor wear causing uneven brightness over time.

History and decline

Developed in Japan in the 1960s, with Ise Electronics (later Noritake Itron) a pioneer, VFDs became ubiquitous in calculators, cash registers, hi-fi equipment and appliances through the 1980s and 1990s. From the 2000s they were progressively displaced by cheaper LCD and OLED displays, though they remain in niche use where brightness and viewing angle matter.
