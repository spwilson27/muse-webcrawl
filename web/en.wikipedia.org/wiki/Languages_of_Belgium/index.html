The Kingdom of Belgium has three official languages: Dutch, French and German. A number of non-official, minority languages and dialects are spoken as well. Belgium's linguistic geography is organised into four language areas fixed by law in 1962–63: the Dutch-language area in the north (Flanders), the French-language area in the south (Wallonia), the small German-language area in the east, and the bilingual Brussels-Capital area.

Roughly 59 per cent of Belgians speak Dutch — in its Belgian varieties commonly called Flemish — as their first language, about 40 per cent speak French, and less than 1 per cent speak German, chiefly in the East Cantons around Eupen and Sankt Vith. Brussels is officially bilingual in Dutch and French, though predominantly French-speaking in practice.

History

At Belgian independence in 1830 French was the language of government and the elite even in much of Flanders; the Flemish movement campaigned through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries for equality of Dutch, achieved in law progressively between 1873 and 1932. The linguistic border was fixed in 1962, and successive state reforms created the language-based Communities (Flemish, French and German-speaking) alongside the Regions.

Politics

Language is a central axis of Belgian politics: there are no nationwide political parties (each splits into Dutch- and French-speaking counterparts), public services and education are organised by language community, and disputes over facilities for speakers in border municipalities, and over Brussels's periphery, remain recurring political issues.

Other languages

Traditional regional languages include Walloon, Picard, Champenois and Lorrain in the Romance south, and Limburgish, Brabantian and West Flemish dialects in the north, alongside Luxembourgish in the Arelerland. Immigrant languages such as Italian, Arabic, Turkish and English are widely spoken in cities, and English is common as a neutral second language, especially in Brussels.
