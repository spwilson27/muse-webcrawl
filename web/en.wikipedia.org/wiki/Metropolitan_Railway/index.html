Metropolitan Railway

The Metropolitan Railway (also known as the Met) was a passenger and freight railway that served London from 1863 to 1933, initially between Paddington and Farringdon and later extending to the city centre, eastern London, and deep into the surrounding countryside. On 10 January 1863 it opened the world's first underground railway. Its extensions into Middlesex, Hertfordshire, and Buckinghamshire created the suburbs later celebrated as Metro-land.

History

Promoted to link the City with the main-line termini at Paddington, Euston, and King's Cross, the Metropolitan was built mainly by cut-and-cover tunnelling and worked by steam locomotives specially designed to condense exhaust steam underground. With the District Railway it completed the Circle in 1884. Under its long-serving manager and later chairman Robert Selbie, the line pushed out to Harrow, Uxbridge, Watford, Rickmansworth, Chesham, and as far as Aylesbury and Verney Junction — over 50 miles from Baker Street — and in 1919 began developing housing estates through its Country Estates company.

Electrification and legacy

The inner lines were electrified in 1905–06, though steam haulage survived on outer sections until 1961. In 1933 the company was absorbed into the London Passenger Transport Board; its lines now form the Metropolitan line and parts of the Circle, Hammersmith & City, Piccadilly, and Bakerloo lines, while the route beyond Amersham is served by Chiltern Railways.
