Palais Garnier

The Palais Garnier (pronounced [palɛ ɡaʁnje]) is a historic opera house in the 9th arrondissement of Paris, France, with a seating capacity of about 1,979. Built for the Paris Opera on the orders of Emperor Napoleon III as part of the Haussmann renovation of Paris, it was designed by architect Charles Garnier and constructed between 1861 and 1875. It is a masterpiece of the Napoleon III / Beaux-Arts style, famous for its lavish marble Grand Foyer, horseshoe auditorium, and the underground lake in its cellars that helped inspire Gaston Leroux's novel The Phantom of the Opera.

History

Garnier, then a little-known architect, won the design competition of 1861. Construction was dogged by difficulties — an underground water table that could not be drained (engineers instead built a cistern, the famous "lake"), the interruption of the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune, and the fall of the Second Empire. The building was finally inaugurated on 5 January 1875. Since the opening of the Opéra Bastille in 1989, the Palais Garnier has been used principally for ballet.

Features

The auditorium is decorated in red and gold with a huge bronze-and-crystal chandelier weighing more than seven tons. In 1964 a new ceiling painted by Marc Chagall was installed over the original. The Grand Foyer, modelled on the gallery of a Renaissance palace, is faced with mirrors and gold leaf. Beneath the stage, the cellar cistern — still used by Paris firefighters for training — and the legend of the "Phantom", supposedly living by the lake, were famously woven into Leroux's 1910 novel and its musical adaptations.
