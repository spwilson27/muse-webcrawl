Milo of Croton (Greek: Μίλων, Mílōn) was a 6th-century BC Greek wrestler from the Magna Graecian city of Croton in southern Italy. He is among the most famous athletes of antiquity, celebrated for his immense strength and for an unmatched record of victories in wrestling at the Olympic and Pythian Games.

Athletic career

According to ancient sources such as Pausanias and Strabo, Milo won the boys' wrestling title at Olympia in his youth and went on to win the men's wrestling competition at five consecutive Olympic Games, beginning in 536 BC. He also won numerous crowns at the Pythian, Isthmian and Nemean Games. His career spanned some three decades, and he was finally defeated, tradition says, only when age had slowed him.

Legends

A host of legends grew around Milo's strength. He was said to have developed his power by lifting and carrying a newborn calf every day until it was a full-grown bull, which he could still shoulder — an early illustration of progressive resistance training. He allegedly carried a four-year-old heifer around the stadium at Olympia and then ate the whole animal in a single day. Another tale says he saved the life of Pythagoras when a roof collapsed, holding up a pillar until the philosopher escaped. His death was equally legendary: attempting to split a tree stump with his bare hands, his fingers became trapped, and he was devoured by wolves.

Legacy

Milo remained a byword for strength throughout antiquity and into the Renaissance, when he was a popular subject for sculpture. The story of the calf is still retold in modern strength training as an origin of the principle of progressive overload.
