Ship model basin

A ship model basin (also called a towing tank) is a large tank of water used in naval architecture to perform hydrodynamic tests on scale models of ships and offshore structures. By towing a model through the water, or by generating waves around it, engineers measure resistance, propulsion performance, seakeeping, and manoeuvring characteristics, and extrapolate the results to the full-size vessel.

Facilities

A conventional towing tank is a long rectangular basin — the largest exceed several hundred metres — spanned by a moving carriage that tows the model at precise speeds while instruments record forces and motions. Wave basins add wavemakers along one side to reproduce sea states for seakeeping tests. Some facilities have a rotating arm for manoeuvring tests, cavitation tunnels for studying propellers, and ice tanks, with refrigerated basins that can freeze a model ice sheet, for polar vessels. Scale models are typically several metres long, built of wood, fibreglass, or foam, and ballasted to the correct displacement and trim.

Scaling

Tests are run at Froude number similarity so that wave-making behaviour matches the full-scale ship; viscous (skin friction) resistance is corrected analytically using the methods standardised by the International Towing Tank Conference (ITTC), founded in 1933. Model basins date to the pioneering tanks of William Froude in the 1870s and remain essential even in the era of computational fluid dynamics, which they are used to validate.

Notable basins

Well-known facilities include MARIN in the Netherlands, the David Taylor Model Basin in the United States, the Krylov Institute in Russia, HSVA in Hamburg, and the basins of the former British Ship Research Association.
