Wenzhou model


Wenzhou became famous for small, family-run private enterprises.

The Wenzhou model (Chinese: 温州模式) is a pattern of economic development named after the coastal city of Wenzhou in Zhejiang Province, China. It is characterised by bottom-up growth driven by private, household-based and small-scale enterprises, in contrast to development led by state-owned industry or foreign investment. Wenzhou entrepreneurs became famous throughout China for producing low-cost light industrial goods — such as shoes, clothing, lighters, and spectacles — through dense networks of family workshops and specialised markets.

Origins

Wenzhou had little arable land and few state investments during the planned-economy era, and its people had an older tradition of commerce and handicrafts. When China's reform and opening began after 1978, local households rapidly set up workshops, often financed through informal lending networks and rotating credit associations rather than state banks. Local officials frequently tolerated or quietly protected private activity that was still ideologically sensitive.

Influence and criticism

The model was widely studied and praised in the 1980s as evidence that private enterprise could drive growth, and "Wenzhou people" gained a reputation across China and abroad as astute merchants. Critics noted weaknesses including counterfeit and low-quality goods in the early years, reliance on informal finance — exposed when a private lending crisis hit the city in 2011 — and difficulties in moving up the value chain. Nonetheless the Wenzhou model remains a standard reference point in discussions of China's private sector.
