Downspout
Also calledDownpipe, drainpipe, leader, conductor
FunctionCarries rainwater from roof gutters to the ground
MaterialsAluminium, galvanised steel, copper, PVC, zinc

A downspout, also called a downpipe, drainpipe or leader, is a vertical pipe that carries rainwater from a building's gutters down to ground level, where it discharges into a drain, a soakaway, a rain barrel or simply onto a splash block. Downspouts protect walls, foundations and landscaping from uncontrolled runoff from the roof.

Design and components

The top of the downspout connects to the gutter outlet, often through angled elbows that bring the pipe from the eaves back against the wall. Straps or brackets fix the pipe to the building. At the bottom, an elbow or shoe directs water away from the foundation, and extensions or buried drain lines may carry it further. Common profiles are rectangular (box) and round, sized — typically 50–100 mm — to the roof area drained. Section lengths are joined with crimped or soldered seams.

Materials and variants

Historically made of wood, cast iron, lead and galvanised steel, modern downspouts are usually aluminium, copper, zinc or uPVC. In some modern and rainscreen designs, rain chains — popular in Japan as kusari-doi — replace enclosed pipes, guiding water down a chain by surface tension. Decorative leader heads (conductor heads) collect water at the top of the pipe and are a feature of traditional and classical architecture.

Maintenance

Downspouts can block with leaves and debris, freeze in cold climates, or detach at joints; gutter guards, strainers and periodic flushing are standard remedies. Poorly functioning downspouts are a common cause of damp walls and basement water ingress.
