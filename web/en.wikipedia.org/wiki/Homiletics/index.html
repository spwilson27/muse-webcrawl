Homiletics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homiletics is the study and art of preaching — the composition and delivery of sermons and religious discourse. Derived from the Greek homilia (ὁμιλία), meaning a conversation or discourse, the term applies in theology to the branch of practical theology concerned with how religious texts, especially Scripture, are proclaimed and applied to hearers.

History

Christian preaching begins with the sermons of Jesus and the apostles recorded in the New Testament and developed in the patristic era, when figures such as John Chrysostom and Augustine of Hippo reflected on preaching; Augustine's De doctrina christiana (Book IV) is an early manual of the preacher's art. Medieval preaching produced collections of model sermons and artes praedicandi ("arts of preaching"). The Reformation placed preaching at the centre of worship, and the discipline of homiletics took recognisably modern form in the 19th century with works such as John Albert Broadus's On the Preparation and Delivery of Sermons (1870).

Approaches

Homiletics draws on biblical exegesis, rhetoric and communication theory. Major approaches include expository preaching, which works through a text verse by verse; topical preaching, organised around a subject; narrative preaching, developed in the later 20th century, which shapes the sermon as a story; and the "New Homiletic", an inductive approach that seeks to recreate for the hearers the experience of the text. Homiletics is a standard subject in seminary curricula and in the formation of ministers, priests and rabbis, where comparable traditions of sermon study exist in Judaism and Islam (the khutbah).
