Brian Mulroney

Martin Brian Mulroney (March 20, 1939 – February 29, 2024) was a Canadian lawyer, businessman, and politician who served as the 18th prime minister of Canada from 1984 to 1993. Leading the Progressive Conservative Party, he won landslide majorities in 1984 and 1988, concluded the Canada–United States Free Trade Agreement and negotiated NAFTA, introduced the Goods and Services Tax, and attempted constitutional reform through the Meech Lake and Charlottetown accords.

Early life

Mulroney was born in Baie-Comeau, Quebec, to Irish Catholic parents; his father was an electrician at a paper mill. He studied at St. Francis Xavier University and law at Université Laval, becoming a labour lawyer in Montreal and a corporate executive, notably president of the Iron Ore Company of Canada. Fully bilingual and well connected in business and politics, he sought the Progressive Conservative leadership in 1976 (losing to Joe Clark) and won it in 1983.

Prime ministership

The 1984 election gave Mulroney the largest majority in Canadian history. His government privatized Crown corporations, deregulated sectors of the economy, and pursued free trade with the United States — the defining issue of the 1988 election, which he won campaigning in favour of the FTA. He played a prominent international role in opposing apartheid in South Africa, in German reunification diplomacy, and in the 1991 Gulf War coalition. Environmental achievements included the Canada–U.S. Acid Rain Treaty (1991). Domestically, failure of the Meech Lake Accord (1990) and rejection of the Charlottetown Accord in a 1992 referendum, along with a recession and the unpopular GST, collapsed his party's support; he resigned in 1993 and the Progressive Conservatives were reduced to two seats that year.

Later life

Mulroney returned to business and law, served on corporate boards, and advised later prime ministers. He received a state funeral in March 2024. Assessments of his tenure emphasize the durable free-trade reorientation of the Canadian economy alongside the constitutional failures that reshaped Canadian politics.
