Hellenistic Judaism

Hellenistic Judaism was a form of Judaism in classical antiquity that combined Jewish religious tradition with elements of Hellenistic (Greek) culture. It flourished from the conquests of Alexander the Great in the late 4th century BCE until the Roman period, especially among the Jewish diaspora in Alexandria, Egypt. Its best-known monument is the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible.

The Septuagint made the Jewish scriptures accessible to the Greek-speaking world.

Background

After Alexander's conquests, Greek language, cities and institutions spread across the eastern Mediterranean and Near East. Large Jewish communities, notably in Alexandria — where Jews may have formed a substantial fraction of the population — adopted Greek as their everyday tongue while retaining Jewish observance to varying degrees. In Judea itself, Hellenisation produced tensions between reforming "Hellenizers" and traditionalists, erupting under the Seleucid king Antiochus IV in the Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BCE), whose victory is commemorated by Hanukkah.

Literature and thought

Hellenistic Jewish literature, much of it preserved only through Christian transmission, includes the Septuagint, the deuterocanonical books (such as Wisdom of Solomon and 2 Maccabees), the works of the historian Josephus, the tragedian Ezekiel, and writings of Aristobulus and others. Its culminating figure is Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE), who interpreted the Torah allegorically through Platonic and Stoic philosophy, elaborating the doctrine of the Logos and profoundly influencing later Jewish, Christian and Islamic thought.

Decline

Hellenistic Judaism waned after the Jewish–Roman wars (66–135 CE) devastated the diaspora communities of Egypt, Cyrenaica and Judea. Greek-speaking Judaism was largely absorbed into early Christianity, which adopted the Septuagint as its Old Testament, while rabbinic Judaism, centred on Hebrew and Aramaic texts, became the normative form of the religion.
