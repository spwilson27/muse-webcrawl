Maple sap

Maple sap is the clear, watery fluid that circulates in the xylem of maple trees (Acer species) and certain other trees, and which is harvested in late winter and early spring to produce maple syrup and related products. The sap of the sugar maple (Acer saccharum) typically contains about 2–3 per cent sugar, mostly sucrose, and tastes faintly sweet.

Flow and collection

Sap flows when freezing nights are followed by thawing days: freeze–thaw cycles create pressure changes in the tree that push sap upward from the roots. A tap hole is drilled into the trunk and fitted with a spile (tap), through which sap drips into a bucket or flows through plastic tubing to a collection tank, often under vacuum in modern operations. The collection season, sometimes called "sugaring season", lasts roughly four to eight weeks depending on climate.

Processing

To make syrup, the sugar shack or sugar house boils the sap down until it reaches the required sugar concentration of about 66–67 per cent; roughly 40 litres of sap yield one litre of syrup. Reverse osmosis is often used first to remove much of the water and save energy. Fresh sap spoils quickly and is sometimes consumed directly as a beverage, as in the Korean drink made from the sap of the painted maple (gorosoe).
