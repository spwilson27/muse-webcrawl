Second Epistle of John

2 John in an early manuscript tradition

The Second Epistle of John, often called Second John and abbreviated 2 John, is a book of the New Testament attributed to John the Apostle — or, in scholarly discussion, to "John the Elder" — and closely related to the First and Third Epistles of John and the Johannine community. With only thirteen verses, it is the second-shortest book in the Bible, written apparently to fit on a single sheet of papyrus.

Content

The letter opens with the author identifying himself simply as "the elder" (ho presbyteros), writing "to the elect lady and her children" — a phrase most interpreters understand as a church and its members, though a literal addressee has also been proposed. The elder rejoices that some of her "children" are "walking in the truth" and repeats the Johannine commandment to love one another.

The letter's main purpose is a warning: "many deceivers have gone out into the world, those who do not confess the coming of Jesus Christ in the flesh; such a one is the deceiver and the antichrist." Readers are told not to receive into their homes or even greet teachers who do not bring the teaching of Christ (verses 7–11) — a passage generally understood in its context of refusing hospitality and patronage to travelling false teachers. The letter closes with the writer's hope to visit in person, since he has "much to write" but does not wish to use "paper and ink".

Authorship and date

Ancient tradition attributed the letter to the Apostle John. Its language and themes are extremely close to 1 John, and modern scholarship usually assigns it to the same author or circle, in or near Ephesus, around the end of the first century AD. Debates about its authorship in antiquity — noted by Eusebius, who placed it among the disputed books — centred on whether "the elder" was the apostle or a distinct John the Elder. It was eventually received into the canon together with the other Johannine letters.

Themes

Despite its brevity the letter unites the characteristic Johannine concerns of truth, love, obedience to the commandments, and the confession of Jesus Christ come in the flesh, alongside sober boundaries against teaching judged to deny that confession.
