The sulfate ion (in IUPAC nomenclature sulphate) is a polyatomic anion with the formula SO42−. It consists of a central sulfur atom surrounded by four equivalent oxygen atoms in a tetrahedral arrangement, in which the sulfur is in oxidation state +6. Salts, acid derivatives (such as hydrogen sulfate, HSO4−) and esters containing this group are all called sulfates; they are industrially and biologically important and are common minerals.

Occurrence and preparation

Sulfates occur naturally in evaporite minerals such as gypsum (calcium sulfate dihydrate), anhydrite, barite (barium sulfate) and epsomite (magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, Epsom salts). In the laboratory and industry, sulfates are formed by dissolving metal oxides, hydroxides or carbonates in sulfuric acid, or by oxidising sulfides and sulfur. Sulfate is abundant in seawater and is a common constituent of natural waters.

Uses

Calcium sulfate is used as plaster, cement and a soil conditioner; copper(II) sulfate as a fungicide and algicide; magnesium sulfate in medicine and agriculture; barium sulfate, insoluble and opaque to X-rays, as a radiocontrast agent and in paints and drilling muds; and sodium lauryl sulfate as a detergent surfactant. In analytical chemistry, sulfate is determined gravimetrically by precipitation as barium sulfate. In biology, bacteria in anaerobic sediments reduce sulfate to hydrogen sulfide, a key step in the sulfur cycle, and organisms activate sulfate to PAPS for sulfation reactions.
