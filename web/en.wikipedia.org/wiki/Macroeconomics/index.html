Macroeconomics

[Image: a skyline of a national capital with its central bank headquarters, representing the whole economy]

Macroeconomics is the branch of Economics that studies an economy as a whole: its output and growth, Inflation, Unemployment, the exchange rate and government budgets, and the policies — monetary and fiscal policy — used to stabilise it. It emerged as a distinct field from John Maynard Keynes's General Theory (1936), written in the shadow of the Great Depression.

Questions and models

Macroeconomists ask why economies fluctuate in business cycles, what determines long-run growth (capital accumulation, technology, institutions — formalised in the Solow growth model), and how policy trades off goals: the Phillips curve, for example, described a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment that broke down in the 1970s stagflation. Aggregate demand and aggregate supply, the IS–LM model and later dynamic general-equilibrium models organise the analysis, while national-income accounting supplies the data.

Schools and debates

The field is famously divided: Keynesian economics stresses demand management; Monetarism, associated with Milton Friedman, stressed money supply and expectations; new classical and real-business-cycle schools emphasised rational expectations and supply shocks; new Keynesian models combine market imperfections with optimising behaviour and underlie most modern central-bank practice. The 2008 Great Recession and the COVID-19 shock revived debate over fiscal activism, debt sustainability (see national debt) and the limits of monetary policy at near-zero interest rates.
