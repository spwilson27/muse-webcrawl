The Proterozoic (IPA: /ˌproʊtərəˈzoʊɪk, ˌprɒt-, -əroʊ-/, from Greek meaning "earlier life") is the third of the four geologic eons of Earth's history, spanning the time interval from 2,500 to 538.8 million years ago, the longest eon of the geologic time scale. It is preceded by the Archean and followed by the Phanerozoic.

Major events

Early in the Proterozoic, photosynthesis by cyanobacteria filled the atmosphere with oxygen in the Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 billion years ago), which also triggered the Huronian glaciation, possibly a "Snowball Earth". Banded iron formations were deposited as dissolved iron in the oceans oxidized. The first eukaryotic cells appeared, having acquired mitochondria (and later chloroplasts) by endosymbiosis, and sexual reproduction evolved during this eon.

The Mesoproterozoic saw the assembly of the supercontinent Rodinia and the evolution of multicellular algae. Stromatolites, which had been abundant and diverse for over a billion years, declined sharply in the late Proterozoic, probably because of grazing by early animals.

Snowball Earth and the Ediacaran

The late Neoproterozoic Cryogenian Period included at least two severe global glaciations — the Sturtian and Marinoan — during which ice may have reached the equator. After the ice melted, the Ediacaran Period (635–538.8 Ma) saw the appearance of the Ediacaran biota: large, soft-bodied multicellular organisms such as Dickinsonia and Charnia, the earliest widely accepted evidence of complex macroscopic life, setting the stage for the Cambrian explosion.

The rock record

Relatively well-preserved Proterozoic sedimentary basins reveal cycles of supercontinent assembly and breakup (Columbia/Nuna, then Rodinia, then Gondwana). Because Proterozoic fossils are mostly microscopic or soft-bodied, the eon was formerly lumped with the Archean as the "Precambrian".
