The Papuan languages are the indigenous languages of the island of New Guinea and neighbouring islands in the western Pacific that do not belong to the Austronesian family. "Papuan" is a negative, geographic label rather than a genealogical one: the term groups together dozens of separate language families and isolates that share a region, not a provable common ancestor. With more than 800 languages, New Guinea is the most linguistically diverse region on Earth.

Distribution and diversity

Papuan languages are spoken across Papua New Guinea, the Indonesian provinces of Papua and West Papua, and in pockets of the Solomon Islands archipelago, Timor and Halmahera. Most have small speech communities — many under a few thousand speakers — reflecting the region's mountainous terrain and long history of small-scale, autonomous social groups. Some, such as Enga, are spoken by over 200,000 people. The lingua franca Tok Pisin, an English-based creole, increasingly dominates public life in Papua New Guinea.

Classification

Twentieth-century scholars, notably Stephen Wurm, proposed vast phyla uniting most Papuan languages, but much of that scheme has not withstood scrutiny. The most widely accepted large grouping is the Trans–New Guinea family, which may cover several hundred languages across the island's highlands and south. Other recognised families include Sepik, Torricelli, Ramu–Lower Sepik, East Bird's Head–Sentani, and the Lakes Plain languages, alongside numerous isolates such as Abinomn and Busa. William Foley's and Malcolm Ross's work has been central to modern classification, often using pronoun systems as diagnostic evidence.

Typological features

Papuan languages are famous among linguists for complex verb morphology, switch-reference systems that track whether consecutive clauses share a subject, clause-chaining with medial verbs, elaborate kinship terminology and, in some areas, unusual features such as very small consonant inventories or extensive noun classification. Many are head-marking and verb-final (SOV).

Endangerment

Although the absolute number of languages is high, many are losing speakers to Tok Pisin, Indonesian and English, especially in towns. Documentation efforts by universities and organisations continue, but a large fraction of Papuan languages remain only sketchily described.
