Enrico Dandolo (c. 1107 – 1205) was the 41st Doge of Venice, reigning from 1192 until his death. Elected in his mid-eighties and blind, he is best known for his central role in the Fourth Crusade, which he steered toward the Venetian assault on Zara in 1202 and the capture and sack of Constantinople in 1204 — events that laid the foundation of Venice's overseas empire.

Early life and career

Dandolo belonged to a prominent Venetian family; his father Vitale Dandolo had been a ducal adviser, and his uncle, also Enrico, was Patriarch of Grado. He first appears in the record in the 1170s during a diplomatic mission to Constantinople, in the aftermath of Emperor Manuel I Komnenos's mass arrest of Venetians in 1171. Tradition held that Dandolo's blindness resulted from an injury or a Byzantine attempt to blind him during these years; modern scholars suggest a cortical visual impairment from a blow to the back of the head is more likely. Despite his blindness he was renowned for his energy, memory, and political skill, and served as ambassador to Constantinople and Ferrara before his election as doge on 21 June 1192.

The Fourth Crusade

In 1201 crusade envoys contracted Venice to transport a large army to the Levant; when far fewer crusaders arrived than expected and could not pay the full fee, Dandolo proposed deferring payment in exchange for help capturing the rebellious port of Zara. Zara fell in November 1202 despite papal prohibition. The crusade then accepted the offer of the exiled Byzantine prince Alexios IV to pay its debts in return for restoring him in Constantinople. After Alexios IV was overthrown and murdered, the crusaders and Venetians stormed Constantinople on 13 April 1204 and subjected it to a notorious three-day sack. Dandolo negotiated Venice's share of the spoils — three-eighths of the city and empire under the Partitio Romaniae — securing Crete and key ports for Venice.

Death and legacy

Dandolo died in Constantinople in 1205, reportedly in his late nineties, and was buried in Hagia Sophia — the only person so honoured there. His tomb slab survives in the gallery of the building. Historians remain divided between admiration for his statecraft and condemnation of his role in the diversion and destruction of the crusade.
