Direct debit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A direct debit or direct withdrawal is a payment transaction in which the payee (the recipient) collects funds directly from the payer's bank account. The payer gives the payee a mandate (authorisation) permitting collections of varying amounts at regular or irregular intervals; the payee then instructs its bank to claim each payment, and the payer's bank debits the account accordingly. Direct debit is used for recurring payments such as utility bills, subscriptions, insurance premiums, and loan repayments.

How it works

Direct debit differs from a standing order, which is set up and controlled by the payer, and from a direct deposit, in which funds are pushed into an account. Because the payee initiates collections, direct debit schemes include consumer protections: in the United Kingdom, the Direct Debit Guarantee offers an immediate refund from the payer's bank for payments taken in error or without advance notice; the SEPA direct debit schemes used in the euro area provide refund rights of eight weeks for authorised collections.

Schemes by region

  • United Kingdom and Ireland: Direct Debit, operated by Pay.UK (formerly Bacs).
  • Euro area: SEPA Core and SEPA B2B direct debit schemes, usable across all SEPA countries with a single mandate.
  • Australia: the Direct Entry system, authorised by a Direct Debit Request.
  • United States: recurring ACH debits through the Nacha network fill a similar role, though they differ in rules and consumer practice.
  • Canada: pre-authorized debits (PADs) under Payments Canada rules.

Advantages and risks

For businesses, direct debit reduces late payments and administrative costs, and payments cannot "bounce" silently like cheques. For payers, payments are automatic, but authorising a third party to withdraw variable amounts requires trust in the scheme's guarantee and in the payee's processes; mandates can be cancelled at any time through the payer's bank.
