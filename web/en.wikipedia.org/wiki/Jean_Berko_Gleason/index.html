Jean Berko Gleason (born 1931) is an American psycholinguist and professor emerita at Boston University, best known for the wug test, an experiment she devised as a doctoral student demonstrating that young children learn productive rules of grammar rather than merely imitating forms they have heard. Her research and textbooks helped define the modern scientific study of language acquisition.

The wug test

In her 1958 study "The Child's Learning of English Morphology," Berko presented preschool and first-grade children with pictures of nonsense creatures and objects — most famously a small bird-like animal called a "wug" — and prompted them to complete sentences such as "This is a wug. Now there is another one. There are two of them. There are two ___." Children supplied "wugs," applying the plural rule to a word they had never heard, and did the same for past-tense and possessive forms. The experiment provided elegant evidence against purely imitative accounts of learning and for the idea that children internalize abstract morphological rules, a conclusion that became a touchstone in debates over innateness and learning in language.

Career and other work

Gleason earned her doctorate at Harvard, studying with Roger Brown, and spent most of her career at Boston University, where she directed the Psycholinguistics Program. Her research ranged across children's acquisition of syntax and morphology, language socialization in families, fathers' speech to children, routines and politeness formulas, and the preservation of her family's heritage language, Hungarian. She wrote the widely used textbook The Development of Language and co-edited the Handbook of Child Language and other reference works.
