Biodiesel

Biodiesel is a renewable, biodegradable fuel manufactured from vegetable oils, animal fats, or recycled restaurant grease. It is a long-chain fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) produced by chemically reacting lipids with an alcohol such as methanol in a process called transesterification. Biodiesel can be used in pure form, designated B100, or blended with petroleum diesel in proportions such as B20 (20 percent biodiesel) and B5.

History

Vegetable oils were used as diesel fuel as early as the 1890s, when Rudolf Diesel demonstrated his engine at the 1900 Paris Exhibition running on peanut oil. The modern biodiesel industry developed in the 1970s and 1980s in response to oil crises, and commercial production grew in Europe and the United States during the 1990s and 2000s, supported by tax incentives and renewable fuel mandates.

Production

In transesterification, triglycerides in oil or fat react with methanol in the presence of a catalyst such as sodium hydroxide, producing methyl esters (biodiesel) and glycerol as a co-product. The fuel is then washed and purified to meet standards such as EN 14214 in Europe and ASTM D6751 in the United States.

Properties and use

Biodiesel has a higher cetane number than most petroleum diesel and contains no sulfur, but has slightly lower energy content and can gel at low temperatures. It is broadly compatible with existing diesel engines, though older rubber seals may degrade in high blends. Emissions of carbon monoxide, particulates, and unburned hydrocarbons are generally lower than for fossil diesel, while nitrogen oxide emissions may be slightly higher.

Environmental debate

Because the carbon dioxide released on combustion was recently absorbed by the feedstock plants, biodiesel is often treated as partly carbon-neutral. Critics point to land-use change — clearing forests or peatland for palm or soy plantations — which can negate climate benefits, and to competition with food production. Waste-oil and algae-based feedstocks are promoted as lower-impact alternatives. See also biofuel and renewable energy.
