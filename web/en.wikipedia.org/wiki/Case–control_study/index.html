Case–control study

Case–control study
FieldEpidemiology
DesignObservational, retrospective
Key measureOdds ratio
Best suited forRare diseases, diseases with long latency

A case–control study is a type of observational study in epidemiology in which two groups are compared: cases, who have the disease or outcome of interest, and controls, who do not. Researchers look back in time (retrospectively) to compare how frequently a suspected risk factor or exposure occurs in each group, in order to determine the factor's relationship to the disease.

Design

Cases are identified from clinics, registries, or populations, and controls are selected from the same source population that gave rise to the cases — ideally people who would have been identified as cases had they developed the disease. Exposure histories are obtained from records or interviews. Because the study starts from the outcome, incidence cannot be measured directly; the association is summarised by the odds ratio, which approximates the relative risk when the disease is rare.

Strengths and weaknesses

Case–control studies are relatively quick and inexpensive, can study several exposures for one disease, and are the design of choice for rare diseases and outbreaks. Their landmark applications include Doll and Hill's 1950 study linking smoking to lung cancer. Drawbacks include vulnerability to selection bias and recall bias, difficulty studying rare exposures, and inability to establish the time order of exposure and disease with certainty.

Variants

Nested case–control and case–cohort studies are conducted within a defined cohort, using stored data and specimens to gain efficiency while reducing bias.
