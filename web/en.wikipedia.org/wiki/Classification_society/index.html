Classification society

A classification society surveyor inspecting a vessel under construction.

A classification society or ship classification society is a non-governmental organization that establishes and maintains technical standards for the construction and operation of ships and offshore structures. Classification societies certify that the construction of a vessel complies with relevant standards and carry out regular inspections in service to ensure continuing compliance.

The origins of ship classification lie in the London marine insurance market of the 18th century: underwriters meeting at Lloyd's Coffee House needed reliable information about the quality of ships they insured, leading to the first register of ships in 1764 and the founding of Lloyd's Register. Bureau Veritas (1828), the American Bureau of Shipping (1862), DNV (1864) and other societies followed, each publishing rules and a register book in which vessels were assigned a "class".

Role and duties

A vessel that meets the society's rules is said to be "in class"; class is usually a condition of insurance and of registration under most flags. Societies survey ships during construction and periodically thereafter, issue class certificates, and — acting as Recognized Organizations — perform statutory surveys and certification on behalf of flag states under conventions such as SOLAS and MARPOL. More than 50 organisations worldwide describe themselves as classification societies; the leading ones are members of the International Association of Classification Societies (IACS), founded in 1968, whose members class over 90 per cent of the world's cargo-carrying tonnage.

Major societies

The largest classification societies by classed fleet include ClassNK (Japan), the American Bureau of Shipping, DNV (Norway), Lloyd's Register (United Kingdom), Bureau Veritas (France), China Classification Society, Korean Register, RINA (Italy), the Indian Register of Shipping and the Russian Maritime Register of Shipping.

Criticism

The industry has been criticised over conflicts of interest — societies are paid by the shipowners whose vessels they certify — and over "class hopping", in which owners move a substandard vessel between societies to avoid rectifying deficiencies. Casualties such as the tankers Erika (1999) and Prestige (2002) prompted reforms, including EU regulation of recognised organisations and IACS measures to track vessels leaving class.
