Organum

An example of early organum notation from a medieval treatise.

Organum is, in general, a plainchant melody with at least one added voice to enhance the harmony, developed within the tradition of Western European Christian liturgical music. In its early stages, organum involved two musical voices: a Gregorian chant melody, and the same melody transposed by a consonant interval, usually a perfect fifth or fourth. Over time, composers came to understand that different modes (or sets of pitches) could be used to sing the chant melody at the fifth or fourth, and the additional voices became increasingly independent, eventually reaching the point at which they assumed distinct identities of their own. Organum is considered the earliest form of polyphonic music in the Western tradition and was a crucial step toward the development of counterpoint.

History

Early organum

The first documented descriptions of organum appear in the ninth-century treatises Musica enchiriadis and Scolica enchiriadis. In this early practice, called parallel organum, the added voice (vox organalis) sang the chant (vox principalis) at a fixed interval below, typically a fourth or fifth. Later treatises, such as Guido d'Arezzo's Micrologus (c. 1026), describe freer practices in which the voices move in contrary and oblique motion, converging on unisons at phrase endings.

Aquitanian polyphony

In the twelfth century, the monastery of St. Martial de Limoges in Aquitaine became a centre of a more florid style. In florid organum, the chant is stretched into long sustained notes (the tenor) while the upper voice sings elaborate melismas above it — a texture known as organum purum. Sections in discant style, in which both voices move in measured rhythm, alternate with the florid passages.

Notre Dame organum

The culmination of the organum tradition came with the school of Notre Dame in Paris, associated with the composers Léonin and Pérotin (late 12th – early 13th century). Léonin compiled the Magnus liber organi, a great book of two-voice organa for the liturgical year. Pérotin expanded the texture to three and even four voices in works such as Viderunt omnes and Sederunt principes. The Notre Dame composers systematised rhythmic notation through the rhythmic modes, laying the groundwork for measured polyphony and, ultimately, the motet.
