The Southern Song (Chinese: 南宋; 1127–1279) was the latter period of the Song dynasty of China. After the Jurchen Jin dynasty captured the Song capital Kaifeng in 1127 and took the northern territories — the Jingkang Incident — the Song prince Zhao Gou re-established the dynasty in the south as Emperor Gaozong, eventually settling the capital at Lin'an (modern Hangzhou).

History

Song armies under generals such as Yue Fei recovered territory in the 1130s, but Gaozong's court favoured settlement: the Treaty of Shaoxing (1141) fixed the border at the Huai River, ceded the north, and required annual tribute to Jin. Yue Fei was executed on fabricated charges in 1142. Thereafter the Southern Song coexisted with Jin for eight decades, punctuated by unsuccessful campaigns on both sides.

From 1234, having allied with the Mongols to destroy Jin, the Song faced the Mongol Empire alone. Despite decades of resistance — notably at the twin fortresses of Xiangyang, which fell in 1273 — the dynasty collapsed: Lin'an surrendered in 1276, and the last imperial court perished at the naval Battle of Yamen in 1279, when the loyalist official Lu Xiufu is said to have jumped into the sea with the child emperor Zhao Bing.

Economy and culture

Confined to China's most productive regions, the Southern Song presided over a commercial revolution: a population of around 100 million, booming maritime trade through ports such as Guangzhou and Quanzhou, printed paper money, and great cities — Lin'an was likely the largest city in the world, with over a million inhabitants. The era saw the maturation of Neo-Confucianism under Zhu Xi, landscape painting of the Ma-Xia school, and advances in gunpowder weapons, shipbuilding and mathematics.
