Bactria (Bactrian: βαχλο, Bakhlo; Persian: باختر) was an ancient Iranian region in Central Asia, centred on the plain between the Hindu Kush mountain range and the Amu Darya (Oxus) river, in what is now northern Afghanistan and southern Tajikistan and Uzbekistan. Its capital was Bactra, now Balkh, in present-day northern Afghanistan.

History

Bactria was home to the Bronze Age Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex, sometimes called the Oxus civilisation, around 2250–1700 BC. Under Cyrus the Great it became a satrapy of the Achaemenid Empire, famed for its wealth and horsemen. Alexander the Great conquered the region after hard campaigning in 329–327 BC, marrying the Bactrian noblewoman Roxana.

After Alexander's death Bactria passed to the Seleucid Empire, but around 255 BC its governor Diodotus declared independence, founding the Greco-Bactrian Kingdom. Greco-Bactrian kings such as Euthydemus and Demetrius expanded into northwestern India, giving rise to the Indo-Greek kingdoms. The region later formed the northern heartland of the Kushan Empire and was a major centre of Buddhism on the Silk Road.

Geography

The Bactrian plain is watered by rivers flowing north from the Hindu Kush to the Amu Darya, supporting irrigated agriculture in an otherwise arid landscape. Its position made Bactria a crossroads of trade between Persia, India, China and the steppe.

Legacy

Bactria gave its name to the Bactrian camel and to the Bactrian language, an Eastern Iranian language written in Greek script under the Kushans.
