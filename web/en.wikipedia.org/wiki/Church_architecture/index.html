Church architecture

Church architecture refers to the architecture of buildings used for Christian worship — churches, cathedrals, chapels, and basilicas. Over two millennia it has produced a succession of distinctive styles that reflect theology, liturgy, technology, and regional culture, from the house churches of the early centuries to the great Gothic cathedrals and the auditorium and megachurch forms of the present day.

Early and Byzantine churches

After the legalization of Christianity in the fourth century, Christians adopted the Roman basilica — a long aisled hall with an apse — for congregational worship, as in Old St. Peter's in Rome. In the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire, centrally planned churches crowned by domes on pendentives culminated in Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (537). Plans were often cruciform, and orientation toward the east became customary.

Medieval styles

Romanesque churches (c. 1000–1200) feature round arches, massive walls, barrel vaults, and cruciform plans with towers. Gothic architecture, beginning with the Abbey of Saint-Denis near Paris in the 1140s, introduced the pointed arch, ribbed vault, and flying buttress, enabling taller, lighter interiors filled with stained glass, as in Chartres, Amiens, and Cologne cathedrals. Regional variants flourished, from English Perpendicular to Italian Gothic.

Renaissance to modern

The Renaissance revived classical orders and centralized, geometrically harmonious plans (Brunelleschi's churches in Florence; St. Peter's Basilica in Rome). The Baroque, fuelled by the Counter-Reformation, added drama, movement, and rich decoration. The nineteenth century brought Gothic Revival dominance for church building. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, modernist architects — Le Corbusier at Ronchamp, and many others — reinterpreted sacred space in concrete and light, while evangelical worship produced flexible auditorium and megachurch designs.

Elements

Common elements include the narthex, nave, aisles, transept, chancel and sanctuary, altar, baptistery and font, pulpit, choir, crypt, cloister (in monastic churches), bell towers or steeples, and stained-glass windows, each shaped by the liturgical practices of the tradition that built it.
