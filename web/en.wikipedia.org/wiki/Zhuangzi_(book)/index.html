Zhuangzi (book)

Zhuangzi (莊子)
Author: attributed to Zhuang Zhou and followers
Date: 4th-2nd century BCE (compiled later)
Language: Classical Chinese
Subject: Daoist philosophy

The Zhuangzi is an ancient Chinese text of fables, parables and dialogues that is one of the two foundational works of Daoism, alongside the Tao Te Ching. It is named for its traditional author, "Master Zhuang" (Zhuang Zhou, c. 369-286 BCE), and is celebrated both as philosophy and as a masterpiece of Chinese literature, full of humour, paradox and imaginative storytelling.

Contents and structure

The received text, edited by the commentator Guo Xiang (d. 312 CE), contains 33 chapters in three groups: the Inner Chapters (1-7), generally regarded as Zhuang Zhou's own work; the Outer Chapters (8-22); and the Mixed Chapters (23-33), written by followers and later hands. Earlier versions were larger — the Han-dynasty bibliography listed 52 chapters.

Famous passages

Among its most famous pieces are the opening "Free and Easy Wandering," describing the giant bird Peng; the "Discussion on Making All Things Equal," with its sceptical questioning of fixed distinctions; and the story in chapter 2 of Zhuangzi dreaming he was a butterfly, then wondering whether he was a man who had dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it was a man. Other celebrated tales feature Cook Ding, whose blade stays sharp by following the natural structure of the ox, and the useless tree that survives precisely because it is good for nothing.

Themes and influence

The text advocates spontaneity (wu wei), freedom from social convention and rigid distinctions, scepticism about language, and acceptance of death as a natural transformation. It profoundly influenced Chinese literature, art and Chan (Zen) Buddhism, and remains widely read in translation.
