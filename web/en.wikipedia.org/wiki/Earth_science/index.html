Earth science

[Image: a cutaway illustration of Earth showing crust, mantle and glowing core, with labelled layers]

Earth science, or geoscience, is the all-encompassing term for the sciences related to the planet Earth, including geology, geophysics, hydrology, oceanography, meteorology, glaciology, soil science and atmospheric science. It studies Earth's structure, composition, processes and history, and increasingly its interactions with human activity.

Earth scientists work on problems from earthquakes and volcanoes to climate change, groundwater, mineral and energy resources, natural hazards and the deep history of life recorded in rocks and ice.

Branches

Geology and geophysics study the solid Earth — plate tectonics, the unifying theory adopted in the 1960s, explains the movement of continents, mountain building, earthquakes and volcanism. Oceanography studies the seas that cover 71% of the surface, and atmospheric science the weather and climate systems above them.

Palaeontology and geochronology reconstruct Earth's 4.54-billion-year history, from its formation and the origin of life through mass extinctions to the appearance of humans in the last fraction of its story.

Methods and importance

Earth science is a field science as much as a laboratory one: mapping, drilling, seismology, satellite remote sensing and computer modelling all contribute. Its findings underpin hazard maps, resource policy, water management and all assessments of climate change, including those of the IPCC-style scientific assessments.

Because Earth is the only accessible example of a habitable planet, Earth science also anchors planetary science's understanding of Mars, Venus and exoplanets.
