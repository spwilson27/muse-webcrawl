The Florida panther is a population of the North American cougar (Puma concolor) found in South Florida, in pinelands, tropical hardwood hammocks and mixed freshwater swamp forests. It was traditionally classified as the subspecies Puma concolor coryi, though later genetic work treats North American cougars as a single subspecies, with the Florida animals managed as a distinct population. It is the only confirmed population of cougars in the eastern United States, and the state animal of Florida.

Decline and recovery

Hunted heavily and deprived of habitat, the population fell to perhaps 20–30 individuals by the early 1990s, suffering inbreeding defects such as kinked tails, cowlicks and heart abnormalities. In 1995, eight female cougars from Texas were released in South Florida to restore genetic diversity — a genetic rescue that reduced defects and tripled numbers over the following decades. Panthers are protected under the Endangered Species Act, and recovery efforts focus on habitat protection, wildlife crossings under highways, and reducing vehicle strikes, which are a leading cause of death.

Biology

Adult males weigh roughly 46–72 kg (100–160 lb) and females 29–45 kg (64–100 lb). Panthers are solitary carnivores that prey mainly on white-tailed deer and feral hogs, as well as raccoons, armadillos and smaller animals. Their remaining range centres on Big Cypress National Preserve, the Everglades and surrounding private ranchlands in southwestern Florida, though males have dispersed north of the Caloosahatchee River in search of territory.
