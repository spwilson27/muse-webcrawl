Kashrut (from Hebrew kasher, "fit") is the set of Jewish religious dietary laws. Food that conforms to the requirements of Jewish law (halakha) is termed kosher; food that is not is called treif (literally "torn"). The basic laws derive principally from Leviticus and Deuteronomy, with details elaborated in rabbinic literature.

Permitted and forbidden animals

Land animals may be eaten only if they both chew the cud and have completely split hooves — cattle, sheep, goats, and deer are permitted, while pigs, horses, and camels are forbidden. Of water creatures, only fish with both fins and scales are permitted, excluding shellfish, crustaceans, and catfish. Most birds of prey and scavengers are forbidden; permitted birds, such as chicken, turkey, duck, and goose, are identified by tradition. All insects are forbidden except certain locusts, traditionally permitted in some communities.

Slaughter and preparation

Meat must come from animals slaughtered by shechita: a swift cut across the throat by a trained slaughterer (shochet) with a perfectly smooth blade. The carcass is inspected for defects, and blood — whose consumption is prohibited — must be removed by salting and soaking (or by broiling). The sciatic nerve and certain fats are also removed.

Separation of meat and dairy

Based on the commandment not to "boil a kid in its mother's milk," meat and dairy products may not be cooked or eaten together. Observant households keep separate sets of dishes, utensils, and cookware for meat and milk, and wait a prescribed interval between eating one and the other. Foods that are neither meat nor dairy, such as fish, eggs, fruits, and vegetables, are pareve (neutral).

Other rules

Additional laws govern Passover (prohibiting leavened grain products), wine (requiring Jewish handling for strict kosher status), produce of the Land of Israel (tithes and the sabbatical year), and the inspection of fruits and vegetables for insects. Modern kosher certification agencies supervise food production worldwide and mark approved products with symbols called hechsherim.

Reasons and observance

Proposed explanations for the laws range from hygiene and health to ethics, holiness, and Jewish identity; traditionally they are observed as divine commandments (chukim) even where the reason is unclear. Levels of observance vary widely among Jews, from strict adherence to selective practice or none.
