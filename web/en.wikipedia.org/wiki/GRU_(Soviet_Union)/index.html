The Main Intelligence Directorate (Russian: Гла́вное разве́дывательное управле́ние, Glavnoye razvedyvatel'noye upravleniye), abbreviated GRU, was the foreign military intelligence agency of the Soviet Army General Staff of the Soviet Union. Unlike the KGB, which reported to the Communist Party leadership and handled domestic security as well as foreign intelligence, the GRU was purely a military organisation, responsible for military intelligence, special forces (Spetsnaz) and support to military operations.

History

The GRU traces its origin to the Registration Directorate of the Red Army Field Staff, created by secret order of Leon Trotsky on 21 October 1918. Renamed and reorganised several times, it became the Fourth Directorate of the General Staff in 1934 and acquired the name Main Intelligence Directorate in 1942. During the Second World War it ran famous spy networks including the Rote Kapelle ("Red Orchestra") in occupied Europe and Richard Sorge's ring in Japan, whose warnings of the German invasion in 1941 were ignored by Stalin.

During the Cold War the GRU recruited agents across NATO states, ran "illegals" and military attaché officers, and supplied intelligence during conflicts from Korea to Afghanistan. Its longest-serving director, Pyotr Ivashutin (1963–1987), built up its satellite reconnaissance and signals capabilities. Famous defectors and exposed officers included Oleg Penkovsky, a GRU colonel who passed crucial intelligence to the West during the Cuban Missile Crisis, and Viktor Suvorov (Vladimir Rezun), whose later books popularised the GRU and Spetsnaz in the West.

Dissolution

After the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, the GRU's assets and personnel in Russia passed in May 1992 to the new Russian Main Intelligence Directorate, which kept the GRU name in common usage.
