The South Sea Bubble was an economic bubble that occurred in Great Britain in 1720, centred on the stock of the South Sea Company. Founded in 1711, the company held a monopoly on British trade with Spanish South America and in 1719–20 proposed to take over a large portion of the British national debt, exchanging government debt for company shares.

Rise

The scheme promised large profits, and the share price rose from about £128 in January 1720 to more than £1,000 by June. The mania spread beyond the South Sea Company itself: dozens of new joint-stock ventures — some fraudulent or absurd — were floated to eager crowds in Exchange Alley, satirised in popular prints and in a famous set of Dutch playing cards. Parliament passed the Bubble Act in June 1720, requiring a royal charter for new joint-stock companies, partly at the company's own urging to suppress rival schemes.

Collapse

Confidence faltered in the summer of 1720. By September the shares had fallen to around £150–200, and by the end of the year they were near their starting level. Thousands of investors, including members of the aristocracy and the scientist Isaac Newton — who is said to have lost some £20,000 and remarked that he "could calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of the people" — were ruined.

Aftermath

A parliamentary inquiry revealed fraud and corruption involving company directors and several ministers. The Chancellor of the Exchequer, John Aislabie, was expelled from the Commons and imprisoned in the Tower. Robert Walpole's skilful management of the crisis — restructuring the debt between the Bank of England and the East India Company, and shielding the court — helped establish his dominance of British politics and his reputation as the first prime minister. The Bubble Act remained in force until 1825.
