Four Deputies

The Four Deputies (Arabic: النُّوَّاب الأربعة, an-Nuwwāb al-Arbaʿa) were the four successive representatives of the twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, during the period known in Twelver Shia Islam as the Minor Occultation (874–941 CE). According to Twelver doctrine, after the death of the eleventh Imam, Hasan al-Askari, his son the Mahdi entered occultation and communicated with the community through these deputies, who conveyed questions and distributed religious taxes on his behalf.

The four deputies

  • Uthman ibn Sa'id al-Asadi (d. c. 880) — previously an agent of the tenth and eleventh Imams; the first deputy.
  • Muhammad ibn Uthman (d. 917) — his son; served as deputy for around forty years and is credited with works on jurisprudence.
  • Husayn ibn Ruh al-Nawbakhti (d. 938) — from the prominent Nawbakhti family.
  • Ali ibn Muhammad al-Samarri (d. 941) — the last deputy.

Shortly before al-Samarri's death, a signed letter (tawqi) attributed to the Imam announced that no further deputy would be appointed and that the Major Occultation had begun — a period that, in Twelver belief, continues until the Mahdi's return. The end of the deputyship raised lasting questions about religious authority during the Occultation, addressed over time by Shia jurists and the doctrine of the general deputyship of the jurists.
