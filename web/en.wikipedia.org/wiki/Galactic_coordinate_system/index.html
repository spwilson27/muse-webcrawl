Galactic coordinate system

The galactic coordinate system is a celestial coordinate system in spherical coordinates, with the Sun as its centre, the primary direction aligned with the approximate centre of the Milky Way galaxy, and the fundamental plane approximately in the galactic plane. It uses the right-handed convention, with longitude measured eastward along the galactic equator from the galactic centre direction.

Coordinates

A position is given by galactic longitude (l) and galactic latitude (b). Latitude is the angle above or below the galactic plane, running from 0° at the plane to +90° at the north galactic pole (in the constellation Coma Berenices) and −90° at the south galactic pole (in Sculptor). Longitude runs from 0° toward the galactic centre in Sagittarius, through 90° in Cygnus, 180° toward the galactic anticenter in Auriga, and 270° in Vela.

Definition

The International Astronomical Union defined the system in 1958, based on observations of neutral hydrogen, specifying the positions of the galactic centre and poles in the equatorial coordinate system for epoch B1950.0; the definition has been carried into the J2000.0 system. In the standard definition the galactic centre direction lies at approximately right ascension 17h 45.6m, declination −28.94°, and the north galactic pole at right ascension 12h 51.4m, declination +27.13°.

Use

The system is standard in galactic astronomy: objects in the Milky Way's disk concentrate near b = 0°, so surveys of stars, gas, and dust are organised in galactic coordinates, and the "zone of avoidance" near the plane explains the scarcity of visible external galaxies there.
