Battle of Columbus (1916)

The Battle of Columbus, also known as the Columbus Raid or the Burning of Columbus, was an attack on the border village of Columbus, New Mexico, on 9 March 1916 by forces of the Mexican revolutionary general Francisco "Pancho" Villa during the Mexican Revolution. Villista raiders attacked the town and the adjacent Camp Furlong, garrisoned by the U.S. 13th Cavalry Regiment; after several hours of fighting they were driven off with heavy losses. The raid provoked the United States to launch the Punitive Expedition into Mexico in pursuit of Villa.

Background

Villa's División del Norte had been crushed by Carrancista forces in 1915, and the United States had recognized Venustiano Carranza's government and embargoed arms to Villa — grievances often cited as Villa's motive, along with the need for arms, horses and supplies. In January 1916 Villistas had murdered American mining engineers at Santa Isabel, Chihuahua, heightening border tension. Villa personally led some 485 men north to Columbus, a village three miles from the border with a small cavalry garrison.

Battle

Attacking before dawn on 9 March, the Villistas burned much of the town while other parties assaulted Camp Furlong. Taken by surprise, the 13th Cavalry rallied under officers including Lt. John P. Lucas, whose machine-gun troop brought its Benét–Mercié guns into action in the streets; Major Frank Tompkins then led a mounted pursuit across the border, chasing the raiders some fifteen miles into Mexico. Villa lost roughly a quarter of his force killed, and five captured raiders were later tried and hanged in New Mexico.

Aftermath

President Woodrow Wilson ordered the Punitive Expedition under Brig. Gen. John J. Pershing, which entered Mexico on 15 March 1916 and pursued Villa for eleven months without capturing him, while training the U.S. Army — including early use of aircraft and motor trucks — for the First World War. Columbus remains the site of commemorations and of Pancho Villa State Park.
