Nitrate film

Nitrate film, or cellulose nitrate film, was the standard motion picture film stock from the industry's beginnings in the 1890s until the early 1950s. Made by coating photographic emulsion on a transparent base of nitrocellulose (celluloid) plasticised with camphor, it produced brilliant images but was highly flammable — chemically similar to guncotton — and subject to irreversible decay. It was replaced for professional use by cellulose acetate "safety film".

History

George Eastman's company introduced roll film on a nitrocellulose base in 1889, and the flexible, transparent stock enabled the motion picture industry. Nitrate remained the only base for 35 mm professional film for sixty years despite its dangers. Hollywood's last nitrate productions were made in 1951, when Kodak discontinued the stock; some countries, including India and Egypt, used nitrate into the late 1950s.

Fire hazard

Nitrate film contains its own oxygen and burns fiercely even when submerged; decomposition releases nitrogen oxides that act as oxidisers, and aged, deteriorated film can self-ignite at temperatures as low as about 40 °C (104 °F) if stored in bulk in hot conditions. Fires in projection booths, studios and archives killed hundreds: the 1929 Glen Cinema disaster in Paisley, Scotland, killed 69 children, and a 1937 fire at a 20th Century Fox storage facility in New Jersey destroyed most of the studio's pre-1935 negatives. Projection booths were rebuilt with fireproofing, automatic shutters and strict handling rules, and shipping nitrate was heavily regulated.

Decay and preservation

Nitrate decomposition proceeds through stages: fading and amber discolouration of the image, emulsion stickiness, blistering and softening of the base, and finally collapse into a brittle or powdery mass, with a characteristic acrid odour. Because the process is autocatalytic, deteriorating reels endanger their neighbours. An estimated majority of all silent-era feature films are lost, many through nitrate decay, fire, or deliberate destruction for the stock's silver content. Modern archives store surviving nitrate in cold, dry vaults and copy it to safety film or digital formats; notable vaults include those of the Library of Congress and the BFI National Archive.

In popular culture

The flammability of nitrate prints features prominently in films such as Cinema Paradiso (1988) and Inglourious Basterds (2009), in which a cinema full of nitrate film is set alight.
