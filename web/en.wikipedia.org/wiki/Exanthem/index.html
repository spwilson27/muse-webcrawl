Exanthem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An exanthem (from Greek exanthema, "a breaking out") is a widespread rash occurring on the outside of the body, usually in children, and often accompanying a systemic illness such as a viral or bacterial infection. Exanthems may be caused by toxins, drugs, microorganisms, or autoimmune disease. A rash confined to mucous membranes inside the body is called an enanthem.

Classic childhood exanthems

Six classic infectious exanthems of childhood were historically numbered. First disease is measles (rubeola), a morbilliform rash spreading from the hairline downward, accompanied by cough, coryza, conjunctivitis and Koplik spots. Second disease is scarlet fever, caused by group A streptococcal toxin, with a sandpaper-textured rash and strawberry tongue. Third disease is rubella (German measles), a milder pink rash with postauricular lymphadenopathy. Fourth disease (Dukes' disease) is now doubted as a distinct entity. Fifth disease is erythema infectiosum, caused by parvovirus B19, famous for its "slapped cheek" appearance followed by a lacy rash on the limbs. Sixth disease is roseola infantum (exanthem subitum), caused by HHV-6 and HHV-7, in which several days of high fever in an infant abruptly end as the rash appears.

Other causes

Beyond the classic six, exanthems occur in chickenpox (varicella), hand, foot and mouth disease, dengue, chikungunya, Zika, secondary syphilis, typhoid fever (rose spots) and Kawasaki disease. Drug eruptions — especially to antibiotics and anticonvulsants — are a major cause in adults, and severe forms such as DRESS syndrome, Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis are medical emergencies.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the morphology and distribution of the rash, associated symptoms, exposure and vaccination history, and season; laboratory tests such as serology or PCR confirm specific viruses. Most viral exanthems resolve without specific treatment, but recognition matters for infection control, pregnancy counselling (rubella and parvovirus B19 can damage the fetus), and identifying vaccine-preventable disease outbreaks.
