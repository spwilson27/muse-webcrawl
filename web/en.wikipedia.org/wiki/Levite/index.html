A Levite (Hebrew: לֵוִי, Lēwî) is a member of the Hebrew tribe of Levi. In the Hebrew Bible, the Levites were set apart for service in the Tabernacle and later the Temple in Jerusalem: they assisted the priests (kohanim, the descendants of Aaron within the tribe), guarded and transported the sanctuary, sang and played music in the Temple service, and taught the people.

Biblical account

According to the Torah, the tribe of Levi was chosen in place of the firstborn of Israel after the Exodus from Egypt. Unlike the other tribes, the Levites received no territorial inheritance in the Land of Israel; instead they were given 48 Levitical cities and supported by tithes (ma'aser) offered by the other Israelites, for "the LORD is their inheritance" (Deuteronomy 18:2).

The Book of Numbers describes their duties around the Tabernacle, assigned by clan — Gershon, Kohath, and Merari. The priests proper were a subset: the descendants of Aaron, brother of Moses, both of the tribe of Levi.

In later Judaism

After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, the Levites' sacrificial role ceased, but their status was retained in synagogue practice: to this day, a Levite is called second to the Torah reading (after a kohen) and traditionally washes the hands of the kohanim before the priestly blessing. Levite status is hereditary in the male line and is often indicated by surnames such as Levi, Levy, Halevi, and Levin.
