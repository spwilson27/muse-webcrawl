Refusenik

Origin: From Russian otkaznik ("person refused")
Context: Soviet Union, 1960s–1980s
Mostly: Soviet Jews denied exit visas

Refusenik was an unofficial term, ultimately from the Russian otkaznik, for individuals — typically Soviet Jews, but also other religious and ethnic minorities — who were denied permission to emigrate from the Soviet Union. The term entered English in the 1970s as the plight of refuseniks became an international human rights cause.

Background

From the late 1960s, growing numbers of Soviet Jews applied for exit visas to emigrate to Israel. Many applications were refused, often on the pretext that the applicant had access to state secrets, and refusal frequently brought dismissal from employment and social ostracism. Some refuseniks waited more than a decade for permission to leave.

Notable refuseniks

Prominent refuseniks included the physicist and human rights activist Anatoly Shcharansky (later Natan Sharansky), who was imprisoned on espionage charges in 1977 and released in 1986, and Ida Nudel, known as the "guardian angel" of refusenik prisoners.

International response

The Jackson–Vanik amendment of 1974 linked United States trade benefits for the USSR to freedom of emigration. Emigration rose sharply in the late 1980s under glasnost, and the refusenik phenomenon ended with the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. The word has since been applied more loosely to other people who refuse something on principle.
