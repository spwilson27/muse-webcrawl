Volcanism

Volcanism, volcanicity or vulcanism is the phenomenon of eruption of molten rock (magma) onto the surface of the Earth or a solid-surface planet, where lava, pyroclastics and volcanic gases erupt through a break in the surface called a vent. It includes all phenomena resulting from and causing magma within the crust or mantle of the body to rise through the crust and form volcanic rocks on the surface.

Processes

Magma forms by partial melting of the mantle or lower crust, driven by decompression (at divergent boundaries and mantle plumes), the addition of volatiles (at subduction zones, where water lowers the melting point), or heat transfer. Being less dense than surrounding rock, magma rises into chambers; when pressure or gas content becomes sufficient it erupts, effusively as lava flows or explosively, with explosivity controlled by magma viscosity and dissolved gas — measured on the Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI).

Features

Volcanic activity builds stratovolcanoes, shield volcanoes, calderas, lava domes, fissures and maars, and produces features such as fumaroles, geysers and hot springs. Large igneous provinces, built by flood-basalt volcanism, are implicated in several mass extinctions. Eruptions can inject sulphate aerosols into the stratosphere with global climate effects, as in the 1816 "Year Without a Summer" following Mount Tambora.

On other bodies

Volcanism is widespread in the Solar System: Io is the most volcanically active body known, with silicate eruptions driven by tidal heating; Enceladus and Triton show cryovolcanism (erupting water and nitrogen ices); Mars hosts Olympus Mons, the largest volcano in the Solar System; and Venus shows evidence of extensive, possibly ongoing volcanism.
