Plant reproductive morphology

Plant reproductive morphology is the study of the physical form and structure (the morphology) of the parts of plants that are concerned, directly or indirectly, with sexual reproduction. Among all living organisms, flowers, as the reproductive structures of angiosperms, are the most varied physically and show a correspondingly great diversity in methods of reproduction. Plants that are not flowering plants (green algae, mosses, liverworts, hornworts, ferns, and gymnosperms such as conifers) also have complex interplays between morphological adaptation and environmental factors in their sexual reproduction.

Alternation of generations

All land plants reproduce through an alternation of generations, in which a haploid gametophyte generation produces gametes and a diploid sporophyte generation produces spores by meiosis. In bryophytes the gametophyte is the dominant, free-living plant, whereas in vascular plants the sporophyte dominates, and in seed plants the gametophytes are reduced to the pollen grain (male) and the embryo sac within the ovule (female).

The flower

The flower of an angiosperm typically consists of four whorls of modified leaves: the sepals (calyx), the petals (corolla), the stamens (androecium), which produce pollen, and the carpels (gynoecium), which contain the ovules. Flowers may be perfect, bearing both stamens and carpels, or imperfect, bearing only one sex. Plants may be monoecious, with male and female flowers on the same individual, or dioecious, with the sexes on separate plants.

Pollination and fertilisation

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a receptive stigma, effected by wind, water, or animals — insects above all. Floral colour, scent, nectar, and shape are adaptations to particular pollinators. After pollination, a pollen tube grows to the ovule, where double fertilisation — unique to angiosperms — produces the diploid zygote and the triploid endosperm that nourishes the seed. The ovary wall typically develops into the fruit, aiding seed dispersal.

Non-flowering plants

Gymnosperms bear their ovules on cones or similar structures rather than in enclosed carpels. Ferns and their allies disperse by spores produced in sporangia, and their small, independent gametophytes (prothalli) bear antheridia and archegonia. Bryophytes likewise produce motile sperm that require water to reach the egg, tying their reproduction to moist habitats.
