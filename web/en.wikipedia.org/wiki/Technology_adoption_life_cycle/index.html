Technology adoption life cycle

The technology adoption life cycle is a sociological model that describes the adoption or acceptance of a new product or innovation according to the demographic and psychological characteristics of defined adopter groups. The model, derived from diffusion research in rural sociology, holds that adoption follows a bell-curve distribution over time, divided into five segments: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards.

Origins

The model builds on studies of hybrid seed corn adoption in Iowa in the 1940s by Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross, and on the adopter categorisation of the Iowa State research tradition. Everett Rogers popularised the framework in his landmark 1962 book Diffusion of Innovations, which became the standard reference on how innovations spread through a social system. Joe M. Bohlen and George M. Beal are credited with early versions of the adopter-category curve applied to farm practices in the 1950s.

Adopter categories

  • Innovators (about 2.5%): venturesome risk-takers eager to try new ideas, often cosmopolite and technically knowledgeable.
  • Early adopters (about 13.5%): respected opinion leaders who adopt early but with discretion; their endorsement helps trigger wider diffusion.
  • Early majority (about 34%): deliberate adopters who take up an innovation just before the average member of the system.
  • Late majority (about 34%): sceptical adopters who follow the average, often under economic necessity or peer pressure.
  • Laggards (about 16%): the last to adopt, oriented toward tradition and suspicious of change.

The chasm

Geoffrey A. Moore's 1991 book Crossing the Chasm adapted the model to high-technology marketing, arguing that a gap — the "chasm" — separates the early adopters (visionaries) from the early majority (pragmatists), because the two groups have very different expectations. Many technology products fail by not crossing this chasm; Moore recommended focusing on a niche "beachhead" segment and building momentum from it.

Applications and criticism

The life cycle model is widely used in marketing, product management and technology strategy to plan launch sequencing, messaging and pricing. Critics note that the category proportions are idealised, that adoption is not always bell-shaped, and that the model says little about discontinuance, reinvention of the innovation during diffusion, or network effects in which an innovation's value depends on how many others have adopted it.
