Technophilia

Technophilia (from Greek τέχνη, technē, "art, skill, craft" and φιλία, philia, "love, friendship") refers generally to a strong enthusiasm for technology, especially new technologies such as personal computers, the Internet, mobile phones, and home cinema systems. The term is used in sociology to examine individuals' interactions with their society and is contrasted with technophobia, an aversion to or fear of technology.

Characteristics

A technophile is a person who embraces new technology eagerly and often adopts it early. Technophiles frequently believe that most social and technical problems can be solved through technological means, a stance related to technological utopianism and solutionism. In the information age, technophilia is expressed in enthusiasm for consumer electronics, software, and networked services, and in subcultures such as hobbyist computing and maker communities.

Scholars have noted that technophilia is strongly associated with categories such as "early adopters" in diffusion-of-innovations research. On a psychological level, enthusiasm for technology has been linked with openness to experience and, in its extreme forms, with compulsive consumption and addiction-like behaviours around devices; these darker aspects are sometimes discussed under the label of the "technological imperative" — the assumption that because something can be built, it should be.

Cultural discussion

Critics of technophilia argue that an uncritical love of technology can obscure its social costs, including environmental harm, labour displacement, privacy erosion, and widening inequality between those with and without access. Defenders respond that enthusiasm for innovation has historically driven improvements in health, communication, and living standards. The term also appears in popular culture, where the "technophile" is a stock character in science fiction and comedy.
