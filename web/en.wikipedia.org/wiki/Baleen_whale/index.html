The baleen whales (systematic name Mysticeti), also called whalebone whales, form a parvorder of the infraorder Cetacea. They are a widely distributed and diverse group of fully aquatic marine mammals. Mysticetes are characterised by having baleen plates for filtering food from water, rather than teeth, and by possessing two blowholes.

Feeding

Baleen is made of keratin — the same material as fingernails and hair — and hangs in rows of plates from the upper jaw. Different species have evolved different filter-feeding strategies: rorquals such as the blue whale and humpback engulf huge volumes of krill- and fish-laden water into a distensible throat pouch and expel the water through the baleen; right whales skim-feed continuously with very fine, long baleen; and gray whales suction-feed on bottom-dwelling invertebrates.

Size and diversity

Baleen whales include the largest animals known to have ever lived. The blue whale reaches about 30 m (98 ft) and 190 tonnes. The group also includes the humpback, fin, sei, minke, bowhead, North Atlantic and southern right whales, and the pygmy right whale. Sixteen species are generally recognised in four families.

Evolution and conservation

Mysticetes diverged from the toothed whales (Odontoceti) roughly 34 million years ago; early forms retained teeth alongside or before baleen. Commercial whaling in the 19th and 20th centuries devastated most populations — several hundred thousand blue whales were killed, for example. Since the International Whaling Commission moratorium of the 1980s some species have recovered substantially (notably humpbacks), while others, such as the North Atlantic right whale, remain critically endangered, threatened now chiefly by ship strikes and fishing-gear entanglement rather than hunting.
