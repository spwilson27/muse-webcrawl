Integral imaging

Integral imaging is a three-dimensional imaging technique that captures and reproduces a light field using a two-dimensional array of microlenses (a fly's-eye lens array) placed in front of a camera sensor or display. First described by Gabriel Lippmann in 1908 as integral photography, it records many small elemental images, each from a slightly different viewpoint.

Reconstruction

When the recorded elemental images are displayed behind a matching lens array, the rays reconstruct a full-parallax 3D scene that changes correctly with viewpoint, without special glasses. Resolution is traded against the number of viewpoints: each elemental image contributes spatial detail, while the array provides angular information.

Applications

Integral imaging has been investigated for 3D television and displays, microscopy, medical imaging and refocusing — a digital cousin of plenoptic (light-field) cameras, which descend from the same principle.

References
