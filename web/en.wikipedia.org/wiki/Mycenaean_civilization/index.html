Mycenaean civilization

Mycenaean civilization (also called Mycenaean Greece) was the last phase of the Bronze Age in ancient Greece, spanning roughly 1750–1050 BC. Named after the palace centre of Mycenae in the Argolid, it represents the first advanced and distinctively Greek civilization in mainland Greece, notable for its palatial states, urban organization, monumental architecture, and the earliest records of the Greek language, written in the Linear B script.

Chronology

Scholars divide Mycenaean civilization into the early period of the shaft graves (c. 1750–1450 BC), the palatial period (c. 1450–1200 BC) in which fortified palace complexes administered redistributive economies, and the post-palatial period (c. 1200–1050 BC) after the palaces fell.

Society and administration

Each palace state was ruled by a king, the wanax, recorded in Linear B as wa-na-ka, supported by a bureaucracy of scribes who inventoried land, livestock, rations, textiles, bronze, and offerings on clay tablets. Beneath the wanax stood the lawagetas ("leader of the people"), landowners, officials, priests, craftsmen, and slaves. The palaces controlled production of prestige goods — perfumed oil, fine pottery, textiles, and metalwork — traded around the Mediterranean from Egypt and the Levant to Italy and beyond.

Warfare and material culture

Mycenaean culture was famously martial: frescoes, seals, and grave goods depict chariots, tower and figure-eight shields, bronze swords and spears, and boar's-tusk helmets. Fortifications at Mycenae, Tiryns, and Athens used Cyclopean masonry. Elite burials included shaft graves and later chamber tombs and great domed tholos tombs such as the Treasury of Atreus.

Religion and language

Linear B tablets name many gods of the later Greek pantheon — Zeus, Hera, Poseidon (especially prominent at Pylos), Athena, Artemis, Hermes, and Dionysus — showing that core elements of Greek religion were already in place. Mycenaean Greek is the oldest attested dialect of Greek, preserved in several thousand tablets from palace archives baked hard in the fires that destroyed the palaces.

Collapse

Around 1200 BC the palatial system collapsed amid widespread destruction, part of the wider Late Bronze Age collapse whose causes remain debated — proposals include invasion or migration (the "Sea Peoples" and Dorian invasion traditions), internal revolt, earthquakes, drought and climate change, and the breakdown of interdependent trade networks. Writing was lost, and Greece entered the Greek Dark Ages; memories of the Mycenaean world survived in oral epic and were later embodied in the Homeric poems.
