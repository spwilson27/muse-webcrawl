Transfer of merit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The transfer of merit is a standard part of Buddhist spiritual practice in which the merit (puñña) gained from a good deed — such as giving alms, observing precepts, or meditation — is dedicated to, or shared with, another being: a deceased relative, all sentient beings, or the devas. Rather than being depleted by sharing, the merit of the giver is traditionally held to increase, much as one lamp may light another without diminishing. In Pali the dedication may be expressed as pattidāna (giving of merit) and the beneficiary's rejoicing in the deed as pattānumodanā.

Practice

In Theravada countries such as Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Laos and Cambodia, merit is commonly transferred at the end of religious observances: after offering food to monks, laypeople pour water from a vessel into a bowl while a dedication verse is chanted, symbolising the transfer of the merit to departed relatives. Funerary observances — including almsgiving on the seventh day, third month and annual anniversaries of a death — are largely structured around generating and transferring merit to the deceased, who are believed to benefit if reborn among the petas (hungry ghosts) able to receive it.

Doctrine and debate

The practice is grounded in texts such as the Tirokuḍḍa Sutta and the Petavatthu, in which the Buddha encourages gifts dedicated to departed relatives. Some commentators have questioned how merit can literally pass between persons if karma is strictly individual; the orthodox resolution is that the transfer works through the beneficiary's rejoicing (anumodanā) in the good deed, which is itself a wholesome mental act generating merit for the one who rejoices. In Mahayana Buddhism, the dedication of merit (pariṇāmanā) is universalised: bodhisattvas dedicate all their merit to the enlightenment of all beings, a practice embodied in the vows of figures such as Kṣitigarbha and in the closing verses of texts like Śāntideva's Bodhicaryāvatāra.

Analogues

The idea that spiritual benefit can be shared has analogues elsewhere: the Hindu practice of śrāddha offerings for ancestors, and, in Catholic theology, the notion of a treasury of merit that informs the doctrine of indulgences — a parallel noted by many comparativists, though the underlying theologies differ.
