Group representation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This article is about representations of algebraic groups in mathematics. For other uses, see Representation theory.


Symmetries of a square acting on a vector space give a concrete representation

In the mathematical field of representation theory, group representations describe abstract groups in terms of bijective linear transformations of a vector space to itself (i.e. vector space automorphisms); in particular, they can be used to represent group elements as invertible matrices so that the group operation can be represented by matrix multiplication.

Formally, a representation of a group G on a vector space V over a field F is a group homomorphism from G to GL(V), the general linear group on V.

Key concepts

A representation is called faithful if the homomorphism is injective. A subspace left invariant by all group elements gives a subrepresentation; a representation with no proper non-trivial invariant subspaces is irreducible. For finite groups over the complex numbers, Maschke's theorem guarantees that every representation decomposes as a direct sum of irreducibles. Characters — the traces of the representing matrices — classify representations and underpin the character table of a finite group.

History and applications

The theory was created by Ferdinand Georg Frobenius in the 1890s and developed by Issai Schur, Richard Brauer, and others. Group representations are central to physics: the symmetries of a physical system are encoded in representations of groups such as the rotation group SO(3), the Poincaré group, and the gauge groups of the Standard Model. They also underlie much of modern number theory through the Langlands program, and are used in chemistry to classify molecular vibrations and crystal structures.
