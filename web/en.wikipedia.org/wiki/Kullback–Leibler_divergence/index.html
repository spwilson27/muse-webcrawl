Kullback–Leibler divergence

In mathematical statistics, the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence (also called relative entropy and I-divergence) is a type of statistical distance: a measure of how one probability distribution P is different from a second, reference probability distribution Q. The KL divergence is defined as the expected value, under P, of the logarithmic difference between the probabilities P and Q — the information lost when Q is used to approximate P.

Definition

For discrete probability distributions P and Q, the KL divergence from Q to P is DKL(P ‖ Q) = Σ P(x) log(P(x)/Q(x)). For continuous distributions the sum is replaced by an integral over the densities. By Gibbs' inequality, the divergence is always non-negative, and it is zero if and only if P and Q are identical almost everywhere. However, it is not symmetric — DKL(P ‖ Q) ≠ DKL(Q ‖ P) in general — and does not satisfy the triangle inequality, so it is a divergence rather than a true metric.

History and applications

The measure was introduced by Solomon Kullback and Richard Leibler in 1951 as the directed divergence between two distributions, building on Claude Shannon's information theory. It has become fundamental in statistics, machine learning and information theory: it underlies model selection and the Akaike information criterion, variational inference (where it is minimised to approximate intractable posterior distributions), the training of generative models, and analyses of topic models and neural network losses. A symmetrised version, the Jensen–Shannon divergence, is also widely used.
