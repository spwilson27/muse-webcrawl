Urashima Tarō


Urashima Tarō riding on the turtle to the Dragon Palace

Urashima Tarō (浦島 太郎) is the protagonist of a famous Japanese folktale of the same name, one of the best-known stories in Japanese folklore. The tale concerns a kind-hearted young fisherman who is rewarded for rescuing a sea turtle with a visit to the undersea Dragon Palace (Ryūgū-jō), and who returns home to find that centuries have passed.

The tale

One day the fisherman Urashima Tarō saves a turtle that children are tormenting on the beach. The next day a giant turtle rises from the sea and informs him that the creature he saved was Otohime, daughter of the Dragon King Ryūjin, and that the king wishes to thank him. Tarō rides the turtle to the palace beneath the sea, where he is feasted and entertained by the princess.

After what seems to be three days, homesick, Tarō asks to return home. The princess gives him a parting gift, a mysterious jeweled box called a tamatebako, warning him never to open it. Back on shore, Tarō finds everything changed: his house is gone, his mother is dead, and the village speaks of him as a figure from legend. Three hundred years have passed. Distraught, he forgets the warning and opens the box; white smoke pours out, and Tarō ages instantly into an old man with white hair — the box had contained his old age.

Origins

The story's earliest ancestor appears in the Nihon Shoki (720) and in a poem in the Man'yōshū, concerning a man named Urashimako of Mizunoe. The tale took its familiar form in the Muromachi period otogi-zōshi versions. It is closely related to other "time slip" tales worldwide, such as the Irish story of Oisín and Rip Van Winkle, and it is a classic example of the otherworld-journey motif. "Urashima effect" is a Japanese colloquial term for returning to find one's surroundings utterly changed, and the story is referenced in physics teaching as a folk parallel to relativistic time dilation.

Legacy

Urashima Tarō appears throughout Japanese popular culture — in songs every Japanese schoolchild once knew, in anime and manga, and in place names along the coasts where the events are said to have occurred. Shrines such as Urashima Jinja in Kyoto Prefecture claim association with the legend.
