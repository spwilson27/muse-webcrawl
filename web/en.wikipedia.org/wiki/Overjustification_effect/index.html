Overjustification effect

The overjustification effect is a phenomenon in social psychology in which offering an expected external incentive for an activity a person already finds intrinsically rewarding — such as money, prizes or good grades — decreases that person's intrinsic motivation to perform the activity. When the reward is later removed, interest in the activity may fall below its original level: the person reattributes their behaviour to the reward ("I did it for the prize") rather than to enjoyment.

Key studies

In the classic experiment by Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett (1973), nursery-school children who enjoyed drawing were promised a "Good Player Award" for drawing with special pens. Two weeks later, these children spent less free time drawing than children who had received the award unexpectedly or not at all. Edward Deci's experiments with puzzle-solving adults found a parallel effect for monetary payment. The findings were integrated into self-determination theory by Deci and Richard Ryan, which holds that rewards experienced as controlling undermine perceived autonomy, while informational feedback can support it.

Explanations and debate

Two accounts dominate. Self-perception theory (Daryl Bem) proposes that people infer their motives from salient causes of their behaviour; a conspicuous reward provides an "overjustifying" explanation that discounts intrinsic interest. Cognitive evaluation theory instead focuses on whether the reward is felt as controlling or as affirming competence. Meta-analyses (notably Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999) conclude that expected tangible rewards reliably undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks, while verbal praise tends to enhance it — though the size and practical importance of the effect remain disputed by some researchers.

Applications

The effect is invoked in debates over paying children for grades, merit pay for teachers, gamification and loyalty schemes, paying blood donors, and the design of workplace incentives: rewards bolted onto activities valued for their own sake can crowd out the very motivation they were meant to reinforce.
