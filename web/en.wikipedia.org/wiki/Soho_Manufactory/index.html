Soho Manufactory

The Soho Manufactory (1766–1848) was one of the first modern factories of the Industrial Revolution.

The Soho Manufactory was an early factory built in Handsworth, then just outside Birmingham, England, by the manufacturer Matthew Boulton and his partner John Fothergill. Completed in 1766 at a cost of about £9,000, it brought under one roof the diverse workshops of Boulton's "toy" trade — buttons, buckles, boxes, japanned and ormolu wares, silver and plated goods — employing around a thousand workers and powered initially by a waterwheel (later supplemented by steam). Its architect was William Wyatt, and its elegant stone facade, fronting the Hockley Brook, made it a showpiece visited by industrial tourists from across Europe.

Significance

The Manufactory is regarded as a landmark of the Industrial Revolution: it integrated design, marketing, and production, applied division of labour and cost accounting systematically, and sold fashionable goods to a growing middle-class market at home and abroad. Boulton boasted of selling "what all the world desires to have — POWER," and the site grew to include the adjacent Soho Mint (1788), where steam-powered machinery struck coins for Britain and foreign governments, and the Soho Foundry (1795), which produced Watt steam engines for the partnership of Boulton and Watt.

Decline and demolition

After Boulton's death in 1809 the businesses gradually declined, and the Manufactory building was demolished in the 1850s (commonly dated 1848–1863 in sources; the main block was pulled down in 1863 as the site was redeveloped for housing). Boulton's home, Soho House, survives nearby as a museum, and the area remains associated with his name and Birmingham's industrial heritage.
