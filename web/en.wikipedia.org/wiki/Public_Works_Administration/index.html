Public Works Administration

The Public Works Administration (PWA), part of the New Deal of 1933, was a large-scale public works construction agency in the United States headed by Secretary of the Interior Harold Ickes. It was created by the National Industrial Recovery Act in June 1933.

Programs

The PWA funded and supervised major infrastructure projects built largely by private contractors, including dams, bridges, hospitals, schools, courthouses, water and sewer systems, and electrification. Famous projects include the Grand Coulee Dam, the Triborough Bridge in New York, the Lincoln Tunnel, and large numbers of schools and municipal buildings across the country.

Approach and impact

Unlike the Works Progress Administration (WPA), which hired the unemployed directly for smaller projects, the PWA aimed to stimulate heavy industry and create jobs indirectly through contracts, insisting on careful planning and high construction standards. It spent over $6 billion during the 1930s. Ickes's insistence on honest, non-political administration made the PWA slower to spend than some relief agencies but gave its works a reputation for quality.

End

The agency's role declined as the country shifted to wartime production, and it was abolished in 1943. Many PWA structures remain in use today.
