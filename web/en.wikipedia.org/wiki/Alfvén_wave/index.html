Alfvén wave

[Image placeholder: diagram of an Alfvén wave propagating along magnetic field lines]

In plasma physics, an Alfvén wave, named after Hannes Alfvén, is a type of magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) wave in which ions oscillate in response to a restoring force provided by an effective tension on the magnetic field lines. The wave propagates along the direction of the magnetic field, somewhat like a wave travelling along a plucked string, with the field lines playing the role of the string and the plasma's mass providing the inertia.

Definition

Alfvén predicted the wave in 1942 in a paper on sunspots, work for which he later received the Nobel Prize in Physics (1970). The wave travels at the Alfvén velocity, vA = B / √(μ0ρ), where B is the magnetic field strength, μ0 the vacuum permeability and ρ the mass density of the plasma. In the solar corona this speed can reach thousands of kilometres per second. Alfvén waves are incompressible in the ideal case: they bend the field without changing the plasma density, unlike the fast and slow magnetosonic waves.

Importance

Alfvén waves are observed throughout space plasmas — in the solar wind, in planetary magnetospheres and in laboratory devices. They are thought to carry energy from the Sun's surface into the corona and solar wind, and are studied as a possible mechanism for coronal heating and for accelerating the solar wind. In magnetic confinement fusion, unwanted Alfvénic instabilities driven by fast particles are an active area of research.
