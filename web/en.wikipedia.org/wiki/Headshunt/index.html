Headshunt

A headshunt (or escape track in the United States) is a short length of railway track used to allow a locomotive to reach the opposite end of its train. Such sidings were often installed to allow locomotives to move from one track to another at termini and small stations without occupying the main line. The term also describes a short spur or neck of track into which a locomotive or wagons are drawn clear of points before reversing direction.

Use

In a typical run-round layout, a locomotive that has hauled a train into a terminal platform draws forward into the headshunt, then reverses through a crossover or loop onto the adjacent track and backs onto the other end of the coaches. Headshunts are also found in goods yards, where they form the neck from which sidings fan out, allowing a shunting locomotive to pull wagons clear of the sidings before propelling them into another road. A headshunt must be long enough to hold the locomotive (or the cut of wagons being shunted) plus a safety margin, and is often protected by a buffer stop or trap points.

Related layouts

Where space is tight, a turntable, traverser or a simple run-round loop may substitute. The related term "shunt neck" is common in British railway usage for the lead track of a yard.
