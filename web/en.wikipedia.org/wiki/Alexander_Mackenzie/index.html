Sir Alexander Mackenzie (c. 1764 – 12 March 1820) was a Scottish explorer and fur trader known for accomplishing the first east-to-west crossing of North America north of Mexico, reaching the Pacific Ocean in 1793 — more than a decade before the Lewis and Clark Expedition — and for his earlier voyage down the river that now bears his name to the Arctic Ocean.

Early life and the fur trade

Born in Stornoway on the Isle of Lewis, Mackenzie emigrated to Montreal in 1779 and entered the fur trade, joining the North West Company in 1787. Posted to the remote Athabasca country, he co-founded Fort Chipewyan on Lake Athabasca in 1788.

Voyage to the Arctic, 1789

Seeking a river route to the Pacific, Mackenzie set out from Fort Chipewyan in June 1789 and followed the great river flowing north-west from Great Slave Lake — guided by Indigenous knowledge along the way — reaching its delta on the Arctic Ocean in July. Disappointed that the river did not lead to the Pacific, he reportedly called it the "River of Disappointment"; it is now the Mackenzie River, Canada's longest.

Crossing to the Pacific, 1792–1793

After studying surveying in England, Mackenzie tried again in October 1792, ascending the Peace River, crossing the Rocky Mountains and descending the Fraser and Bella Coola river systems with the help of Indigenous guides. On 20 July 1793 he reached the Pacific at Dean Channel near Bella Bella, where he inscribed on a rock, in vermilion grease: "Alexander Mackenzie, from Canada, by land, 22nd July 1793." This was the first recorded crossing of the North American continent north of Mexico.

Later life

His account, Voyages from Montreal (1801), became a bestseller and influenced both British policy and Thomas Jefferson's planning of American exploration. Knighted in 1802, Mackenzie served in the legislature of Lower Canada and later retired to Scotland, where he died in 1820.
