Johnson–Nyquist noise

Also calledThermal noise, Johnson noise
First measuredJohn B. Johnson, 1926–27
Explained byHarry Nyquist, 1928
Key formulaNoise power = 4kTRΔf (voltage spectral density)

Johnson–Nyquist noise (also called thermal noise or Johnson noise) is the electronic noise generated by the thermal agitation of charge carriers — usually electrons — inside an electrical conductor at equilibrium, regardless of any applied voltage. It is present in every resistor and sets a fundamental limit on the sensitivity of electronic circuits and measurements.

History and theory

The phenomenon was first measured by John Bertrand Johnson at Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1926 and published in 1928; his colleague Harry Nyquist provided the theoretical explanation the same year using thermodynamics and equipartition. Nyquist's result shows that the mean-square noise voltage across a resistor R at absolute temperature T, over bandwidth Δf, is proportional to 4kTRΔf, where k is Boltzmann's constant — the noise is white, with power spectral density independent of frequency until quantum corrections matter at high frequencies or low temperatures.

Applications

Johnson–Nyquist noise limits amplifiers, radio receivers and imaging sensors, and conversely can be exploited for precision thermometry: measuring the noise of a resistor gives its absolute temperature. It is also the basis of the Kirchhoff-law–Johnson-noise secure key exchange scheme.
