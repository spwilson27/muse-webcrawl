The Twenty-six Martyrs of Japan were a group of Catholics who were executed by crucifixion on 5 February 1597 at Nagasaki, Japan, on the orders of the ruler Toyotomi Hideyoshi. The group consisted of three Japanese Jesuits including the preacher Paul Miki, six European Franciscan missionaries including Peter Baptist and Felipe de Jesús, and seventeen Japanese laymen, among them three young altar boys. They are the first canonised martyrs of the Church in Japan.

Background

Christianity had been introduced to Japan by Francis Xavier in 1549 and spread rapidly, gaining perhaps 300,000 converts by the 1590s. Hideyoshi, initially tolerant, grew suspicious of the missionaries' ties to Spanish and Portuguese colonial power. The immediate trigger for the arrests was the wreck of the Spanish galleon San Felipe off Shikoku in 1596, whose pilot was reported to have boasted that missionaries prepared the way for Spanish conquest.

Arrest and journey

The twenty-six were arrested in Kyoto and Osaka in December 1596 and January 1597. A portion of each man's left ear was cut off, and they were marched roughly 800 kilometres through winter weather to Nagasaki, preaching and singing along the way. Two additional Christians who followed the procession to minister to the condemned were arrested and added to the group, bringing the total to twenty-six.

Execution and veneration

On Nishizaka Hill in Nagasaki the martyrs were fastened to crosses and killed with spears. Onlookers collected relics, and accounts of the execution spread through the Catholic world. The twenty-six were beatified in 1627 and canonised by Pope Pius IX on 8 June 1862, the first saints canonised from the Far East. The site is now marked by the Twenty-Six Martyrs Museum and Monument in Nagasaki, and the Oura Church nearby is dedicated in their memory.
