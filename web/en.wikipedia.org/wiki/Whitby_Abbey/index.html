Whitby Abbey is a ruined Benedictine abbey overlooking the North Sea on the East Cliff above Whitby in North Yorkshire, England. The first monastery on the site was founded in 657 AD by the Anglo-Saxon king Oswy of Northumbria, with Hilda of Whitby as its first abbess. It hosted the Synod of Whitby in 664, at which the Northumbrian church decided to adopt the Roman method of calculating Easter.

History

The monastery was home to the poet Cædmon, the earliest English poet whose name is known. The settlement was destroyed by Danish Viking raiders in the 9th century and refounded as a Benedictine house after the Norman Conquest. The surviving Gothic church ruins date mainly from the 13th century. The abbey was suppressed in 1539 during the Dissolution of the Monasteries under Henry VIII, and its buildings were subsequently quarried for stone; the nave was also damaged by German naval shelling in December 1914.

In literature

The abbey ruins and the town of Whitby feature in Bram Stoker's 1897 novel Dracula: the Demeter runs aground at Whitby, and Count Dracula comes ashore in the form of a black dog, ascending the 199 steps to St Mary's churchyard beside the abbey.
