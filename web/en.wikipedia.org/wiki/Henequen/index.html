Henequen (Agave fourcroydes) is an agave whose leaves yield a hard fibre traditionally used to make rope and twine. It is native to Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, where it was domesticated by the Maya — known in Yucatec Maya as ki — and became the region's defining export crop in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Cultivation and fibre

The plant grows a rosette of thick, spiny leaves up to about 2.5 metres long on a short stem. Leaves are harvested after several years, then crushed and scraped (traditionally by machine decorticators) to extract the long white fibres, which are dried in the sun and baled. The fibre is similar to sisal, which comes from Agave sisalana; henequen fibre is somewhat coarser and was used mainly for binder twine, sacks, hammocks, and ropes.

History

From the 1880s, demand for binder twine from North American grain harvests made henequen "green gold" (oro verde) for Yucatán. Vast haciendas organised production under harsh labour conditions, generating great wealth for a small planter oligarchy. The Mexican Revolution and the later invention of synthetic fibres collapsed the industry in the mid-20th century, though henequen is still produced for speciality goods and traditional crafts. Henequen is also used to produce a distilled agave spirit in Yucatán.
