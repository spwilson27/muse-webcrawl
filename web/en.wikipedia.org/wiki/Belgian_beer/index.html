Belgian beer

Belgian beer culture encompasses a wide variety of styles, flavours, and brewing traditions, and is renowned throughout the world. In 2016, UNESCO inscribed Belgian beer culture on its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Belgium produces hundreds of different beers, from pale lagers and wheat beers to spontaneously fermented lambics and strong abbey ales.

History

Brewing in the region dates back to the Middle Ages, when monasteries brewed beer for pilgrims and for their own sustenance. After the French Revolution destroyed many monastic breweries, brewing revived in the 19th and 20th centuries. The Trappist breweries — where monks brew within monastery walls — became famous for their strong dubbels and tripels. After the Second World War, Belgian brewing consolidated around large lager producers, but a revival of interest in traditional styles from the 1970s onward, championed by writers such as Michael Jackson, restored the country's reputation for diversity.

Styles

Notable Belgian styles include:

  • Trappist and abbey ales — strong, complex ales such as Westvleteren, Chimay, and Rochefort.
  • Lambic and gueuze — spontaneously fermented sour beers from the Pajottenland, sometimes flavoured with cherries (kriek) or raspberries (framboise).
  • Flanders red ale and oud bruin — aged, tart ales of West and East Flanders.
  • Witbier — wheat beers spiced with coriander and orange peel, such as Hoegaarden.
  • Saison — dry, refreshing farmhouse ales from Wallonia.
  • Belgian pale ale and golden ale — e.g. Duvel, a strong golden ale.

Serving customs

Most Belgian beers are traditionally served in their own branded glass, the shape of which is said to enhance the beer's aroma and flavour. Beer is also used extensively in Belgian cooking, for example in the stew carbonnade flamande.
