Long-exposure photography

Long-exposure, time-exposure, or slow-shutter photography involves using a long-duration shutter speed to sharply capture the stationary elements of images while blurring, smearing, or obscuring the moving elements. Long-exposure photography captures one element that conventional photography does not: time.

The paths of bright moving objects become clearly visible — clouds form broad bands, vehicle lights draw bright streaks, ocean waves appear misty, and stars trace arcs across the night sky (star trails).

Technique

Long exposures are typically achieved with a tripod to keep the camera steady and shutter speeds ranging from a fraction of a second to several hours. In daylight, neutral-density filters are used to reduce the light reaching the sensor so that long shutter speeds can be achieved without overexposure. In digital cameras, "bulb" mode and intervalometers allow very long single exposures, while multiple shorter exposures can be stacked to reduce noise.

Applications

Night and low-light photography

At night, long exposures capture city lights, fireworks, lightning and the Milky Way; light painting involves moving a light source through the frame during the exposure.

Motion effects

Exposures of a few seconds render waterfalls and surf as smooth, mist-like textures and blur moving crowds, while exposures of minutes can make busy streets appear empty.

History

In the earliest decades of photography, all exposures were long because of the low sensitivity of photographic materials; portraits required sitters to hold still, aided by head braces. As emulsions and sensors grew faster, long exposure became a deliberate creative choice rather than a necessity.
