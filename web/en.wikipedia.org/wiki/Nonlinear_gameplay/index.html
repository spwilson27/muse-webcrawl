Nonlinear gameplay

A video game with nonlinear gameplay presents players with challenges that can be completed in a number of different sequences. By contrast, a game with linear gameplay forces the player through a fixed sequence of challenges. Nonlinearity may appear in branching storylines, multiple endings, optional objectives, open worlds, or freedom in how individual encounters are approached.

Forms of nonlinearity

Early examples include the open exploration of The Legend of Zelda (1986) and the mission freedom of Elite (1984). Branching narratives, as in visual novels and role-playing games such as Fallout, let player choices alter the plot. The sandbox game removes win conditions almost entirely, while the immersive sim (e.g., Deus Ex) offers emergent solutions to problems using interacting systems. "Open world" design, popularized by Grand Theft Auto III (2001), combines a large free-roaming map with a loosely ordered set of missions.

Design considerations

Nonlinear design increases player agency and replay value but raises development cost: content must be authored for paths many players will never see, and testing difficulty rises with the number of possible sequences. Designers often use gating (abilities or keys that unlock areas) to blend freedom with a controlled difficulty curve, as in the "Metroidvania" genre.
