Moabite is an extinct Canaanite language, closely related to Biblical Hebrew, spoken in the ancient kingdom of Moab east of the Dead Sea during the early first millennium BC. It is known from a small corpus of inscriptions written in a script closely related to the Phoenician and Hebrew alphabets.

Corpus

The principal Moabite text is the Mesha Stele (c. 840 BC), a 34-line royal inscription of King Mesha found at Dhiban (biblical Dibon) in 1868, now in the Louvre. Other texts include the shorter Kerak Inscription, a few fragmentary inscriptions and seals, and an inscription found at el-Kerak and fragments from Dibon. Together these provide the main evidence for Moabite grammar and vocabulary.

Features

Moabite closely resembles Hebrew in phonology, morphology, and syntax. Notable features include the plural ending -în (as opposed to Hebrew -îm), the preservation of certain diphthongs, and the use of the relative pronoun 'šr. Because of the small corpus and its similarity to Hebrew, some scholars treat Moabite as a dialect of a broader Canaanite continuum rather than a sharply separate language. Differences from Hebrew, such as the article and certain verb forms, suggest close kinship with the dialects spoken in Ammon and Edom as well.
