Rapa Nui (also called te reʻo Rapa Nui) is an Eastern Polynesian language spoken on Easter Island (Rapa Nui), a special territory of Chile in the southeastern Pacific Ocean. It is the native language of the Rapa Nui people, descendants of the Polynesian voyagers who settled the island centuries ago.

Characteristics

Like other Polynesian languages, Rapa Nui has a small phoneme inventory — ten consonants and five vowels, with vowel length contrastive — and a basically verb-initial word order, extensive use of particles to mark tense, aspect, and case, and productive reduplication. The glottal stop, written with an apostrophe-like character (ʻ), is a full consonant, as in the island's name Rapa Nui itself. The language is closely related to Tahitian and Marquesan, though the island's extreme isolation gave it distinctive features; heavy contact with Spanish since Chile's annexation in 1888 has added many loanwords.

Status and revitalization

Most islanders are bilingual in Spanish, and intergenerational transmission of Rapa Nui has weakened, leading UNESCO to classify the language as definitely endangered, with only about 2,500 fluent speakers. Revitalization efforts include Rapa Nui language immersion in kindergartens (such as the Hōŋaʻa Reʻo programme) and schooling, bilingual signage, and cultural festivals celebrating Rapa Nui identity. The island's unique rongorongo script — wooden tablets incised with glyphs — remains undeciphered and is not a writing system for the modern language.
