Robinson–Patman Act

EnactedJune 19, 1936
Citation15 U.S.C. §§ 13–13b, 21a
AmendsClayton Antitrust Act of 1914, Section 2
SponsorsSen. Joseph T. Robinson; Rep. Wright Patman

The Robinson–Patman Act of 1936 is a United States federal law that prohibits anticompetitive price discrimination by producers, manufacturers and distributors of commodities. Formally an amendment to Section 2 of the Clayton Act, it forbids a seller from charging different prices to different purchasers of goods of like grade and quality where the effect may be substantially to lessen competition — most famously, to injure small retailers competing with large chains.

Background

The Act grew out of the 1930s backlash against chain stores such as A&P, which could extract lower wholesale prices and promotional allowances unavailable to independent grocers. Investigations by the Federal Trade Commission documented these advantages, and Congress responded with a statute designed to protect small businesses from discriminatory pricing.

Provisions and defences

The Act prohibits price discrimination, certain brokerage allowances, and discriminatory promotional services, but provides defences: price differences may be justified by cost differences in manufacture or delivery, by changing market conditions, or by meeting a competitor's price in good faith. Sellers and knowing buyers may both be liable; the FTC and private plaintiffs can enforce it.

Criticism and enforcement

Economists have long criticised the Act for protecting competitors rather than competition and potentially raising consumer prices; government enforcement largely ceased for decades, with most actions brought privately. The law nonetheless remains in force and has periodically returned to enforcement attention.
