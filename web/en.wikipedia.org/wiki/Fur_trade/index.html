Fur trade (North America)
Peak period17th – mid-19th centuries

The fur trade is the worldwide buying and selling of animal pelts, an industry of major historical importance in the colonisation of North America, Siberia and the North Pacific. From the 1500s to the 1800s, European demand for felt hats, coats and trimmings drove French, British, Dutch, Russian and American traders deep into indigenous territories; in exchange for furs, Native peoples received metal tools, firearms, cloth and alcohol — exchanges that transformed indigenous economies, alliances and warfare, and spread epidemic disease.

Voyageurs carrying trade goods and furs by canoe were the transport backbone of the Canadian fur trade.

North America

The beaver trade began in the St Lawrence valley in the early 1600s. France's colony of New France depended on it, licensing coureurs des bois and later employing voyageurs to move goods between Montreal and the Great Lakes. England's Hudson's Bay Company (chartered 1670) traded from posts on Hudson Bay, and after Britain took Canada in 1763 the rival Montreal-based North West Company pushed as far as the Pacific before the two merged in 1821. In the United States, John Jacob Astor's American Fur Company and the Rocky Mountain rendezvous system organised the mountain men of the 1820s–30s, while Russian traders hunted sea otters along the Alaska coast. The trade declined as silk replaced beaver felt in fashion and beaver populations collapsed from over-trapping.

Siberia and beyond

Russia's expansion across Siberia from the 1580s was likewise driven by fur — above all sable — exacted as tribute (yasak) from indigenous peoples, and furs formed a major share of the Muscovite state's revenue. Today the fur trade continues in farmed mink and fox and in regulated wild trapping, but it is shadowed by animal-welfare campaigns, fashion-house fur bans and import restrictions in many countries.
