A bill of rights, sometimes called a declaration of rights or a charter of rights, is a formal list of the most important rights of the citizens of a country, intended to protect those rights against infringement by public authorities and private citizens. Bills of rights may be entrenched in a constitution — making them difficult to amend — or contained in ordinary legislation. They are a cornerstone of liberal constitutionalism.

History

The concept descends from documents such as Magna Carta (1215) and the English Petition of Right (1628). The Bill of Rights 1689, enacted after the Glorious Revolution, settled the rights of Parliament and subjects against the Crown in England — guaranteeing free elections, freedom of speech in Parliament, and prohibiting excessive bail and cruel and unusual punishments. The Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) generalised the idea into universal principles. The United States Bill of Rights — the first ten amendments to the U.S. Constitution, ratified in 1791 — protects freedoms of speech, religion, and the press, the right to bear arms, due process, trial by jury, and other liberties, and has been applied to state governments through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Modern bills of rights

After the Second World War, rights protection expanded internationally through the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and the European Convention on Human Rights (1950). Many democracies adopted constitutional charters, such as Canada's Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982) and South Africa's Bill of Rights (1996). The United Kingdom, traditionally without a codified charter, incorporated the European Convention through the Human Rights Act 1998. Debates continue over whether entrenched bills of rights improperly transfer power from elected legislatures to judges.
