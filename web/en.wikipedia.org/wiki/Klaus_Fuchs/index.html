Emil Julius Klaus Fuchs (29 December 1911 – 28 January 1988) was a German theoretical physicist and atomic spy who supplied information from the American, British, and Canadian Manhattan Project to the Soviet Union during and shortly after World War II. While at the Los Alamos Laboratory, Fuchs was responsible for many significant theoretical calculations relating to the first nuclear weapons and, later, early models of the hydrogen bomb. After his conviction in 1950, he served nine years in prison in the United Kingdom, then migrated to East Germany, where he resumed his career as a physicist.

Early life

Fuchs was born in Rüsselsheim, Grand Duchy of Hesse, the third of four children of Emil Fuchs, a Lutheran pastor and professor of theology, and Else Wagner. He studied mathematics and physics at the University of Leipzig and the University of Kiel. A member first of the Social Democratic Party and then, in 1932, of the Communist Party of Germany, Fuchs fled to Britain after the Reichstag fire of 1933, fearing arrest by the Nazi regime. He continued his studies at the University of Bristol under Nevill Mott, earning a PhD in 1937, and then worked with Max Born at the University of Edinburgh, receiving a DSc in 1939.

Wartime work

At the outbreak of the Second World War, Fuchs was interned briefly as an enemy alien, first on the Isle of Man and then in Canada, but was released in 1941. He returned to Edinburgh and was recruited by Rudolf Peierls to work on the British atomic bomb project, known as Tube Alloys. He became a British subject in 1942. In 1943 he travelled to the United States as part of the British mission to the Manhattan Project, working first at Columbia University on gaseous diffusion and then, from 1944, at Los Alamos under Hans Bethe in the Theoretical Division, where he worked on implosion calculations for the plutonium bomb.

Espionage

Driven by his communist convictions, Fuchs began passing information to Soviet intelligence in 1941, initially through the GRU and later the NKGB. At Los Alamos he provided detailed data on the plutonium weapon design, including the implosion lens system, and later, after returning to Britain to head the theoretical physics division at Harwell, information on the uranium isotope separation programme and early hydrogen bomb concepts. His couriers included Harry Gold in the United States. Fuchs's espionage is widely regarded as having significantly accelerated the Soviet atomic bomb programme.

Arrest, trial and imprisonment

Fuchs came under suspicion after signals intelligence (the Venona project) identified a source codenamed "CHARLES". After surveillance and interrogation by MI5 officer William Skardon, Fuchs confessed in January 1950. He was arrested on 2 February 1950 and charged under the Official Secrets Act. At his trial at the Old Bailey on 1 March 1950, he pleaded guilty and was sentenced by Lord Goddard to fourteen years' imprisonment, the maximum for the offence. Stripped of his British citizenship, he served nine years, mostly at Wakefield Prison, and was released in June 1959 for good behaviour.

Later life in East Germany

On his release, Fuchs emigrated to the German Democratic Republic. He resumed scientific work at the Central Institute for Nuclear Research at Rossendorf, near Dresden, eventually becoming its deputy director. He was elected to the Academy of Sciences and the central committee of the Socialist Unity Party, and received several East German honours, including the Karl Marx Order. He married Margarete Keilson in 1959. Fuchs died in East Berlin on 28 January 1988.

Assessment

Historians debate the precise value of Fuchs's information, but there is broad agreement that his reports on implosion and on the gaseous diffusion process saved the Soviet programme considerable time. His case also intensified American distrust of British security, contributing to restrictions on nuclear information sharing.
