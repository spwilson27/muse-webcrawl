Mobile phones and driving safety

Mobile phones and driving safety concerns the relationship between the use of mobile phones while operating a motor vehicle and the risk of traffic collisions. A large body of research indicates that using a phone while driving — particularly for texting or other visual-manual tasks — significantly increases crash risk, because it diverts the driver's visual, manual and cognitive attention from the road. The issue is a major element of the wider problem of distracted driving.

Risk

Epidemiological and naturalistic driving studies have consistently found that phone use while driving raises the probability of a crash or near-crash. Case-crossover studies of crash-involved drivers estimated that talking on a phone roughly quadrupled crash risk, a figure comparable to driving at the legal blood-alcohol limit in some jurisdictions. Texting is generally considered more dangerous still, because it combines all three forms of distraction: the driver's eyes leave the road, one or both hands leave the wheel, and attention is absorbed by composing or reading a message. Naturalistic studies using instrumented vehicles found that dialling, reaching for a phone and texting were among the riskiest secondary tasks a driver can perform, with texting associated with a many-fold increase in safety-critical events for novice drivers.

Cognitive distraction

Even hands-free conversation can impair driving. Research on inattention blindness suggests that drivers engaged in a phone conversation may fail to perceive objects directly in their field of view, and reaction times to hazards increase. Because of this, many safety researchers argue that the cognitive component of distraction persists whether or not the phone is held, though visual-manual tasks remain the most hazardous. Simulator studies show that phone conversations reduce the processing of traffic-relevant information and narrow the driver's useful field of view.

Legislation

Many countries and sub-national jurisdictions restrict phone use while driving. The United Kingdom banned handheld phone use while driving in 2003, with penalties later increased. In the United States, regulation is set at state level: most states ban texting for all drivers, and a growing number prohibit all handheld use. The European Union and countries such as Australia, Japan and India have similar restrictions. Enforcement and penalty severity vary widely, and studies of the effect of bans on crash rates have produced mixed results, partly because hands-free use usually remains legal.

Technology and countermeasures

Countermeasures include "do not disturb while driving" modes on smartphones, apps that block incoming notifications, in-vehicle voice assistants, and driver-monitoring systems that warn of inattention. Public education campaigns, employer policies and graduated licensing restrictions for young drivers are also widely used. Safety bodies such as the U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and the World Health Organization have published guidance urging drivers to keep phones out of reach or switched off while driving.
