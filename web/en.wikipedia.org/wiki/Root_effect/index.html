Root effect

The Root effect is a physiological phenomenon found in the blood of many fish, in which a decrease in pH (an increase in acidity or carbon dioxide concentration) greatly reduces both the oxygen affinity and the oxygen-carrying capacity of haemoglobin. It is a more extreme version of the Bohr effect, and it is named after the British physiologist R. W. Root, who described it in 1931.

Mechanism

In the Bohr effect, falling pH shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the right — haemoglobin's affinity falls, but at high oxygen partial pressures the blood can still become fully saturated. In the Root effect, protonation stabilizes the T (tense) state of haemoglobin so strongly that even at very high oxygen partial pressures the molecule cannot fully convert to the R (relaxed) state; maximum saturation can drop well below 100 percent. The effect arises from a cluster of ionizable groups, including a serine residue in fish haemoglobins where most vertebrate haemoglobins have an asparagine, which locks the T state when protonated.

Function

The Root effect is essential for filling the swim bladder of fish. Blood passing through the rete mirabile, a countercurrent bundle of capillaries near the swim bladder, is acidified by lactic acid and carbon dioxide released from a gas gland. Because of the Root effect, haemoglobin unloads oxygen even against a high oxygen pressure, allowing oxygen to accumulate in the swim bladder — in deep-sea fish, against pressures of hundreds of atmospheres.

A similar mechanism works in the eye. The choroid rete behind the retina acidifies the blood, and Root-effect haemoglobin releases oxygen to supply the poorly vascularized retina with the high oxygen tensions it requires.

Occurrence

The Root effect is widespread among teleost fish, especially those with swim bladders or a choroid rete, and is also found in some other aquatic animals. It is absent from the blood of air-breathing land vertebrates, whose haemoglobins show only the ordinary Bohr effect.
