Medical test

Medical test
TypesLaboratory (blood, urine), imaging, physiological, genetic, biopsy
PurposesDiagnosis, screening, monitoring, prognosis
Key measuresSensitivity, specificity, predictive values

A medical test is a medical procedure performed to detect, diagnose or monitor diseases, disease processes, susceptibility, or to determine a course of treatment. Medical tests include blood tests and other laboratory analyses of body fluids and tissues, medical imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound), physiological measurements (electrocardiography, spirometry), genetic tests, and examination of biopsies by pathology.

Purposes

Tests are used for diagnosis in people with symptoms; screening of asymptomatic people at risk (for example mammography or newborn heel-prick tests); monitoring of disease progress or treatment response; and prognosis. Examples include tumour markers such as prostate-specific antigen (PSA), iron studies including transferrin saturation in haemochromatosis, and vision tests in optometry.

Accuracy

No test is perfect. Performance is described by sensitivity (the proportion of people with the disease who test positive) and specificity (the proportion without the disease who test negative). Whether a positive result means disease is likely also depends on how common the condition is in the population tested (pre-test probability), which is why screening low-risk populations yields many false positives. Results are often interpreted against a reference range derived from healthy populations, and increasingly as part of precision medicine, which tailors interpretation and treatment to the individual.

Risks and limitations

Tests carry costs and harms: pain, radiation exposure, false alarms leading to further invasive investigation, overdiagnosis of conditions that would never have caused harm, and anxiety. Good practice — and bodies such as the Choosing Wisely campaign — emphasise ordering tests only when the result is likely to change management.

Regulation

In-vitro diagnostic tests are regulated as medical devices (for example by the FDA in the United States and under the IVDR in the European Union), and laboratories are subject to quality accreditation, internal controls and external proficiency testing.
