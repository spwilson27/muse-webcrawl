Boulton and Watt was an early British engineering and manufacturing firm in the business of designing and making marine and stationary steam engines. Founded in the English West Midlands around Birmingham in 1775 as a partnership between the English manufacturer Matthew Boulton and the Scottish engineer James Watt, the firm had a major role in bringing the Industrial Revolution to Britain and the rest of the world.

History

The partnership was formed after Watt patented his separate-condenser steam engine in 1769 and Boulton, owner of the Soho Manufactory, acquired a share in the patent. An Act of Parliament in 1775 extended Watt's patent until 1800, giving the firm a near-monopoly on efficient steam engines. The first engines were erected in 1776, initially for pumping water from mines in Cornwall and for blowing air into blast furnaces.

In the 1780s Watt's improvements — the double-acting engine, the sun-and-planet gear that converted reciprocating motion into rotary motion, and the parallel motion linkage — let Boulton and Watt engines drive machinery in textile mills, flour mills and breweries. The firm famously charged royalties based on one third of the fuel savings compared with the older Newcomen engine.

The Soho Foundry

As demand grew, the partners opened the Soho Foundry in 1795–96, one of the first purpose-built factories for steam-engine manufacture, where components were made to standardised designs and assembled under one roof. James Watt's sons, James Watt Jr. and Matthew Robinson Boulton, took over the business in the early 19th century, and the firm diversified into marine engines, mint machinery (it built the steam-powered Royal Mint presses and coining equipment for mints worldwide) and gas lighting.

Legacy

By the time the original patents expired in 1800, roughly 500 Boulton and Watt engines had been built. Surviving engines, such as the 1812 Lap Engine and the Crofton Beam Engines, are preserved in museums. The firm's archive is a major source for the history of the Industrial Revolution, and Watt's name was later given to the SI unit of power, the watt.
