Tomato

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Ripe tomatoes on the vine]

The tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is the edible berry of the tomato plant, a species originating in western South America and Central America. It is consumed raw in salads, cooked in sauces, soups and countless dishes, and processed into juice, paste, ketchup and canned tomatoes; it is the world's most valuable vegetable crop by production among vegetables.

Brought to Europe after the Spanish colonisation of the Americas, the tomato was long grown as an ornamental and suspected of poison (its leaves are toxic and its acidity leached lead from pewter). By the 19th century it had become indispensable to Italian cuisine — pizza, pasta sauces — and to cuisines worldwide, from Indian curries to Mexican salsas.

1. Botany and varieties

Botany and varieties

Botanically a fruit (a berry), the tomato is treated in cooking as a vegetable — a distinction the US Supreme Court endorsed for tariff purposes in Nix v. Hedden (1893). Thousands of varieties range from cherry and plum (Roma) tomatoes to beefsteaks and treasured heirlooms; the San Marzano of Campania holds DOP status for sauce.
