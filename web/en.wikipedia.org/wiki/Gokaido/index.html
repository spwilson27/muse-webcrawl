Gokaido

The Gokaidō (Japanese: 五街道, "five routes") were the five principal highways of Edo-period Japan, radiating from Nihonbashi in Edo (modern Tokyo) and maintained by the Tokugawa shogunate. They were travelled by daimyō processions on the sankin-kōtai system of alternate attendance, by officials, merchants and pilgrims, and were lined with post stations (shukuba) providing lodging, food, horses and porters.

The routes

The most famous of the five was the Tōkaidō, linking Edo with Kyoto via the coast, immortalised in Hiroshige's woodblock series The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō. The Nakasendō offered an inland alternative through the Japan Alps; post towns such as Magome and Tsumago on its Kiso Valley section are now celebrated preserved settlements. The other three routes were shorter: the Kōshū Kaidō to Kai Province, the Ōshū Kaidō north toward Mutsu, and the Nikkō Kaidō leading to the shrine of Tokugawa Ieyasu.

Administration

The shogunate regulated the highways through road magistrates (dōchū-bugyō), maintained milestones (ichirizuka) at regular intervals, and required travellers to carry permits at checkpoints (sekisho), which especially monitored the movement of women and firearms. The starting point for all distances in Japan remains Nihonbashi, where a marker still stands today. Many modern highways and the Tōkaidō Shinkansen follow the corridors of the old routes.
