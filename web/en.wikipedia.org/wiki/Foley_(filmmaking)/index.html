Foley (filmmaking)

For other uses, see Foley (disambiguation).

Foley is the reproduction of everyday sound effects that are added to films, videos, and other media in post-production to enhance audio quality. These reproduced sounds, named after sound-effects artist Jack Foley, can be anything from the swishing of clothing and footsteps to squeaky doors and breaking glass. Foley sounds are used to enhance the auditory experience of the movie. Foley can also be used to cover up unwanted sounds captured on the set of a movie during filming, such as the sounds of passing airplanes or traffic.

History

What is now called Foley originated as adding sounds live from a radio play studio in the early 1920s. Jack Donovan Foley began work with Universal Studios in 1914 during the silent movie era. When Warner Studios released its first film to include sound, The Jazz Singer, Universal knew it needed to stay competitive and called for any employees who had radio experience to come forward. Foley became part of the sound crew that turned Universal's then-silent Show Boat into a musical. Because microphones of the time could not pick up more than dialogue, other sounds had to be added after the film was shot. Foley and his small crew projected the film on a screen while recording a single track of audio that captured their live sound effects. Their timing had to be perfect so that footsteps and closing doors synchronized with the actors' motions in the film.

Process

Foley is created by the Foley artist on a Foley stage, a specialized sound studio equipped with viewing equipment and recording space. The Foley artist watches the film and creates the sounds in real time, using props ranging from shoes and clothing to broken glass, chairs, and doors. Foley is usually broken down into three main categories: feet (footsteps), moves (the movement of clothing), and specifics (any other individual sounds, from a door creak to a glass breaking).

Common tricks

Foley artists use many ordinary objects to simulate sounds: cornstarch in a leather pouch simulates the crunch of snow; a pair of gloves sounds like bird wings flapping; a heavy staple gun combined with other sounds can simulate a gunshot; and broken celery can be used to simulate bone breaks.
