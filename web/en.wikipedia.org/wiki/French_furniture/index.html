French furniture

French furniture encompasses the furniture made in France from the Renaissance to the present, reaching its greatest influence in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when the French court set the standard for European luxury. Parisian cabinetmakers (ébénistes), organized in guilds and often working for the Crown's Garde-Meuble, developed the marquetry, ormolu mounts, and lacquer techniques that define the classic French styles.

Styles by reign

The named royal styles structure the traditional history: the monumental baroque of Louis XIV, exemplified by the marquetry of André Charles Boulle; the lighter, asymmetrical Régence and the curving, shell-carved Louis XV rococo, with pieces such as the commode, the bureau à cylindre, and the bergère armchair; and the straighter neoclassical Louis XVI style, with fluted tapering legs and ribbon carving, associated with makers such as Jean-Henri Riesener and Georges Jacob. The bureau du Roi of Louis XV, completed by Jean-François Oeben and Riesener, is among the most famous pieces of furniture ever made.

Empire to Art Deco

The Empire style of Napoleon's reign favoured mahogany and classical motifs; nineteenth-century France produced revival styles and the bentwood and art furniture movements, and in the twentieth century French designers contributed notably to Art Nouveau (Émile Gallé, Louis Majorelle) and Art Deco (Jacques-Émile Ruhlmann).

Provincial furniture

Alongside court furniture, France developed rich regional traditions — the carved oak and walnut armoires, farmhouse tables, and rush-seated chairs of the provinces, collectively known as mobilier provincial or French provincial furniture.
