Suburb

A suburb (more broadly suburban area) is an area within a metropolitan area which is predominantly residential but may also include commercial districts. A suburb may exist either as part of a larger city or urban area, or as a separate municipal entity. The name derives from the Latin suburbium, formed from sub ("below" or "adjacent to") and urbs ("city") — a settlement at the edge of, and subordinate to, a city.

History

Suburbs have existed since ancient cities, but the modern suburb emerged in the 19th century as railways and tramways allowed middle-class residents to live outside crowded industrial city centers while commuting to work. In Britain, planned "villa suburbs" and later the garden city movement of Ebenezer Howard shaped suburban design. In the United States, suburbs expanded dramatically after the Second World War with mass-produced developments such as Levittown, federally backed mortgages, and the Interstate Highway System, producing low-density, car-dependent settlement patterns. Australia and Canada developed similarly extensive suburbs, while in many European and Asian cities "suburbs" may instead be dense districts of apartment blocks — sometimes, as with the Parisian banlieues, associated with social disadvantage rather than affluence.

Characteristics

Suburbs are generally characterized by lower population density than inner cities, separation of residential from commercial and industrial uses through zoning, single-family detached housing, and dependence on private automobiles. They are often associated with commuting, home ownership, and a particular cultural image of family life, and are contrasted with both the urban core and rural areas. Sociologists and planners debate their environmental footprint, social homogeneity, and fiscal sustainability, prompting movements such as New Urbanism that seek denser, walkable, mixed-use suburban forms.

Terminology around the world

In Australia and New Zealand, "suburb" simply denotes a named locality within a city, regardless of density or distance from the center. In the United Kingdom, suburbs are districts on the outskirts of a town or city, often incorporated into it. In the United States, suburbs may be independent municipalities with their own governments, making metropolitan governance fragmented across many jurisdictions.
