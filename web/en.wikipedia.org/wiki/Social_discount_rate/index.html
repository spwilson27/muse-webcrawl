Social discount rate

Social discount rate
FieldWelfare economics, cost–benefit analysis

The social discount rate (SDR) is the discount rate used in computing the value of funds spent on social projects: it reflects society's relative valuation of present versus future consumption, and is central to cost–benefit analysis of public investments whose effects span generations, above all climate change policy.

The Ramsey rule

The dominant framework derives the rate from the Ramsey growth model: r = δ + ηg, where δ is the pure rate of time preference (including a small allowance for extinction risk), η is the elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption, and g is the growth rate of per-capita consumption. Disagreements over the SDR are largely disagreements over these parameters — especially δ, an ethical quantity. Nicholas Stern's 2006 Stern Review used a near-zero pure time preference (δ = 0.1%), giving an SDR of about 1.4% and implying aggressive near-term climate mitigation; William Nordhaus and others argued for rates of 3–5%, closer to observed market returns.

Small changes in the discount rate hugely change long-run valuations

Practice

Governments publish official rates: the UK Treasury's Green Book uses 3.5%, declining for very long horizons; the US OMB historically used 7% and 3% scenarios, revising the default to 2% in 2023. Because a constant positive rate makes damages a century hence nearly worthless, some economists advocate declining (hyperbolic) discount schedules for intergenerational projects.
