Recession

[Image: a closed shopfront with a 'for lease' sign during an economic downturn]

A recession is a significant, widespread and prolonged downturn in economic activity — falling output, rising Unemployment, declining incomes and trade. A common rule of thumb defines it as two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP, but in the United States the official arbiter is the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), whose Business Cycle Dating Committee weighs output, employment, income and sales and declares peaks and troughs retrospectively. An especially severe or long recession is called a depression, though no formal boundary exists.

Causes and features

Recessions arise within the business cycle from demand shocks (financial crises, collapsing asset prices, abrupt policy tightening), supply shocks (the 1970s oil crises), or external events (the COVID-19 pandemic, which produced the shortest US recession on record in 2020). Their features include falling investment and consumer spending, rising bankruptcies, credit contraction and — via Okun's law — unemployment rising by roughly half the percentage shortfall in output. An inverted yield curve, in which short-term interest rates exceed long-term ones, has preceded most modern US recessions.

Responses

Governments and central banks respond with monetary policy easing (lower interest rates, quantitative easing) and fiscal stimulus (higher spending, tax cuts, automatic stabilisers such as unemployment benefits), aiming to shorten the downturn without igniting Inflation. The deepest modern episodes — the Great Depression of the 1930s and the Great Recession following the 2008 financial crisis — reshaped economic policy and theory for generations.
