William John Macquorn Rankine (5 July 1820 – 24 December 1872) was a Scottish mathematician, physicist and engineer, and a founding contributor — along with Rudolf Clausius and William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) — to the science of thermodynamics.

Early career

Rankine was educated at home and briefly at the University of Edinburgh. He worked on Irish and Scottish railway projects as a civil engineer before turning to academic science, publishing on metal fatigue in railway axles, ship design and soil mechanics — his theory of earth pressure on retaining walls (Rankine's theory) is still used in geotechnical engineering.

Thermodynamics

From 1849 Rankine developed a molecular-vortex theory of heat and produced the first formal statements of the laws of thermodynamics. His Manual of the Steam Engine and Other Prime Movers (1859) introduced the Rankine cycle, the idealised thermodynamic cycle of steam power plants, and his name is preserved in the Rankine absolute temperature scale (degrees Rankine, °R), the Fahrenheit-based counterpart of the kelvin.

Later years

Rankine became the first Regius Professor of Civil Engineering and Mechanics at the University of Glasgow in 1855 and first president of the Institution of Engineers in Scotland. A keen amateur musician, he wrote comic songs, including one about the second law of thermodynamics. He died in Glasgow in 1872, aged only 52.
