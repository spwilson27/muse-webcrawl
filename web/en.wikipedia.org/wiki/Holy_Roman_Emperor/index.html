Holy Roman Emperor

The Holy Roman Emperor (German: Römisch-deutscher Kaiser) was the ruler of the Holy Roman Empire, a multi-ethnic complex of territories in western and central Europe that lasted from the early Middle Ages until 1806. The office is conventionally dated from the coronation of Charlemagne by Pope Leo III in 800, or from Otto the Great in 962, and was elective for most of its history, though from 1440 it was held almost continuously by the House of Habsburg.

Role and election

The emperor was elected by the prince-electors, whose number and identity were fixed at seven by the Golden Bull of 1356. The elected ruler took the title "King of the Romans" and was traditionally crowned emperor by the Pope in Rome; the last papal coronation was that of Charles V in 1530, after which emperors styled themselves "Elected Roman Emperor". The emperor was regarded as the secular head of Christendom and protector of the Church, but his practical power depended on his own hereditary lands and on negotiation with the imperial estates.

History

After Charlemagne's empire divided, the imperial title lapsed in the West until Otto I revived it in 962. The Salian and Hohenstaufen emperors clashed with the papacy in the Investiture Controversy and over Italy; Frederick Barbarossa and Frederick II were among the most powerful medieval holders. The Great Interregnum (1254–1273) weakened the office, and the later Middle Ages saw a shift toward the German lands. The Reformation and the Peace of Westphalia (1648) further limited imperial authority, leading Voltaire to quip that the Empire was "neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire". Francis II dissolved the Empire on 6 August 1806 under pressure from Napoleon, ending the thousand-year office.
