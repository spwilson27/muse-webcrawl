RegionCanadian Arctic, Alaska, Greenland

An inuksuk (plural inuksuit; Inuktitut: ᐃᓄᒃᓱᒃ, "in the likeness of a human") is a type of stone cairn built by the Inuit and other peoples of the Arctic region of North America. These stone landmarks and structures were used for navigation, as a point of reference, a marker for travel routes, fishing places, camps, hunting grounds, and places of veneration, and in some cases to mark food caches or to help herd caribou.

Inuksuit vary in size and shape. The largest are several metres tall, while the smallest may be only a few stacked stones. A human-shaped inuksuk is properly called an inunnguaq ("imitation of a person"), although in common usage the term inuksuk is applied to all forms.

Functions

Inuksuit served many practical purposes in the largely featureless Arctic landscape. Lines of inuksuit could channel caribou during drives toward waiting hunters, much like fences. Single markers indicated safe passages through lakes and waterways, pointed toward good fishing spots, or marked caches of meat. Some inuksuit were aligned so that a traveller lining up two or more markers could follow a safe route across the land. Others had spiritual or commemorative significance, marking graves or places where significant events occurred.

Cultural significance

The inuksuk has become a widely recognised symbol of the Canadian North and of Inuit culture. A stylised inuksuk appears on the flag and coat of arms of the territory of Nunavut, and on the flag of Nunatsiavut. Inukshuk Point, near Rankin Inlet, was designated a National Historic Site of Canada in 1969, recognising a concentration of more than one hundred inuksuit. An inuksuk also served as a symbol of the 2010 Winter Olympics in Vancouver, whose mascot Ilanaaq was based on the figure, and inukshuk sculptures mark Canadian embassies and Parks Canada sites around the world.
