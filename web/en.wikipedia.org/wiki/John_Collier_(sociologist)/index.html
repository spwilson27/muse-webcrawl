John Collier (4 May 1884 – 8 May 1968) was an American social reformer and Native American advocate. As Commissioner of Indian Affairs from 1933 to 1945 under President Franklin D. Roosevelt, he reversed decades of federal policy aimed at forced assimilation and the breakup of tribal lands, advancing the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, the centrepiece of what became known as the "Indian New Deal."

Early life and reform career

Collier was born in Atlanta, Georgia, to a prominent family; his father was a banker and mayor of Atlanta. Educated at Columbia University and in Paris, he moved to New York and worked in immigrant community organisation at the People's Institute, developing a philosophy that prized cultural pluralism and the survival of folk communities against industrial mass society. In 1919 he moved west, and in 1920 he first visited Taos Pueblo in New Mexico, an experience he described as a spiritual turning point. Exposure to Pueblo religion and to the federal campaign against Native dances convinced him that Native cultures needed defending rather than eradicating.

Advocacy in the 1920s

As executive secretary of the American Indian Defense Association (founded 1923), Collier led the successful fight against the Bursum Bill, which would have taken Pueblo lands, and publicised abuses in the administration of Indian affairs, including the catastrophic Meriam Report findings of 1928. His lobbying helped shape a generation's reform consensus.

Commissioner of Indian Affairs

Appointed by Roosevelt's Interior Secretary Harold Ickes in 1933, Collier ended the allotment policy that had stripped tribes of some 90 million acres, restored religious freedom — ending bans on ceremonies such as the Sun Dance — and promoted the Indian Reorganization Act, which encouraged tribal self-government under written constitutions and created a revolving credit fund. He also used Civilian Conservation Corps programs for reservation employment (the Indian CCC) and founded the Indian Arts and Crafts Board. His policies remained controversial: some tribes voted against reorganization, and stock-reduction programs in Navajo country left lasting resentment. He resigned in 1945.

Later life

Collier taught sociology at the City College of New York and wrote several books, including The Indians of the Americas (1947), and founded the Institute of Ethnic Affairs. He died at Taos in 1968, regarded as the most consequential Indian commissioner of the twentieth century.
