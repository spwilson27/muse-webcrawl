The Oghuz Turks (Middle Turkic: ٱغُز, Oγuz) were a western Turkic people who spoke the Oghuz branch of the Turkic language family. In the 8th century they formed a tribal confederation known as the Oghuz Yabgu State between the Caspian and Aral seas on the Central Asian steppe. During the 10th century, large numbers of Oghuz migrated westward and southward; many converted to Islam and entered the service of, or fought against, the powers of the Iranian world.

History

The name Oghuz is generally derived from the Turkic word ok ("arrow" or tribe) with a plural suffix, hence "the tribes." Chinese sources mention the Oghuz (Wugu) as early as the 2nd century BCE. After the collapse of the Göktürk khaganates, the Oghuz confederation — traditionally of 24 tribes — consolidated on the Syr Darya steppe, ruled by a yabgu.

The Seljuks and after

From one Oghuz clan, the Qınık, arose the Seljuk family, whose followers conquered Iran, Iraq, and much of Anatolia in the 11th century, founding the Great Seljuk Empire and, later, the Sultanate of Rum. Other Oghuz groups founded dynasties including the Ghaznavids' rivals, the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu, the Ottoman Empire — descended from the Kayı tribe — and the Afsharid and Qajar dynasties of Iran.

Descendants and legacy

The southwestern (Oghuz) Turkic languages are today spoken by the Turks of Turkey, Azerbaijanis, Turkmens, Gagauz, and smaller groups — together well over 100 million speakers. The legendary Oghuz Khan and the epic tales of Dede Korkut remain central to the national traditions of Turkey, Azerbaijan, and Turkmenistan.
