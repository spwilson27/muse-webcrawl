The Mann Gulch fire was a wildfire that broke out on August 5, 1949, in the Gates of the Mountains area of the Helena National Forest in Montana, United States. A crew of 15 elite Forest Service smokejumpers parachuted in to fight it; confronted by a sudden blowup, 13 of them were killed, in one of the most studied disasters in wildland firefighting history.

The disaster

Led by foreman Wagner "Wag" Dodge, the crew descended to the north side of Mann Gulch. As they moved toward the Missouri River, shifting winds drove the fire across the gulch below them, cutting off escape and racing uphill faster than the men could run on the steep, grassy slope. Dodge survived by lighting an "escape fire" ahead of the main blaze and lying down in its burned-out ashes — a technique then little understood by his crew, who did not follow him into it.

Legacy

The tragedy transformed smokejumper training and fire-behaviour research. It was analysed in John Maclean-adjacent scholarship and immortalised by Norman Maclean's posthumous book Young Men and Fire (1992), which won the National Book Critics Circle Award. Markers now stand where each firefighter fell.
