Catherine Susan "Kitty" Genovese (July 7, 1935 – March 13, 1964) was an American bartender who was raped and stabbed to death outside the apartment building where she lived in the Kew Gardens neighborhood of Queens in New York City. The circumstances of her murder — and the reported failure of bystanders to intervene — became emblematic of urban apathy and gave rise to the psychological concept known as the "bystander effect".

Murder

In the early hours of March 13, 1964, Genovese returned home from her job managing a bar in Hollis, Queens. Winston Moseley, a 29-year-old Manhattan native, followed her and attacked her twice near her apartment building on Austin Street. She died of her wounds. Moseley was arrested days later and confessed; he was convicted of murder and sentenced to death, later commuted to life imprisonment. He died in prison in 2016.

The "38 witnesses" story and later research

A New York Times article published two weeks after the murder reported that 38 witnesses had watched the attack from their windows without calling police. The story seared itself into the American public consciousness and inspired the social psychology experiments of Bibb Latané and John Darley, who formalised the bystander effect and diffusion of responsibility. Subsequent investigations — including court records, books such as Kevin Cook's Kitty Genovese (2014) and the documentary The Witness (2015) — found the original account substantially exaggerated: few witnesses saw or understood the attack, some did call out or contact police, and a neighbour, Sophia Farrar, held Genovese as she died. Nevertheless, the case permanently shaped research on helping behaviour and led New York to adopt reforms; it is also frequently cited in the history of the 911 emergency number, though the single nationwide number was adopted in 1968 partly in response to broader concerns about emergency reporting.
