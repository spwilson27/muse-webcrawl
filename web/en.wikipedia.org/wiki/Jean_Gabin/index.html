Gabin, the defining face of French cinema's golden age.

Jean Gabin (born Jean-Alexis Moncorgé; May 17, 1904 – November 15, 1976) was a French actor and singer, regarded as one of the greatest stars of French cinema. The central figure of the poetic realist films of the 1930s, he played tragic working-class heroes — deserters, gangsters, and men trapped by fate — in a run of classics that made him an icon of French popular culture, and he sustained a second major career after the war as a patriarchal figure of authority.

Early life and career

Gabin was born in Paris to cabaret performers and raised in Mériel, north of the city. After odd jobs he followed his parents into show business, singing and dancing in music halls and operetta, and toured South America with a troupe. Sound cinema made him: his naturalistic delivery and rough charm stood out in films such as Maria Chapdelaine (1934) and La Bandera (1935), directed by Julien Duvivier.

Stardom in the 1930s

With Duvivier's Pépé le Moko (1937), Jean Renoir's La Grande Illusion (1937) and La Bête humaine (1938), and Marcel Carné's Le Quai des brumes (1938) and Le Jour se lève (1939), Gabin became the emblematic hero of poetic realism — a proletarian figure of doomed dignity, delivering lines like "T'as de beaux yeux, tu sais" ("You have beautiful eyes, you know") to Michèle Morgan, which entered the national memory. After the fall of France he went to Hollywood, made two films, then joined the Free French forces and served with distinction in North Africa and France.

Postwar career

Gabin's early postwar films fared poorly, but Touchez pas au grisbi (1954), as an aging gangster, relaunched him. For the next two decades he was French cinema's supreme patriarch: landowners, police commissioners (he played Maigret three times), tycoons, and old-school criminals, often paired with younger stars such as Alain Delon and Jean-Paul Belmondo, and with the dialogue writer Michel Audiard. He won the Volpi Cup at Venice twice and the Silver Bear at Berlin twice. In parallel he built a successful farming and horse-breeding life in Normandy.

Death and legacy

Gabin died of a heart attack in 1976. He remains a symbol of a certain French masculinity and of the classic era of French film; the Prix Jean Gabin for promising young actors and museums devoted to him attest to his enduring place in French culture.
