Redshift survey

In astronomy, a redshift survey is a survey of a section of the sky to measure the redshift of astronomical objects, usually galaxies but sometimes other objects such as galaxy clusters or quasars. Because redshift is proportional to distance for nearby objects (through Hubble's law), a redshift survey allows astronomers to construct a three-dimensional map of the large-scale structure of the universe in that field of view.

Method

Surveys combine images taken through filters or photometry with follow-up spectroscopy, often using multi-object spectrographs or fibre positioners that can record hundreds of spectra at once. The measured redshifts are combined with the objects' angular positions to build a "wedge diagram" of cosmic structure, revealing clusters, filaments, superclusters and voids.

Major surveys

  • CfA Redshift Survey (begun 1977): the first large survey; its second phase produced the famous "stickman" diagram and revealed the Great Wall (1989), then the largest known structure in the universe.
  • 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey (1997–2002): measured redshifts for over 220,000 galaxies with the Anglo-Australian Telescope.
  • Sloan Digital Sky Survey (from 2000): mapped over a million galaxies and quasars, producing the largest 3D maps of the universe of their time.
  • DESI (Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, from 2021): designed to measure tens of millions of galaxies and quasars to probe dark energy.

Scientific uses

Redshift surveys measure the growth of cosmic structure, baryon acoustic oscillations (used as a "standard ruler" for cosmology), galaxy evolution, and the parameters of the standard cosmological model, including the nature of dark matter and dark energy.
