Amlodipine, sold under the brand name Norvasc among others, is a calcium channel blocker medication used to treat high blood pressure, coronary artery disease (CAD) and variant angina (also called Prinzmetal angina or coronary artery vasospasm). It is taken by mouth. It works by causing blood vessels to relax and widen, and for angina it improves blood supply to the heart muscle.

Medical uses

Amlodipine is used in the management of hypertension, chronic stable angina, and vasospastic angina. It may be used alone or in combination with other antihypertensives, including in fixed-dose combinations with benazepril, olmesartan, valsartan, or perindopril. Unlike some calcium channel blockers, it is considered safe in people with heart failure.

Adverse effects

The most common side effect is peripheral oedema — swelling of the ankles and feet — occurring in a dose-dependent fashion. Other common effects include headache, flushing, palpitations, dizziness, and fatigue. Amlodipine can also cause gingival enlargement. Because it is metabolised by CYP3A4, strong inhibitors of that enzyme can raise amlodipine levels; consumption of grapefruit can have a similar effect.

Pharmacology

Amlodipine is a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker that inhibits the transmembrane influx of calcium ions into vascular smooth muscle and cardiac muscle, with a greater effect on vascular smooth muscle. It has a long elimination half-life (30–50 hours), permitting once-daily dosing, and a slow onset of action that avoids abrupt falls in blood pressure.

History

Pfizer's UK research group developed amlodipine in the 1980s; it was patented in 1982 and approved for medical use in 1990. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is one of the most commonly prescribed medications in the United States.
