Green belt (United Kingdom)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The green belt is a policy and land-use designation used in land-use planning in the United Kingdom to retain areas of largely undeveloped, wild, or agricultural land surrounding or neighbouring urban areas. Green belts discourage urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open; their most important attribute is openness.

The concept originated with the London Metropolitan Green Belt, first proposed in the 1930s and formalised under the Town and Country Planning Act 1947 and a 1955 circular inviting local authorities to establish green belts elsewhere. There are now 14 green belts in England covering about 1.6 million hectares — roughly 13 percent of the country — plus belts in Scotland, Wales, and around Belfast.

Purposes

Official policy gives green belts five purposes: to check the unrestricted sprawl of large built-up areas; to prevent neighbouring towns merging; to safeguard the countryside from encroachment; to preserve the setting of historic towns; and to assist urban regeneration by encouraging the recycling of derelict urban land.

Debate

Green belts are popular with the public but debated by economists and planners: critics argue they push up house prices, restrict development, and protect much land of modest landscape quality, while supporters emphasise their role in containing sprawl and providing accessible countryside. Some policy now distinguishes lower-quality "grey belt" land.
