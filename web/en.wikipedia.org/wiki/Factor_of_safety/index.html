Factor of safety

Factor of safety
DefinitionYield (or ultimate) strength ÷ applied stress
AbbreviationFoS, FS, SF
Used inStructural, mechanical, civil engineering

In engineering, a factor of safety (FoS), also known as (and sometimes used interchangeably with) a safety factor (SF), expresses how much stronger a system is than it needs to be for an intended load. Safety factors are often calculated using detailed analysis because comprehensive testing is impractical on many projects, such as bridges and buildings, but the structure's ability to carry a load must be determined to a reasonable accuracy.

Definition

Many systems are intentionally built much stronger than needed for normal usage to allow for emergency situations, unexpected loads, misuse, or degradation. The factor of safety is commonly expressed as the ratio of the material's yield strength (or ultimate strength) to the maximum stress expected in service. A factor of safety of 2, for example, means a component can withstand twice the expected maximum load before yielding or failing.

Margin of safety

A related measure, the margin of safety (MoS), is often used in aerospace and structural design. When a structure includes a required factor of safety, the margin of safety is the additional capacity beyond that requirement: MoS = (failure load ÷ (design load × FoS)) − 1. A positive margin indicates the design satisfies the requirement; a negative margin does not necessarily mean the structure will fail, only that it violates the prescribed factor.

Choice of values

Appropriate design factors depend on the uncertainty in loads and material properties, the consequences of failure, and the cost of overdesign. Lifting equipment may use factors of 4–6, pressure vessels commonly around 3–4, and aircraft structures, where weight is critical and analysis is precise, factors near 1.5. Building codes and standards bodies prescribe minimum values for many applications. Where failure would endanger life, higher factors are chosen; ductile materials, whose yielding gives warning, may justify lower factors than brittle materials of equal strength.

Yield and ultimate factors

Two different safety factors can be calculated: one relative to the yield strength, beyond which the component is no longer elastically functional, and one relative to the ultimate strength, at which the component actually breaks. The ratio of ultimate to yield strength is itself a property of the material; design practice checks both criteria for many structural applications.
