Antonio Stradivari (c. 1644 – 18 December 1737) was an Italian luthier and the foremost maker of stringed instruments of his era. His Latinised surname, Stradivarius, and the abbreviation "Strad" are terms often used to refer to his instruments, which are regarded as among the finest ever made and command prices in the millions of dollars.

Life

Stradivari was born in Cremona, then part of the Spanish-ruled Duchy of Milan. He is believed to have been apprenticed to Nicolò Amati, grandson of Andrea Amati, the founder of the Cremonese school of violin making. By about 1680 he had established his own workshop in Cremona, where he worked for nearly six decades, assisted later in life by his sons Francesco and Omobono.

Instruments

Stradivari made roughly 1,100 instruments over his career, of which about 650 survive, including some 512 violins, as well as violas, cellos, guitars, harps and mandolins. His so-called "golden period" (c. 1700–1725) produced instruments such as the Messiah violin of 1716, prized for their power, tonal beauty and elegant proportions, including the characteristic long pattern and rich orange-red varnish.

Legacy

Stradivarius instruments are played by leading soloists worldwide and remain benchmarks against which modern violin making is measured. The secrets of his varnish and methods have been the subject of extensive scientific study; no complete explanation of their acoustical qualities has achieved consensus.
