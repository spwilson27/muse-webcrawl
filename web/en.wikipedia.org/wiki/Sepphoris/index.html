Sepphoris (Greek: Σεπφωρίς), known in Hebrew as Tzippori, was a major ancient Jewish city in the Galilee, now an archaeological site and national park in northern Israel, a few kilometres from Nazareth. The historian Josephus called it the "ornament of all Galilee".

History

Sepphoris became the capital of Galilee under Herod Antipas, who refortified it after a revolt in 4 BC crushed by the Romans. Unlike many Jewish towns, Sepphoris sided with Rome during the Great Revolt of 66–70 AD and was spared destruction. In the 2nd century AD it hosted the Sanhedrin for a time under Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi, the redactor of the Mishnah, who is traditionally said to have lived there for many years. The city later had a significant Christian presence and declined after the earthquake of 363 AD and the Arab conquest.

Archaeology

Excavations have uncovered a paved Roman street grid with colonnades, a theatre seating about 4,500, wealthy villas, a synagogue with a zodiac mosaic, and an elaborate water system including a reservoir and aqueduct. The city's most famous find is a mosaic portrait of a beautiful woman from a Dionysiac villa — dubbed the "Mona Lisa of the Galilee" — alongside a celebrated Nile festival mosaic. Because of its proximity to Nazareth, Sepphoris is often discussed in connection with the world of the historical Jesus, though it figures in no gospel narrative.
