Elf-arrow

Elf-arrow
Also known asElf-shot; fairy arrow
NaturePrehistoric flint arrowhead in folklore
RegionScotland, Ireland, Scandinavia, northern Europe
Believed effectSudden illness or death of people and livestock ("elf-shot")

An elf-arrow, elf-bolt or fairy arrow was, in British and Scandinavian folklore, the name given to prehistoric flint arrowheads after their original purpose had been forgotten. Because these small, sharp stones turned up in fields without obvious explanation, country people believed they were weapons shot by elves or fairies at people and cattle, causing sudden disease — a condition called elf-shot.

Belief and symptoms

Victims of elf-shot were said to suffer sudden stabbing pains, cramps, paralysis or wasting illnesses, and cattle struck by elf-arrows might sicken or die without visible injury — the invisible arrow being the cause. Similar beliefs appear across northern Europe: in Anglo-Saxon England, medical texts such as the Leechbook of Bald contain charms against "elf-shot" (Old English ælfsogoða), and in Scandinavian folklore elves (álfar) were blamed for "elf-blows" causing illness.

Cures and protection

Folk remedies included charms and prayers, herbal preparations, and touching the afflicted person or animal with an elf-arrow itself — a found arrowhead dipped in water, the water then being given to the patient to drink. Elf-arrows were also worn as amulets, mounted in silver and carried as protection against witchcraft and disease; parish ministers and folklorists recorded many such practices in Scotland and Ireland into the 19th century.

Archaeological identification

With the rise of antiquarianism and archaeology, the objects were recognised as the products of Neolithic and Bronze Age flint-knapping. The folklore surrounding them is now studied as evidence of how pre-modern societies explained both ancient artefacts and sudden illness. The term survives in museum collections, where flint arrowheads are sometimes still catalogued with their folk name.
