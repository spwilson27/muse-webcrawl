Hubris

Hubris (from Ancient Greek ὕβρις, hybris) describes excessive pride, arrogance, or self-confidence, especially a pride that defies the gods or the natural order and leads to the transgressor's downfall. In ancient Greek religion and literature, hubris was not merely a feeling but a category of behaviour — dishonouring or outrageous treatment of another — that invited retribution, often personified as Nemesis.

Icarus, who flew too close to the sun, is a staple example of hubris.

Ancient Greek usage

In classical Athens, hybris was also a legal term: the graphē hybreōs allowed prosecution of acts meant to dishonour or humiliate a victim, including assault. Aristotle, in the Rhetoric, defines hubris as doing or saying things that bring shame on the victim, not for personal gain but for the pleasure of demonstrating superiority. Scholars debate how far the term implied a religious offence against divine order in ordinary legal use.

In literature

Hubris is a central theme of Greek tragedy. Figures such as Oedipus, Creon, and Xerxes in Aeschylus's Persians are brought low after overstepping their proper limits. In myth, Icarus falls after ignoring his father's warning, and Arachne is punished for claiming skill equal to Athena's. The tragic pattern — hubris followed by ate (ruinous folly) and nemesis — shaped later Western storytelling about pride and downfall.

Modern usage

In modern English, hubris generally means exaggerated self-confidence or pride that blinds a person — often a political or business leader — to risk and criticism. Historians and journalists apply it to failures from military overreach to corporate collapse. The related concept of the "hubris syndrome" has been proposed in medical and political commentary to describe personality changes sometimes observed in leaders long in power.
