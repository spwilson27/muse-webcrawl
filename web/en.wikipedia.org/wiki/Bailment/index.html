Bailment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A valet parking attendant receiving car keys — a classic bailment for mutual benefit]

Bailment is a legal relationship in common law in which the owner of a tangible personal asset (chattel) transfers physical possession, but not ownership, of it to another person — the bailee — for a purpose, after which the goods are to be returned or dealt with as directed. The person delivering the goods is the bailor.

Elements and examples

Everyday bailments include leaving a car with a mechanic or valet, checking a coat, hiring equipment, and storing goods in a warehouse. A bailment requires delivery of possession and acceptance by the bailee; mere custody by an employee is generally not a bailment.

Duties of the bailee

Traditionally, the standard of care varied with who benefited: a bailee holding goods solely for the bailor's benefit owed slight care, one holding goods for their own benefit owed great care, and in a bailment for mutual benefit ordinary care was required. Modern law generally applies a standard of reasonable care in the circumstances. The bailee must not misuse the goods and must redeliver them on demand; if goods are lost, the bailee may bear the burden of showing absence of negligence.

Types

Bailments may be gratuitous or for reward (hire, pledge, deposit, carriage), and special regimes govern common carriers, innkeepers and warehousemen, who are often subject to strict liability as insurers of the goods.
