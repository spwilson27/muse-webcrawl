Les Nabis (from the Hebrew word for "prophets") were a group of young French artists active in Paris around 1888–1900 who played an important transitional role between Impressionism and Post-Impressionism on the one hand and modern art on the other. The group formed around Paul Sérusier's painting The Talisman, painted under Gauguin's guidance at Pont-Aven in 1888.

Ideas and style

Following Gauguin's Synthetism, the Nabis rejected naturalistic representation in favour of expressive colour, flattened forms, bold outlines and decorative surface pattern. Maurice Denis's famous 1890 dictum — that a painting "before being a war horse, a nude woman or some anecdote, is essentially a flat surface covered with colours assembled in a certain order" — became a founding statement of modernist formalism. Members adopted pseudo-Hebrew or joking "secret" names (Denis was the "Nabi of the beautiful icons") and shared studios and mystical, literary interests.

Members and work

Core members included Sérusier, Denis, Pierre Bonnard, Édouard Vuillard, Ker-Xavier Roussel, Henri-Gabriel Ibels and the Dutch artist Jan Verkade, with associates such as Georges Lacombe, Paul Ranson, Aristide Maillol and later Odilon Redon and Félix Vallotton. The Nabis worked across easel painting, posters, lithography, book illustration, theatre design (for Lugné-Poe's Théâtre de l'Œuvre) and decorative panels — Vuillard's public garden panels and Bonnard's intimate interiors ("intimism") are characteristic. They exhibited together from 1891 until the group dispersed around 1900–1905, largely after a final exhibition at Bernheim-Jeune.

Legacy

The Nabis helped establish the autonomy of colour and design in painting, influenced the Fauves and Orphism, and modelled the idea of an avant-garde brotherhood for later movements.
