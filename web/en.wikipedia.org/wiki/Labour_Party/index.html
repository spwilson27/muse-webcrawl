Labour Party

"Labour Party" is the name of political parties in many countries around the world, generally adhering to social democratic or democratic socialist traditions and historically linked to trade unions. This article most commonly refers to the Labour Party of the United Kingdom, founded in 1900, one of the two main British political parties alongside the Conservative Party.

United Kingdom

The British Labour Party grew out of the Labour Representation Committee (1900), founded by trade unions and socialist societies including the Independent Labour Party and the Fabian Society. Keir Hardie was its first parliamentary leader. Labour first formed a minority government under Ramsay MacDonald in 1924. The party's landmark government of 1945–51, led by Clement Attlee, created the National Health Service and the modern welfare state. Later Labour prime ministers include Harold Wilson, James Callaghan, Tony Blair, and Gordon Brown. Under Keir Starmer, Labour won a landslide victory in the 2024 general election and returned to government.

Other countries

Labour parties exist in many Commonwealth and other nations, often affiliated with Socialist International or the Progressive Alliance: the Australian Labor Party (note the spelling), the New Zealand Labour Party, the Labour Party of Ireland, Norway's Labour Party (Arbeiderpartiet), Malta's Labour Party, Israel's Labor Party, and Labour parties in Barbados, Jamaica, Mauritius, and elsewhere. Most were founded in the late 19th or early 20th century by trade union movements.

Ideology

Labour parties typically advocate a mixed economy, workers' rights, public services, and redistribution through taxation and welfare. Many have moved toward the political centre since the 1980s ("Third Way"), while retaining union links and commitments to social justice.
