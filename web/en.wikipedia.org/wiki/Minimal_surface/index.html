Minimal surface

In mathematics, a minimal surface is a surface that locally minimizes its area. This is equivalent to having zero mean curvature at every point. The term "minimal surface" is used because these surfaces originally arose as surfaces that minimized total surface area subject to some constraint. Physical models of area-minimizing minimal surfaces can be made by dipping a wire frame into a solution of soap, forming a soap film, which is a minimal surface whose boundary is the wire frame.

Definitions

Several equivalent definitions are used in differential geometry. A smooth surface in three-dimensional Euclidean space is minimal if and only if its mean curvature vanishes identically. A surface is also minimal if it is a critical point of the area functional for all compactly supported variations — that is, a stationarity condition — though such critical points need not always be true area minimizers, which is why the name is considered something of a misnomer.

History

The theory of minimal surfaces began with Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who in 1762 posed the problem of finding the surface of least area stretched across a given closed contour. Leonhard Euler had earlier, in 1744, discovered the catenoid — the surface of revolution of a catenary — as a minimal surface. Jean Baptiste Meusnier described the helicoid in 1776 and proved that a surface is minimal exactly when its mean curvature is zero. In the nineteenth century, the Belgian physicist Joseph Plateau formulated what became known as Plateau's problem: proving the existence of a minimal surface with a given boundary. It was solved independently by Jesse Douglas and Tibor Radó around 1930, work for which Douglas received one of the first Fields Medals in 1936.

Examples

Classical examples of minimal surfaces include the plane (the trivial example), the catenoid, the helicoid (the only ruled minimal surface besides the plane), and the Scherk surfaces. More elaborate examples discovered in the late twentieth century, such as Costa's minimal surface (1982) and the Costa–Hoffman–Meeks surfaces, showed that complete embedded minimal surfaces of finite topology could be far more intricate than previously imagined.

Applications

Minimal surfaces arise in materials science (in the structure of block copolymers and biomembranes), in architecture — notably the tensile roof structures of Frei Otto, such as the Munich Olympic Stadium — and in molecular engineering and nanotechnology, where minimal-surface geometries are exploited for their favourable structural and transport properties.
