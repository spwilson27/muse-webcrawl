Victory garden

Victory gardens, also called war gardens or food gardens for defense, were vegetable, fruit, and herb gardens planted at private residences and public parks in the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and Germany during the First and Second World Wars. Governments encouraged them to reduce pressure on the public food supply, supplement rations, and boost civilian morale by giving non-combatants a tangible way to support the war effort.

A Second World War victory garden

First World War

The idea took organised form in 1917 when American publisher Charles Lathrop Pack founded the National War Garden Commission, promoting "war gardens" to ease food shortages in war-torn Europe and at home. Posters urged citizens to "sow the seeds of victory", and gardening manuals were distributed on a mass scale.

Second World War

The campaign reached its peak in the Second World War. In Britain, the Ministry of Agriculture's "Dig for Victory" campaign turned parks, lawns and even the moat of the Tower of London into allotments; the number of allotments roughly doubled. In the United States the Department of Agriculture and the Office of Civilian Defense promoted victory gardens through films and leaflets; by 1944 an estimated 20 million American gardens produced roughly 8 million tons of food — often cited as around 40 percent of the nation's fresh vegetables. Famous demonstration gardens included one on the White House grounds under Eleanor Roosevelt.

Legacy

Victory gardens largely vanished after 1945, but the model has been revived in campaigns for local food, sustainability and self-sufficiency. In 2009 a new kitchen garden was planted on the White House South Lawn under Michelle Obama, explicitly invoking the victory-garden precedent, and interest in home food gardening has surged repeatedly during later crises.
