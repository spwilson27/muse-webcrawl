Rennet (/ˈrɛnɪt/) is a complex set of enzymes produced in the stomachs of ruminant mammals. Its key component, chymosin, is a protease that curdles the casein in milk, causing it to separate into solid curds and liquid whey. Rennet is therefore a fundamental ingredient in most cheesemaking: it gives curds their firmness and allows the cheesemaker to drain and press them. In addition to chymosin, natural rennet contains other enzymes, such as pepsin and lipase.

Mechanism

Chymosin acts with high specificity on κ-casein, cleaving a single bond (between phenylalanine 105 and methionine 106) that stabilises the casein micelles in milk. Once this protective "hairy layer" is cut away, the micelles aggregate in the presence of calcium ions and form a gel — the curd. This two-stage process (enzymatic cleavage, then aggregation) is why rennet-set cheese differs from acid-set cheese such as paneer or queso fresco, in which casein precipitates directly at its isoelectric point.

Sources

Animal rennet

Traditional rennet is extracted from the inner mucosa of the fourth stomach chamber (the abomasum) of young, unweaned calves; rennet from lambs and kids is used for some regional cheeses. According to legend, cheesemaking itself was discovered when milk stored in a pouch made from an animal's stomach curdled. Because calf rennet is limited in supply and relatively expensive, it is now reserved for many traditional and appellation-protected cheeses.

Vegetable and microbial coagulants

Some plants produce milk-curdling proteases: the flower of the cardoon thistle is used for Portuguese and Spanish cheeses such as Serra da Estrela and torta del Casar, and fig sap, nettle and other plants have folk uses. Microbial rennets are produced by moulds such as Rhizomucor miehei.

Fermentation-produced chymosin

Since the 1990s, much of the world's cheese has been made with fermentation-produced chymosin (FPC): the calf chymosin gene is expressed in microbes such as Kluyveromyces lactis or Aspergillus niger, yielding pure bovine chymosin identical to the animal enzyme. It was among the first products of genetic engineering approved in food production, and it is accepted for most vegetarian-labelled cheeses, though not for all appellation rules or organic standards.
