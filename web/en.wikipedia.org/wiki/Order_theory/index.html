Order theory

Order theory

Field: Mathematics

Key objects: Partial orders, lattices, total orders

Order theory is a branch of mathematics that investigates the intuitive notion of order using binary relations. It provides a formal framework for describing statements such as "this is less than that" or "this precedes that". Orders are everywhere in mathematics and related fields such as computer science: the familiar orderings of numbers, the subset relation on sets, divisibility among integers, and the dependency relations among tasks are all instances of the same abstract idea.

Basic definitions

A partial order is a binary relation ≤ over a set that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive. A set equipped with a partial order is called a partially ordered set, or poset. If every two elements of a poset are comparable it is a total order (or linear order), exemplified by the real numbers. Weaker structures include preorders, which drop antisymmetry, and strict orders, defined by an irreflexive transitive relation <.

Within a poset one studies greatest and least elements, maximal and minimal elements, upper and lower bounds, and the supremum (least upper bound) and infimum (greatest lower bound) of subsets. A poset in which every two elements have a supremum and an infimum is a lattice; lattices with additional completeness or distributivity properties — complete lattices, Boolean algebras, Heyting algebras — underpin logic, universal algebra and semantics.

Functions between orders

Maps between posets that preserve order are called monotone or order-preserving; isomorphisms of orders are order-isomorphisms. Galois connections, adjoint pairs of monotone maps between posets, arise in formal concept analysis and abstract interpretation. Fixed-point theorems such as the Knaster–Tarski theorem guarantee that monotone maps on complete lattices have fixed points, a result with applications from set theory to program analysis.

Applications

Order theory is used in domain theory for the semantics of programming languages, in the theory of social choice, in scheduling and dependency management, and throughout algebra, where ordered groups, rings and fields are studied. Well-orders, in which every non-empty subset has a least element, are foundational for transfinite induction and the theory of ordinal numbers.
