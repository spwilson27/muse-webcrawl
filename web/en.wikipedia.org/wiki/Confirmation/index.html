Confirmation

Anointing with chrism during the rite of confirmation

Confirmation is one of the seven sacraments of the Catholic Church, and a sacrament or rite in many other Christian traditions, including Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, Anglicanism and Lutheranism. It is understood as the sacrament in which the Holy Spirit is conferred in a special way, strengthening and "confirming" the grace received in baptism. In the Roman Catholic Church it is one of the three sacraments of initiation, along with baptism and the Eucharist. In Eastern churches it is called chrismation, after the holy oil (chrism or myron) with which the newly baptised are anointed, and it is normally administered immediately after baptism, including to infants.

Origins and practice

The rite developed from the post-baptismal anointing and laying on of hands described in early Christian practice and associated in the New Testament with passages such as Acts 8 and Acts 19, in which the apostles lay hands on baptised believers who then receive the Holy Spirit. In the Latin West, as baptism of infants became universal and bishops could not be present at every baptism, the anointing and hand-laying were reserved to the bishop and separated in time from baptism, becoming a distinct sacrament administered when the child reached the age of reason or adolescence. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) affirmed confirmation as a true sacrament against Reformation critiques.

Effects and form

Catholic theology teaches that confirmation imprints an indelible sacramental character on the soul, deepens baptismal grace, increases the gifts of the Holy Spirit — wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety and fear of the Lord — and strengthens the recipient to witness to the faith. The essential form is the anointing of the forehead with chrism, accompanied by the words "Be sealed with the Gift of the Holy Spirit" (in the Byzantine rite) or, in the current Latin rite, Accipe signaculum doni Spiritus Sancti. The ordinary minister in the Latin Church is the bishop, though priests may confirm in defined circumstances; in the East, priests chrismate using chrism consecrated by a bishop.

In other traditions

Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches administer chrismation to infants immediately after baptism, followed by first communion. Anglican and Lutheran churches retain confirmation as a rite of mature reaffirmation of baptismal vows, typically in adolescence, though they generally do not number it among the dominical sacraments. Many Reformed and Baptist traditions practise a public profession of faith rather than confirmation, and some groups, such as Quakers and the Salvation Army, do not practise it at all. The Latter-day Saint movement administers confirmation by the laying on of hands shortly after baptism, conferring the gift of the Holy Ghost.

Analogous rites

Confirmation functions in many communities as a rite of passage marking religious adulthood, comparable in social function to the Jewish bar and bat mitzvah or to secular coming-of-age ceremonies such as the Nordic non-religious ("humanist") confirmations held in Norway and elsewhere.
