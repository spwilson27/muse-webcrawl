Disenchantment

In social science, disenchantment (German: Entzauberung) is the cultural rationalization and devaluation of religion apparent in modern society. The concept was borrowed by the sociologist Max Weber from Friedrich Schiller and developed in his 1917 lecture "Science as a Vocation", where he described the fate of the modern age as the displacement of magical and mystical explanations of the world by science, calculation, and bureaucratic rationality.

Weber's account

Weber argued that in a disenchanted world "one need no longer have recourse to magical means in order to master or implore the spirits … technical means and calculations perform the service". Intellectualization does not mean people understand the conditions of their lives in detail, but rather that they believe everything could in principle be mastered by calculation, leaving no place for mysterious incalculable forces. Disenchantment is thus a key element of Weber's theory of rationalization and of secularization, with ambivalent consequences: it brings calculability and control, but also a loss of overarching meaning, which Weber associated with the "iron cage" of modern economic order.

Criticism and later use

Later scholars have questioned whether disenchantment ever fully occurred. Historians of science and religion point to the persistence and reinvention of enchantment in modernity — in spiritualism, occult revivals, and contemporary popular culture — while philosophers such as Charles Taylor describe modern identity as haunted by the loss of a once-enchanted cosmos. The term is also used more loosely for the disappointment of political or personal illusions, as in the "disenchantment of the world" felt after failed revolutions.
