Schema (psychology)

Schema
FieldCognitive psychology
Key figuresF. C. Bartlett, Jean Piaget, Ulric Neisser
RelatedScript, stereotype, mental model

In psychology and cognitive science, a schema (plural: schemata or schemas) is a pattern of thought or behaviour that organises categories of information and the relationships among them. Schemas are mental frameworks that help individuals interpret the world, predict events, and fill in gaps in incoming information.

History

The concept was given psychological form by Frederic Bartlett in his 1932 book Remembering, in which experiments with the folk tale "The War of the Ghosts" showed that people reconstruct memories through their existing cultural schemas, distorting unfamiliar details toward familiar patterns. Jean Piaget used schemas as the basic units of knowledge that children assimilate new experiences into, and accommodate when experience does not fit. The idea was revived in the cognitive revolution of the 1970s and applied to reading, memory, and social cognition.

Types and effects

Psychologists distinguish person schemas, event schemas (or scripts — such as the sequence of events in a restaurant), role schemas, and self-schemas. Schemas make processing efficient but also create systematic biases: information consistent with a schema is remembered more readily, ambiguous information is interpreted schema-consistently, and contradictory information may be ignored or re-explained. Schemas are therefore central to theories of stereotyping and prejudice.

Clinical use

In clinical psychology, schema therapy (developed by Jeffrey Young) treats long-standing maladaptive schemas formed in childhood as the basis of personality disorders and chronic psychological problems.
