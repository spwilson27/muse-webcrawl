Under the DDM, a share is worth the present value of all expected future dividends.

The dividend discount model (DDM) is a method of valuing the price of a stock by theoretically using the formula that a stock is worth the sum of all of its future dividend payments, discounted back to their present value. In other words, DDM is used to value stocks based on the net present value of the future dividends. The theory was laid out by John Burr Williams in The Theory of Investment Value (1938) and is a foundation of fundamental analysis.

The general model is:

P0 = Σt=1∞ Dt / (1 + r)t

where P0 is the current fair price, Dt the expected dividend at time t, and r the required rate of return (cost of equity).

Gordon growth model

The best-known special case, associated with Myron J. Gordon and Eli Shapiro (1956), assumes dividends grow forever at a constant rate g less than the discount rate:

P0 = D1 / (r − g)

From this formula one can back out an expected return r = D1/P0 + g, the sum of the dividend yield and the growth rate. The model is most appropriate for mature, stable dividend-paying firms in steady state.

Multi-stage models

Because few firms grow at a constant rate forever, practitioners use two-stage or three-stage models, in which dividends are projected explicitly during a supernormal growth period and the Gordon formula supplies a terminal value thereafter. Variants include the H-model, which assumes a linear decline in the growth rate.

Limitations

The DDM cannot value non-dividend-paying shares directly, is extremely sensitive to small changes in the assumed growth rate and discount rate, and ignores gains realised through share repurchases, which many firms use in place of dividends. It also shares the limits of assuming a constant cost of equity.
