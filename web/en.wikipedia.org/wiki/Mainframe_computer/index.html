Mainframe computer

A mainframe computer, informally called a mainframe or big iron, is a computer used primarily by large organizations for critical applications such as bulk data processing for statistics, enterprise resource planning, and handling of very large volumes of transactions. Mainframes are distinguished by high reliability, security, extensive input/output capacity, and backward compatibility with older software.

History

Mainframes emerged in the 1950s, with systems from IBM, UNIVAC, Burroughs, and others. IBM's System/360, launched in 1964, established a compatible family of machines and came to define the category. Although minicomputers, workstations, and later cloud servers eroded their market, mainframes — particularly the IBM Z series — remained central to banking, insurance, airlines, retail, and government data processing.

Characteristics

Modern mainframes support simultaneous operation of multiple operating systems, often through logical partitions and hypervisors. They emphasize reliability, availability, and serviceability (RAS), massive transaction throughput measured in millions of instructions per second, and the ability to run workloads written decades earlier without modification. The term derives from the large cabinets, called "frames," that housed early computer systems.
