Evolutionarily stable strategy

An evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) is a strategy (or set of strategies) in game theory which, if adopted by a population in a given environment, is impenetrable — that is, it cannot be invaded by any alternative strategy that is initially rare. The concept was introduced by John Maynard Smith and George R. Price in a landmark 1973 paper in Nature, "The logic of animal conflict", and it became the central solution concept of evolutionary game theory, applying game-theoretic reasoning to evolving populations in biology.

Definition

Formally, a strategy S is an ESS if, for every alternative strategy T ≠ S:

  1. E(S, S) ≥ E(T, S) — a population playing S does at least as well against itself as a rare mutant playing T does against it; and
  2. if E(T, S) = E(S, S), then E(S, T) > E(T, T) — the resident strategy does better against the mutant than the mutant does against itself.

    3. Here E(A, B) is the expected payoff (fitness) of playing strategy A against an opponent playing B. An ESS is a refinement of a Nash equilibrium: every ESS is a Nash equilibrium, but not every Nash equilibrium is evolutionarily stable.

    The Hawk–Dove game

    The classic illustration is the Hawk–Dove game, in which animals contest a resource of value V. Hawks always fight, risking injury costing C; Doves display but retreat from Hawks. If V > C, pure Hawk is the ESS. If C > V, neither pure strategy is stable, and the ESS is a mixed strategy — playing Hawk with probability V/C — or equivalently a population polymorphic in that proportion.

    Applications

    ESS analysis has been applied to animal aggression and ritualised contests, sex ratios, mating systems, territoriality, signalling and honesty, cooperation and altruism (in relation to kin selection), and the evolution of social behaviour generally. In economics and the social sciences, the concept underlies evolutionary models of conventions, norms and institutional stability.

    History and development

    Maynard Smith developed the idea while reviewing a manuscript by George Price — the American scientist whose Price equation transformed the theory of selection. Their 1973 collaboration formalised how frequency-dependent selection can stabilise behaviour without any assumption of group benefit. Maynard Smith's 1982 book Evolution and the Theory of Games consolidated the field, and he received the Crafoord Prize in part for this work.
