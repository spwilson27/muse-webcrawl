Cupressaceae


Sequoiadendron giganteum, a member of Cupressaceae

Cupressaceae is a conifer family, the cypress family, with worldwide distribution. The family includes 27–30 genera (17 monotypic), which include the junipers and redwoods, with about 130–140 species in total. They are monoecious, subdioecious or (rarely) dioecious trees and shrubs up to 116 m (381 ft) tall. The bark of mature trees is commonly orange- to red-brown and of stringy texture, often flaking or peeling vertically; in some species it is smooth, scaly, or hard and square-cracked.

Description

The leaves are scale-like, 1–15 mm long, or needle-like (in juvenile foliage and in junipers), arranged in opposite decussate pairs or whorls. The seed cones are small, woody or fleshy and berry-like in Juniperus, with one to several seeds per scale. The family contains some of the largest and longest-lived trees on Earth: the giant sequoia (Sequoiadendron giganteum), the coast redwood (Sequoia sempervirens) — the tallest living tree species — and alerce (Fitzroya cupressoides), individuals of which live for more than 3,000 years.

Taxonomy and distribution

Modern circumscription merges the former family Taxodiaceae (the redwoods, bald cypresses and their relatives) into Cupressaceae on the basis of molecular phylogenetics. The family is divided into subfamilies including Sequoioideae, Taxodioideae, Callitroideae and Cupressoideae. Members occur on all continents except Antarctica, in habitats from Arctic-alpine juniper scrub to temperate rainforests and Mediterranean maquis. Many species — cypresses, arborvitae (Thuja), junipers, and Leyland cypress — are widely planted as ornamentals and for durable, aromatic timber.
