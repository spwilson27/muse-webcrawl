Maksutov telescope

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Maksutov (also called a "Mak") is a catadioptric telescope design that combines a spherical mirror with a weakly negative meniscus lens to correct spherical aberration and off-axis aberrations such as coma. It was patented in 1941 by the Russian optician Dmitri Dmitrievich Maksutov; a similar design was independently developed by the Dutch astronomer Albert Bouwers.

Design

In the most common form, the Maksutov–Cassegrain, light enters through a thick, deeply curved meniscus corrector lens, reflects from a spherical primary mirror, and is reflected again by a small secondary spot — often an aluminised patch on the inner surface of the corrector — through a hole in the primary to the focal plane. Because all surfaces are spherical, the design is comparatively easy to manufacture to high precision, giving excellent contrast and sharp images in a short, sealed tube, at the cost of weight and long cool-down times for the thick corrector.

Uses

Maksutov–Cassegrains are popular amateur astronomy telescopes in apertures of 90–180 mm, valued for lunar and planetary observing, and have been used as telephoto lenses (mirror lenses) and in military and satellite optics. A variant with a separate secondary mirror, sometimes called a Rumak or a Gregory Maksutov, offers a flatter field.
