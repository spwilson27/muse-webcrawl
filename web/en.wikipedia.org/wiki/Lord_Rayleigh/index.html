John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh (12 November 1842 – 30 June 1919), was an English mathematical physicist who made extensive contributions to both theoretical and experimental physics. He succeeded his father as Baron Rayleigh in 1873 and succeeded James Clerk Maxwell as Cavendish Professor of Physics at Cambridge (1879–1884).

Scientific work

Rayleigh's name is attached to a remarkable range of phenomena. Rayleigh scattering — the scattering of light by particles much smaller than its wavelength, with intensity proportional to the inverse fourth power of the wavelength — explained why the sky is blue. He analysed surface waves in solids (Rayleigh waves), the stability of fluid flows (the Rayleigh–Taylor instability, Rayleigh–Bénard convection), the resolution limit of optical instruments (the Rayleigh criterion), and the statistics of random walks and radiation (the Rayleigh–Jeans law). His two-volume Theory of Sound (1877–78) remains a classic of acoustics.

Discovery of argon

In the 1890s Rayleigh noticed that nitrogen extracted from air was consistently denser than nitrogen prepared chemically. Working with William Ramsay, he isolated the cause: a previously unknown inert gas, argon, announced in 1894. For this work Rayleigh received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1904, while Ramsay received the Chemistry prize the same year. Rayleigh also made precision measurements of electrical standards and served as president of the Royal Society (1905–1908) and Chancellor of the University of Cambridge.
