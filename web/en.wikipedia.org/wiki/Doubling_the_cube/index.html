Doubling the cube

Doubling the cube, also known as the Delian problem, is an ancient geometric problem. Given the edge of a cube, the problem requires the construction of the edge of a second cube whose volume is double that of the first. As with the related problems of squaring the circle and trisecting the angle, doubling the cube is now known to be impossible to construct using only a compass and straightedge, but even in ancient times solutions were known using other tools.

The legend

According to a legend reported by later commentators, the citizens of Delos consulted the oracle of Apollo during a plague (430 BC) and were told to double the size of the god's cubical altar. Unable to do so, they consulted Plato, who interpreted the oracle as a rebuke to the Greeks for neglecting mathematics and geometry.

Reduction to two mean proportionals

Hippocrates of Chios reduced the problem to finding two mean proportionals between a segment and one of twice its length: if a : x = x : y = y : 2a, then x3 = 2a3, so x is the required edge. Solutions using conic sections (Menaechmus), a special curve (the cissoid of Diocles), or sliding/mechanical devices (Archytas, Eratosthenes' mesolabe) were given in antiquity.

Impossibility proof

The edge to be constructed is ∛2 times the original. In 1837 Pierre Wantzel proved that ∛2 is not a constructible number: compass-and-straightedge constructions can only produce numbers in field extensions whose degree is a power of two, while ∛2 has degree 3 over the rationals. Hence the construction is impossible with those tools alone.

Solutions with other tools

The cube can be doubled using a marked ruler (neusis construction), conic sections, or origami: paper-folding axioms (the Huzita–Hatori axioms) permit solving cubic equations, and a fold due to Margherita Beloch constructs ∛2.
