Guard of honour

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A guard of honour (Commonwealth English), honor guard (American English), or ceremonial guard, is a group of people, typically drawn from the military, appointed to perform ceremonial duties: receiving or guarding a head of state or other dignitaries, the fallen in war, or state property such as flags and national monuments. The term also applies to those who stand guard at ceremonial and sporting occasions.

Military ceremonies

Guards of honour are a standard feature of state visits. A visiting head of state, accompanied by the host, inspects a formed body of soldiers — often one hundred or more — while a military band plays and a 21-gun salute may be fired. Permanent ceremonial guards include the troops mounting the King's Guard at Buckingham Palace, the Old Guard (3rd U.S. Infantry Regiment) guarding the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at Arlington National Cemetery, and similar units at the Kremlin, the Arc de Triomphe, and war memorials worldwide. Guards at tombs of unknown soldiers typically perform an elaborate, precisely timed changing ritual.

Other uses

In sport, a guard of honour is formed when players of one team line up to applaud another team — for example, a championship-winning football team in its remaining matches, or a retiring player leaving the field. In cricket, batsmen may be applauded through a corridor formed by the fielding side. Guards of honour also feature at military and state funerals, at weddings involving service personnel (forming a sabre or sword arch), and in scouting and cadet organisations.
