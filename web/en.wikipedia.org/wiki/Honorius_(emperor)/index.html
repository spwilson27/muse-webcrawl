Flavius Honorius (9 September 384 – 15 August 423) was Roman emperor in the West from 393 to 423. The younger son of Theodosius I, he was proclaimed Augustus at the age of eight and succeeded his father in the western half of the empire in 395, while his elder brother Arcadius ruled the East. His long reign saw the sack of Rome by the Visigoths in 410, a defining moment in the decline of the Western Roman Empire.

Early reign and Stilicho

Because Honorius was a child, real power lay with his guardian, the general Stilicho, whose daughters Maria and Thermantia successively married the emperor. Stilicho campaigned against the Visigoths under Alaric and defeated the invasions of Radagaisus (406), but the crossing of the Rhine by Vandals, Alans and Suebi at the end of 406 overwhelmed the western defences, and the usurper Constantine III seized Britain and Gaul. In 408, after Arcadius's death, Honorius's court at Ravenna turned on Stilicho, who was executed — a decision that removed the empire's ablest commander on the eve of Alaric's final advance.

The sack of Rome

Alaric blockaded Rome three times; on 24 August 410 his Visigoths entered the city and plundered it for three days — the first time Rome had fallen to a foreign enemy in eight centuries. Honorius, safe behind the marshes of Ravenna, refused concessions. A famous (and probably apocryphal) anecdote in Procopius relates that Honorius initially mistook the news "Rome has perished" as referring to his pet chicken, which was also named Roma.

Later reign

After 410 the general Constantius gradually restored a measure of order: the Visigoths were settled in Aquitaine as foederati (418), and Constantius married Honorius's sister Galla Placidia and was briefly co-emperor as Constantius III before dying in 421. Honorius died of dropsy on 15 August 423, leaving no heir; after the usurpation of Joannes, the East installed his young nephew Valentinian III as western emperor.

Legacy

Historians from Gibbon onwards have judged Honorius harshly as a weak and passive ruler under whom the West lost Britain and much of Gaul and Spain, though modern scholarship stresses the structural crises of the empire and notes that the western state survived, battered, throughout his reign.
