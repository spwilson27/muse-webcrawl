Gut microbiota

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The gut microbiota is the community of microorganisms — mostly bacteria, plus archaea, fungi and viruses — living in the digestive tracts of animals, including the roughly 38 trillion bacteria of the human gut. Together with their genes (the gut microbiome) they act as a metabolic 'organ', deeply influencing digestion, immunity and even mood and behaviour.

Functions

Gut microbes ferment fibres the body cannot digest, producing short-chain fatty acids that feed the colon lining, and they synthesise vitamins K and B. They train the immune system, crowd out pathogens, and metabolise drugs and bile acids. The community is seeded at birth, shaped by diet, antibiotics and environment, and differs markedly between individuals.

Health and research

Imbalances (dysbiosis) are associated with inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, diabetes, allergies and other conditions, though cause and effect are often unclear. Treatments and interventions include probiotics, dietary change and faecal microbiota transplantation — highly effective for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection. The field expanded rapidly with DNA sequencing, which revealed most gut species without needing to culture them.
