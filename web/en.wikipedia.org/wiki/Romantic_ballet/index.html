Romantic ballet


Period: c. 1830s–1870s
Centres: Paris, St Petersburg, Copenhagen
Key works: La Sylphide (1832), Giselle (1841)

Romantic ballet is the era of classical ballet corresponding to the Romantic movement in art and literature, flourishing roughly from the 1830s to the 1870s. It elevated the ballerina to dominance, introduced dancing en pointe as an expressive device, and favoured stories of unrequited love between mortals and supernatural beings — sylphs, wilis, and spirits — set between a realistic first act and a moonlit, ethereal second act (the ballet blanc).

Characteristics

The Romantic tutu — a bell-shaped skirt of layered white tulle reaching below the knee — and gaslight stage effects created an atmosphere of weightlessness and otherworldliness. Choreography emphasised port de bras and floating illusions, with the male dancer increasingly relegated to supporting and partnering the ballerina. The librettos reflected the Romantic fascination with the exotic, the medieval, and the unattainable beloved.

Landmark works and artists

  • La Sylphide (Paris, 1832), choreographed by Filippo Taglioni for his daughter Marie Taglioni, whose apparently effortless pointe work defined the era; August Bournonville's Danish version (1836) still survives in the repertory of the Royal Danish Ballet.
  • Giselle (1841), choreographed by Jean Coralli and Jules Perrot to Adolphe Adam's score, created for Carlotta Grisi — the supreme surviving Romantic ballet.
  • Pas de Quatre (1845), Jules Perrot's divertissement uniting Taglioni, Grisi, Fanny Cerrito, and Lucile Grahn.
  • Other leading ballerinas: Fanny Elssler, famed for character dance such as the cachucha, and Emma Livry.

Legacy

As the centre of ballet moved to Imperial Russia, the Romantic inheritance fed into the grand classical ballets of Petipa, who retained the ballet blanc structure within works like Swan Lake and The Sleeping Beauty. Bournonville's school preserved the Romantic repertoire and style in Copenhagen into the present day.
