The Anointing of the Sick is one of the seven sacraments of the Catholic Church. It is administered to a Catholic who, having reached the age of reason, begins to be in danger of death or serious decline due to sickness or old age, conferring a special grace of spiritual strength, forgiveness of sins, and sometimes bodily healing if expedient for salvation. Its biblical basis is cited in the Letter of James 5:14–15.

History

In the Middle Ages the sacrament came to be administered primarily to the dying and acquired the name Extreme Unction — the "last anointing", following baptism, confirmation and ordination in the sequence of anointings. The Council of Trent (1551) defended its sacramental character against Protestant criticism. The Second Vatican Council's constitution Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963) taught that it is "not a sacrament for those only who are at the point of death", and the revised rite promulgated by Paul VI in 1972 restored the name Anointing of the Sick.

Rite

The priest lays hands on the sick person in silence, blesses (or uses pre-blessed) oil of olives, and anoints the forehead and hands, saying: "Through this holy anointing may the Lord in his love and mercy help you with the grace of the Holy Spirit. May the Lord who frees you from sin save you and raise you up." The sacrament may be celebrated individually or communally at Mass, and may be repeated if the illness worsens.

Effects

The Catechism of the Catholic Church lists the sacrament's effects as union with the Passion of Christ, strength and courage to endure suffering, forgiveness of sins (if penance is impossible), restoration to health if conducive to salvation, and preparation for passing over to eternal life.
