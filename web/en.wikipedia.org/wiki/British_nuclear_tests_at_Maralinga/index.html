The British nuclear tests at Maralinga were a series of nuclear weapons tests conducted by the United Kingdom in the remote desert of South Australia between 1956 and 1963. Maralinga was the main site for Britain's atmospheric test programme after earlier trials at the Montebello Islands and Emu Field. The tests — and the contamination they left behind — became a major controversy, particularly over their effects on Aboriginal people and Australian and British service personnel.

The trials

The site, in the traditional lands of the Maralinga Tjarutja people, was chosen for its remoteness. The first series, Operation Buffalo (September–October 1956), comprised four detonations, including one dropped from a Valiant bomber — the first British air-dropped nuclear test. Operation Antler followed in 1957 with three further tests. Alongside the major trials, hundreds of "minor trials" — experiments involving plutonium and other radioactive materials — dispersed contamination over a wide area; the most polluting, the Vixen B series (1960–63), spread several kilograms of plutonium around the Taranaki site.

Effects and controversy

Aboriginal communities were moved from the area, but some people remained in or passed through the test zone, and fallout drifted over populated areas; the "black mist" that passed over Wallatinna station after the 1953 Totem test at Emu Field became emblematic of the dangers. In 1984–85 the McClelland Royal Commission investigated the tests, criticising the British and Australian authorities for inadequate safety and lack of informed consent toward Aboriginal people.

Clean-up and legacy

A major rehabilitation programme, completed in 2000 at a cost of over A$100 million (to which the UK contributed £20 million), removed contaminated soil and debris; control of the land was progressively returned to the Maralinga Tjarutja, with the final section handed back in 2014. Veterans' campaigns for recognition and compensation, and studies of health effects in participants and Aboriginal communities, have continued into the 21st century, and in 2022 the Australian government awarded a medal to nuclear test participants.
