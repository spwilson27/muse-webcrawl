Scratching

EquipmentTwo turntables (or one), DJ mixer with crossfader

Scratching, sometimes referred to as scrubbing, is a DJ and turntablist technique of moving a vinyl record back and forth on a turntable while manipulating the mixer's crossfader, producing percussive and pitched sounds. It is one of the foundational techniques of hip hop music and of turntablism, transforming the record player from a playback device into a musical instrument.

History

The technique is credited to Grand Wizard Theodore, who, as a teenager in the Bronx in about 1975, discovered the sound accidentally while holding a record still as his mother spoke to him, then developed it into a deliberate performance technique. Grandmaster Flash refined scratching with precision cueing systems, and performers such as Grand Mixer DXT (then DST), who scratched on Herbie Hancock's Grammy-winning "Rockit" (1983), brought it to mainstream audiences. In the 1990s, crews such as the Invisibl Skratch Piklz and the X-Ecutioners, and the DMC World DJ Championships, codified scratching into a competitive art.

Techniques

  • Baby scratch: the record is simply moved back and forth with the fader open.
  • Forward / stab scratches: rhythmic pushes of the record combined with quick crossfader cuts.
  • Transform: clicking the fader on and off while the record moves continuously, named after its resemblance to the sound of the Transformers cartoon characters.
  • Flare: invented by DJ Flare, using fader clicks at the ends of the record's motion to multiply sounds.
  • Crab: rapid fader taps with three or four fingers, developed by DJ Qbert and others.
  • Chirp: a forward-and-back motion combined with a single fader cut, associated with DJ Jazzy Jeff.

Equipment and notation

Scratching is performed on direct-drive turntables (classically the Technics SL-1200) fitted with slipmats and durable cartridges, and a battle mixer with a sharp crossfader curve. Digital vinyl systems (such as Serato Scratch Live) emulate the technique with timecode records. Attempts to notate scratches include the Turntablist Transcription Methodology (TTM), which graphs record movement over time.
