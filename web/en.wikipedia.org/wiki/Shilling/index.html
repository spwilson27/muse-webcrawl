Shilling

The shilling is a historical coin and unit of currency formerly used in the United Kingdom and other Commonwealth countries, and still used as the name of the currencies of several East African states. The British shilling, abbreviated "s" or written as part of the £sd system (pounds, shillings, pence), was worth one twentieth of a pound sterling, or twelve old pence.

History

The word derives from a Germanic root for a gold or silver coin of small value; in Anglo-Saxon England the scilling was a unit of account. Shilling coins were first struck in England under Henry VII, around 1502, when the coin was known as the testoon. Under the £sd system, twenty shillings made a pound and twelve pence made a shilling. Scottish shillings circulated at different values before the monetary union of 1707.

From 1816, under the Great Recoinage, the shilling was struck in sterling silver, and from 1947 in cupronickel. On Decimal Day, 15 February 1971, the United Kingdom decimalised its currency: the shilling was withdrawn, with the new five pence coin initially identical in size and value to the shilling coin, which remained in circulation as 5p until 1990.

Shillings today

The name survives in the currencies of Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and Somalia (the Kenyan shilling, Ugandan shilling, Tanzanian shilling, and Somali shilling) — legacies of the East African shilling of British East Africa. "Shilling" also survives in British slang ("bob") and in idioms such as "to take the King's shilling".
