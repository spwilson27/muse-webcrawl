Full titleThe Pilgrim's Progress from This World, to That Which Is to Come

The Pilgrim's Progress from This World, to That Which Is to Come is a 1678 Christian allegory written by John Bunyan. It is regarded as one of the most significant works of English literature, has been translated more often than any book except the Bible, and has never been out of print since its first publication.

Plot

Written as a dream narrative, the first part follows Christian, who flees the City of Destruction bearing a great burden of sin on his back and journeys along the narrow way to the Celestial City. He passes through the Slough of Despond, Vanity Fair (where his companion Faithful is martyred), the Delectable Mountains and the land of Beulah, battles the fiend Apollyon and the Giant Despair, and is accompanied part of the way by Hopeful, before crossing the River of Death into the Celestial City. The second part (1684) tells of the pilgrimage of Christian's wife Christiana and their children, guided by Great-heart.

Background and influence

Bunyan, a Bedfordshire tinker and Nonconformist preacher, began the work during one of his imprisonments for preaching without a licence. Its plain style, vivid characters and allegorical place-names — the Slough of Despond, Vanity Fair, the Delectable Mountains — passed into the language and influenced writers from Charlotte Brontë and Louisa May Alcott to C. S. Lewis and William Thackeray, whose novel Vanity Fair takes its title from Bunyan's.
