Volunteer computing
ModelDistributed computing with donated idle resources

Pioneering projectsSETI@home, Folding@home, distributed.net

Volunteer computing is a type of distributed computing in which members of the public donate unused computing resources — CPU and GPU cycles on home computers and phones — to research-oriented projects. Since the late 1990s it has mobilised processing power rivalling large supercomputers for tasks that divide naturally into many independent work units.

History

Early projects included the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS, 1996) and distributed.net's code-breaking efforts (1997). The launch of SETI@home in 1999, which analysed radio-telescope data for extraterrestrial signals and attracted millions of participants, popularised the model through attractive screensaver clients. Its team developed BOINC (Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing, 2002), a general middleware platform now hosting projects in astronomy, climate modelling (ClimatePrediction.net), mathematics and biomedicine. Folding@home, studying protein folding outside BOINC, briefly exceeded one exaFLOPS during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.

How it works

Volunteers install a client that downloads work units from project servers, computes them during idle time and returns results, which are typically validated by replicating each unit on multiple machines and awarding "credit". Challenges include heterogeneous and unreliable hosts, result verification, and volunteer retention, addressed through credit systems, teams and forums.
