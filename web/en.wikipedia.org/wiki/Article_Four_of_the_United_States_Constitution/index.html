Article Four of the United States Constitution


Article Four addresses relations among the states and between the states and the federal government.

Article Four of the United States Constitution outlines the relationship between the various states, as well as the relationship between each state and the United States federal government. It also empowers Congress to admit new states and administer federal territories and other federal lands. Article Four is sometimes called the "States' Relations" article.

Section 1: Full Faith and Credit

The Full Faith and Credit Clause requires each state to give "Full Faith and Credit" to the public acts, records and judicial proceedings of every other state. This ensures, for example, that a court judgment or a marriage recognised in one state must generally be recognised in others. Congress is empowered to prescribe the manner of proving such acts and their effect; it has done so in statutes including the Defense of Marriage Act's recognition provision (later superseded in effect by Obergefell v. Hodges and the Respect for Marriage Act).

Section 2: Privileges and Immunities; extradition

The Privileges and Immunities Clause provides that "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States", preventing a state from discriminating against citizens of other states in respect of fundamental rights, subject to reasonable justification. The section also contains the Extradition Clause, requiring a state to deliver a person charged with a crime who has fled from another state back to that state on demand of its executive authority. A third clause, concerning the return of fugitive slaves, became obsolete with the Thirteenth Amendment.

Section 3: New states and federal property

The Admissions Clause empowers Congress to admit new states into the Union, but forbids carving a new state out of an existing one, or by joining states, without the consent of the state legislatures concerned as well as of Congress — a provision tested by the admission of West Virginia during the Civil War. The Property Clause (Territorial Clause) gives Congress power to make "all needful Rules and Regulations" respecting the territory and other property of the United States, the basis of federal public-land law and of Congress's authority over territories such as Puerto Rico and Guam.

Section 4: Republican government and protection

The Guarantee Clause provides that the United States "shall guarantee to every State in this Union a Republican Form of Government" and shall protect each state against invasion and, on application of the state, against domestic violence. The Supreme Court has generally treated claims under this clause as non-justiciable political questions, beginning with Luther v. Borden (1849).
