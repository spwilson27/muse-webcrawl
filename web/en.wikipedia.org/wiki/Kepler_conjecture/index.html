Kepler conjecture

The Kepler conjecture, named after the 17th-century mathematician and astronomer Johannes Kepler, is a mathematical theorem about sphere packing in three-dimensional Euclidean space. It states that no arrangement of equally sized spheres filling space has a greater average density than that of the cubic close packing (face-centred cubic) and hexagonal close packing arrangements. The density of these arrangements is slightly greater than 74%: π / (3√2) ≈ 0.74048.

History

Kepler stated the conjecture in his 1611 pamphlet Strena seu de Nive Sexangula ("On the Six-Cornered Snowflake"), written as a New Year's gift for his patron and inspired by correspondence with Thomas Harriot about the stacking of cannonballs. Although the densest packing seemed intuitively obvious, a rigorous proof eluded mathematicians for nearly four centuries. Carl Friedrich Gauss proved in 1831 that the face-centred cubic packing is the densest among lattice packings, but non-lattice arrangements remained open. The problem formed the first part of the sphere-packing portion of Hilbert's eighteenth problem (1900).

Proof

In 1998, Thomas Hales announced a proof by exhaustion, involving the checking of many individual cases using complex computer calculations. The proof, developed with Samuel Ferguson, divided the problem into thousands of cases involving "tame plane graphs" and linear programming bounds. Published in the Annals of Mathematics in 2005 after years of review, the referees reported being "99% certain" of its correctness. Because the computer-assisted proof could not be fully verified by hand, Hales launched the Flyspeck project to produce a completely formal proof; the formal verification was completed in August 2014 using the HOL Light and Isabelle proof assistants.
