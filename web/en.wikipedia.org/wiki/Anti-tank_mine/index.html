Anti-tank mine

An anti-tank mine (abbreviated to "AT mine") is a type of land mine designed to damage or destroy vehicles including tanks and armoured fighting vehicles. Anti-tank mines typically require greater pressure to detonate — commonly 100 to 300 kilograms — than anti-personnel mines, though many can be fitted with tilt-rod or magnetic-influence fuzes and anti-handling devices.

Design

Blast AT mines, such as the Soviet TM-46/TM-57 family and the American M15, contain a large explosive charge (5–10 kg of TNT or similar) in a metal, plastic or wooden casing. The explosion breaks tracks, damages wheels and suspension, and may breach the hull floor. Shaped-charge and explosively formed penetrator designs attack armour more directly. Minimum-metal and plastic-bodied mines complicate detection.

Use

AT mines are laid in minefields to deny terrain, canalise enemy movement and protect positions. They are widely stockpiled; unlike anti-personnel mines, they are not banned by the 1997 Ottawa Treaty, though their use is regulated by Amended Protocol II of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons.

Countermeasures

Clearing methods include mine ploughs and rollers, line charges (such as MICLIC), flails and manual detection by engineers.
