The Shimabara Rebellion (島原の乱, Shimabara no ran) was an uprising of Japanese Christian peasants and rōnin in the Shimabara Peninsula and Amakusa Islands of Kyushu in 1637–1638, during the Edo period. It was the largest civil conflict of the Tokugawa era and the last major armed resistance to the shogunate until the Boshin War. Its suppression led directly to the final expulsion of the Portuguese and the completion of Japan's sakoku policy of national seclusion.

Causes

The Shimabara domain, formerly ruled by the Christian daimyō Arima family, passed to Matsukura Shigemasa, whose heavy taxation — including levies to fund construction of Shimabara Castle — drove peasants to desperation. His successor Katsuie continued harsh persecution of the region's many hidden Christians, who had once formed a large share of the population before Christianity was banned. Drought, famine-level tax demands in rice the peasants did not have, and brutal suppression of Christian practice combined to ignite revolt in late 1637; a parallel rising broke out in the Amakusa Islands under similar conditions.

The rebellion

The rebels rallied around the charismatic teenage leader Amakusa Shirō, said to perform miracles, and thousands took up arms under banners bearing Christian inscriptions. After initial successes against domain forces, some 37,000 rebels and their families withdrew into the abandoned Hara Castle on the coast. A shogunal army eventually numbering over 100,000 besieged the castle; Dutch ships from Hirado, under Nicolaes Couckebacker, controversially bombarded the fortress from the sea at the shogunate's request. Starved out after months of siege, the castle fell in a final assault on April 12, 1638; virtually all inside, including Amakusa Shirō, were killed.

Consequences

The shogunate blamed Portuguese missionaries and traders for encouraging Christianity: in 1639 the Portuguese were expelled entirely, leaving only the Dutch and Chinese confined to Nagasaki. Anti-Christian enforcement intensified (the fumi-e trampling tests and the temple-registration system), Christianity in Japan went underground as the "hidden Christians" (kakure kirishitan), and Japan entered two centuries of tightly controlled foreign contact.
