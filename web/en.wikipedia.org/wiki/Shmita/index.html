Shmita

The shmita (Hebrew: שמיטה, "release"), also called the sabbatical year or shǝvi'it (שביעית, "seventh"), is the seventh year of the seven-year agricultural cycle mandated by the Torah for the Land of Israel. During shmita, the land is left to lie fallow, agricultural activity is largely forbidden, debts are remitted, and produce that grows on its own is considered ownerless and available to all.

Biblical sources

The laws appear in Exodus 23, Leviticus 25, and Deuteronomy 15. Leviticus commands that the land observe a Sabbath of rest every seventh year; Deuteronomy adds the release of debts at the year's end. After seven shmita cycles comes the Jubilee (yovel) year, with its own additional rules.

Observance

Classical rules prohibit planting, pruning, harvesting for commerce, and other field work, though minimal maintenance to prevent loss of trees is permitted. Because the produce of the shmita year carries sanctity (kedushat shevi'it), it must be consumed rather than wasted or traded normally. The legal device of heter mekhira — selling the land to a non-Jew for the year — was adopted by many early Zionist settlements and remains controversial among authorities. Another mechanism, the otzar beit din, distributes produce communally. Hillel's prozbul historically circumvented the cancellation of private loans.

Recent cycles

Recent shmita years include 2007–08, 2014–15, and 2021–22 (5782 in the Hebrew calendar). In modern Israel, shmita observance is a recurring point of negotiation between religious and secular communities and shapes agricultural import policy.
