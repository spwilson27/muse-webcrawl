Lou Harrison

Lou Silver Harrison (May 14, 1917 – February 2, 2003) was an American composer associated with the West Coast experimental tradition. A student of Henry Cowell and Arnold Schoenberg, he was a pioneer of percussion music, of composition in just intonation, and of fusing Western instruments with Asian musical traditions, especially the Javanese gamelan.

Life and career

Born in Portland, Oregon, and raised in California, Harrison studied with Cowell in San Francisco and later with Schoenberg in Los Angeles. With John Cage he organised early percussion concerts and composed works such as the Double Music (1941). In New York in the 1940s he worked as a critic under Virgil Thomson and conducted the first complete performance of a Charles Ives symphony (the Third, 1946), winning Ives a Pulitzer Prize. After a breakdown he returned to California, settling in Aptos for the rest of his life.

Music

Harrison's output exceeds 300 works: the opera Rapunzel and Young Caesar, the Suite for Violin and American Gamelan, the Piano Concerto written for Keith Jarrett, and four symphonies. With his partner William Colvig he built "American gamelan" instruments from aluminium tubes and other found materials, and composed extensively for them using just intonation tuning systems. A lifelong advocate of Esperanto and pacifist and gay-rights causes, Harrison embodied an ideal of melodic, dance-infused "world music" long before the term existed.

Legacy

Influenced by Asian, Native American and early European music, Harrison is remembered as one of the most distinctive and joyful American composers of the twentieth century; festivals and a 2017 centenary brought renewed attention to his work.
