Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction

Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction
TypeOscillating chemical reaction
DiscoveredBoris Belousov (1950s); developed by Anatol Zhabotinsky (1960s)
Key reagentsBromate, malonic acid, metal catalyst (e.g. ferroin)

The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, or BZ reaction, is one of the best-known examples of an oscillating chemical reaction and a classic demonstration of non-equilibrium thermodynamics. In an unstirred solution it produces striking expanding rings and spiral waves of colour.

History

Soviet chemist Boris Belousov discovered in the 1950s that a mixture of potassium bromate, cerium sulfate, malonic acid and citric acid in dilute sulfuric acid oscillates in colour as cerium ions alternate between oxidation states. His results were initially rejected as seemingly violating thermodynamics. Biophysicist Anatol Zhabotinsky rediscovered and extended the work in the 1960s, substituting ferroin as the catalyst-indicator.

Mechanism

The mechanism is complex, involving dozens of elementary steps. The simplified Field–Körös–Noyes (FKN) mechanism explains the oscillation as a switching between a radical process that consumes bromide ion and an autocatalytic process that produces it, generating feedback that repeatedly resets the system. The reaction is far from equilibrium, so no thermodynamic laws are violated.

Patterns and significance

In thin layers, the BZ reaction forms target patterns and rotating spirals similar to patterns seen in biological systems such as cardiac tissue and slime mould aggregation. It became a cornerstone example in the study of self-organisation, dissipative structures and excitable media.
