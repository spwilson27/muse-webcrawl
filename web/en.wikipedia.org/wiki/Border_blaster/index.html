Border blaster

Border blaster
Type: High-power radio station near an international border
Classic location: Northern Mexico, broadcasting into the United States
Typical power: 50,000–500,000 watts
Heyday: 1930s–1970s

A border blaster is a high-power radio station located just across an international border, broadcasting into a neighbouring country at signal strengths far beyond what that country permits its own stations. The term is most associated with Mexican stations along the border with the United States, which from the 1930s onward beamed English-language programming across much of North America.

History

US regulation capped American AM stations (ultimately at 50,000 watts), while Mexico allowed much higher power. Entrepreneurs such as John Romulus Brinkley, who had lost his Kansas licence and promoted dubious medical treatments, established stations like XER and XERA at Villa Acuña with powers reportedly reaching 500,000 watts or more. Other famous border stations, including XERB, XEG, and XERF, broadcast country music, rhythm and blues, preaching, mail-order advertising, and later rock and roll, introducing many American listeners to music their local stations would not play.

Culture and decline

Border radio shaped American popular culture: disc jockey Wolfman Jack became famous broadcasting from XERF in the 1960s, and the stations inspired songs such as ZZ Top's "Heard It on the X." International agreements, changing regulation in Mexico, and the rise of FM and television eroded the border blasters' reach, though some of the Mexican "X" stations continue as powerful clear-channel outlets.
