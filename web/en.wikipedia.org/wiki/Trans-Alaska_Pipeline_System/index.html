The Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS) is an oil transportation system spanning Alaska, including the trans-Alaska crude-oil pipeline, 11 pump stations, several hundred miles of feeder pipelines, and the Valdez Marine Terminal. It carries oil from the North Slope of Alaska, where the Prudhoe Bay oil field was discovered in 1968, south across the state to the ice-free port of Valdez on Prince William Sound.

Construction

Construction followed the passage of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline Authorization Act in 1973, signed by President Richard Nixon amid the 1973 oil crisis, which cut off legal challenges. Building the line was one of the largest private construction projects of its era, employing tens of thousands of workers at its peak and costing roughly $8 billion. The first oil flowed on 20 June 1977.

Design

Roughly half of the 48-inch pipeline is elevated above ground on vertical support members fitted with heat pipes, because burying a hot oil line would thaw the permafrost and destabilise the pipe. The line crosses three mountain ranges and hundreds of rivers and streams, and zigzags to absorb thermal expansion and seismic movement; it survived the magnitude-7.9 Denali fault earthquake of 2002 without rupturing.

Throughput and incidents

Flow peaked in 1988 at around 2.1 million barrels per day. In March 1989 the tanker Exxon Valdez ran aground after loading at Valdez, causing a major oil spill, though the spill was from the tanker rather than the pipeline itself. Declining North Slope production has since reduced throughput to a fraction of the peak, complicating operations because slow-moving oil cools in the pipe.
