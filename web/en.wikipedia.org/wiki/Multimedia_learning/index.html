Multimedia learning is learning that occurs when students build mental representations from words (spoken or printed) and pictures (illustrations, animations, video). The field was defined largely by the psychologist Richard E. Mayer, whose Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML) explains how such learning works and what instructional designs promote it.

Cognitive theory

CTML rests on three assumptions supported by cognitive science: (1) the human information-processing system has separate channels for visual/pictorial and auditory/verbal material (drawing on Allan Paivio's dual coding theory); (2) each channel has a severely limited capacity, as described by cognitive load theory; and (3) learning requires active processing — selecting relevant material, organizing it into coherent representations, and integrating it with prior knowledge.

Design principles

From dozens of experiments, Mayer derived evidence-based principles, including:

  • Multimedia principle — people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone.
  • Coherence principle — extraneous words, pictures and sounds ("seductive details") hurt learning and should be removed.
  • Modality principle — graphics are better explained by spoken narration than by on-screen text.
  • Redundancy principle — narration plus graphics is better than narration, graphics and identical on-screen text.
  • Segmenting principle — learners do better when a lesson is broken into user-paced segments.
  • Signaling principle — cues that highlight the organization of essential material improve learning.

Applications

The principles guide the design of textbooks, e-learning, simulations and educational video, and have been extended to learning in virtual reality, games and with interactive media.
