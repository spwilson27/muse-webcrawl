Re-education through labor

Re-education through labor (Chinese: 劳动教养; pinyin: láodòng jiàoyǎng), abbreviated laojiao, was a system of administrative detention in the People's Republic of China. Active from 1957 until its abolition in 2013, it allowed police committees to sentence individuals to terms of up to four years in labor camps without trial or judicial review. Those detained were typically accused of minor offenses such as petty theft, prostitution, drug use, or political and religious dissent that did not rise to the level of criminal prosecution.

History

The system was formally established by a decision of the State Council in August 1957, in the context of the Anti-Rightist Campaign. It was designed as a measure combining punishment, employment placement, and ideological reform for people considered insufficiently serious offenders for the criminal justice system but deemed in need of "re-education."

During the Cultural Revolution the system expanded, and in 1979 supplementary regulations fixed the maximum term at three years, extendable by one year. Decisions were made by "re-education through labor management committees" dominated by public security organs, and detainees had little practical recourse.

Criticism and abolition

Human rights organizations long criticized laojiao for bypassing courts, enabling arbitrary detention, and exposing detainees to harsh conditions and coerced labor. High-profile cases, such as that of Tang Hui, a mother detained after petitioning for harsher punishment of her daughter's rapists, intensified domestic criticism in 2012. In November 2013 the Third Plenum of the 18th Central Committee announced the system's abolition, and the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress formally repealed the relevant regulations on 28 December 2013. Remaining detainees were released and their sentences not carried out.
