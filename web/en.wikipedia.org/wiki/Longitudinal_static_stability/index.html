Longitudinal static stability

In flight dynamics, longitudinal static stability is the stability of an aircraft in the longitudinal, or pitching, plane. It describes the tendency of an aircraft, disturbed in pitch, to generate a restoring pitching moment that returns it toward its trimmed condition.

An aircraft is statically stable in pitch if an increase in angle of attack produces a nose-down (negative) pitching moment about the centre of gravity, and a decrease produces a nose-up moment. Mathematically this requires the slope of the pitching moment coefficient curve with respect to angle of attack, dCm/dα, to be negative.

Contributions

The wing alone is usually destabilising because its aerodynamic centre lies ahead of typical centre-of-gravity positions. The horizontal tailplane, acting at a long moment arm behind the centre of gravity, provides the principal stabilising contribution: an increase in angle of attack increases tail lift and pitches the nose down. The fuselage and engine nacelles generally contribute adverse (destabilising) moments.

Static margin and neutral point

The neutral point is the centre-of-gravity position for which the aircraft is neutrally stable (dCm/dα = 0). The static margin is the distance between the centre of gravity and the neutral point, expressed as a fraction of the mean aerodynamic chord; the centre of gravity must lie ahead of the neutral point for positive static stability. Larger static margins give stronger stability but heavier stick forces and trim drag, so transport aircraft often fly with modest margins, and some military aircraft are designed unstable and rely on flight control systems (relaxed static stability).
