Gene synthesis

Gene synthesis, also called artificial gene synthesis, is a group of methods in synthetic biology used to construct genes from nucleotides de novo, without the need for an initial template DNA sample. It allows researchers to obtain custom DNA sequences — optimized, mutated, or entirely novel — as physical molecules, typically supplied cloned into a plasmid by commercial providers.

Automated phosphoramidite synthesis produces the oligonucleotides assembled into genes

Methods

Most gene synthesis begins with chemical synthesis of short single-stranded oligonucleotides (typically 40–200 bases) by the phosphoramidite method. Overlapping oligonucleotides are then assembled into full-length double-stranded constructs by techniques such as polymerase cycling assembly (PCA), ligase chain reaction, or isothermal Gibson assembly. Errors arise mainly from deletions and substitutions in the synthetic oligonucleotides, so constructs are usually sequence-verified, and error-correction steps using mismatch-binding enzymes or hybridization selection may be applied.

Design considerations

Before synthesis, sequences are typically codon-optimized for the expression host, screened to remove unwanted motifs (cryptic splice sites, extreme GC content, repeats), and checked against sequence-screening databases. Declining costs — from several dollars per base pair in the 1990s to a few cents per base pair for standard genes — have made synthesis a routine substitute for molecular cloning.

Applications and biosecurity

Gene synthesis underlies much of modern biotechnology: producing recombinant proteins, engineering metabolic pathways, constructing viral genomes for research (such as the poliovirus genome synthesized in 2002), and assembling whole bacterial genomes, including the synthetic Mycoplasma genome produced by the J. Craig Venter Institute. Because the same technology could be used to obtain pathogens or toxins, providers screen orders against lists of regulated agents, and synthesis of complete genomes of dangerous pathogens has raised dual-use concerns addressed by international screening frameworks.
