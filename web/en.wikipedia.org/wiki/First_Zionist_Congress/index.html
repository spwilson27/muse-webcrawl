First Zionist Congress

The First Zionist Congress was the inaugural meeting of the Zionist Organization, convened by Theodor Herzl in Basel, Switzerland, from 29 to 31 August 1897. Some 200 delegates from across Europe and beyond gathered to establish a political movement for the creation of a Jewish homeland in Palestine. The congress adopted the Basel Program, founded the World Zionist Organization, and is regarded as a foundational event of modern political Zionism.

Background

Herzl, a Viennese journalist, had published Der Judenstaat ("The Jewish State") in 1896, arguing that antisemitism — dramatised for him by the Dreyfus affair in France — could be answered only by a Jewish state secured by public law. He originally planned the congress for Munich, but opposition from the local Jewish community and the German rabbinate, who feared charges of dual loyalty, forced a move to Basel. Delegates were required to buy a "shekel", a membership token that also served as a franchise for electing delegates.

Proceedings

Herzl presided in formal dress, insisting on the decorum of a parliament to lend the young movement legitimacy. Max Nordau presented a survey of the condition of Jews worldwide. The congress adopted the Basel Program, which declared: "Zionism seeks to establish a home for the Jewish people in Palestine secured under public law," and listed the means to that end — promoting Jewish settlement in Palestine, organising world Jewry through local and general bodies, strengthening Jewish national sentiment, and obtaining the consent of governments. The congress created the Zionist Organization with Herzl as its first president, an executive (Actions Committee), a newspaper, Die Welt, and plans for a Jewish Colonial Trust bank.

Significance

Herzl famously wrote in his diary on 3 September 1897: "At Basel I founded the Jewish State. If I said this out loud today I would be greeted by universal laughter. In five years perhaps, and certainly in fifty years, everyone will perceive it." The State of Israel was proclaimed in 1948, fifty-one years later. The congress set the pattern for the World Zionist Congress, which still meets, and its Basel Program remained the movement's platform until the Jerusalem Program of 1951.
