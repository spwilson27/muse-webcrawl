The percussion cap, also called a percussion primer, is a small copper or brass cup containing a shock-sensitive explosive mixture (originally mercury fulminate) used to ignite the gunpowder charge in a firearm. Fitted over a hollow nipple on the breech, it detonates when struck by the hammer, sending a jet of flame through the nipple into the main charge. Introduced in the 1820s, the percussion system replaced the flintlock on military and sporting arms worldwide within a few decades.

History

The Reverend Alexander John Forsyth patented a fulminate ignition system in 1807, using a "scent-bottle" lock that metered loose fulminate powder. Various pill, tube, and tape primers followed. The self-contained cap is usually credited to the artist Joshua Shaw, who developed copper caps by 1814–1816 in America and patented them in 1822, though Joseph Manton and others disputed priority. Joseph Egg, Joseph Manton, and London gunmakers commercialised the cap in Britain in the early 1820s.

Use

Caplock (percussion lock) weapons — rifles, pistols, and revolvers such as the Colt Navy — were loaded with powder and ball from the muzzle (or into revolver chambers), and a fresh cap pressed onto each nipple. Percussion ignition was far more reliable than flint in wet weather and had a faster lock time. Musket caps are larger, with flanged skirts; winged "top hat" caps were used on some military arms. Percussion caps remain in use today for muzzleloading sport shooting and re-enactment, and the principle survives in the primer of every modern centre-fire cartridge.
