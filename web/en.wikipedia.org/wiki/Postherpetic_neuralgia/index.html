Postherpetic neuralgia

SpecialtyNeurology, dermatology
SymptomsBurning, stabbing pain, allodynia in the area of a previous shingles rash
ComplicationsDepression, sleep disturbance, disability
Risk factorsOlder age, severe shingles rash or pain, immunocompromise
PreventionShingles vaccination (Shingrix)

Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is neuropathic pain that persists after the rash of shingles (herpes zoster) has healed. It is the most common complication of shingles, which itself results from reactivation of the varicella zoster virus — latent in dorsal root ganglia since a previous chickenpox infection — when cell-mediated immunity wanes with age or immunosuppression. PHN is commonly defined as pain lasting at least 30 to 90 days (definitions vary, often three months) after the onset of the zoster rash in the affected dermatome.

Signs and symptoms

Pain is typically described as burning, stabbing, shooting or throbbing, and is frequently accompanied by allodynia — severe pain from stimuli that do not normally hurt, such as the touch of clothing or a light breeze — and by areas of numbness or itching. The thoracic dermatomes and the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve are commonly involved. Pain may be constant or intermittent, can disturb sleep, and in older patients is a significant cause of depression, social withdrawal and loss of independence. Roughly 5–30% of people with shingles develop PHN depending on the definition used and the population studied; risk rises steeply with age, with a substantial fraction of patients over 80 affected, and pain may persist for months or years, sometimes indefinitely.

Pathophysiology

During shingles, the virus destroys neurons and nerve fibers in the ganglion and peripheral nerve and alters pain processing in the spinal cord (central sensitization). Loss of epidermal nerve fibers, ectopic impulse generation and deafferentation changes together sustain the pain after the skin lesions heal.

Treatment and prevention

Evidence-based treatments include tricyclic antidepressants (such as amitriptyline and nortriptyline), gabapentinoids (gabapentin and pregabalin), topical lidocaine 5% patches or plaster, high-concentration capsaicin 8% patches, and, where necessary, opioids or tramadol, often in combination. Early antiviral treatment of shingles (e.g., valaciclovir) shortens the rash and acute pain but has a more limited effect on PHN incidence. The most effective preventive measure is vaccination: the recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) is over 90% effective at preventing shingles and substantially reduces PHN, and is recommended for adults over 50 and for younger immunocompromised adults in many countries.
