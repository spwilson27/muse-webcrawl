Shahanshah

Shahanshah (Persian: شاهنشاه, romanized: Šāhanšāh, lit. "King of Kings") is a royal title that originated in ancient Persia and was used by Iranian monarchs for more than two and a half millennia. It is the Middle and Modern Persian form of the Old Persian xšāyaθiya xšāyaθiyānām and corresponds closely to the Akkadian šar šarrāni and the Greek basileus basileōn. The feminine form is Shahbanu ("Lady King" / empress).

History

The idea of a "king of kings" arose because the ancient Near East and the Iranian world consisted of many local rulers: a monarch who held sway over numerous subordinate kings claimed the higher rank. The title is attested for rulers of Elam and Media, and was adopted most famously by the Achaemenid Empire founded by Cyrus the Great, whose Great Kings ruled from Egypt to Central Asia. Under the Parthian (Arsacid) dynasty, "King of Kings" became the standard royal title, appearing on coins as ΒΑΣΙΛΕΥΣ ΒΑΣΙΛΕΩΝ. The Sasanian Empire (224–651 AD) continued the usage, and the title carried with it claims of universal dominion and divine sanction through the royal glory (farr).

Later use

After the Arab conquest of Persia, the title survived in Iranian culture and usage. Medieval Iranian dynasties such as the Buyids revived it, and later the Mughal emperor's Persianate titles and the Ottoman sultans' style şehinşah reflected its prestige. In modern Iran, Mohammad Reza Shah adopted Shahanshah as his principal imperial title, and celebrated it during the 2,500-year celebration of the Persian Empire in 1971; the imperial state was formally styled "Imperial State of Iran," with the Shahanshah as head of state, until the Iranian Revolution of 1979 abolished the monarchy.

The title also influenced the vocabulary of other cultures: the Ethiopian emperors' style Negusa Nagast ("King of Kings") and the Indian Maharajadhiraja express the same concept.
