Financial statement

Financial statements (or financial reports) are formal records of the financial activities and position of a business, person, or other entity. Relevant financial information is presented in a structured manner and in a form that is easy to understand. They typically include four basic statements accompanied by management discussion and notes:

  • Balance sheet (statement of financial position) — reports assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time, following the equation Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
  • Income statement (profit and loss statement) — reports revenues, expenses, and profit or loss over a period.
  • Cash flow statement — reports cash generated and used by operating, investing, and financing activities over a period.
  • Statement of changes in equity — explains movements in owners' equity over the period.

Purpose and users

Financial statements are used by investors, lenders, managers, regulators, and tax authorities to assess performance, liquidity, solvency, and stewardship, and to make economic decisions. Publicly traded companies must publish audited statements — in the United States, the annual Form 10-K and quarterly Form 10-Q filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

Standards and audit

Statements are prepared under accounting frameworks such as IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards, used in most of the world) or US GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles), and rest on conventions including accrual accounting, the going-concern assumption, and consistency. An independent audit provides reasonable assurance that the statements are free of material misstatement; auditors issue an opinion, which may be unqualified, qualified, adverse, or a disclaimer. Misstatements and frauds — as at Enron and WorldCom — have driven reforms such as the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002.

Analysis

Users analyse statements with ratio analysis (liquidity, profitability, leverage ratios), trend and common-size analysis, and comparison against peers. Notes to the financial statements disclose accounting policies, contingencies, segment information, and other detail essential to interpretation.

History

Double-entry bookkeeping, codified by Luca Pacioli in 1494, underlies modern statements. Standardised, audited reporting for outside investors developed with the rise of joint-stock companies and securities markets in the 19th and 20th centuries.
