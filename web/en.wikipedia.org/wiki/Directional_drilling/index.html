Directional drilling

Directional drilling (or slant drilling) is the practice of drilling non-vertical wells. It can be broken down into three main groups: oilfield directional drilling, directional boring (horizontal directional drilling, HDD) for utilities, and drilling in-seam in coal mines for methane extraction.

Applications

Directional drilling allows a single surface location to reach a large subsurface area, enables wells to reach reserves beneath cities, lakes or environmentally sensitive areas, and permits multiple wells from one offshore platform. Horizontal sections greatly increase the contact between wellbore and reservoir, and are essential to modern shale oil and gas production, where horizontal laterals are combined with hydraulic fracturing. Relief wells drilled to intersect a blowing-out well are another critical application, used famously in the 2010 Deepwater Horizon response.

Techniques

Early directional work used whipstocks to deflect the bit. Modern practice uses downhole mud motors with a bent sub, and rotary steerable systems that adjust trajectory while the whole drill string rotates. Measurement-while-drilling (MWD) and logging-while-drilling (LWD) tools transmit inclination, azimuth and formation data to the surface, allowing directional drillers to steer within thin target zones — a practice called geosteering. The "build rate" of a well is usually expressed in degrees per 100 feet of hole.

History

Intentional directional drilling began in the 1920s and 1930s in California and Texas, partly to reach offshore deposits from onshore locations at Huntington Beach and partly amid scandals over wells slanted under neighbouring leases. The Eastman Company pioneered survey instruments and controlled drilling services in that era.
