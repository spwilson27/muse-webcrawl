Wave–particle duality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diffraction pattern produced by electrons, showing wave behaviour of matter]
Electron diffraction demonstrates the wave nature of matter.

Wave–particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that fundamental entities of the universe, such as photons and electrons, exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties. A central idea of the theory, it can be considered paradoxical from a classical point of view, and it serves as a metaphor for the notion that particles and waves are inadequate concepts for describing quantum objects.

Historically, the duality arose from the debate over whether light was composed of particles (Newton) or waves (Huygens, Young, Maxwell). Albert Einstein's 1905 light-quantum hypothesis revived the particle view to explain the Photoelectric effect; in 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed the converse for matter — that electrons have wavelengths λ = h/p — confirmed by electron diffraction experiments of Davisson and Germer and of G. P. Thomson in 1927.

The double-slit demonstration

The Double-slit experiment exhibits duality in its purest form: individual particles arrive at a detector as localised dots (particle-like), while the accumulated pattern shows interference fringes (wave-like). Bohr's principle of complementarity held that wave and particle descriptions are both necessary and mutually exclusive aspects of a single reality, revealed by different experimental arrangements.

Modern understanding

In contemporary Quantum field theory, there are neither classical waves nor classical particles, but quantum fields whose excitations are detected as particles; the duality is regarded as a heuristic artifact of the theory's historical development. Interference has now been demonstrated with objects as large as molecules of hundreds of atoms, while decoherence theory explains why macroscopic objects do not display it.
