Escalation of commitment

Escalation of commitment is a human behaviour pattern in which an individual or group, facing increasingly negative outcomes from a decision or investment, nevertheless continues to commit further resources to it rather than withdraw. The phenomenon, closely related to the sunk cost fallacy, was named and systematically studied by Barry M. Staw in his 1976 paper "Knee-deep in the big muddy", which drew its title from the escalation of the Vietnam War.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. Self-justification theory holds that decision-makers escalate to convince themselves and others that their initial decision was correct. Prospect theory suggests that people become risk-seeking when facing sure losses, preferring a gamble on recovery over accepting a definite loss. Other contributing factors include personal responsibility for the original decision, organisational norms against admitting failure, and the difficulty of deciding that a project is truly hopeless.

Examples

Famous cases include the prolonged development of Concorde (giving rise to the phrase "Concorde fallacy"), failing megaprojects continued long past their point of viability, and military commitments such as the Vietnam War and the Shoreham Nuclear Power Plant, where billions were spent on a plant that never operated commercially.

De-escalation

Research on countermeasures includes separating initial decision-makers from later evaluations, setting explicit stopping rules in advance, using outside reviewers, and making the costs of persistence salient.
