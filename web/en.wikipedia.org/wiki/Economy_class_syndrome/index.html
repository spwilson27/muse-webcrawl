Economy class syndrome

Economy class syndrome
ClassificationForm of venous thromboembolism (traveler's thrombosis)
Risk factorsProlonged immobility, long-haul travel, prior DVT, thrombophilia, obesity, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, recent surgery, cancer
PreventionWalking, calf exercises, hydration, compression stockings, anticoagulants for high-risk travellers

Economy class syndrome is a popular term for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism associated with prolonged sitting on long-haul flights, particularly in the cramped seating of economy class. The medical literature prefers the broader term traveler's thrombosis or travel-related venous thromboembolism, since the condition also occurs after long journeys by car, bus, or train, and in business and first class as well. Prolonged immobility causes venous stasis in the legs; combined with dehydration, low cabin humidity, and individual risk factors, a clot may form and later embolise to the lungs, sometimes fatally.

Evidence and risk

Studies, including the World Health Organization's WRIGHT project, found that journeys longer than about four hours roughly double the risk of venous thromboembolism, though the absolute risk for a healthy traveller remains low (on the order of one symptomatic event per several thousand long flights). The term entered public awareness after well-publicised deaths of young passengers following long flights, such as the widely reported 2000 case of Emma Christoffersen after a flight from Australia to London.

Prevention

Recommended measures include standing and walking periodically, performing seated calf exercises, staying hydrated, avoiding alcohol and sedatives, and wearing graduated compression stockings. Travellers at high risk may be advised low-molecular-weight heparin or other prophylaxis by their physician.
