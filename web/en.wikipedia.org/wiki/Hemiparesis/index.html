Hemiparesis

Hemiparesis, or unilateral paresis, is weakness of one entire side of the body — the arm, leg and often the face on the same side — while hemiplegia denotes complete paralysis of that side. Hemiparesis is a common consequence of stroke and other lesions of the brain or spinal cord affecting the motor pathways.

Causes

The most common cause in adults is stroke: an infarct or haemorrhage in the motor cortex, internal capsule or brainstem weakens the opposite (contralateral) side of the body, because most corticospinal fibres cross in the medulla. Other causes include traumatic brain injury, brain tumours and abscesses, demyelinating disease such as multiple sclerosis, and infections. In children, the most common cause is cerebral palsy, in which the weakness may date from injury around the time of birth; a milder pattern affecting mainly the leg is called hemiparetic cerebral palsy.

Signs

Weakness typically shows an upper-motor-neuron pattern: the arm flexors and leg extensors are relatively stronger, producing a characteristic posture and circumducting gait. Associated signs may include spasticity, brisk reflexes, an extensor plantar response (Babinski sign), facial weakness sparing the forehead, and, with dominant-hemisphere lesions, aphasia.

Treatment and rehabilitation

Treatment addresses the underlying cause (for example, thrombolysis in acute ischaemic stroke) and focuses on rehabilitation: physiotherapy and occupational therapy, constraint-induced movement therapy for the arm, orthoses, functional electrical stimulation, and treatment of spasticity with botulinum toxin or oral agents. Recovery is greatest in the first months after stroke but can continue for years.
