The Sun is the star at the centre of the Solar System. It is a nearly perfect sphere of hot plasma, heated to incandescence by nuclear fusion reactions in its core, radiating energy mainly as visible light, ultraviolet and infrared radiation. It is by far the most important source of energy for life on Earth.

The Sun accounts for about 99.86% of the Solar System's total mass. Its diameter is about 1.39 million kilometres, 109 times Earth's, and its volume could hold over a million Earths. It is a G-type main-sequence star, informally a "yellow dwarf", though its light is white. Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth.

Composition and structure

The Sun is composed mainly of hydrogen (~73%) and helium (~25%). In the core, at about 15 million degrees, hydrogen fuses into helium via the proton–proton chain, converting about 4 million tonnes of matter into energy every second. Energy works outward through the radiative zone by photon diffusion and through the convection zone by churning plasma, reaching the visible surface, the photosphere, which is marked by granulation and darker, cooler sunspots.

Above the photosphere lie the chromosphere and the corona, the Sun's extended outer atmosphere, visible during total solar eclipses and heated — by a mechanism still investigated — to millions of degrees. A constant stream of charged particles, the solar wind, flows outward and shapes comet tails, aurorae and the heliosphere.

Solar activity

The Sun's magnetic activity follows an approximately 11-year cycle of sunspot numbers, solar flares and coronal mass ejections, which can disturb radio communications, satellites and power grids on Earth — events monitored by NASA and other space-weather services. Historic quiet periods, such as the Maunder Minimum of the 17th century, have been linked (contentiously) to cooler European climates.

Life cycle

The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a collapsing molecular cloud, as described in the Solar System article. It is roughly halfway through its main-sequence life. In about five billion years it will expand into a red giant, engulfing the inner planets or at least rendering Earth uninhabitable long before, then shed its outer layers as a planetary nebula and retire as a slowly cooling white dwarf.

The Sun in culture

The Sun has been central to religions and calendars worldwide — from the Egyptian Ra and the Greek Helios/Apollo to Stonehenge's solstitial alignments and the Inca Inti. The Milky Way's position, timekeeping, and the very definition of the day and year all derive from humanity's relationship with it.
