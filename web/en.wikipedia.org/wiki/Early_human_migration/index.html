Early human migration

Early human migration is the earliest migration and expansion of modern and archaic humans across the continents. An early phase saw Homo erectus spread from Africa across Eurasia roughly 1.8 million years ago. Anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) evolved in Africa, and the most widely supported model — the recent African origin or "Out of Africa" model — holds that a major expansion out of Africa beginning roughly 70,000–60,000 years ago populated the rest of the world.

Out of Africa

Fossil evidence from sites such as Jebel Irhoud in Morocco (c. 300,000 years old) suggests modern humans were present across Africa far earlier than once thought. Early excursions into the Levant, evidenced by the Misliya and Skhul/Qafzeh fossils (roughly 185,000 and 120,000–90,000 years ago), appear not to have led to lasting settlement. The successful expansion followed routes either across the Bab el-Mandeb strait into Arabia or through the Nile corridor and Sinai.

Peopling of the continents

Humans reached South and Southeast Asia by about 60,000 years ago, and Australia (Sahul) by at least 50,000–65,000 years ago, requiring sea crossings through Wallacea. Europe was settled by roughly 45,000 years ago, where modern humans encountered and interbred with Neanderthals; East Asian populations also interbred with Denisovans. The Americas were peopled last: genetic and archaeological evidence points to an entry via Beringia during the late glacial period, roughly 20,000–15,000 years ago, followed by rapid expansion down the Pacific coast and into the interior.

Genetic evidence

Mitochondrial DNA and Y-chromosome studies support a single major dispersal out of Africa, with population diversity declining with distance from Africa — a serial founder effect. Ancient DNA has revealed admixture with archaic populations: most non-African genomes carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry, and some Oceanian populations carry up to about 5% Denisovan ancestry.
