ASCII art

ASCII art is a graphic design technique that uses computers for presentation and consists of pictures pieced together from the 95 printable characters defined by the ASCII Standard from 1963. The term is also loosely used to refer to text-based visual art in general. ASCII art can be created with any text editor and is often used with free-form languages; most examples require a fixed-width font such as Courier for presentation.

History

The earliest typewriter art dates to the late 19th century, and line-printer art was common in early computing centres from the 1960s, producing banners and pictures on continuous stationery. With the spread of teleprinters, bulletin board systems and later the Internet, ASCII art flourished in the ANSI and textmode art scenes of the 1980s and 1990s, where groups competed to produce elaborate text graphics. Simpler ASCII figures also became ubiquitous in email signatures, Usenet posts and chat, and gave rise to emoticons such as :-) and the shrug ¯\_(ツ)_/¯.

Techniques

Artists compose images by hand in a text editor, or use software that converts raster images into characters by mapping brightness to character density — for example rendering darker areas with characters such as @, #, or %. Common styles include line art, solid (block) art used in the cracking and warez scenes, and grey-scale art using varying character densities. Related forms include ANSI art, which adds colour and extended characters, and Unicode art, which draws on a much larger character set.
