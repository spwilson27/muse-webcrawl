Jean-Baptiste Lamarck

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744–1829) was a French naturalist who proposed the first coherent theory of evolution, set out in his Philosophie zoologique (1809). Although his mechanism — the inheritance of characteristics acquired during an organism's life — was later rejected, Lamarck was a pioneer of invertebrate zoology and one of the first to argue that species change over time.

Theory

Lamarck proposed that organisms are driven toward greater complexity, and that they adapt by use and disuse of organs: structures used more grow stronger and are passed on enlarged, as in the stock example of giraffes stretching for leaves lengthening their necks over generations. He also held that new simple life arises continually by spontaneous generation.

Legacy

Darwin's natural selection displaced Lamarck's mechanism, and August Weismann's experiments and modern genetics showed that acquired changes to the body are generally not inherited. 'Lamarckism' became shorthand for that discredited idea, though historians note Lamarck's real stature — he coined the term 'invertebrate', classified clouds, and held a major post at the Paris museum. Modern epigenetics, which does allow some environmentally induced effects to persist across generations, is sometimes loosely compared to his ideas, though it is not a return to Lamarckism.
