Thorvaldsens Museum (Danish: Thorvaldsens Museum) is a single-artist museum in Copenhagen, Denmark, dedicated to the art of Danish neoclassical sculptor Bertel Thorvaldsen (1770–1844), who lived and worked in Rome for most of his life. The museum is located on the small island of Slotsholmen, next to Christiansborg Palace, in central Copenhagen. It was the first public museum building in Denmark and remains one of the country's most distinctive works of 19th-century architecture.

History

Thorvaldsen bequeathed his works and art collections to the city of Copenhagen, and funds were raised by public subscription to build a museum for them. The site — the former royal boathouse and wagon sheds of Christiansborg — was granted by King Christian VIII. The architect Michael Gottlieb Bindesbøll transformed the complex in 1839–1848 into a museum in a colourful, classicising style inspired by ancient Roman and Pompeian buildings. Thorvaldsen returned to a hero's welcome in 1838, but died in 1844, four years before the museum opened on 18 September 1848; he is buried in its central courtyard, beneath a bed of roses.

Building

The exterior is famous for Jørgen Sonne's huge frieze, painted in fresco around the walls, depicting Thorvaldsen's triumphal homecoming to Copenhagen in 1838 — crowds of citizens, sailors unloading the crates of his sculptures, and the sculptor himself. The building is crowned by a bronze quadriga of Victoria driving four horses, modelled after Thorvaldsen's work. Inside, strong Pompeian colours — deep blues, reds and ochres — and richly patterned mosaic floors and painted ceilings designed by Bindesbøll and executed by leading Danish artists form a jewel-box setting for the white marble statues.

Collection

The museum holds virtually the complete works of Thorvaldsen: original plaster models for nearly all his sculptures, including Jason with the Golden Fleece, the Christus and the Twelve Apostles made for Copenhagen's Church of Our Lady, Ganymede, the Three Graces, and numerous portrait busts and monuments. It also preserves his personal collections — paintings by his contemporaries, antiquities (Greek vases, gems, coins, Egyptian objects), books and drawings — and his own drawings and sketches, giving an unusually complete picture of a major neoclassical artist.
