Custom of the sea

The custom of the sea is a phrase describing a set of customs, traditions, and practices followed by sailors and other maritime communities. In its most infamous usage, the term refers to the tradition, among shipwrecked survivors adrift at sea, of resorting to cannibalism — including the drawing of lots to select a victim — when food ran out and survival seemed otherwise impossible.

The practice

Under the unwritten code, lots would be drawn and the loser killed and eaten so that others might live. Killing a dying or already incapacitated person, rather than drawing lots, was also rationalized under the custom. Notable cases include the whaleship Essex (1820), sunk by a sperm whale, whose survivors in open boats drew lots; and the case of the Francis Spaight (1835).

R v Dudley and Stephens

The custom was tested in English law after the yacht Mignonette sank in 1884. Survivors Tom Dudley and Edwin Stephens killed the cabin boy, Richard Parker, who was already ill, and fed on his body. Rescued and tried in R v Dudley and Stephens (1884), they were convicted of murder; the court rejected necessity as a defence to murder, establishing a landmark precedent. Their death sentences were commuted to six months' imprisonment.

Other customs

More benign customs of the sea include the captain going down with the ship (or at least being last to leave), women and children first (the "Birkenhead drill"), maritime burial rites, and ceremonies for crossing the equator.
