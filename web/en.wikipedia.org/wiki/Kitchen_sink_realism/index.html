Kitchen sink realism

Kitchen sink realism Also called: Kitchen sink drama
Movement: British theatre, film, television and art, late 1950s–1960s
Associated with: "Angry young men," John Osborne, British New Wave cinema
Key works: Look Back in Anger (1956), A Taste of Honey (1958), Saturday Night and Sunday Morning (1960)

Kitchen sink realism (or kitchen sink drama) was a British cultural movement that developed in the late 1950s and early 1960s in theatre, art, film, novel and television drama. It depicted the domestic lives of working-class Britons — often "angry young men" — living in cramped rented accommodation, working in dead-end jobs and spending their free time in pubs, with an emphasis on social issues such as poverty, abortion, homelessness, class and failing marriages. The term derives from an expressionist painting by John Bratby, who painted kitchen interiors with an almost aggressive realism.

Theatre

The movement is usually dated from John Osborne's play Look Back in Anger, premiered at the Royal Court Theatre in 1956, whose protagonist Jimmy Porter voices the frustrations of post-war youth. Shelagh Delaney's A Taste of Honey (1958), Arnold Wesker's trilogy and the work of the Royal Court under George Devine extended the movement, as did the long-running television soap Coronation Street (from 1960).

Film

In cinema the movement merged with the British New Wave: films such as Room at the Top (1959), Saturday Night and Sunday Morning (1960), A Taste of Honey (1961) and The Loneliness of the Long Distance Runner (1962), directed by figures including Tony Richardson, Karel Reisz and Lindsay Anderson, shot on location in industrial northern towns with unknown or regional actors. Its gritty social realism remained a lasting influence on British film and television drama.
