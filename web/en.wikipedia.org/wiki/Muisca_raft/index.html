Muisca raft

Muisca raft (Balsa Muisca)
Culture: Muisca
Date: c. 1200–1500 CE
Material: Gold alloy (tumbaga)
Found: Pasca, Cundinamarca, 1969
Location: Gold Museum, Bogotá

The Muisca raft (Spanish: Balsa Muisca) is a famous pre-Columbian gold votive figure created by the Muisca people of the Colombian Andes. It depicts a ceremonial scene on a raft and is closely associated with the legend of El Dorado.

Description

Cast by the lost-wax method in tumbaga, an alloy of gold and copper, the raft measures about 19.5 cm long. A central, larger figure — interpreted as the chief or zipa — stands adorned with headdress and ornaments, surrounded by smaller attendants and oarsmen on a log raft. Small hanging pieces suggest banners or standards.

Interpretation

The scene is widely read as the investiture ceremony held at Lake Guatavita, in which a new ruler, covered in gold dust, made offerings of gold and emeralds to the gods before diving into the lake — the ritual at the heart of the El Dorado legend.

Discovery

The piece was found in 1969 by farmers in a cave in the municipality of Pasca, Cundinamarca, inside a ceramic vessel. It is now a centerpiece of the Gold Museum (Museo del Oro) in Bogotá, run by the Bank of the Republic.
