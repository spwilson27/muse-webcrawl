Kublai Khan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Kublai Khan in Chinese style, from a Yuan dynasty album]

Kublai Khan (23 September 1215 – 18 February 1294), grandson of Genghis Khan, was the fifth khagan of the Mongol Empire and the founder of China's Yuan dynasty. Elected khagan in 1260 after a succession war with his brother Ariq Böke, he completed the Mongol conquest of China by destroying the Song dynasty in 1279 — the first foreign ruler to govern all of China — and ruled from his new capital, Khanbaliq (modern Beijing). His court famously received the Venetian traveller Marco Polo, whose account introduced Kublai's wealth and empire to Europe.

Conquest of the Song

Kublai adopted Chinese advisers, institutions and the dynastic name Yuan (from 1271), moved his capital south from Karakorum, and waged a long, sophisticated war against the Southern Song — using Chinese engineers, a navy and siege experts to take the fortress cities of Xiangyang (1273) and finally Hangzhou (1276), with the last Song loyalists drowning at Yamen in 1279. His rule ranked subjects in a hierarchy (Mongols, Central Asians, northern Chinese, southern Chinese) and patronised Buddhism, especially its Tibetan form.

Overreach and death

Kublai's expeditions beyond China largely failed: two invasions of Japan (1274, 1281) were wrecked by typhoons — the kamikaze — and campaigns in Vietnam and Java ended in withdrawal. Paper-money inflation and court faction troubled his later years. He died in 1294; the Yuan dynasty he founded lasted in China until 1368, while the wider Mongol Empire had already become four separate khanates.
