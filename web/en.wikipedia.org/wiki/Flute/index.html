The flute is a musical instrument in the woodwind family. Unlike woodwind instruments with reeds, a flute is a type of aerophone, which produces its sound from the flow of air across an opening. In the Hornbostel–Sachs classification, flutes are classified as edge-blown aerophones.

Flutes are the earliest known identifiable musical instruments, with examples from the Palaeolithic era found in the Swabian Jura region of present-day Germany dating to around 40,000 years ago. A number of flutes dating to about 53,000 to 43,000 years ago have been found in Slovenia; whether the oldest of these — the Divje Babe flute — was played by Neanderthals remains debated.

Types

The standard modern type for orchestral and chamber work is the Western concert flute, a transverse flute held horizontally, typically made of metal and keyed with the Boehm system developed by Theobald Boehm in the mid-19th century. Its smaller sibling is the piccolo; larger members include the alto and bass flutes. End-blown flutes, in which the player blows into the end of the tube, include the Japanese shakuhachi and the Arabic ney. The Indian bansuri is a side-blown bamboo flute associated with the god Krishna. Duct flutes such as the recorder direct air over the edge through a channel.

Acoustics

Sound is produced when a stream of air directed across the embouchure hole strikes the sharp edge of the hole, setting up oscillations in the air column inside the tube. The pitch is governed by the effective length of the tube, altered by opening and closing the finger holes or keys. Overblowing at the octave and other harmonics extends the range.

Repertoire and use

The flute holds a prominent place in the orchestra, in chamber music, and as a solo instrument; composers from Vivaldi and Bach to Debussy wrote extensively for it. It is also central to Irish traditional music (usually simple-system wooden flutes) and to many folk traditions worldwide.
