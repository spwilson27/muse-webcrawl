Ziegler–Natta catalyst

A Ziegler–Natta catalyst is a catalyst used in the synthesis of polymers of 1-alkenes (alpha-olefins). Two broad classes are employed: heterogeneous supported catalysts based on titanium compounds, used with organoaluminium co-catalysts such as triethylaluminium, and homogeneous catalysts based on complexes of titanium, zirconium or hafnium, usually used with methylaluminoxane.

History

The catalysts are named after Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta. In 1953 Ziegler discovered that mixtures of titanium tetrachloride and diethylaluminium chloride polymerized ethylene at atmospheric pressure to give high-density polyethylene; Natta then used similar catalysts to polymerize propylene into crystalline, stereoregular (isotactic) polypropylene. Ziegler and Natta shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1963 for their discoveries.

Applications

Ziegler–Natta catalysis made possible the industrial production of high-density polyethylene and isotactic polypropylene — plastics that could not be made satisfactorily by earlier free-radical processes — and remains a mainstay of the polyolefins industry, which produces tens of millions of tonnes of polymer annually. The catalysts control the stereochemistry (tacticity) of the polymer chain, which determines crystallinity and mechanical properties.
