Mark Sanford Granovetter (born October 20, 1943) is an American sociologist and professor at Stanford University. He is best known for his work in social network theory and economic sociology, particularly his theory of the spread of information in social networks known as "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) — one of the most cited papers in the social sciences.

Career

Granovetter received his A.B. in history from Princeton University in 1965 and his Ph.D. in sociology from Harvard University in 1970, where he studied under Harrison White. He taught at Northwestern University, Stony Brook University, and Johns Hopkins University before joining Stanford in 1992. He has served as editor of the journal Sociological Theory and is a member of the National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences.

The strength of weak ties

In his 1973 paper "The Strength of Weak Ties", published in the American Journal of Sociology, Granovetter argued that weak social ties — acquaintances rather than close friends — are often more valuable for spreading information, including news of job openings, because they bridge otherwise disconnected clusters of people. Strong ties tend to move in the same circles and know the same things; weak ties connect an individual to distant parts of the social network. His book Getting a Job (1974, revised 1995), based on interviews with professional, technical, and managerial workers in a Boston suburb, showed empirically that most people find work through personal contacts, and usually through acquaintances seen only occasionally.

Economic sociology

Granovetter is regarded as a founder of the modern field of economic sociology. His 1985 paper "Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness" argued that economic behaviour is neither as atomised as economists assume ("undersocialised") nor as rule-bound as some sociologists claim ("oversocialised"), but is embedded in ongoing networks of social relations. He later developed these ideas in Society and Economy: Framework and Principles (2017).

Threshold models

Granovetter also introduced threshold models of collective behaviour (1978), showing how small differences in the distribution of individuals' thresholds for acting — for example to join a riot, adopt an innovation, or change jobs — can produce dramatically different aggregate outcomes, a framework influential in the study of diffusion and social change.
