Rubble pile

The rubble-pile asteroid 101955 Bennu

In astronomy, a rubble pile is a celestial body that consists of many pieces of rock held together by self-gravity rather than being a single monolithic object. Rubble piles form when an asteroid or comet is shattered by a collision and the fragments gravitationally re-accumulate.

Properties

Rubble piles have high porosity — much of their volume is empty space — and consequently low bulk density. Because they have almost no tensile strength, they deform and reshape under tidal forces and spin; rapid rotation tends to make them oblate or top-shaped, with material migrating to the equator. Asteroids visited by spacecraft, including 25143 Itokawa, 162173 Ryugu and 101955 Bennu, have proven to be rubble piles covered in boulders and regolith.

Formation and behaviour in impacts

An impact energetic enough to disrupt a parent body need not disperse it: fragments with low relative velocities fall back together. This explains why many asteroids larger than a few hundred metres are thought to be rubble piles. Their loose structure affects planetary-defence strategies, since a rubble pile absorbs and redistributes impact energy differently from a solid body — a consideration in missions such as NASA's DART, which struck the rubble-pile moonlet Dimorphos in 2022.
