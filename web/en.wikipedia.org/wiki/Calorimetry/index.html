Calorimetry

[Hero image placeholder: bomb calorimeter]

Calorimetry is the science of measuring the heat of chemical reactions or physical changes, and of determining the heat capacity of substances. Measurements are made with a device called a calorimeter. The word derives from the Latin calor (heat) and the Greek metron (measure).

History

The first ice calorimeter was used in the winter of 1782–83 by Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace to measure the heat released in chemical processes from the amount of ice melted. Joseph Black had earlier developed the concept of latent heat in the 1760s. In the 19th century, calorimetry underpinned the development of thermodynamics and thermochemistry, notably through Hess's law (1840).

Methods

Bomb calorimetry

A bomb calorimeter measures the heat of combustion at constant volume: a sample is burned in pure oxygen inside a sealed steel vessel (the "bomb") immersed in water, and the temperature rise of the water gives the energy released. It is used to determine the energy content of fuels and foods.

Differential scanning calorimetry

In differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), a sample and a reference are heated at a controlled rate and the difference in heat flow between them is recorded, revealing phase transitions such as melting, glass transitions and crystallisation. DSC is widely used in materials science, pharmaceuticals and polymer characterisation.

Direct and indirect calorimetry

In physiology, direct calorimetry measures an organism's heat output, while indirect calorimetry estimates energy expenditure from oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production, and is standard in metabolic studies.

Applications

Calorimetry is used in chemistry, food science (calorie labelling), nutrition, materials research, and particle physics, where calorimeter detectors measure the energy of particles in collider experiments.
