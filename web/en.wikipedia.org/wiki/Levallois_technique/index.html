Levallois technique

PeriodMiddle Paleolithic (from c. 300,000 years ago)
IndustryMousterian and related industries

Practised byNeanderthals and early Homo sapiens

The Levallois technique is a name given by archaeologists to a distinctive type of stone knapping developed by early humans during the Middle Paleolithic. Its defining feature is the careful preparation of a stone core so that a flake of predetermined size and shape can be struck from it with a single blow — a method that implies planning depth and a mental template of the intended product.

Method

The knapper shapes a nodule of flint or similar stone by removing flakes around its edges to create a domed upper surface (often compared to a tortoise shell) meeting a flatter striking platform. The platform is faceted to control the fracture. A final blow then detaches a large, thin flake — the Levallois flake — with sharp edges all around, which can be used as-is or retouched into scrapers and points. Variants produce elongated blades or triangular points.

History and significance

The technique takes its name from 19th-century finds at Levallois-Perret, a suburb of Paris. It appears widely from around 300,000 years ago across Africa, Europe, and western Asia, associated both with Neanderthals (in the Mousterian industries of Europe) and with early anatomically modern humans in Africa. Because the finished flake's form is determined before it is struck, the Levallois method is often cited as evidence of sophisticated cognition in archaic humans.
