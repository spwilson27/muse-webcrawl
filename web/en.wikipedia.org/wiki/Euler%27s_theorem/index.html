Euler's theorem

In number theory, Euler's theorem (also known as the Fermat–Euler theorem or Euler's totient theorem) states that, if n and a are coprime positive integers, then a raised to the power of Euler's totient function φ(n) is congruent to 1 modulo n: aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). It generalises Fermat's little theorem, which is the special case where n is prime.

Proofs and properties

A standard proof observes that the set of residues coprime to n forms a multiplicative group modulo n of order φ(n); by Lagrange's theorem, any element raised to the order of the group gives the identity. The theorem means exponentiation modulo n can be reduced: for coprime a, exponents may be reduced modulo φ(n), a fact used throughout cryptography and modular arithmetic.

Applications

Euler's theorem is fundamental to the RSA cryptosystem, whose correctness rests on the theorem applied to a modulus that is the product of two primes. It also underlies the computation of modular inverses and multiplicative orders, and admits the Carmichael function as a sharper exponent.
