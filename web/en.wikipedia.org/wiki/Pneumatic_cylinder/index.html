Pneumatic cylinder

Pneumatic cylinders (sometimes known as air cylinders) are mechanical devices which use the power of compressed gas to produce a force in a reciprocating linear motion. Like hydraulic cylinders, they force a piston to move in the desired direction; the piston is a disc or cylinder, and the piston rod transfers the force it develops to the object to be moved.

Operation

Compressed air (or another gas) is admitted into the cylinder barrel, where it acts on the face of the piston. Because the effective area on one side of the piston may be reduced by the piston rod, the forces available on extension and retraction strokes generally differ. Directional control valves meter air into and out of the cylinder, while flow-control valves regulate speed.

Types

  • Single-acting cylinders use compressed air to move the piston in one direction only; a spring (or gravity or an external load) returns it.
  • Double-acting cylinders use air pressure for both extension and retraction.
  • Rodless cylinders carry the load on a carriage coupled to the piston through the barrel slot or by a magnetic coupling, saving installation length.
  • Rotary actuators convert pneumatic power into oscillating rotary motion, often via a rack and pinion.
  • Telescopic cylinders use nested stages to achieve a long stroke from a short retracted length.

Construction

A typical cylinder consists of a barrel (tube), end caps, a piston with seals, a piston rod, and mounting hardware. Barrels are commonly made of aluminium, brass, or stainless steel. Cushioning at the ends of the stroke decelerates the piston to reduce shock. Magnetic piston rings are often fitted so that reed or solid-state sensors can detect piston position.

Applications

Pneumatic cylinders are ubiquitous in factory automation: clamping, pressing, ejecting, lifting, and material handling. They are favoured where clean, fast, inexpensive linear motion is required, and are a standard component of pneumatic control systems.
