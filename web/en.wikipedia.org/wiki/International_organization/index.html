International organization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An international organization (or international institution) is an organisation established by a treaty or other instrument governed by international law and possessing its own legal personality, with member states as its dominant participants. International organisations (IOs) are a principal feature of modern world politics, numbering in the hundreds at the intergovernmental level — from universal bodies (the UN, WTO, WHO) to regional unions (EU, AU, ASEAN) and functional agencies (UPU, ICAO, IAEA) — surrounded by tens of thousands of international NGOs.

History and functions

The form descends from 19th-century technical unions (International Telegraph Union, 1865; Universal Postal Union, 1874) and river commissions, generalised by the League of Nations and the UN system. IOs provide forums for bargaining, set and monitor rules, pool resources, deliver services (peacekeeping, aid, health emergencies), generate authoritative knowledge, and socialise states into norms. Member states delegate authority to secretariats and courts whose autonomy — bureaucratic, judicial, expert — is itself a major research subject (principal–agent analysis, organisational culture).

Debates

Critiques target IOs' democratic deficits, great-power capture, bureaucratic pathology and uneven effectiveness; defenders note they remain the only machinery for problems — pandemics, climate, financial stability — that no state can solve alone. Withdrawals and funding crises (UNESCO, WHO episodes) periodically test the system's resilience.
