State capture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

State capture is a type of systemic political corruption in which private interests significantly influence a state's decision-making processes to their own advantage, through illicit, non-transparent channels — shaping the very laws, rules and regulations (the “rules of the game”) rather than merely bending their implementation (which is ordinary administrative corruption). The term was coined by World Bank researchers (Hellman, Jones and Kaufmann, 2000) studying transition economies, where oligarchs purchased legislation, court decisions and central-bank policy from captured states.

The concept's emblematic modern case is South Africa's “state capture” under President Jacob Zuma and the Gupta family (documented by the Zondo Commission, 2018–22), which gave the phrase global currency; cognate diagnoses include regulatory capture (see Regulatory capture), “crony capitalism”, and the oligarchic politics of several post-communist states. Capture is diagnosed by its fingerprints: procurement steered to connected firms, captured appointments to prosecutors, tax authorities and state enterprises, and media ownership concentrated among beneficiaries. Countermeasures run from asset-declaration and lobbying transparency to prosecution — with the political difficulty that captured institutions must reform themselves.
