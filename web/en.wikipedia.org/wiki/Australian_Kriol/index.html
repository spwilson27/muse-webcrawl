Australian Kriol

Australian Kriol is an English-based creole language spoken by Aboriginal people across northern Australia, from the Kimberley in Western Australia through the Northern Territory to Cape York in Queensland. It developed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries from pidgin English used on cattle stations, missions and pearling fleets, and it is now the first language of many thousands of speakers — often estimated at up to 20,000–30,000 people — making it the most widely spoken Indigenous Australian language by number of speakers.

History

Kriol arose where Aboriginal people of many language groups were brought together under colonisation — especially at the Roper River Mission (now Ngukurr) in the Northern Territory, often identified as its birthplace around 1908. Children growing up in these settlements creolised the pidgin into a full language, which then spread with population movements, stock work and missions across the north. Regional varieties differ substantially; speakers of the "heavy" Kriol of the Roper region and the "light" varieties closer to English are not always mutually intelligible.

Features

Kriol vocabulary is mainly from English but its grammar is its own, shaped by Aboriginal languages: it marks dual and trial pronouns (e.g. yunmi "you and I"), inclusive and exclusive "we" (mela distinctions), transitive verb suffixes (-im) shared with other Pacific creoles, and future and continuous markers such as garra and -bat. Words may differ sharply in meaning from their English sources (e.g. kilim means "hit", not "kill").

Status and use

Long stigmatised as "broken English", Kriol is increasingly recognised as a language in its own right. A complete Kriol Bible, the Holi Baibul, was published in 2007 — the first full Bible translation in an Australian Indigenous language. Kriol is used in some bilingual education and health interpreting, and has a growing presence in music, media and social media.
