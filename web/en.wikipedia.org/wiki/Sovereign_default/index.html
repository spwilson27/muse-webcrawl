Sovereign default

A sovereign default is the failure or refusal of the government of a sovereign state to pay back its debt in full when due. Unlike companies, sovereigns cannot be liquidated, and creditors have limited legal recourse — there is no international bankruptcy court — so defaults are usually resolved by negotiated restructuring in which bondholders accept reduced payments, longer maturities, or both, often coordinated with the International Monetary Fund.

Causes and history

Defaults arise from unsustainable borrowing, collapses in revenue (especially for commodity exporters), war, or the refusal of a new regime to honor a predecessor's debts. History is rich in examples: Spain defaulted repeatedly in the 16th and 17th centuries; many Latin American states defaulted in the 1820s and again in the debt crisis of the 1980s; Russia defaulted in 1918 and again in 1998; Argentina's 2001 default, then the largest ever, was followed by fifteen years of litigation with holdout creditors. The euro area crisis produced Greece's 2012 restructuring, the largest sovereign debt write-down in history, and episodes in Sri Lanka, Zambia, and Lebanon in the 2020s.

Consequences

Defaults typically bring exclusion from capital markets, currency collapse, banking crises (since domestic banks hold government debt), and deep recessions — though research finds market exclusion is often shorter than feared. Because sovereign debt is held throughout the financial system, the management of defaults is a central problem of international finance and of public debate in the public sphere.
