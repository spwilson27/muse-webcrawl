Spriggina is a genus of early animal known from fossils of the Ediacaran period, about 555 million years ago. The type species, Spriggina floundersi, comes from the Ediacara Hills of South Australia and is the fossil emblem of that state. The organism grew to about 3–5 centimetres long and consisted of a crescent- or horseshoe-shaped "head" region and a long, segmented body that tapered toward the rear.

Description

The body segments of Spriggina show glide reflection symmetry — the segments on the two sides are offset rather than directly opposite — a pattern characteristic of many Ediacaran organisms. Unlike many of its contemporaries, however, Spriggina appears genuinely bilaterian in organisation, with a differentiated front end, which has led many researchers to regard it as one of the earliest known members of the lineage leading to today's bilaterally symmetrical animals.

Classification and significance

The discoverer, Martin Glaessner, initially interpreted Spriggina as a polychaete annelid worm. Others have proposed that it was an early arthropod, a rangeomorph frond, or a member of an extinct phylum. Its combination of a distinct head, segmentation, and possible appendage-like structures keeps it at the centre of debates about the origins of bilaterians and the roots of the Cambrian explosion. Trackways and feeding traces associated with similar organisms suggest it may have crawled over microbial mats on the sea floor, grazing or preying on smaller organisms.

The genus was named in honour of Reg Sprigg, the geologist who discovered the Ediacara fossils in 1946, and the species honours Ben Flounders, an amateur fossil collector who assisted the early excavations.
