Ocean-effect snow

Ocean-effect snow (also called sea-effect or bay-effect snow) is precipitation produced when cold air masses move over warmer ocean or large bay waters, picking up moisture that falls as snow over nearby land. It is the marine counterpart of lake-effect snow, which occurs over large lakes such as the North American Great Lakes.

Formation

When frigid air — often polar or Arctic air — flows over relatively warm open water, heat and moisture fluxes from the surface destabilise the lower atmosphere. Convective clouds organise into bands aligned with the wind, and where these bands persist over the same coastal area they can produce intense, localised snowfall with sharp boundaries between heavy snow and clear skies. The phenomenon requires a substantial temperature difference between the water surface and the air at about 850 hPa, commonly cited as at least 13 °C, and a long fetch over open water.

Regions affected

Ocean-effect snow is common on the eastern coasts of Asia, where Siberian air crosses the Sea of Japan and produces the famously deep snows of Japan's "Snow Country" on Honshu and Hokkaido. In North America, bay-effect snow occurs around Cape Cod Bay and Massachusetts Bay, and sea-effect snow affects parts of Atlantic Canada and New England. In Europe, cold easterly flows over the relatively warm Baltic Sea generate heavy snow on Sweden's east coast, and northerly outbreaks over the North Sea can bring sea-effect showers to eastern England and Scotland. Similar effects occur over the Black Sea coast of Turkey.

Impacts

Because the snow bands are narrow, totals can vary from a dusting to tens of centimetres over short distances, making forecasting difficult and travel hazardous in affected areas while nearby locations remain dry.
