Jalali calendar

The Jalali calendar is a solar calendar that was compiled during the reign of the Seljuk sultan Jalal al-Din Malik-Shah I in 1079, primarily by a commission of astronomers that included Omar Khayyam, working at the observatory in Isfahan. It is the historical predecessor of the modern Iranian (Solar Hijri) calendar used in Iran and Afghanistan.

Structure

The Jalali year begins at the vernal equinox, determined by astronomical observation, and consists of twelve months. The first six months have 31 days, the next five have 30 days, and the last month has 29 days in normal years and 30 in leap years.

Accuracy

Leap years in the Jalali system follow an intercalation pattern of cycles (commonly described as 33-year cycles with occasional 29-year breaks) that keeps the calendar closely aligned with the tropical year. The resulting mean year of about 365.2424 days is slightly more accurate than the Gregorian calendar's 365.2425 days, accumulating an error of roughly one day in about 5,000 years. Because the epoch and leap rule are tied to the observed equinox at the meridian of Tehran in the modern Solar Hijri version, the calendar is sometimes described as an observation-based rather than purely arithmetic calendar.

History

The calendar reform was commissioned after centuries of drift in the old Persian calendar. The era (epoch) of the Jalali calendar was set to 15 March 1079 CE in the Julian calendar. The reform also fixed Nowruz, the Persian New Year, to the first day of spring. The calendar was used for imperial fiscal and astronomical purposes in Persia for centuries, and its month names and structure survive in the official calendars of Iran and Afghanistan today.
