Pancreatic lipase family

The pancreatic lipase family is a family of evolutionarily related lipase enzymes that hydrolyze ester bonds in lipids. Its best-known member is pancreatic triacylglycerol lipase (PNLIP), the principal enzyme responsible for digesting dietary triglycerides in the small intestine. Family members share a conserved alpha/beta hydrolase fold, a catalytic triad, and a lid domain that regulates access to the active site at lipid–water interfaces.

Members

In humans, the family includes pancreatic lipase (PNLIP), pancreatic lipase-related proteins 1, 2, and 3 (PNLIPRP1, PNLIPRP2, PNLIPRP3), bile salt-stimulated lipase (CEL, also called carboxyl ester lipase), and related enzymes such as lipoprotein lipase and hepatic lipase, which belong to the broader lipase superfamily. Pancreatic lipase-related protein 2 has broader substrate specificity than PNLIP and plays a role in digesting milk fat in infants, while CEL acts on cholesterol esters and fat-soluble vitamin esters.

Function and mechanism

Pancreatic lipase is secreted by the pancreas into the duodenum, where it cleaves triglycerides at the sn-1 and sn-3 positions to yield free fatty acids and 2-monoacylglycerol, which can be absorbed by the intestinal lining. Because bile salts coat fat droplets and inhibit lipase binding, the small protein colipase anchors pancreatic lipase to the lipid surface. The drug orlistat, used to treat obesity, works by inhibiting pancreatic and gastric lipases, reducing fat absorption.

Clinical significance

Mutations in PNLIP and family members such as CEL have been associated with pancreatic dysfunction; a CEL hybrid allele causes a syndrome of diabetes and exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (MODY8). Loss of pancreatic lipase activity in pancreatic insufficiency leads to fat malabsorption and steatorrhea, treated with enzyme replacement therapy.
