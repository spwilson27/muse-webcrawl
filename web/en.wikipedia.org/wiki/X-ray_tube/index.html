An X-ray tube is a vacuum tube that converts electrical input power into X-rays. It is the source of X-radiation in radiography, computed tomography, fluoroscopy, radiotherapy simulation, and industrial inspection. The availability of this controllable source of X-rays created the field of radiography, in which the absorption of X-rays by tissues or materials is used to form an image of internal structures.

How X-rays are produced

A heated filament at the cathode emits electrons by thermionic emission. A high voltage applied between cathode and anode accelerates the electrons across the vacuum, and they strike a metal target — usually tungsten, or an alloy such as tungsten–rhenium — set into the anode. The collisions produce X-rays in two ways: bremsstrahlung ("braking radiation"), a continuous spectrum produced as electrons are deflected by atomic nuclei, and characteristic radiation, produced when an incident electron ejects an inner-shell electron and an outer electron drops down to fill the vacancy, emitting a photon of a specific energy. Only about 1% of the input energy becomes X-rays; the rest appears as heat in the anode, which is why many tubes rotate the anode (a rotating-anode tube) or circulate oil to spread and remove the heat.

History

Early X-ray sources were cold-cathode gas tubes of the Crookes type, which were fickle in output. William D. Coolidge's hot-cathode tube (the Coolidge tube, 1913) put current and voltage under independent control and made X-ray generation reproducible. Modern tubes use rotating anodes, focal spots of controlled size, and metal or glass envelopes with elaborate cooling.

Applications

Diagnostic tubes produce spectra filtered and collimated for imaging; CT scanners use high-heat-capacity tubes that rotate around the patient. Other tubes serve in X-ray fluorescence analysis, crystallography, security scanning, and food inspection.
