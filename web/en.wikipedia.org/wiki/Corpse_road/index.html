Corpse road

Corpse roads were routes along which coffins and corpses were carried from outlying settlements to the churchyards licensed for burial, principally in medieval and early modern Britain. They are also known as burial roads, coffin roads, coffin lines, lyke or lych ways, funeral roads, or bier ways. The Scots and northern English word lyke and the Germanic element in lychgate both relate to the word for a corpse.

Origins

In the Middle Ages, mother churches held exclusive burial rights for wide parishes, and fees attached to burial made them reluctant to surrender those rights. Inhabitants of remote hamlets therefore had to transport their dead — on horse-drawn biers or carried by relays of bearers — sometimes over very long distances of upland terrain. Routes over the Pennines, the Lake District (such as the corpse road from Mardale to Shap, made famous by the drowned village beneath Haweswater), and from Wasdale to Eskdale crossed high, difficult ground.

Features and folklore

Corpse roads are associated with features such as coffin stones, large flat boulders beside the path where bearers rested the coffin, and wayside crosses. Rich folklore surrounds them: the corpse was believed to confer fertility on fields it passed over, but also to establish a permanent right of way; spirits were thought to travel in straight lines, so roads sometimes zigzagged to confuse them, and the dead were carried feet-first to prevent the ghost looking back toward home. Lights seen along such routes were interpreted as corpse candles or will-o'-the-wisps foretelling death.

Elsewhere

Comparable funeral routes existed elsewhere in Europe, including the Netherlands and Germany, where straight doodwegen (death roads) led to cemeteries.
