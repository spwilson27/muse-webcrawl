Voting age

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A voting age is a minimum age established by law that a person must attain before they become eligible to vote in a public election. The most common voting age worldwide is 18; the twentieth century's trend was downward from 21 to 18 (the United Kingdom in 1969; the United States by the Twenty-sixth Amendment, 1971, driven by the Vietnam-era slogan “old enough to fight, old enough to vote”; most democracies followed in the 1970s).

A minority of jurisdictions set the age at 16 (Austria, for all elections since 2007 — the first EU state to do so; Malta, Argentina, Brazil (optional at 16), Cuba, Ecuador, Nicaragua and others; and for devolved elections Scotland and Wales), and a few at 17 (Greece; East Timor) or higher (several states at 20 or 21, and the UAE's indirect system apart). Campaigns for votes at 16 cite consistency (work, tax, military-enlistment ages), habit-formation research (first-time voters in stable households vote at higher rates) and intergenerational fairness on long-horizon issues; opponents cite developmental science and the line-drawing arbitrariness of any threshold. Studies of Austria's reform find 16–17-year-olds voting at rates comparable to slightly older first-timers, evidence contested in interpretation but central to the debate.
