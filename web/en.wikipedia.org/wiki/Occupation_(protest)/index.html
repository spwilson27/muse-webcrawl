Occupation (protest)

Occupation, as a protest tactic, is the seizure and holding of a space — a factory, office, university building, public square, or piece of land — by protesters who refuse to leave until their demands are met or they are removed. It is a form of nonviolent (or predominantly nonviolent) direct action, closely related to the sit-in, in which physical presence becomes both the medium of protest and a lever of disruption: occupying a workplace halts production, while occupying symbolic public space creates a visible, continuing demonstration.

History

Workers' occupations have a long history in the labour movement. Italian factory workers occupied their plants during the Biennio Rosso of 1919–20, and the sit-down strikes of the 1930s — most famously the Flint sit-down strike of 1936–37 against General Motors — won union recognition in the United States; similar tactics spread through France in 1936 and again in May 1968. Student occupations of university buildings marked the protests of the 1960s and the anti-apartheid divestment campaigns of the 1980s. Land occupations by the landless, as in Brazil's MST movement, use occupation to claim redistribution directly.

Occupy movement and after

The tactic gave its name to the Occupy movement that began with Occupy Wall Street in September 2011, when protesters camped in New York's Zuccotti Park under the slogan "We are the 99%," and encampments modelled on it spread worldwide. Occupation camps typically create miniature self-governing communities — with kitchens, libraries, and general assemblies — presenting themselves as "prefigurative" alternatives to the order they protest. Subsequent movements, from Hong Kong's Umbrella Movement occupation of 2014 to climate protest camps, have used similar methods. Authorities commonly respond with negotiation, injunctions, or eviction, and occupiers risk arrest for trespass, which participants often accept as part of the tactic's cost.
