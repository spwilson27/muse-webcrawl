Platt Amendment

The Platt Amendment was a 1901 amendment to a United States Army appropriations bill, named after Senator Orville H. Platt of Connecticut, that stipulated the conditions under which American troops would withdraw from Cuba following the Spanish–American War. It effectively made Cuba a U.S. protectorate.

Provisions

The amendment barred Cuba from signing treaties that impaired its independence or allowed foreign powers to gain control of the island, limited its public debt, and gave the United States the right to intervene "for the preservation of Cuban independence" and the maintenance of a stable government. Cuba was also required to sell or lease lands to the United States for coaling and naval stations — the basis of the Guantánamo Bay naval base lease. Cuba incorporated the amendment into its constitution and a 1903 treaty as a condition of U.S. withdrawal.

Repeal and legacy

Invoked to justify U.S. interventions and occupations of Cuba in 1906–09, 1912, and 1917–22, the amendment was deeply resented by Cuban nationalists. Most of it was abrogated in 1934 under Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy, though the Guantánamo lease survived. The amendment remains a potent symbol of U.S. domination in Cuban political memory.
