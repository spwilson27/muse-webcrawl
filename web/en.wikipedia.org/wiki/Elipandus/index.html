Elipandus (Latin: Elipandus Toletanus; c. 716 – c. 808) was the Archbishop of Toledo from about 754 or 782 until his death, and a leading figure in the theological controversy known as Spanish Adoptionism. Writing from Toledo in Muslim-ruled al-Andalus, Elipandus taught that Jesus Christ, in his human nature, was the Son of God only by adoption, a position that provoked fierce opposition from theologians in the Frankish kingdom.

Life

Little is known of Elipandus's early life. He became archbishop of Toledo, the ancient Visigothic capital, which by his time lay within the Emirate of Córdoba. As metropolitan of Toledo he held authority over much of the Christian church in Iberia, including the northern Christian kingdoms that remained outside Muslim control.

Adoptionist teaching

In a letter written around 784–785 to the abbot Fidel, Elipandus asked about the views of Migetius, a rigorist preacher. In developing his own Christology, Elipandus distinguished between Christ's divine nature, in which he was the natural Son of God, and his human nature, in which he was Son of God by adoption (adoptivus). He drew on older Spanish liturgical and patristic language, and his teaching was taken up by Felix of Urgell in the Pyrenean diocese of Urgell within the Frankish realm.

Controversy and condemnation

The doctrine was attacked by Beatus of Liébana and Etherius of Osma in their Apologeticum (c. 785), and by Alcuin of York, adviser to Charlemagne. Pope Hadrian I condemned the teaching in letters of 785 and 791. A series of Frankish councils — Regensburg (792), Frankfurt (794), and Friuli (796/797) — condemned Adoptionism, and Felix of Urgell was compelled to recant more than once. At the Synod of Aachen in 800, Felix debated Alcuin and renounced the doctrine again. Elipandus himself, living beyond Frankish jurisdiction in Toledo, was never deposed and died still holding his see, probably around 808.

Legacy

Elipandus's letters and fragments of his writings survive largely through the works of his opponents. Historians debate whether his teaching was a genuine revival of an older heresy, a misunderstanding of traditional Spanish terminology, or a Christology shaped by dialogue with Islam, which strongly emphasises that God has no son by nature.
