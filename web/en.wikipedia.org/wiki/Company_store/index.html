Company store

A company store in a coal company town; miners paid in scrip often had no choice but to shop there.

A company store is a retail store owned and operated by the employer of the customers who shop there, typically in a company town where the employer also owned housing and other facilities. Company stores were characteristic of remote mining, logging, and mill settlements of the 19th and early 20th centuries, particularly in the coalfields of Appalachia in the United States, but also in Britain and elsewhere.

Operation and the truck system

In isolated settlements the company store might be the only shop available. Employers often paid wages in private currency known as scrip (the "truck system"), redeemable only at the company store, and extended credit against future wages. Prices and rents were widely alleged to be inflated, and debt to the store could tie workers to the employer in a system critics compared to debt bondage. The practice was immortalised in Merle Travis's song "Sixteen Tons" (1946), popularised by Tennessee Ernie Ford, with the line "I owe my soul to the company store." In Britain, the Truck Acts from 1831 onward progressively required payment of wages in cash and restricted truck, while in the United States several states passed anti-scrip laws, though enforcement was uneven.

Decline

Company stores declined with unionisation, labour legislation, better roads and car ownership, and the decline of isolated extractive settlements. In some places, including coal towns, company stores lingered into the mid-twentieth century. Not all were exploitative — some offered competitive prices and extensive credit that workers valued — and company stores survive today in attenuated form as staff shops and factory outlets.
