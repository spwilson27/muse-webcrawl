Vipassanā (Pali; Sanskrit: vipaśyanā) means "insight" or "clear seeing" in Buddhism. It is the practice of developing insight into the true nature of reality — impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), and non-self (anattā) — and is one of the two main kinds of Buddhist meditation, alongside samatha (calming meditation). In the modern era, the Burmese Vipassana movement made insight meditation widely accessible to laypeople and it spread worldwide.

Origins and texts

The term appears in the early Buddhist texts, where vipassanā is cultivated together with samatha. The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, on the four foundations of mindfulness, is the classic textual basis for insight practice. Commentaries such as the Visuddhimagga describe stages of insight knowledge (ñāṇa) leading toward liberation.

The Burmese Vipassana movement

In the 19th and 20th centuries, Burmese monks such as Ledi Sayadaw promoted meditation for laypeople, a major innovation. Mahasi Sayadaw systematised the "noting" method, closely watching the rising and falling of the abdomen and labelling experiences. U Ba Khin and his student S. N. Goenka popularised ten-day residential courses taught in silence, which spread internationally from the 1970s onward. In the West, teachers associated with the Insight Meditation Society in Massachusetts adapted the practice, influencing the modern mindfulness movement.

Practice

Vipassana practice typically involves sustained, moment-to-moment attention to bodily sensations, feelings, and mental events as they arise and pass away, without clinging or aversion. Retreatants often observe noble silence and follow a strict schedule. The intended fruit is experiential insight into the three marks of existence and, ultimately, liberation from suffering.
