Drive hunting

Drive hunting is a hunting technique in which game animals are herded or driven toward waiting hunters, obstacles, or traps. Used since prehistoric times — for example, driving bison over cliffs at "buffalo jumps" — the method remains in use both for terrestrial game and, controversially, for dolphins and small whales in drive fisheries.

Terrestrial drive hunting

In many European traditions, beaters move through cover making noise to flush game such as wild boar, deer and pheasants toward stationary shooters. Driven grouse and pheasant shooting in Britain, and the French battue, are well-known forms. Indigenous peoples of the North American Plains used drive lanes of stone cairns (inuksuit-like markers) to funnel caribou and bison toward hunters or cliff edges, as at Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump in Alberta.

Dolphin drive hunting

Dolphin drive hunting involves herding dolphins and small whales into a bay or cove using boats and underwater noise, where they are slaughtered for meat or captured alive for the aquarium trade. The practice is most associated with Taiji, Japan — the subject of the 2009 documentary The Cove — and with the grindadráp pilot-whale drives of the Faroe Islands. It is opposed by animal welfare organizations and banned or heavily restricted in many countries.
