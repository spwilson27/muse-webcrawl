Sadako Sasaki (佐々木 禎子, 7 January 1943 – 25 October 1955) was a Japanese girl who became a victim of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima by the United States near the end of World War II. She was two years old when exposed to the bombing, survived, but developed leukemia a decade later. While hospitalised, she undertook folding one thousand origami paper cranes (senbazuru), inspired by the Japanese belief that doing so would grant a wish. She has become a symbol of the innocent victims of nuclear warfare and of the peace movement.


Children's Peace Monument, Hiroshima

Life

Sadako was born in Hiroshima and was at home, about 1.6 kilometres (1 mi) from Ground Zero, when the atomic bomb was dropped on 6 August 1945. She was blown out of a window but survived with no apparent injuries. In November 1954, however, she developed swellings on her neck and behind her ears; in January 1955 she was diagnosed with leukemia, attributed to radiation exposure, and was admitted to the Hiroshima Red Cross Hospital in February 1955.

One thousand cranes

In August 1955, her best friend Chizuko Hamamoto visited her in hospital and told her the Japanese legend that anyone who folds one thousand paper cranes would be granted a wish by the gods. Sadako set out to fold the cranes, using medicine wrappings and any other paper she could find, including paper from other patients' get-well gifts. Popular accounts say she completed the thousand and continued folding; some accounts hold that she folded only 644 before dying and that her friends completed the remainder. She died on the morning of 25 October 1955, at the age of 12.

Legacy

After her death, Sadako's classmates raised funds for a memorial to her and all the child victims of the bomb. In 1958, the Children's Peace Monument was unveiled in Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park, topped by a statue of Sadako holding a golden crane. At its foot is the inscription: "This is our cry. This is our prayer. Peace in the world." Schoolchildren around the world continue to send paper cranes to the monument. Her story was popularised internationally by the novel Sadako and the Thousand Paper Cranes (1977) by Eleanor Coerr.
