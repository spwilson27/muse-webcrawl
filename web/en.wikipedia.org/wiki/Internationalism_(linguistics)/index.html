Internationalism (linguistics)

An internationalism or international word is a loanword that occurs in several languages with the same or at least similar meaning and etymology. These words exist in "several different languages as a result of simultaneous or successive borrowings from the ultimate source". Pronunciation and orthography are similar so that the word is understandable between the different languages.

Words such as "hotel", "taxi" and "telephone" are recognized across many languages.

Origins

Many internationalisms derive from Latin and Greek scientific and cultural vocabulary spread through education, religion and technology: democracy, biology, telephone, atom. Others spread with trade, colonialism, and modern media from languages such as French (hotel, ballet), Italian (piano, opera), Arabic (coffee, algebra), and increasingly English (computer, internet, okay).

Use in constructed languages

Designers of international auxiliary languages, notably Interlingua, systematically exploited internationalisms, selecting word forms attested across a set of source languages (principally English, French, Italian, Spanish/Portuguese, with German and Russian as secondary controls) so that the resulting vocabulary would be immediately intelligible to educated speakers of those languages.

Further aspects

Internationalisms may differ subtly in meaning between languages ("false friends"), and their form is adapted to each language's phonology and spelling—e.g. English nation, French nation, German Nation, Russian natsiya. The study of international vocabulary overlaps with etymology, lexicology and language contact research.
