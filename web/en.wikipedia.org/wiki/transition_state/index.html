Transition state

The transition state sits at the energy maximum along the reaction coordinate.

The transition state of a chemical reaction is a particular configuration along the reaction coordinate. It is defined as the state corresponding to the highest potential energy along this reaction coordinate. At this point, the reacting molecules collide or rearrange such that old bonds break and new bonds form; proceeding in either direction along the coordinate leads to reactants or products. It is often marked with the double dagger (‡) symbol.

Transition state theory, developed in 1935 by Henry Eyring, Meredith Evans, and Michael Polanyi, explains reaction rates in terms of an equilibrium between reactants and an "activated complex" at the transition state, and leads to the Eyring equation. The energy difference between reactants and the transition state — the activation energy — largely determines the reaction rate, so catalysts work by stabilizing transition states or providing pathways with lower-energy ones.

Observation

Transition states are fleeting (existing for femtoseconds) and cannot be isolated like intermediates; they are inferred from kinetics, kinetic isotope effects, computational chemistry (locating saddle points on potential energy surfaces), and ultrafast spectroscopy. Transition-state analogues — stable molecules mimicking the geometry and charge of a transition state — are potent enzyme inhibitors and an important strategy in drug design.
