Acid-base disorder

Acid-base disorders are pathological states that disturb the acid–base homeostasis of the body, shifting the pH of arterial blood outside its normal range of 7.35–7.45. They are classified by the direction of the pH change — acidosis (low pH) or alkalosis (high pH) — and by the primary mechanism: respiratory (a change in carbon dioxide tension) or metabolic (a change in bicarbonate or in acids other than carbonic acid).

Classification

DisorderPrimary changeCompensation
Metabolic acidosis↓ HCO₃⁻Hyperventilation (↓ PaCO₂)
Metabolic alkalosis↑ HCO₃⁻Hypoventilation (↑ PaCO₂)
Respiratory acidosis↑ PaCO₂Renal HCO₃⁻ retention
Respiratory alkalosis↓ PaCO₂Renal HCO₃⁻ excretion

The lungs adjust PaCO₂ within minutes by altering ventilation, while renal compensation — excreting acid and regenerating bicarbonate — takes hours to days. Mixed disorders, in which two or more primary processes coexist (common in critical illness), are identified with tools such as the anion gap, the delta ratio, and rules for expected compensation (e.g., Winter's formula).

Causes

Metabolic acidosis arises from acid overproduction (diabetic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis), bicarbonate loss (severe diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis), or failure to excrete acid in renal failure; the anion gap separates these causes. Metabolic alkalosis commonly follows vomiting or diuretic use. Respiratory acidosis results from hypoventilation (COPD, sedative overdose, neuromuscular weakness), and respiratory alkalosis from hyperventilation (pain, anxiety, sepsis, altitude, pregnancy).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on arterial blood gas analysis together with electrolytes, renal function, and clinical context. Treatment targets the underlying cause; sodium bicarbonate is reserved for selected severe cases, and mechanical ventilation may be required for respiratory failure.
