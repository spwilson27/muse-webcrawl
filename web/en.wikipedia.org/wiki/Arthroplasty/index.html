Arthroplasty

Arthroplasty (literally "joint formation") is an orthopedic surgical procedure in which an arthritic or dysfunctional joint surface is replaced, remodeled or realigned to restore function and relieve pain. The most common forms are total joint replacements of the hip and knee, in which the joint is replaced with a prosthesis made of metal alloys, ceramics and medical-grade polyethylene.

Types

Replacement arthroplasty substitutes the joint surfaces with implants; total hip replacement and total knee replacement are among the most successful operations in medicine, performed over a million times a year worldwide. Resurfacing arthroplasty caps the bone ends with a thin metal shell, preserving more bone. Interpositional and excision arthroplasty, older techniques that interpose tissue or remove bone, are now used only in special circumstances, such as infection or small joints of the hand and foot.

Indications and procedure

Arthroplasty is indicated when conservative treatment fails for end-stage osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, avascular necrosis, post-traumatic arthritis or certain fractures, most notably displaced hip fractures in older patients. Implants may be fixed with bone cement (polymethylmethacrylate) or by uncemented press-fit designs that encourage bone ingrowth. Modern practice emphasises rapid recovery protocols, minimally invasive approaches and, increasingly, computer-navigated or robot-assisted alignment.

Risks and outcomes

Most patients obtain major pain relief and restored mobility, with modern hip and knee prostheses commonly lasting 15–25 years or more. Complications include infection, dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, deep vein thrombosis, wear and aseptic loosening, which may require revision surgery. The field was transformed in the 1960s by Sir John Charnley's low-friction hip arthroplasty at Wrightington Hospital.
