Degaussing

Degaussing is the process of decreasing or eliminating a magnetic field, named after the gauss, a unit of magnetic field strength, and associated with the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss. The term is used in several distinct settings.

Naval degaussing

A steel-hulled ship acquires a magnetic signature from the Earth's field, which can trigger magnetic naval mines. During the Second World War the British developed degaussing — wrapping ships with electrical cables and passing current through them, or "wiping" the hull with strong electromagnets — to neutralise this signature. Navies established degaussing stations where vessels' fields were measured and trimmed; ships still carry degaussing coils with current adjusted to heading and latitude.

Displays and media

Colour cathode-ray tube monitors become magnetised, producing colour blotches; most CRTs degauss automatically at switch-on using a built-in coil and thermistor, accompanied by a characteristic hum and twang.

Degaussing is also a method of destroying data on magnetic storage media: a degausser exposes tapes and hard disk platters to a powerful alternating or pulsed magnetic field, randomising the magnetic domains and erasing servo tracks, which usually renders hard drives inoperable. Because it leaves no recoverable data, degaussing is an approved sanitisation method for classified media in several government standards.
