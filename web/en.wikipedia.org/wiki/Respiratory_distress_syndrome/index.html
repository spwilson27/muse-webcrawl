Respiratory distress syndrome

Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) is a breathing disorder most commonly arising in two distinct settings. Infant respiratory distress syndrome, also called hyaline membrane disease, affects premature newborns whose lungs have not yet produced enough pulmonary surfactant, causing the alveoli to collapse with each breath. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a life-threatening form of respiratory failure in older children and adults in which widespread lung inflammation — from causes such as pneumonia, sepsis, aspiration or major trauma — floods the alveoli with fluid.

Infant respiratory distress syndrome

Surfactant, a phospholipid mixture produced by type II pneumocytes from about the 24th week of gestation, lowers surface tension in the alveoli. In its absence the lungs are stiff and poorly aerated, and the baby shows rapid breathing, grunting, chest recession and cyanosis shortly after birth. Antenatal corticosteroids given to mothers at risk of preterm delivery accelerate lung maturity; treatment includes CPAP, mechanical ventilation and instillation of exogenous surfactant. Before modern neonatal care, RDS was a leading cause of infant death — it famously killed Patrick Bouvier Kennedy, the newborn son of US President John F. Kennedy, in 1963.

Acute respiratory distress syndrome

ARDS is defined by the Berlin criteria: acute onset of hypoxaemia with bilateral lung infiltrates not explained by cardiac failure. Management is supportive, centred on lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volumes, prone positioning in severe cases, and treatment of the underlying cause; mortality in severe ARDS remains substantial, and the syndrome was a major cause of death in the COVID-19 pandemic.
