Bowfishing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A bowfisher shooting from a lighted boat.

Bowfishing is a method of fishing that uses specialized archery equipment to shoot and retrieve fish. Fish are shot with a barbed arrow that is attached by a special line to a reel mounted on the bow. Historically, bowfishing was practiced by Indigenous peoples around the world for subsistence; today it is mainly a sport, and in North America it is most often pursued for rough fish such as carp, gar, and bowfin that are not generally taken by anglers.

Equipment

A bowfishing setup differs from ordinary target archery equipment in several ways. The bow is usually a compound or recurve bow of moderate draw weight. Arrows are heavy, made of solid fiberglass or carbon composite, and carry a barbed point designed to penetrate a fish and hold it during retrieval. There are no fletchings, since the arrow is shot over short distances, often from a boat into shallow water. The line—braided synthetic cord rated well above 100 pounds test—is stored on a large-capacity reel, either a bottle-style reel or a spincast reel mounted to the bow, or a hand spool in traditional setups.

Because fish are seen below the surface, refraction makes a fish appear higher and closer than it actually is, so bowfishers learn to aim low—often well below the visible image of the fish. Polarized sunglasses help during the day; at night, powerful lights powered by generators or battery banks are mounted around the gunwales of flat-bottomed boats or airboats to illuminate the shallows.

Species and regulations

Bowfishing is generally restricted to non-game or "rough" fish species, which vary by jurisdiction. Common targets in the United States include common carp, grass carp, bighead and silver carp (invasive Asian carp), longnose and alligator gar, suckers, buffalo fish, and bowfin. In coastal waters, some jurisdictions permit bowfishing for rays, skates, and certain sharks. A few states allow the taking of catfish or paddlefish in limited seasons. Many fisheries managers encourage bowfishing as a control method for invasive carp, and tournaments—some sponsored by the Bowfishing Association of America—can remove many tons of carp from a single waterbody in one night.

Regulations vary widely: most areas require a standard fishing licence, and rules may govern shooting near swimming areas, the discharge of arrows across public roads or waterways, and the proper disposal of harvested fish. Bowfishing for game fish such as bass or trout is prohibited in nearly all jurisdictions.

History

Fishing with bow and arrow is ancient. Aboriginal Australians, Amazonian peoples, and Native American groups such as the Seminole all used bows to take fish in shallow water long before European contact. The modern sport took shape in the United States in the mid-20th century and grew rapidly with the spread of specialized equipment and night-fishing tournaments in the 1990s and 2000s.
