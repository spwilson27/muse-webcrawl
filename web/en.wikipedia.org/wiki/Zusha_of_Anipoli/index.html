Rabbi Meshulam Zusha of Anipoli (c. 1730 – 1800), Hebrew: רבי משולם זושא מאניפולי, often called Reb Zusha, was a prominent Hasidic rabbi of the third generation of Hasidic leaders and a disciple of the Maggid of Mezeritch. He was the younger brother and close companion of Rabbi Elimelech of Lizhensk, the author of the Noam Elimelech. Zusha is celebrated in Hasidic tradition for his humility, simplicity, and absolute devotion to God, and he is the subject of many beloved Hasidic tales.

Life

Zusha and Elimelech were born to Rabbi Eliezer Lipa and Mirel in Podolia. The brothers spent years as wandering ascetics — the period known as galut ("exile") — travelling from town to town, practising self-mortification and bringing Jews closer to repentance, before settling to study under Dov Ber, the Maggid of Mezeritch, the successor of the Baal Shem Tov.

After the Maggid's death, Zusha settled in Anipoli (Hannopil) in Volhynia, where he lived in great poverty and led a small circle of disciples. He refused wealth and honour, and is said to have wept that he did not fear the heavenly tribunal asking him why he was not Moses — only why he was not Zusha, why he did not fulfil his own potential. This saying, preserved by Martin Buber, became one of the most famous anecdotes of Hasidic spirituality.

Teachings and legacy

Zusha left no written works of his own; his teachings were collected by his disciples in works such as Menorat Zahav. He emphasized humility, joy in worship, love of every Jew, and serving God with one's own unique character rather than imitating others. His grave in Hannopil, beside that of the Maggid of Mezeritch and Rabbi Yehuda Leib of Anipoli, remains a pilgrimage site. His yahrzeit, 2 Shevat, draws thousands of visitors annually.
