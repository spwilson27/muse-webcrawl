Bernard Berenson (June 26, 1865 – October 6, 1959) was an American art historian specialising in the Italian Renaissance. His attributions of Old Master paintings made him the most influential connoisseur of his era, and his judgements shaped the great American collections of the early twentieth century.

Early life and education

Berenson was born Bernhard Valvrojenski in Butrimonys, in present-day Lithuania, then part of the Russian Empire, to a Jewish family. The family emigrated to Boston in 1875 and changed its name to Berenson. He studied at Boston Latin School and Harvard University, where he came under the influence of Charles Eliot Norton, and graduated in 1887. A travelling fellowship took him to Italy, where he devoted himself to the study of Renaissance painting.

Career

Working in the tradition of Giovanni Morelli's method of attribution through minor details, Berenson published influential studies of the Venetian, Florentine and Sienese painters and of Lorenzo Lotto. His lists of works by each master became the standard reference for dealers and collectors. From 1907 he advised the art dealer Joseph Duveen, a lucrative arrangement that brought him great wealth but also persistent questions about conflicts of interest, since his certificates could transform a painting's market value. He advised collectors including Isabella Stewart Gardner, whose Boston museum is filled with works he selected.

Villa I Tatti and legacy

Berenson settled at the Villa I Tatti near Florence, which became a celebrated salon visited by scholars and writers. He bequeathed the villa, his library and his collection to Harvard University; it is now the Harvard University Center for Italian Renaissance Studies. His diaries and his book Sketch for a Self-Portrait record a life devoted to what he called the "life-enhancing" qualities of art.
