Donald Johanson

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Donald Johanson kneeling at the Hadar excavation site in Ethiopia in 1974]
Donald Johanson kneeling at the Hadar excavation site in Ethiopia in 1974

Donald Carl Johanson (born 28 June 1943) is an American paleoanthropologist who discovered the fossilised skeleton of a female Australopithecus afarensis — 'Lucy' — in the Afar desert of Ethiopia on 24 November 1974, one of the most famous fossil finds of the twentieth century. With Tim White and Yves Coppens he named the species A. afarensis (1978), and he founded the Institute of Human Origins (1981).

Lucy and afarensis

Johanson's team at Hadar recovered some 40 percent of a skeleton, dated to 3.2 million years ago, whose pelvis and knee proved habitual bipedalism in a small-brained hominin — reordering the human story (walking before big brains). His popular books (Lucy: The Beginnings of Humankind, 1981) made him the public face of the field; his disputes with the Leakey family and the taxonomic wars over afarensis's place (Johanson placed it as the single ancestor of both later australopithecines and Homo) defined a combative era of the science.

Later career

The Institute of Human Origins moved from Berkeley to Arizona State University in 1997; Johanson continued fieldwork and broadcasting (the Nova series 'In Search of Human Origins') while Lucy toured the world — including a controversial US exhibition from 2007 — as Ethiopia's ambassador and science's most recognisable fossil.
