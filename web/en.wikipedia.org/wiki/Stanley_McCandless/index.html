Stanley McCandless

Stanley Russell McCandless (9 May 1897 – 4 August 1967) was an American lighting designer and educator, widely regarded as the founder of modern stage lighting design in the United States. He taught at the Yale School of Drama for decades and developed the influential McCandless method of lighting the stage.

Career

McCandless studied architecture at Harvard University and worked in architectural lighting before turning to the theatre. He joined the faculty of the Yale School of Drama in the 1920s, where he taught lighting design for over forty years and helped make Yale a leading center for theatrical design education. His students included many of the mid-twentieth century's most prominent designers.

The McCandless method

Published in his 1932 book A Method of Lighting the Stage, the McCandless method divides the stage into acting areas. Each area is lit by at least two instruments from opposite sides at roughly 45-degree angles: one warm in color, one cool. This cross-lighting sculpts the actor's form, creating a sense of depth and naturalism, while front lighting provides general visibility. A third element — specialized or motivated light — adds atmosphere. The system became the standard starting point for lighting design in American educational and professional theatre.

Legacy

Beyond his method, McCandless championed the recognition of lighting design as an artistic discipline in its own right rather than a mere technical craft. His textbooks, including Syllabus of Stage Lighting, shaped generations of designers, and his approach remains a foundation of lighting pedagogy today.
