Manor house

A manor house is the principal house of a landed estate in the historical land ownership system of manorialism, typically in the countryside. Manor houses were historically the meeting place of the manorial court and often formed the nucleus of a village. The manor house was the residence of the lord of the manor or his administrator, and was distinct from castles, which were primarily military in purpose, although many manor houses in the medieval period were fortified to some degree.


An English manor house, showing the characteristic stone construction and gabled roof of the Tudor period.

History

The manor house developed in England and across Europe during the Middle Ages as the administrative and residential centre of a manor, a unit of land tenure under the feudal system. Early manor houses were often simple hall houses, in which a single large great hall served as the main living, dining, and judicial space for the household.

During the Tudor and Elizabethan periods, many manor houses were rebuilt in more comfortable and ostentatious styles, reflecting the growing wealth and status of the gentry. The great rebuilding of rural England in the late 16th and 17th centuries produced many of the surviving manor houses, built of brick or stone with mullioned windows, multiple gables, and formal gardens.

In the 18th and 19th centuries, the manor house remained the social centre of the parish, and the squire or lord of the manor played an important role in local government. The decline of the agricultural estate system in the 20th century, together with death duties and rising maintenance costs, led to the sale or demolition of many manor houses, although large numbers survive as private homes, hotels, and museums.

Architecture

Manor houses vary greatly in size and style, from modest farmhouses to substantial country seats. Common features include a great hall, a solar or parlour for the family's private use, service rooms such as a buttery and pantry, and a detached or attached kitchen. Many have a chapel, either within the house or nearby. Materials were usually local: stone in the Cotswolds and Yorkshire, timber framing in the Welsh Marches and Cheshire, and brick in East Anglia.

Manor houses in other countries

Equivalent houses exist throughout Europe and in former colonial territories. In France the manoir is a smaller country house, distinct from the grander château. In Poland the dwór served a similar function, while in Scandinavia manor houses are known as herregård (Danish and Norwegian) or herrgård (Swedish). In the colonial United States, houses on large plantations, particularly in the South and the Mid-Atlantic, are sometimes termed manor houses.
