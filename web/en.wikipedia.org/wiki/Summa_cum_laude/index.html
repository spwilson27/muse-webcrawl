Summa cum laude

Summa cum laude is a Latin phrase meaning "with the highest honour" or "with the highest praise". It is the highest of the three traditional Latin honours used in North America and some other countries to recognise outstanding academic achievement at graduation, ranking above magna cum laude ("with great honour") and cum laude ("with honour").

Usage

Latin honours are awarded by colleges and universities mainly in the United States, Canada, and the Philippines, and in a number of European institutions. Each institution sets its own criteria. Most commonly, summa cum laude is reserved for students in the top band of the graduating class — often the top 1–5 per cent by grade point average, for example a GPA of 3.9 or higher on a 4.0 scale — though some universities instead require a faculty vote, a senior thesis, or other distinction. Because criteria vary, the honour is not strictly comparable between institutions.

History

The Latin honours system was introduced in the United States in the nineteenth century; Harvard College adopted Latin honours in 1869, and the practice spread widely in the following decades. Amherst College had used a Latin honours system as early as 1881 under president Julius Seelye. In earlier centuries the phrase was used by some European universities, including for doctoral examinations, before the modern class-rank model developed.

Elsewhere

Related distinctions exist in other systems: German universities award doctorates graded summa cum laude as the highest examination mark; British universities instead classify honours degrees (first class, upper second, and so on). In Italy the top degree mark of 110/110 may be accompanied by lode (honours). Some institutions have debated or dropped Latin honours in favour of percentile-based or departmental distinctions, but the three-tier Latin scheme remains the most widely recognised form in American higher education.
