Mali Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Great Mosque of Djenné, the largest mud-brick building in the world]

The Mali Empire (c. 1235–c. 1670) was a Mandinka empire in West Africa, founded by Sundiata Keita after his victory at the Battle of Kirina, which became the dominant power of the western Sudan. Mali controlled the southern termini of the trans-Saharan trade routes and the goldfields of Bure and Bambuk; its wealth rested on gold and salt. Its ninth mansa, Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337), made a pilgrimage to Mecca so lavish that it depressed the price of gold in Cairo for years, and put Mali on the world map of European cartographers. Its cities, above all Timbuktu and Djenné, became centres of Islamic scholarship.

Sundiata and the empire's structure

Sundiata's victory over the Sosso king Sumanguru (c. 1235) founded the empire, celebrated in the griot-told Epic of Sundiata. Mali was a federation of tributary kingdoms and provinces governed by appointed farins under the mansa, with the Gbara assembly of clan leaders. Its army, agriculture along the Niger bend, and control of trade made it larger — at its height under Musa and his brother Sulayman — than any state of contemporary western Europe.

Decline

After Sulayman's death (c. 1360), succession disputes weakened the centre; the Tuareg seized Timbuktu (1433), the Mossi raided, and the rising Songhai Empire stripped away the eastern provinces in the later 15th century. A rump Mali survived in the south-west until the 17th century, fragmented by civil war and Bambara pressure. Its legacy — the Mandinka epic tradition, Timbuktu's manuscripts (some 700,000 survive), and the very idea of a wealthy African empire — endures in Mali, Guinea and the wider region.
