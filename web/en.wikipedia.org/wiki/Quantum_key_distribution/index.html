Quantum key distribution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of the BB84 protocol: Alice sending polarised photons through a quantum channel to Bob, with a public discussion channel]
The BB84 quantum key distribution protocol.

Quantum key distribution (QKD) is a secure communication method that implements a cryptographic protocol involving components of quantum mechanics. It enables two parties to produce a shared random secret key known only to them, usable as a key for encryption, with the guarantee that any eavesdropping attempt disturbs the quantum transmissions and can be detected — security based on physical law rather than computational assumptions.

The founding protocol, BB84, was proposed by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984: Alice sends photons in randomly chosen polarisation bases; Bob measures in random bases; they keep only matching-basis results, and sacrifice a sample to estimate the error rate — which any interceptor (bound by the No-cloning theorem and measurement disturbance) necessarily raises. Artur Ekert's E91 protocol (1991) uses Quantum entanglement and Bell tests. QKD runs over metropolitan fibre networks and via satellite (China's Micius demonstrated intercontinental quantum-secured calls in 2017), though security agencies note practical caveats — implementation flaws, trusted relays, and comparison with post-quantum classical cryptography — leaving QKD a powerful but debated technology.

Limitations

QKD distributes keys; it does not encrypt by itself (the key is used with one-time pads or AES). Photon loss limits distance (hence satellites and, prospectively, quantum repeaters); real devices deviate from ideal models, and side-channel attacks have repeatedly broken commercial implementations — prompting measurement-device-independent protocols. Standards bodies (notably NSA and NCSC) advise that for most purposes post-quantum algorithms are the more practical path.
