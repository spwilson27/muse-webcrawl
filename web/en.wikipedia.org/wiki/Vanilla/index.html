Vanilla

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Vanilla pods and a vanilla flower, with a split pod showing seeds]

Vanilla is a flavouring derived from the seed pods of orchids in the genus Vanilla, chiefly Vanilla planifolia, native to Mexico. The pods ("beans") are green and flavourless when picked; months of curing — sweating, sun-drying and conditioning — develop the dark, fragrant bean full of tiny seeds whose aroma, dominated by the compound vanillin, is one of the world's most popular flavours. Madagascar and Indonesia are the largest producers, and vanilla is among the most expensive spices by weight after saffron.

History

The Totonac people of Mexico's Gulf coast cultivated vanilla, which the Aztecs used to flavour the cacao drink xocolatl. Spanish conquistadors brought it to Europe, but the plants would not fruit outside Mexico: the orchid must be pollinated by a specific bee (or, as was discovered, by hand). In 1841 the enslaved boy Edmond Albius on Réunion invented a practical hand-pollination method, which spread vanilla cultivation to the Indian Ocean colonies — and he remains honoured in Madagascar and Réunion for it.

Cultivation and curing

Every flower must be hand-pollinated within hours of its single-morning opening, making vanilla extraordinarily labour-intensive. Curing kills the pod, then alternates sweating and drying for weeks, followed by months of conditioning in boxes. Grades and origins — Bourbon vanilla (Madagascar/Réunion), Mexican, Tahitian (V. tahitensis, more floral) — differ noticeably.

Uses and substitutes

Vanilla perfumes ice cream, custards, cakes, chocolate and soft drinks; "plain vanilla" has become a byword for the default option precisely because of its ubiquity. Most vanilla flavouring in the world is now synthetic vanillin (made from lignin, guaiacol or other sources), since natural vanilla is scarce and costly, but whole-bean vanilla remains the connoisseur standard.
