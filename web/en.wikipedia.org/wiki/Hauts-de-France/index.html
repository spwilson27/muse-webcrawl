Hauts-de-France (literally "Heights of France," referring to its northern position on the map) is the northernmost region of France. It was created by the territorial reform of 2014 through the merger of the former regions of Nord-Pas-de-Calais and Picardy, effective 1 January 2016. Its prefecture and largest city is Lille. Not to be confused with the smaller department Hauts-de-Seine near Paris.

Geography

The region borders Belgium to the northeast, the English Channel and the Strait of Dover to the north — facing England across the Channel Tunnel's French terminal at Coquelles near Calais — Normandy to the west, Île-de-France to the south, and Grand Est to the east. The landscape is mostly low-lying plains and gentle hills, with ports at Calais, Boulogne-sur-Mer, and Dunkirk.

History

The territory combines historic Flanders, Artois, and Picardy, long contested between France, Spain, and the Low Countries. It was a major coal-mining and textile basin during industrialisation — its mining basin is now a UNESCO World Heritage site — and a devastating battlefield in both world wars, including the Somme (1916) and Arras. The provisional name "Nord-Pas-de-Calais-Picardie" was replaced by "Hauts-de-France" in 2016 after a public consultation, partly among school students.

Economy and culture

Hauts-de-France is a logistics crossroads between Paris, London, and Brussels, and retains significant automotive, rail, and agri-food industries. Cultural landmarks include the cathedrals of Amiens (the largest Gothic cathedral in France) and Beauvais, the Louvre-Lens museum, and the belfries of the north. The region's mixed French, Picard, and Flemish heritage is reflected in its carnival traditions, notably the Dunkirk Carnival.
