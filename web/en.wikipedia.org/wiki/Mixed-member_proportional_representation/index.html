Mixed-member proportional representation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mixed-member proportional representation (MMP) is a mixed electoral system in which voters cast two votes — one for a local representative in a single-member district, one for a party — and the party vote determines each party's total share of seats, with district winners' seats counted towards that total and list seats topping it up to proportionality ("compensatory" mixing). Its archetype is Germany's personalised proportional system, used for the Bundestag since 1949 (with roots in some Länder) and adopted, in variants, by New Zealand (since 1996, after a referendum-driven reform), Bolivia, Lesotho, and (as the additional-member system) Scotland and Wales.

Properties and problems

MMP promises the best of both worlds — local constituency representation and overall proportionality — at the price of complexity: overhang seats (when a party wins more district seats than its party vote entitles it to) disturb the arithmetic and have repeatedly forced German reforms (culminating in the 2023 fix of the chamber at 630 seats by subordinating district wins to party-vote coverage); decoy-list strategies in some countries have gamed the compensation mechanism; and two classes of members (district and list) provoke status debates. Evaluations generally find MMP performs as designed on proportionality and representation while retaining single-member accountability — the standard exhibit for reform commissions in plurality countries.
