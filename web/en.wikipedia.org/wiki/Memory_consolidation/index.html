Memory consolidation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Memory consolidation is a category of processes that stabilize a memory trace after its initial acquisition. It involves the strengthening and reorganization of neural representations so that memories become resistant to interference and decay. Consolidation is usually described at two levels: synaptic consolidation, which occurs within the first few hours after learning, and systems consolidation, which can take weeks to years.

Synaptic consolidation

Synaptic consolidation depends on the late phase of long-term potentiation (LTP), requiring gene expression and new protein synthesis in the neurons that encode the memory. It is during this labile window that memories can be disrupted by trauma, electroconvulsive shock, or pharmacological agents such as protein synthesis inhibitors — the basis of retrograde amnesia gradients first described by Ribot's law (1881).

Systems consolidation

The standard model of systems consolidation holds that declarative memories initially depend on the hippocampus, which binds distributed cortical representations; over time, through repeated reactivation — prominently during sleep, when hippocampal sharp-wave ripples coordinate replay of waking experience — memories become increasingly dependent on the neocortex and independent of the hippocampus. The multiple trace theory proposes instead that episodic memories remain hippocampus-dependent, with each reactivation creating new traces.

Reconsolidation

Retrieved memories can return to a labile state and require restabilization — a process termed reconsolidation — which allows memories to be updated or modified. This phenomenon, demonstrated prominently in fear conditioning studies from around 2000, has attracted interest for potential clinical treatments of PTSD and addiction.

History

The term was introduced around 1900 by German researchers Georg Elias Müller and Alfons Pilzecker, who observed that new learning interfered with earlier material. The case of patient H.M. (1957), who could not form new long-term declarative memories after hippocampal removal, and David Marr's 1971 model of hippocampal–cortical interaction, were key influences on modern theory.
