Dachau was a Nazi concentration camp opened on 22 March 1933 near the town of Dachau, about 16 km northwest of Munich. It was the first regular concentration camp established by the Nazi regime, served as a model and training ground for the SS camp system, and operated for the entire twelve years of Nazi rule.

History

Dachau was built on the grounds of a former munitions factory for the regime's political opponents — communists, social democrats, trade unionists and others. Under commandant Theodor Eicke it developed the organisational template, regulations and guard formations (the SS-Totenkopfverbände) later used across the camp system. Over time its prisoner population expanded to include Jews (especially after Kristallnacht in 1938), Roma, Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexual men, clergy — including a large group of Polish priests — and prisoners from across occupied Europe.

Conditions and crimes

Prisoners endured forced labour, hunger, disease, brutal punishments and medical experiments, including high-altitude, hypothermia and malaria experiments conducted by SS doctors such as Sigmund Rascher. At least 41,500 deaths are documented. As Allied forces approached, thousands of prisoners were sent on death marches southward; about 30,000 surviving prisoners were liberated by the US Army on 29 April 1945.

Memorial

After use as an internment and displaced-persons camp, the site became a memorial in 1965 at the initiative of survivors. The Dachau Concentration Camp Memorial Site, with its museum, reconstructed barracks, religious memorials and the International Monument, receives around a million visitors a year.
