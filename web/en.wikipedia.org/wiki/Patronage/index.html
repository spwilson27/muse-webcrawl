Patronage

Patronage is the support, encouragement, privilege, or financial aid that an organisation or individual bestows upon another. In politics, the term denotes the power of a government or office-holder to appoint supporters to jobs, award contracts, or distribute favours — the "spoils system" — in contrast to appointment by merit. In the arts, it describes the historical system by which rulers, churches, and wealthy families commissioned and sustained artists, composers, and scholars.

Political patronage

Patron–client relations are among the oldest structures of politics: in Rome, patrons protected clients in law and public life in return for political support. In modern democracies, patronage powered the great urban political machines of the United States — of which Tammany Hall in New York and the Chicago organisation of Richard J. Daley are the classic cases — which exchanged jobs, contracts, and services for votes and loyalty. The assassination of President James A. Garfield in 1881 by a disappointed office-seeker spurred civil-service reform: the Pendleton Act (1883) began replacing federal patronage appointments with merit-based examination, a shift repeated in many countries, though senior political appointments, ambassadorships, and government contracts remain patronage terrain everywhere.

Political scientists distinguish patronage (jobs for supporters) from pork-barrel spending (projects for districts) and from clientelism more broadly, and debate its effects: machines delivered welfare and integration for immigrants before the welfare state, but patronage also entrenches incumbents, degrades administration, and shades into corruption when public resources are diverted for private or party benefit.

Patronage of the arts

Until the rise of markets and state arts funding, most Western art was produced under patronage: the Medici in Florence, popes commissioning Michelangelo and Raphael, courts employing Haydn and Mozart, and churches commissioning music, painting, and architecture. The patron's tastes and prestige shaped the work, yet the system produced much of the canonical art of Europe; modern equivalents include arts councils, corporate sponsorship, and crowdfunding platforms.
