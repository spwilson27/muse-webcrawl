Great Fire of Smyrna

Smyrna burning, photographed from a ship in the harbour, September 1922.

The Great Fire of Smyrna was a fire that destroyed much of the port city of Smyrna (modern İzmir, Turkey) in September 1922, in the final phase of the Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922). The fire began on 13 September 1922, four days after Turkish forces under Mustafa Kemal recaptured the city from Greek administration, and burned for several days, devastating the Armenian and Greek quarters along the waterfront.

Events

Following the collapse of the Greek army in Anatolia, hundreds of thousands of Greek and Armenian refugees crowded into Smyrna hoping for evacuation by sea. The fire started in the Armenian quarter and, fanned by wind, consumed the European and Christian districts while the Turkish and Jewish quarters were largely spared. Trapped between the flames and the sea, refugees filled the quay; Allied warships in the harbour initially declined to take refugees aboard, though hundreds of thousands were eventually evacuated by Greek and Allied vessels. Estimates of the dead range from several thousand to tens of thousands, and more than 400,000 people were left homeless by the destruction.

Responsibility and legacy

Who set the fire remains fiercely disputed: Greek and Armenian accounts attribute it to Turkish soldiers and irregulars acting to drive out Christian inhabitants and seize property; Turkish accounts blame Armenian or Greek arsonists seeking to deny the city to the Turks. Historians continue to debate the evidence. The fire marked the end of Smyrna's cosmopolitan, majority-Christian character and preceded the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey, under which the city's remaining Greeks were expelled. Giles Milton's Paradise Lost: Smyrna 1922 is a well-known popular account of the catastrophe.
