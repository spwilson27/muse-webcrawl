Karl Landsteiner (14 June 1868 – 26 June 1943) was an Austrian-American biologist, physician, and immunologist. He discovered the ABO blood groups in 1900, which made blood transfusion a safe and routine medical practice, and later identified the Rh factor with Alexander S. Wiener. Landsteiner received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1930 "for his discovery of human blood groups."

Early life and education

Landsteiner was born in Baden bei Wien, near Vienna, the son of Leopold Landsteiner, a prominent journalist and newspaper publisher, and Fanny Hess. His father died when Karl was six, and he was raised by his mother. He studied medicine at the University of Vienna, receiving his medical degree in 1891. Even as a student he worked on chemical problems, publishing a paper on the influence of diet on the composition of the ash of blood.

Research in Vienna

After further chemical training in Zurich, Würzburg, and Munich, Landsteiner returned to Vienna and worked in pathological anatomy. In 1900 he observed that blood serum from some individuals agglutinated the red blood cells of others. In his landmark 1901 paper he described three blood groups—A, B, and C (later renamed O)—based on the presence of antigens on red cells. A fourth group, AB, was identified in 1902 by his colleagues Alfred von Decastello and Adriano Sturli. These discoveries explained why some transfusions succeeded and others killed the recipient.

Rockefeller Institute and the Rh factor

In 1923 Landsteiner emigrated to the United States and joined the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research in New York. With Philip Levine he discovered additional blood group systems, the M, N, and P factors, in 1927. In 1940, with Alexander Wiener, he identified the Rh (Rhesus) factor, which proved central to understanding hemolytic disease of the newborn. Landsteiner also made important contributions to the study of syphilis serology, the pathology of poliomyelitis—he demonstrated with Erwin Popper in 1908 that polio was caused by a filtrable virus—and the chemistry of haptens, laying foundations of modern immunochemistry.

Death and legacy

Landsteiner suffered a heart attack in his laboratory on 24 June 1943 and died two days later. His work transformed transfusion medicine, surgery, obstetrics, and forensic science. 14 June, his birthday, is now celebrated as World Blood Donor Day.
