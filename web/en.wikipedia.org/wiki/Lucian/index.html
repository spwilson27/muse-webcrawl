Lucian

Lucian of Samosata (c. AD 125 – after 180) was a Syrian satirist and rhetorician of the Second Sophistic who wrote in Greek. Famous for his wit, irony and mastery of the comic dialogue, he satirised superstition, religious credulity, philosophical pretension and the vanities of his age. More than eighty works attributed to him survive, including the fantastic voyage A True Story, often described as an ancestor of science fiction.

Life

Born in Samosata on the Euphrates to a family of modest means, Lucian was apprenticed to a sculptor uncle before abandoning the trade — a story he tells with mock contrition — and acquiring a Greek education. He travelled widely as a professional lecturer and court pleader, visited Rome and Gaul, and in old age held a minor administrative post in Egypt. His native tongue was probably Aramaic, making his flawless Attic Greek a deliberate triumph of the Second Sophistic.

Works

In dialogues such as the Dialogues of the Dead and Dialogues of the Gods he deflates Olympian myth and human ambition; The Passing of Peregrinus and Alexander the False Prophet attack religious charlatans (and incidentally give precious, hostile evidence about early Christianity); How to Write History is a sharp essay on historiographical method. A True Story, a parody of travellers' tales, sends its narrator to the Moon, inside a whale, and to the Isles of the Blessed.

Influence

Lucian was a favourite of the Renaissance humanists — Erasmus and Thomas More translated him — and his ironic dialogue form influenced writers from Swift to Anatole France. His epigrams also appear in the Greek Anthology.
