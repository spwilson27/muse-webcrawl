In electromagnetism, the permittivity of a material is a measure of how an electric field affects, and is affected by, the material — specifically, how much electric flux is generated per unit charge in it. It determines how much a material resists the formation of an electric field within itself, and hence how much charge a capacitor of given geometry can store. The permittivity of free space, ε0, is a fundamental physical constant.

Definition

Permittivity relates the electric displacement field D to the electric field E in a medium:

D = εE

The relative permittivity εr (often called the dielectric constant) is the ratio of a material's permittivity to that of vacuum:

εr = ε / ε0

For vacuum εr = 1; for air it is about 1.0006; for water about 80 at room temperature and low frequencies; common insulating materials fall between 2 and 10.

Mechanisms

When an electric field is applied to a dielectric, bound charges within the material shift slightly from their equilibrium positions — electronic, ionic and dipolar polarisation — producing an internal field that partly cancels the applied one. In real materials permittivity is generally complex and frequency-dependent: the imaginary part accounts for dielectric loss, and the permittivity of many materials falls as frequency rises through each polarisation mechanism's relaxation range.

Applications

Permittivity governs the capacitance of capacitors, the speed of signals in cables and printed circuit boards, the design of antennas and waveguides, and the interaction of electromagnetic waves with matter, including microwave heating and radar absorption.
