Mosasaurs (from Latin Mosa meaning the "Meuse river", and Greek σαύρος sauros meaning "lizard") comprise a group of extinct, large marine reptiles from the Late Cretaceous. Their first fossil remains were discovered in a limestone quarry at Maastricht on the Meuse in 1764. Mosasaurs are now considered close relatives of snakes and monitor lizards, within the squamate reptiles. They breathed air, were powerful swimmers, and were viviparous — giving birth to live young rather than laying eggs.

Description

Mosasaurs had double-hinged jaws and flexible skulls (much like those of snakes), enabling them to gulp down large prey. Their limbs were modified into hydrofoil-like flippers for steering, while propulsion came from a powerful tail — in later species tipped with a downward kink supporting a crescent-shaped fluke, much like a shark's. The smallest known mosasaur was about 1 m long; the largest, Mosasaurus hoffmannii, may have reached 15–17 m.

Discovery and history

The Maastricht skull, the "grand animal fossile des carrières de Maestricht," was seized by French revolutionary troops and taken to Paris, where Georges Cuvier studied it and recognised it as an extinct marine lizard — an early landmark in the science of extinction. Adriaan Gilles Camper and others debated its affinities; the name Mosasaurus was coined by William Conybeare in 1822. Mosasaur fossils were later found worldwide, including in the chalk deposits of Kansas, once part of the Western Interior Seaway, and are among the classic fossils of the "Bone Wars" era.

Paleobiology

As apex predators of the Late Cretaceous seas, mosasaurs fed on fish, ammonites, marine turtles, plesiosaurs, birds, and smaller mosasaurs; fossil stomach contents and tooth-marked ammonite shells document their diet. Like other marine reptiles of the Mesozoic, they disappeared in the end-Cretaceous mass extinction 66 million years ago.
