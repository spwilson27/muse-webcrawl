Goldstone boson

In particle and condensed matter physics, Goldstone bosons or Nambu–Goldstone bosons (NGBs) are bosons that appear necessarily in models exhibiting spontaneous breakdown of continuous symmetries. They were discovered by Yoichiro Nambu in the context of the BCS theory of superconductivity, and their general existence was proved by Jeffrey Goldstone, Abdus Salam and Steven Weinberg. Goldstone's theorem states that for each spontaneously broken continuous symmetry there is a corresponding massless (or, if the symmetry is only approximate, light) spinless particle.

Examples

The classic examples are the pions of quantum chromodynamics, which are pseudo-Goldstone bosons of the spontaneously broken approximate chiral symmetry of the strong interaction; their small but non-zero mass reflects the fact that the symmetry is also explicitly broken by quark masses. In condensed matter physics, phonons are Goldstone bosons of broken translational symmetry, and magnons (spin waves) arise from broken rotational symmetry of spins. In superconductivity, the would-be Goldstone mode is absorbed into the electromagnetic field, giving the photon a mass inside the superconductor — the condensed-matter analogue of the Higgs mechanism.

Goldstone's theorem

The theorem applies to theories with a continuous internal symmetry group that is not a symmetry of the vacuum state. If the symmetry group G is spontaneously broken to a subgroup H, the number of Goldstone bosons equals the number of broken generators, dim G − dim H. When the symmetry is a gauge symmetry rather than a global one, the Goldstone bosons are instead "eaten" by the gauge bosons, which acquire mass; this is the Higgs mechanism of the Standard Model, in which three Goldstone modes become the longitudinal components of the W and Z bosons.

Pseudo-Goldstone bosons

If the symmetry is approximate — explicitly broken by small terms in the Lagrangian as well as spontaneously — the resulting particles are called pseudo-Goldstone bosons (or pseudo-Nambu–Goldstone bosons) and have small masses proportional to the explicit breaking. The pions and kaons are the standard examples.
