Empowerment

Empowerment

Field: Community psychology, social work, management, development studies

Meaning: Increasing the capacity of individuals or groups to make choices and transform them into action

Key figures: Julian Rappaport, Paulo Freire (influence)

Empowerment is the degree of autonomy and self-determination in people and in communities, enabling them to represent their interests responsibly and to act on their own authority. The term originated in community psychology and the social movements of the 1960s and 1970s, and has since been applied in development, management, feminism, education, and health promotion.

Origins

The psychologist Julian Rappaport popularised empowerment as the central concept of community psychology in the 1980s, defining it as a process by which people, organisations, and communities gain mastery over their affairs. It drew on earlier currents: the civil rights and women's movements, Paulo Freire's pedagogy of critical consciousness (conscientização), and self-help traditions.

Dimensions

Empowerment is usually analysed at individual (psychological), organisational, and community levels. Psychological empowerment involves perceived control, self-efficacy, and critical awareness of one's social environment. In development economics, the economist Naila Kabeer defines empowerment as the expansion of people's ability to make strategic life choices, through the interplay of resources, agency, and achievements; the World Bank has similarly described it as increasing the capacity of individuals or groups to make choices and convert them into desired actions and outcomes.

Applications

  • Women's empowerment — promoting women's autonomy, education, economic participation, and political representation; measured in indices and enshrined in UN Sustainable Development Goal 5.
  • Employee empowerment — management practice of delegating authority and involving workers in decision-making.
  • Youth and patient empowerment — involving young people, or patients in healthcare, as active participants rather than passive recipients.

Criticism

Critics note that "empowerment" has become a vague buzzword, easily co-opted for marketing or for shifting responsibility from institutions to individuals, and that genuine empowerment requires changes in power relations, not only in perceptions.
