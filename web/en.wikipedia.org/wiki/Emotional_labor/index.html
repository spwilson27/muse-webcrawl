Emotional labor

Emotional labor is the act of managing one's emotional expression, or suppressing and evoking particular feelings, in order to fulfil the emotional requirements of a job. The term was first coined by the sociologist Arlie Hochschild in her 1983 book The Managed Heart, which studied flight attendants and bill collectors and the feeling rules their employers imposed on them.

Surface and deep acting

Hochschild distinguished surface acting, in which a worker changes only outward expressions — smiling while feeling irritated, for example — from deep acting, in which the worker tries to actually modify their inner feelings to match the required display. Research has linked habitual surface acting to emotional exhaustion and burnout, whereas deep acting tends to be less harmful and can increase feelings of personal accomplishment.

Applications

Emotional labor is most visible in service work: hospitality, retail, health care, teaching, and customer service, where employers prescribe friendly, calm, or empathetic displays. It is unevenly distributed by gender and occupation; jobs held disproportionately by women frequently carry unrecognised expectations of warmth and care. In popular usage since the 2010s the term has broadened to cover unpaid emotion management in households and relationships, such as remembering birthdays, soothing conflicts, and organising social life, a usage scholars note differs from Hochschild's original workplace-focused definition.

Criticism and research

Critics argue that the commodification of feeling can alienate workers from their own emotions, while other researchers emphasise that skilled emotional labor can be a source of professional pride and genuine connection. Organisational studies examine how display rules, autonomy, and supervisor support affect outcomes such as job satisfaction, turnover, and stress-related illness.
