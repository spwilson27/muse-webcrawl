Fining in metallurgy is the process of refining pig iron by oxidising and removing much of its carbon (and other impurities such as silicon and manganese), converting brittle cast iron into malleable wrought iron. The term is also applied more generally to the refining of metals by oxidation of impurities.

Finery forge

In the traditional finery forge, used in Europe from the late medieval period and central to the Walloon process, pig iron was remelted in a charcoal hearth under an air blast from bellows. The blast oxidised carbon from the molten metal, which was stirred and worked by the finer until it thickened into a pasty lump (a loup) as its melting point rose. The lump was then hammered under a trip hammer to expel slag and consolidate it into bar iron.

Later developments

Henry Cort's puddling furnace (patented 1784) achieved the same decarburisation in a coal-fired reverberatory furnace, greatly expanding output during the Industrial Revolution. Both processes were displaced in the later 19th century by the Bessemer converter and open-hearth furnace, which produced steel directly and on a far larger scale. The word "fining" survives in glassmaking (removal of bubbles, or "seeds", from molten glass) and in refining terminology for precious metals.
