Dorothy Mary Crowfoot Hodgkin (1910–1994) was a British chemist who pioneered the use of X-ray crystallography to determine the structures of complex biological molecules, winning the 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Her laboratory solved the structure of penicillin (1945), vitamin B12 (1956) — then the most complex molecule yet determined — and insulin (1969, after 34 years of work), results that transformed medicine and biochemistry and required some of the earliest uses of computers in chemistry.

Life and work

Hodgkin developed severe rheumatoid arthritis from her late twenties, continuing bench work with adapted equipment. At Oxford she taught Margaret Thatcher chemistry, and she later became a prominent campaigner for peace and for scientists' access across Cold War divides.

Method

X-ray crystallography infers atomic positions from diffraction patterns — mathematically demanding 'phase problem' work that Hodgkin's team advanced with Patterson methods and early electronic computers.
