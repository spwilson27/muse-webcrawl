Gibbs–Thomson effect

The Gibbs–Thomson effect (also called the Kelvin effect in this context) describes how the melting point of a small crystal decreases as its size decreases, because of the influence of surface energy on chemical potential.

Gibbs–Thomson effect
Field: Thermodynamics, materials science
Named after: J. Willard Gibbs, J. J. Thomson
Related: Kelvin equation, Ostwald ripening

Equation

The melting temperature Tm(r) of a crystal of radius r is lowered roughly in proportion to 1/r: ΔT ∝ γT∞ / (r ρ ΔHf), where γ is surface energy, ΔHf the latent heat of fusion, and ρ density. Equivalently, liquid in small pores freezes at lower temperatures.

Applications

The effect underlies thermoporometry (measuring pore sizes from freezing-point depression), explains frost heave and premelting, and is mathematically analogous to the vapour-pressure increase over curved droplets described by the Kelvin equation. It also drives Ostwald ripening, where larger particles grow at the expense of smaller ones.
