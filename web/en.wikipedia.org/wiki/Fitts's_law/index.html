Fitts's law

A one-dimensional pointing task: movement time depends on distance to the target and target width.

Fitts's law (often cited as Fitts' law) is a predictive model of human movement primarily used in human–computer interaction and ergonomics. This scientific law predicts that the time required to rapidly move to a target area is a function of the ratio between the distance to the target and the width of the target. Fitts's law is used to model the act of pointing, either by physically touching an object with a hand or finger, or virtually, by pointing to an object on a computer monitor using a pointing device.

It was proposed by American psychologist Paul Fitts in 1954.

Original formulation

Fitts defined an index of difficulty (ID) for a pointing task, measured in bits:

ID = log2(2D / W)

where D is the distance from the starting point to the centre of the target and W is the width of the target measured along the axis of motion. The movement time (MT) is then modelled as a linear function of the index of difficulty:

MT = a + b · ID

where a and b are empirical constants determined by the device and experimental conditions.

Shannon formulation

The variant most commonly used in human–computer interaction, proposed by Scott MacKenzie following Claude Shannon's information theory formulation, is:

ID = log2(D / W + 1)

This form avoids negative values of ID for very easy tasks and generally fits experimental data better. The reciprocal of the slope b is interpreted as an index of performance, in bits per second, characterising the information capacity of the human motor system for a given input device.

Applications

Fitts's law has wide implications for user interface design:

  • Frequently used controls should be large and placed close to the user's current pointer position.
  • Screen edges and corners are especially easy to acquire, because the pointer stops at the boundary, giving them effectively infinite width; this is why menus and taskbars are often placed at screen edges.
  • Pie menus and radial menus can be faster than linear menus because their items are equidistant from the centre.
  • Enlarging a target (for example, expanding menus or "bubble cursors") reduces acquisition time.

Extensions and criticisms

The law has been extended to two-dimensional targets, to motion through constrained paths (described by the steering law), and to other effectors such as eye gaze and touch input. Criticisms note that the law describes rapid, aimed movements best, and that accuracy requirements, target shape, and practice effects complicate its direct application. Nevertheless it remains one of the most robust and widely replicated results in experimental psychology and HCI.
