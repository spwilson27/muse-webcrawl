Dara Shikoh (20 March 1615 – 30 August 1659), also known as Dara Shukoh, was the eldest son and heir apparent of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan. A scholar-prince of deep mystical bent, he is remembered for his efforts to find common ground between Hinduism and Islam, above all his Persian translation of the Upanishads. He was defeated by his younger brother Aurangzeb in the war of succession of 1657–59, paraded through Delhi in disgrace, and executed on a charge of apostasy.

Early life and education

Dara was the eldest surviving son of Shah Jahan and Mumtaz Mahal. He was kept close to his father's court, given high rank and titles, but limited military command experience — a weakness that later told against him. Under the influence of the Qadiri Sufi saint Mian Mir and his successor Mullah Shah Badakhshi, Dara devoted himself to Sufi scholarship and to the study of other religions, corresponding with Hindu ascetics such as Baba Lal Bairagi.

Works

Dara wrote several works on Sufism, including the Safinat-ul-Auliya, a biographical dictionary of Sufi saints, and the Majma-ul-Bahrain ("The Confluence of the Two Oceans"), a comparative treatise arguing for the essential unity of Sufi and Vedantic thought. His most consequential project was the Sirr-e-Akbar ("The Greatest Secret", 1657), a Persian translation of fifty Upanishads made with the help of Banaras pandits; through a later Latin rendering it reached the philosopher Arthur Schopenhauer, who praised it highly.

War of succession and death

When Shah Jahan fell ill in 1657, Dara's brothers contested the throne. Aurangzeb defeated Dara at the Battle of Samugarh in May 1658 and again in subsequent engagements. Betrayed by an Afghan chieftain whose life he had once saved, Dara was captured, brought to Delhi, paraded through the streets on a filthy elephant, tried by a court of ulema, and beheaded on 30 August 1659. He was buried in an unmarked grave in Humayun's Tomb complex.

Legacy

Dara has become a symbol of a tolerant, pluralist Mughal tradition set against Aurangzeb's orthodoxy, and modern historians continue to debate how different Mughal India might have been had he succeeded.
