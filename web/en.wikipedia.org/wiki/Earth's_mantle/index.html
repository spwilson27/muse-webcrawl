Earth's mantle

The Earth's mantle is a layer of silicate rock between the crust and the outer core. It has a mass of 4.01 × 1024 kg and makes up 67% of the mass of Earth. It has a thickness of approximately 2,900 kilometres (1,800 mi), making up about 84% of Earth's volume. It is predominantly solid but, on geological time scales, behaves as a viscous fluid, sometimes described as having the consistency of caramel. Partial melting of the mantle at mid-ocean ridges produces oceanic crust, and partial melting at subduction zones produces continental crust.

Structure

The mantle is divided into the upper mantle, extending from the Moho discontinuity at the base of the crust down to about 410 km, a transition zone between roughly 410 and 660 km depth, and the lower mantle from 660 km to the core–mantle boundary at about 2,890 km. Temperature increases with depth from roughly 200 °C at the crust boundary to about 4,000 °C near the core. Although solid, the mantle's silicate material is ductile enough to convect very slowly, and this mantle convection drives plate tectonics.

Composition

The upper mantle is composed mainly of olivine, pyroxenes, and garnet — a rock type known as peridotite. With increasing pressure, minerals transform: olivine converts to wadsleyite and ringwoodite in the transition zone, and below 660 km to bridgmanite and ferropericlase, which make the lower mantle the largest single mineral reservoir in the Earth. Seismic discontinuities at 410 and 660 km depth mark these phase changes.

Exploration

No drill has ever reached the mantle; the deepest borehole, the Kola Superdeep Borehole, penetrated only the crust. Knowledge of the mantle comes from seismic waves, laboratory experiments at high pressure, xenoliths carried to the surface by volcanoes, and ophiolites — slabs of oceanic lithosphere thrust onto continents.
