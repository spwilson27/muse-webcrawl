Elasticity of substitution

[Hero image placeholder: isoquant curves]

The elasticity of substitution is a measure in economics of the flexibility with which one input (or good) can be substituted for another. Formally, it is the percentage change in the ratio of two inputs used (or goods consumed) divided by the percentage change in their relative prices (equivalently, the marginal rate of technical substitution), holding output (or utility) constant. It was introduced independently by John Hicks and Joan Robinson in 1932–1933.

Definition

For a production function with inputs capital K and labour L, the elasticity of substitution σ is:

σ = d ln(K/L) / d ln(MRTS)

where MRTS is the marginal rate of technical substitution. A higher σ means inputs can be exchanged more easily in response to price changes.

Special cases

  • σ = 0 — Leontief (fixed-proportions) production: inputs must be used in rigid proportions; isoquants are L-shaped.
  • σ = 1 — Cobb–Douglas production function.
  • σ → ∞ — perfect substitutes; isoquants are straight lines.
  • Between 0 and 1 inputs are gross complements in relative terms; above 1 they substitute readily.

The constant elasticity of substitution (CES) production function, developed by Arrow, Chenery, Minhas and Solow (1961), generalizes Cobb–Douglas by allowing σ to be an arbitrary constant, and is widely used in macroeconomics and growth theory.

Applications

The elasticity of substitution between capital and labour is a key parameter in debates about inequality and the labour share of income: if σ > 1, accumulation of capital tends to raise capital's share. It also governs substitution between goods in consumer theory, between energy and other inputs, and between domestic and imported goods in trade models (the Armington elasticity).
