The Party of Democratic Socialism (German: Partei des Demokratischen Sozialismus, PDS) was a democratic socialist political party in Germany active between 1989 and 2007. It was the legal successor to the Socialist Unity Party of Germany (SED), which ruled the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) as the sole governing party until 1990.

History

Origins

The PDS emerged from the SED during the peaceful revolution of 1989. At an extraordinary party congress in December 1989, the SED renamed itself SED-PDS, and on 4 February 1990 it became simply the PDS. Reformist lawyer Gregor Gysi became its chairman, and the party adopted a programme renouncing the SED's previous authoritarian rule and committing itself to democratic socialism.

In unified Germany

After German reunification in 1990, the PDS became a regional party largely confined to the eastern states (the former East Germany), where it regularly won strong support and participated in state governments, notably in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Berlin, in coalition with the Social Democratic Party (SPD). In the Bundestag it relied on directly elected constituency seats and, in 1998, cleared the five-percent threshold nationwide for the first time.

Merger into The Left

In 2005, the PDS formed an electoral alliance with the western-based Electoral Alternative for Labour and Social Justice (WASG), running jointly as The Left Party.PDS in the federal election of that year. On 16 June 2007 the two organisations formally merged to create the party The Left (Die Linke), bringing the PDS's independent existence to an end.

Ideology

The PDS described itself as a socialist party advocating a democratic socialist alternative to capitalism. Its support was concentrated among voters in the new federal states who felt disadvantaged by the process of reunification, as well as trade unionists, intellectuals, and former SED members and officials.

Election results

In the last free East German general election of March 1990, the PDS won 16.4 per cent of the vote. In reunified Germany its federal vote share ranged between about 4 and 5 per cent nationally in the 1990s, with considerably higher results — sometimes above 20 per cent — in the eastern states.
