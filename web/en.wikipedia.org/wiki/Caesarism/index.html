Caesarism

Caesarism is a political concept describing rule by a charismatic strongman who claims to embody the will of the people while concentrating power in his own hands, typically arising amid the breakdown of parliamentary or republican institutions. The term derives from Julius Caesar and became common in 19th-century European political debate.

Development of the idea

French and German writers applied "Caesarism" to Napoleon Bonaparte and especially to Napoleon III, whose regime Karl Marx dissected in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852), developing the related concept of Bonapartism. Max Weber later used Caesarism for plebiscitary leadership in mass democracies, while Antonio Gramsci distinguished "progressive" from "reactionary" Caesarism in his Prison Notebooks. Oswald Spengler predicted Caesarism as the final political form of declining civilizations.

Usage

In modern commentary the word is used loosely — sometimes pejoratively — for plebiscitary, personalist rule that bypasses institutions, and it overlaps with concepts such as populism, Bonapartism, and Peronism. Scholars caution that the term's meanings vary widely depending on the author's theory of the state.
