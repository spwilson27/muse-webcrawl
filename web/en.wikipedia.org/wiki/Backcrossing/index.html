Backcrossing

Backcrossing is a breeding method in which a hybrid organism is crossed with one of its parents (or with an individual genetically identical to a parent). It is a fundamental technique in plant and animal breeding and in genetics research, used to transfer a desirable trait from a "donor" line into the genetic background of an elite "recurrent" line, or to produce near-isogenic lines differing at a single locus.

Backcrossing
FieldGenetics, plant and animal breeding
PurposeIntrogression of a trait into a recurrent parent background
Typical generationsBC1, BC2, … (often 5–7 for near-isogenic lines)
Expected recurrent genome75% after BC1; 93.75% after BC3; → ~99% after BC6

Principle

An F1 hybrid carries on average 50% of each parent's genome. Crossing it back to the recurrent parent yields BC1 offspring averaging 75% recurrent genome; each further backcross halves the remaining donor fraction, so after six backcrosses the selected line is on average more than 99% recurrent parent, while selection retains the target gene from the donor. A segment of donor DNA flanking the target gene ("linkage drag") is carried along and may bring unwanted traits, a problem reduced by marker-assisted selection using DNA markers to choose offspring with minimal donor background.

Applications

Backcrossing is routinely used to move disease-resistance genes from wild relatives into crop cultivars, to move transgenes into commercial varieties, and in mouse genetics to transfer mutations onto standard inbred strain backgrounds ("congenic" strains). In conservation, deliberate backcrossing of hybrids to a threatened parent species is sometimes debated as a means of genetic rescue, though introgression can also threaten species integrity.
