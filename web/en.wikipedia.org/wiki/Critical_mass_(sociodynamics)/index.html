Critical mass (sociodynamics)

In social dynamics, critical mass is a sufficient number of adopters of a new idea, technology or innovation in a social system so that the rate of adoption becomes self-sustaining and creates further growth. The term is a metaphor borrowed from nuclear physics, where a critical mass of fissile material is needed to sustain a chain reaction.

Concepts

Critical mass is closely related to network effects and the diffusion of innovations described by Everett Rogers, whose S-shaped adoption curve accelerates once innovators and early adopters are joined by the early majority. Mark Granovetter's threshold models show how collective behaviour — riots, strikes, technology adoption — can cascade when enough individuals with low thresholds act, making the outcome sensitive to the distribution of thresholds in the population rather than to average preferences alone.

Examples

Examples include the fax machine and telephone, worthless alone but valuable once many people had them; social media platforms, which must reach enough users in a community to be self-sustaining; and collective action movements such as the Critical Mass bicycle rides, in which a sufficiently large group of cyclists can occupy road space safely. In gender and organizational research, "critical mass" has been applied to the proportion of women on boards or in legislatures needed to change group dynamics, often put near 30 percent.
