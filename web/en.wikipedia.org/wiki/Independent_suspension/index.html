Independent suspension

Independent suspension is a suspension system in which each wheel on the same axle is allowed to move vertically independently of the others. It contrasts with a beam axle (dependent) system, in which the wheels are linked so that movement of one affects the other. Independent suspension generally improves ride comfort and handling because bumps affecting one wheel are not transmitted across the vehicle, and unsprung mass can be lower.

Types

Common designs include the MacPherson strut, which combines the shock absorber and steering pivot in one unit and is widely used at the front of passenger cars; double wishbone suspension, which offers favourable control of wheel geometry; multi-link systems; and trailing-arm and swing-axle designs historically used at the rear, as in the original Volkswagen Beetle and early Chevrolet Corvair.

History

Independent front suspension appeared on production cars in the 1930s — notably on Mercedes-Benz and General Motors models — and became standard on most cars during the later 20th century. Fully independent suspension on all four wheels is now common, though solid axles persist on heavy trucks and off-road vehicles for strength and articulation.
