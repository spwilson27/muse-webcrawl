Euler–Mascheroni constant

Image: Euler–Mascheroni constant (placeholder)

The Euler–Mascheroni constant (also called Euler's constant) is a mathematical constant usually denoted by the lowercase Greek letter gamma (γ). It is defined as the limiting difference between the harmonic series and the natural logarithm:

γ = limn→∞ ( Σk=1n 1/k − ln n ) ≈ 0.57721 56649 01532 86060 …

History

The constant first appeared in work by Leonhard Euler in 1734; Lorenzo Mascheroni later computed it to many decimal places and popularised the symbol γ. It arises throughout analysis and number theory — in the study of the gamma function, the digamma function, the Riemann zeta function, and in results such as Mertens' theorems about the distribution of prime numbers.

Properties

Remarkably, it is not known whether γ is rational or irrational, nor whether it is transcendental. It has been computed to hundreds of billions of digits. Continued fraction and integral representations abound; for example, γ equals the negative of the derivative of the gamma function at 1, and it appears in the Laurent expansion of the zeta function around its pole at s = 1.
