John Nash (18 January 1752 – 13 May 1835) was an English architect of the Georgian and Regency eras, responsible for much of the layout of Regency London under the patronage of the Prince Regent (later George IV). He was a pioneer of the picturesque in architecture and urban planning, and his most famous works include the Royal Pavilion in Brighton, the redesign of Buckingham Palace, Marble Arch, Regent Street, and the terraces around Regent's Park.

Early career

Nash trained under Sir Robert Taylor, then worked as a builder and architect in Wales, where he designed country houses and picturesque cottages and rebuilt several cathedrals and gaols. Returning to London in the 1790s, he entered partnership with landscape designer Humphry Repton and became a fashionable architect of country houses in Gothic, Italianate and picturesque styles.

Work for the Prince Regent

From about 1811 Nash became the Prince Regent's favoured architect. His masterplan linked a new processional route—Regent Street—from Carlton House to the new Regent's Park, ringed by stucco terraces that created the impression of palaces. At Brighton he transformed the Marine Pavilion into the Royal Pavilion (1815–1822), an Indo-Saracenic fantasy with Chinese interiors. He also enlarged Buckingham House into Buckingham Palace, though cost overruns and the king's death in 1830 led to his dismissal and the completion of the palace by Edward Blore.

Legacy

Nash's London set-pieces— stucco terraces, sweeping crescents and the quadrant of Regent Street—defined the Regency townscape. His career ended in controversy over expenditure, and he retired to the Isle of Wight, where he died in 1835.
