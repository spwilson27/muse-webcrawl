The Duchy of Swabia (German: Herzogtum Schwaben) was one of the five stem duchies of the medieval German kingdom, arising in the early tenth century on the territory of the earlier Alamannia in what is now southwestern Germany (Baden-Württemberg and Bavarian Swabia), with Alsace and, for a time, much of German-speaking Switzerland. Its dukes were among the great princes of the Holy Roman Empire, and from 1079 the duchy was held—with interruptions—by the Hohenstaufen family, which also produced a line of German kings and emperors.

History

After the Alamannic dukedom was suppressed by the Carolingians in the eighth century, the region was ruled by counts and royal officials. In the disorder of the early tenth century, the Hunfriding count Burchard I was recognized as duke (917), and the duchy was definitively established under his successors; Burchard II helped Henry the Fowler secure the German kingship and married into the royal house. The duchy changed hands among several families—the Conradines, the Ottonian Liudolfings, and others—before Frederick of Büren received it in 1079 from Henry IV, founding the Hohenstaufen tenure. Under the Hohenstaufen, dukes of Swabia such as Frederick II "the One-Eyed" and his nephew Frederick Barbarossa (duke 1147–1152, then emperor) made the duchy the dynasty's power base.

Dissolution

With the execution of Conradin, the last Hohenstaufen, in 1268 and the Great Interregnum, ducal authority collapsed and was never effectively restored, despite a brief nominal revival (Rudolf II of Austria was invested with the title in 1289–90, and the title lingered in Habsburg usage until 1313). The duchy fragmented into hundreds of territories—imperial cities, counties such as Württemberg, margraviates such as Baden, bishoprics, and abbeys—one of the most politically splintered landscapes of the old Empire. The cultural-linguistic region of Swabia (Schwaben) and its Alemannic dialects persist today.
