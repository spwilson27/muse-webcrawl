Mu'tazila

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

The Mu'tazila (Arabic: المعتزلة, al-muʿtazila, "those who withdraw") was a school of Islamic theology (kalām) that flourished in Basra and Baghdad from the 8th to the 10th centuries CE. Renowned for its emphasis on reason and rational inquiry, the Mu'tazila held that religious truths must be consistent with human reason, and it played a central role in translating and engaging with Greek philosophy in the Islamic Golden Age.

Origins and the five principles

Tradition traces the school to Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ (d. 748), said to have "withdrawn" from the circle of Ḥasan al-Baṣrī over the question of whether a grave sinner remained a Muslim: the Mu'tazilite answer was that such a person occupied an "intermediate position" — neither believer nor unbeliever. The school's mature doctrine rested on five principles (al-uṣūl al-khamsa):

  • Divine unity (tawḥīd) — God is absolutely one; His attributes are not eternal entities distinct from His essence, and the Qur'an is therefore created rather than eternal.
  • Divine justice (ʿadl) — God does not will evil; humans have free will and are responsible for their acts.
  • The promise and the threat — God will reward the obedient and punish the unrepentant sinner.
  • The intermediate position — the grave sinner is neither Muslim nor infidel in this life.
  • Commanding right and forbidding wrong — an obligation to promote good and oppose evil.

Influence and decline

Under the caliph al-Ma'mūn (r. 813–833), Mu'tazilite doctrine became official state theology, and the caliph instituted the miḥna, an inquisition requiring scholars to affirm the createdness of the Qur'an; the scholar Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal famously resisted. Later caliphs abandoned the doctrine, and Ashʿarite theology, which tempered Mu'tazilite rationalism, became dominant in Sunni Islam, though Mu'tazilite ideas survived within Shia (especially Zaydi and Twelver) thought. Modern Muslim reformers have often drawn on the Mu'tazila as an indigenous precedent for rationalism.
