Pierre Henri Hugoniot, founder of the theory of shock waves.

Pierre Henri Hugoniot (5 June 1851 – 18 February 1887) was a French naval engineer and scientist who first gave a complete theory of the propagation of shock waves in gases, work that now underlies compressible flow, ballistics and detonation physics.

Career

Hugoniot entered the École Polytechnique in 1870, serving as an artillery officer during the Franco-Prussian War and its aftermath, and then joined the naval artillery corps. He became professor of mechanics and ballistics at the naval artillery school at Lorient. He died of illness at Nantes in 1887, aged only 35, shortly before the publication of his major memoir.

Shock relations

In papers of 1887–1889, published in the Journal de l'École Polytechnique, Hugoniot analysed the discontinuities in pressure, density and velocity across a shock front. Applying conservation of mass, momentum and energy to a moving discontinuity, he derived the relations now known as the Rankine–Hugoniot equations, after William Rankine, who had obtained related results in 1870.

The "Hugoniot curve" (or Hugoniot locus) gives all states reachable from a given initial state by a single shock; combined with the Rayleigh line it determines shock strength. Hugoniot also showed that a shock is necessarily accompanied by an increase of entropy, and studied the propagation of waves in gases in terms recognisably close to the modern method of characteristics.

Legacy

His name attaches to the Hugoniot elastic limit in solid mechanics and to shock-polar analysis in aerodynamics. His posthumous recognition grew with the development of high-speed flight, explosives research and astrophysical shock theory in the twentieth century.
