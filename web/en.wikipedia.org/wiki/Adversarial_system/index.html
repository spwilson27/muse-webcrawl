Adversarial system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The adversarial system is the legal system in which two advocates represent their parties' positions before an impartial person or group of people, usually a judge or jury, who attempt to determine the truth of the case. It is the system of common-law countries. Its premise is that truth emerges best from a contest between partisan presentations of evidence and argument, with the court acting as neutral umpire enforcing the rules of evidence and procedure.

Features include party control of investigation and proof, oral advocacy and cross-examination, exclusionary rules of evidence, and (in criminal cases) the presumption of innocence and proof beyond reasonable doubt. Critics argue that adversarial procedure favours wealthy parties, rewards forensic skill over truth, produces “trial by ambush”, and in criminal justice generates pressure for plea bargaining that bypasses trial altogether. Reforms in many common-law countries have shifted toward judicial case management, narrowing the pure adversarial model.

Contrast

The alternative is the inquisitorial system of civil-law countries, where judges actively investigate and question witnesses. In practice the systems converge: civil-law judges rely on party submissions, and common-law judges manage cases actively; but the allocation of initiative remains a real and consequential difference.
