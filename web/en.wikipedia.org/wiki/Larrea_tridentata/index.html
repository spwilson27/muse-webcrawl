Larrea tridentata

Larrea tridentata
Common names: creosote bush, greasewood, chaparral
Family: Zygophyllaceae
Range: deserts of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico

Larrea tridentata, called creosote bush and greasewood as a plant and chaparral as a medicinal herb, is a flowering evergreen shrub of the family Zygophyllaceae. It is one of the most characteristic and abundant plants of the deserts of North America, dominating large parts of the Mojave, Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico.

Description

The shrub typically grows 1–3 metres tall, with numerous slender, irregularly branching stems arising from a central crown. Its small, dark-green leaves consist of two leaflets joined at the base and are coated with a resinous varnish that reduces water loss and gives the plant its distinctive smell—especially pungent after rain, the "smell of desert rain". Bright yellow five-petalled flowers appear mainly after rains, followed by fuzzy white seed capsules.

Ecology

Creosote bush is extraordinarily drought tolerant: it can lose most of its leaves and enter dormancy, resume growth when water returns, and photosynthesize whenever conditions allow. Its resin contains the antioxidant nordihydroguaiaretic acid (NDGA), which deters herbivores and many competing seedlings. Stands of creosote often form remarkably even, widely spaced patterns across desert flats, reflecting competition for water.

The species reproduces both by seed and by clonal sprouting from the crown. As a clone grows outward and its centre dies, it forms an expanding ring. One such ring in the Mojave Desert, known as King Clone, is about 20 metres across and has been estimated at roughly 11,700 years old—making it one of the oldest known living organisms on Earth.

Uses

Indigenous peoples of the Southwest used creosote bush medicinally for a wide range of complaints, and the resin was used as a glue and sealant. Sold as the herbal supplement "chaparral", it was marketed in the twentieth century as a cancer cure and antioxidant, but reports of liver toxicity led to warnings by regulators, and its efficacy is unproven.
