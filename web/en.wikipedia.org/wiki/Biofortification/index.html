Biofortification

Hero image: Golden Rice, the best-known biofortified crop, whose grains are orange from beta-carotene.

Biofortification is the process of increasing the nutritional value of food crops through selective breeding, genetic engineering, or agronomic practices — enhancing the concentration and bioavailability of essential micronutrients such as iron, zinc, and vitamin A (as provitamin A carotenoids) in the edible portions of staple crops. It aims to combat "hidden hunger" — micronutrient malnutrition affecting an estimated two billion people — by reaching rural populations who have limited access to supplements or industrially fortified foods.

Methods

Conventional breeding exploits natural genetic variation in nutrient content within crop gene pools and is the basis of most released biofortified varieties. Genetic engineering can introduce nutrient pathways absent in the crop, as in Golden Rice, engineered to produce beta-carotene in the grain. Agronomic biofortification applies micronutrient fertilizers — for example zinc or selenium to soils — to enrich the harvested crop, though its effects are transient and must be repeated each season.

Major programs and crops

The HarvestPlus program, part of the CGIAR research network, has led the development and release of conventionally bred biofortified staples since 2003: vitamin A orange sweet potato in Mozambique and Uganda, iron beans in Rwanda, iron pearl millet in India, zinc rice in Bangladesh, and zinc wheat in Pakistan and India. Efficacy trials of orange sweet potato and iron beans demonstrated measurable improvements in children's vitamin A and iron status. Howarth Bouis, who championed the approach, shared the 2016 World Food Prize with colleagues from the International Potato Center for their work on biofortification.

Evaluation

Supporters emphasize biofortification's cost-effectiveness: once varieties are released, farmers can grow nutrient-dense food with no recurring intervention. Critics of genetically engineered examples, particularly Golden Rice, have raised regulatory, agronomic, and socioeconomic objections, and its deployment in the Philippines and elsewhere was delayed for two decades by controversy, despite broad scientific endorsement of its safety. Biofortification is generally presented as complementary to supplementation, industrial fortification, and dietary diversification rather than a replacement.
