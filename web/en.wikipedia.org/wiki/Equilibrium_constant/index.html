Equilibrium constant

The equilibrium constant of a chemical reaction is the value of its reaction quotient at chemical equilibrium, the state at which the forward and reverse reactions proceed at equal rates and the composition of the system no longer changes. It quantifies the position of equilibrium: a large value means the reaction lies far to the right (products favoured), a small value that it lies to the left.

Definition

For a general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the thermodynamic equilibrium constant is defined in terms of activities: K = (aCc aDd) / (aAa aBb). In practice, constants expressed in concentrations (Kc) or partial pressures (Kp) are widely used. The constant is related to the standard Gibbs free energy change by ΔG° = −RT ln K, and its temperature dependence is given by the van 't Hoff equation.

Types of equilibrium constants

  • Dissociation constants: acid dissociation constants Ka and base constants Kb.
  • Formation (stability) constants for metal–ligand complexes.
  • Solubility products Ksp for sparingly soluble salts.
  • Binding constants in biochemistry, whose reciprocals are dissociation constants.

History

The law of mass action was formulated by Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage in the 1860s; van 't Hoff connected the constant to thermodynamics in the 1880s. Equilibrium constants can be predicted from spectroscopic and molecular data via statistical thermodynamics, or measured spectrophotometrically, potentiometrically or by calorimetry.
