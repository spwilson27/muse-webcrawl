Artemisia tridentata, commonly known as big sagebrush, Great Basin sagebrush or sagebrush, is an aromatic shrub from the family Asteraceae. It grows in arid and semi-arid conditions throughout a range of cold desert, steppe, and mountain habitats in the Intermountain West of North America.

Big sagebrush and other Artemisia species are dominant over large areas of the Great Basin. It is a keystone species for wildlife, providing food and shelter for species such as the greater sage-grouse, pronghorn, and pygmy rabbit.

Description

Big sagebrush is a coarse, many-branched, pale-grey shrub growing 0.5–3 metres tall. The leaves are wedge-shaped, 1–3 cm long, with three (occasionally five) blunt teeth at the tip, covered in fine silvery hairs. The plant produces small yellow flowers in late summer and autumn. Its strong pungent odour, especially when wet, comes from camphor and other terpenoids.

Ecology

The species has a deep taproot together with spreading surface roots, allowing it to draw water from both deep and shallow soil. Sagebrush steppe ecosystems are threatened by altered fire regimes, overgrazing, and the spread of invasive annual grasses such as cheatgrass.

Uses

Indigenous peoples of North America used sagebrush as a medicinal plant for headaches, colds, and wounds, and as a ceremonial smudge. It is of low value as livestock forage in summer but can be an important winter browse for wild ungulates.
