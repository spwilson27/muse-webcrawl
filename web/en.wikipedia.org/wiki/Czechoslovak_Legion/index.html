The Czechoslovak Legion (Czech: Československé legie) was a volunteer armed force of Czechs and Slovaks formed during the First World War to fight alongside the Entente Powers against the Central Powers. Legions were raised principally in Russia, France and Italy from émigrés, prisoners of war and deserters from the Austro-Hungarian Army. Their military and political achievements helped persuade the Allies to recognise an independent Czechoslovak state in 1918.

Legionaries in Siberia, 1918.

Legion in Russia

The largest legion was formed in the Russian Empire, beginning with the Česká družina (Czech Company) in 1914. After the February Revolution of 1917, recruitment among Czech and Slovak prisoners of war expanded rapidly, and the force distinguished itself at the Battle of Zborov (July 1917). By 1918 the Legion in Russia numbered some 60,000 men organised as the Czechoslovak Army Corps.

Revolt and the Siberian anabasis

Following the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, the Legion sought evacuation to France via Vladivostok along the Trans-Siberian Railway. In May 1918, clashes with the Bolsheviks — the so-called Revolt of the Czechoslovak Legion — escalated into open warfare, and the Legion seized control of long stretches of the railway across Siberia. This intervention influenced Allied policy in the Russian Civil War. The Legion was finally evacuated from Vladivostok by ship in 1920, completing a famous journey sometimes called the "Siberian anabasis". On returning home, legionaries formed the backbone and officer corps of the new Czechoslovak Army.

Legions in France and Italy

In France, Czech and Slovak volunteers served first in the French Foreign Legion — the "Nazdar" Company fought at Arras in 1915 — and later in an autonomous Czechoslovak army formed in 1917–18. In Italy, a legion of some 20,000 men, largely former prisoners of war, fought on the Piave front in 1918.

Legacy

The Legion's story became a founding myth of the First Czechoslovak Republic, promoted by President Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, who had organised the legions politically abroad. Legionaries' literature, memorials and the Legion Bank (Legiobanka) in Prague kept the memory alive; under the communist regime the legionaries were persecuted and their role suppressed, before being rehabilitated after 1989.
