Sicilian Vespers

The Sicilian Vespers (Italian: Vespri siciliani) was a successful rebellion on the island of Sicily that broke out at Easter 1282 against the rule of Charles I of Anjou, the French-born king who had ruled the Kingdom of Sicily since 1266.

Outbreak

According to tradition, the revolt began at the Church of the Holy Spirit outside Palermo at the hour of vespers (evening prayer) on Easter Monday, when an incident involving a French soldier and a Sicilian woman sparked violence. Within weeks some 13,000 French men and women were slain, and the Sicilians offered the crown to Peter III of Aragon, whose wife Constance held the Hohenstaufen claim.

War and aftermath

The ensuing War of the Sicilian Vespers involved Aragon, France, Naples and the Papacy. By the Peace of Caltabellotta (1302), Sicily became a separate kingdom under a cadet Aragonese line, while Charles's heirs retained Naples on the mainland – a division that shaped southern Italy for centuries.

Legacy

The Vespers became a symbol of Sicilian resistance, celebrated in Verdi's opera Les vêpres siciliennes (1855) and invoked during Italian unification.
