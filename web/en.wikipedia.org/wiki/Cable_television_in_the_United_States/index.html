Cable television in the United States

[Image placeholder: A coaxial cable headend serving a small American town, 1970s]

Cable television first became available in the United States in 1948. By 1989, 53 million US households had subscribed to cable television, a figure that peaked at roughly 100 million subscribers in the early 2000s. In recent decades cable has lost subscribers to satellite television, telephone-company video services, and internet streaming — a trend known as cord-cutting.

Origins: community antenna television

Cable began as "community antenna television" (CATV): entrepreneurs in mountain towns of Pennsylvania, Arkansas and Oregon erected large antennas on hilltops in 1948 to capture distant broadcast signals and redistributed them by coaxial cable to households that could not receive over-the-air television. Through the 1950s and 1960s systems added distant independent stations, multiplying channel choice beyond the three broadcast networks.

Satellite distribution and the cable boom

In 1975 HBO became the first network to deliver programming by satellite, letting local cable systems nationwide carry the same feed; Ted Turner's WTCG (later TBS) followed as the first "superstation." Satellite distribution sparked a boom in basic cable networks — ESPN (1979), CNN (1980), MTV (1981) — and a franchising and consolidation wave that produced giant multiple-system operators such as Comcast, Charter (Spectrum), Cox and Time Warner Cable.

Regulation

Federal oversight rests principally with the Federal Communications Commission. Key legislation includes the Cable Communications Policy Act of 1984, which largely deregulated rates; the Cable Television Consumer Protection and Competition Act of 1992, which re-imposed rate regulation and introduced retransmission consent and must-carry rules; and the Telecommunications Act of 1996, which phased out most remaining rate regulation. Local governments issue franchises, while public, educational and governmental access channels are a common franchise condition.

Decline and convergence

US cable video subscriptions peaked around 2000–2005. Since then, satellite, fibre services and especially streaming platforms have drawn away tens of millions of households. The major cable operators have refocused on broadband internet — where cable's DOCSIS technology serves a majority of US fixed-broadband homes — bundling video as a secondary product.
