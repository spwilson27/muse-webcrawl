The QF 2-pounder naval gun, commonly called the pom-pom because of the sound of its firing, was a British 40 mm autocannon used as an anti-aircraft gun by the Royal Navy and other Commonwealth and Allied navies. The name came from the early version's resemblance to the Maxim gun's action and the sound it made. It is not to be confused with the Ordnance QF 2-pounder anti-tank gun used by the British Army.

Development and service

The gun was developed by Vickers from the Vickers 2-pounder of the First World War. The definitive Mark VIII was mounted in single, quadruple and — most famously — octuple mountings on cruisers, battleships, aircraft carriers and smaller vessels, providing dense short-range fire against dive-bombers and torpedo aircraft. The Mark VIII fired a 40×158mmR cartridge at about 115 rounds per minute per barrel and was fed by 14-round steel-link belts joined into longer lengths.

Early-war experience showed the pom-pom's limitations: its relatively low muzzle velocity and short effective range, and the lack of tracer and radar fire control in early mountings. It was progressively supplemented and replaced by the 20 mm Oerlikon and the superior Bofors 40 mm gun, but octuple pom-poms remained in service on major warships through the end of the Second World War and into the post-war period. Examples survive in museums, including aboard HMS Belfast in London.
