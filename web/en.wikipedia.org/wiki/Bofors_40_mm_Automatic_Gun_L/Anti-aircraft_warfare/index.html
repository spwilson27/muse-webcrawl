Anti-aircraft warfare

Anti-aircraft warfare is the counter to aerial warfare and includes all measures designed to nullify or reduce the effectiveness of hostile air action. It consists of surface-based air defence systems — guns, missiles and electronic systems operated from land or ships — and historically included barrage balloons, searchlights and observer networks. NATO defines anti-aircraft warfare (AAW) as "measures taken to defend a maritime force against attacks by airborne weapons launched from aircraft, ships, submarines and land-based sites."

History

The first anti-aircraft weapons were improvised during the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, when the French used balloons to communicate from besieged Paris and the Prussians fielded a modified Krupp gun to shoot at them. In the First World War, dedicated anti-aircraft artillery, time-fuzed shells and acoustic locators became standard, and the German 8.8 cm Flak guns of the Second World War were among the most feared weapons of that conflict. Radar-directed fire control and the proximity fuze greatly increased effectiveness late in that war — radar-directed guns with proximity-fuzed shells shot down large numbers of V-1 flying bombs in 1944.

After 1945, surface-to-air missiles (SAMs) progressively replaced heavy guns for medium- and high-altitude defence: systems such as the Soviet S-75 (which shot down Gary Powers' U-2 in 1960), the American Nike and Patriot families, and man-portable missiles (MANPADS) such as Stinger. Guns remain in use for short-range defence, including against drones, a role in which radar-guided cannon such as the German Gepard proved prominent in the war in Ukraine.
