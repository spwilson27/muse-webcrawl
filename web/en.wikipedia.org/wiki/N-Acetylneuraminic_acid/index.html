N-Acetylneuraminic acid

Chemical formula: C11H19NO9
Molar mass: 309.27 g/mol
Abbreviation: Neu5Ac, NANA
Class: Sialic acid (monosaccharide)

N-Acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac or NANA) is the predominant sialic acid found in human cells and many other mammals. Sialic acids are nine-carbon monosaccharides that typically terminate the carbohydrate chains of glycoproteins and glycolipids on cell surfaces, where they play important roles in cell–cell recognition and signalling.

Biological roles

Neu5Ac decorates the outer surfaces of cells and secreted proteins. It serves as a binding site for a range of pathogens and toxins: influenza viruses attach to host cells via sialic acid receptors, and the viral neuraminidase enzyme cleaves it to release progeny virions. The human immune system uses sialic acids as markers of "self", recognised by Siglec receptors.

Synthesis and metabolism

In humans, Neu5Ac is synthesised from UDP-N-acetylglucosamine through the action of the bifunctional enzyme GNE and related enzymes. Mutations in GNE cause hereditary inclusion body myopathy. Humans cannot synthesise the related sialic acid Neu5Gc owing to an inactivated CMAH gene, though small amounts of Neu5Gc are incorporated from dietary sources such as red meat.
