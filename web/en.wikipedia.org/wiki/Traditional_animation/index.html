Traditional animation

Traditional animation (also known as cel animation or hand-drawn animation) is a technique in which each frame of a film is drawn by hand. The process was the dominant form of animation in cinema and television throughout most of the 20th century. Drawings are made or photocopied onto transparent acetate sheets called cels, which are filled in with paints on the reverse side and photographed one frame at a time over a painted background using a rostrum camera.


Hand-drawn frames and cels in traditional animation

Process

Production begins with a script and storyboard, followed by a recorded soundtrack, an animatic, and character and layout design. Lead animators draw the key frames (key poses) of a scene; assistants and inbetweeners draw the frames between them. Drawings are tested in pencil form, then traced in ink onto cels, painted, and photographed in sequence over backgrounds, typically at 24 frames per second, with many scenes animated "on twos" (one drawing per two frames). Studios developed aids such as the multiplane camera, used by Walt Disney's studio for depth effects, and xerography, which transferred drawings to cels photographically.

History

The technique was pioneered in the 1910s by animators such as Winsor McCay (Gertie the Dinosaur, 1914) and industrialised by studios including Bray Productions and Disney. It produced landmark features from Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937) through the Disney Renaissance and the works of Studio Ghibli. From the 1990s, computer systems such as Disney's CAPS replaced physical cels with digital ink and paint, and 3D computer animation largely displaced the technique in feature films, though hand-drawn animation continues in television, anime and independent film.
