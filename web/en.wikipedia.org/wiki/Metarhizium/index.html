Metarhizium

Metarhizium is a genus of entomopathogenic fungi in the family Clavicipitaceae. Since the 1880s, when Ilya Mechnikov first experimented with Metarhizium anisopliae (formerly Entomophthora anisopliae), species in this genus have been studied and deployed as biological control agents against insect pests. The collective fungal cells of these species give infected insects a characteristic green coloration, and the diseases they cause are sometimes called "green muscardine disease".

Species and host range

The genus includes species such as M. anisopliae (a species complex of generalist insect pathogens), M. acridum (specialized on locusts and grasshoppers), M. robertsii, M. brunneum, M. flavoviride and M. majus. Collectively they infect hundreds of insect species. Many Metarhizium species also live as endophytes and rhizosphere symbionts of plants, promoting plant growth and transferring insect-derived nitrogen to their hosts.

Biological control

Metarhizium products are used against locusts and grasshoppers (e.g., Green Muscle, based on M. acridum, developed by the LUBILOSA programme for Africa), termites, spittlebugs on sugarcane, thrips, ticks and malaria-carrying mosquitoes. Conidia are applied as oil-based sprays; after landing on the insect cuticle they germinate, penetrate with appressoria and enzymes, proliferate in the hemocoel as blastospores, and produce toxins such as destruxins that kill the host, from whose cadaver new green conidia emerge.

Genetic engineering

Researchers have engineered M. pingshaense to express spider and scorpion toxins, dramatically accelerating mosquito mortality in semi-field trials in Burkina Faso — an approach studied for malaria control.
