Macromolecule

A macromolecule is a very large molecule important to biological processes, such as a protein or nucleic acid. Macromolecules are composed of thousands of covalently bonded atoms and typically have molecular masses of thousands to millions of daltons. The term was coined by Hermann Staudinger in the 1920s, whose proposal that materials like rubber and cellulose consisted of long covalent chains was initially controversial but ultimately won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1953.

Types

The four major classes of biological macromolecules are proteins (polymers of amino acids), nucleic acids — DNA and RNA (polymers of nucleotides), carbohydrates or polysaccharides (polymers of sugars), and lipids (though lipids are often not true polymers). Synthetic macromolecules include plastics such as polyethylene and nylon, and synthetic fibres and rubbers.

Properties

Macromolecules are usually assembled from repeating monomer units by polymerisation, and their function depends on three-dimensional structure as well as sequence. They are characterised by techniques including gel electrophoresis, size-exclusion chromatography, ultracentrifugation, and mass spectrometry.
