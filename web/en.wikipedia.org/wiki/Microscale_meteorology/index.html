Microscale meteorology

Microscale processes govern flow in the lowest part of the atmosphere

Microscale meteorology is the study of short-lived atmospheric phenomena smaller than the mesoscale, generally about 1 kilometre or less across and lasting from seconds to a few hours. It focuses on the processes in the atmospheric boundary layer — the lowest part of the troposphere, directly influenced by the Earth's surface — including turbulence, surface fluxes of heat and moisture, and flow around buildings, trees and terrain features.

Subject matter

Microscale phenomena include dust devils, small-scale turbulence and gusts, thermals, urban heat island circulations at street level, land and sea breeze fronts at fine resolution, frost pockets and cold-air drainage, and the dispersion of pollutants from stacks and traffic. Because turbulent transport at these scales cannot be resolved directly by weather models, it is represented with parameterisations informed by similarity theory, notably Monin–Obukhov similarity theory for the surface layer.

Methods

Researchers use instrumented towers with sonic anemometers and fast-response gas analysers (the eddy covariance technique), tethered balloons, small unmanned aircraft, wind tunnels, and large-eddy simulation models. Applications include wind energy siting, air quality forecasting, aviation safety, agriculture (frost protection and spraying), and urban planning for ventilation and pedestrian comfort.
