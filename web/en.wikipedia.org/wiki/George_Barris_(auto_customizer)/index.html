George Barris (auto customizer)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


George Barris beside one of his famous custom creations.

George Barris (November 20, 1925 – November 5, 2015) was an American designer and builder of many famous Hollywood custom cars, most notably the Hirohata Merc and the Batmobile from the 1966 Batman television series. Together with his brother Sam, he founded Barris Kustom Industries in Los Angeles, California, and became known as the self-proclaimed "King of the Kustomizers."

Early life

Barris was born in Chicago, Illinois, in 1925, the son of Greek immigrants. After the death of his parents, he and his brother Sam were sent to live with relatives in Roseville, California. The brothers developed an early interest in automobiles, building model cars and restoring a 1925 Buick that they received in exchange for restaurant work.

Career

After World War II, Barris moved to Los Angeles and opened a custom shop in Bell, later relocating to Lynwood. The brothers' early breakthrough was the Hirohata Merc, a radically customized 1951 Mercury built for Bob Hirohata, widely regarded as one of the most influential custom cars ever built. Barris Kustom cars appeared in numerous films and television programs, and the shop supplied vehicles to studios throughout the 1950s and 1960s.

The Batmobile

Barris is best remembered for the Batmobile created for the 1966 Batman TV series starring Adam West. Built in just a few weeks from the 1955 Lincoln Futura concept car, the car featured a bubble canopy, bat-themed styling cues, and a host of gadgets. The original car remained in Barris's collection for decades and sold at auction in 2013 for approximately $4.6 million.

Other notable creations

Barris's shop created or customized vehicles for productions including The Beverly Hillbillies truck, the Munster Koach and Drag-u-la for The Munsters, the cars of Knight Rider, and numerous vehicles for Elvis Presley and other celebrities. He was also involved in the design of the General Lee era of television car culture, though his claims regarding some creations have been disputed by historians.

Later life and legacy

Barris remained an active promoter of custom car culture until his death in Encino, California, in 2015, shortly before his 90th birthday. His work helped define the Southern California custom car scene and brought kustom culture into mainstream American popular culture.
