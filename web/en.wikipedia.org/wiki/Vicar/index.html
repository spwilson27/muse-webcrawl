Vicar

A vicar (from Latin vicarius, "substitute") is a representative, deputy, or substitute; anyone acting "in the person of" or agent for a superior. The term is used in several Christian churches for particular clerical offices, and in the Church of England it is the everyday title of a parish priest in charge of a parish.

A Church of England vicar outside a parish church.

Church of England

Historically, many English parish livings were appropriated to monasteries or other rectors, who collected the greater tithes while appointing a vicar to serve the parish in exchange for the lesser tithes. A parish priest who was himself the rector received all the tithes. After the upheavals of the Reformation and later reforms, the distinction lost its financial significance, and today "vicar" is the most common title for an Anglican incumbent; the residence is a vicarage. Since the late 20th century, vicars may also work in team ministries, and related titles include "priest in charge" and "team vicar".

Roman Catholic Church

In the Roman Catholic Church, a vicar is a deputy: the Pope is styled the Vicar of Christ; a vicar general is the principal deputy of a diocesan bishop; an episcopal vicar assists the bishop in a particular area; and a parochial vicar is an assistant priest in a parish (formerly called a curate in English-speaking countries).

Other uses

In the Episcopal Church in the United States, a vicar is the priest in charge of a mission congregation, as opposed to a rector of a self-supporting parish. In Lutheran churches the term may denote a trainee pastor serving an internship year.
