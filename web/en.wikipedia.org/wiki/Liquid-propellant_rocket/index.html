Liquid-propellant rocket

Liquid-propellant rocket

Propellants: Liquid fuel + liquid oxidizer (bipropellant), or monopropellant

First flight: 1926 (Robert H. Goddard)

Advantages: High specific impulse, throttleable, restartable

A liquid-propellant rocket or liquid rocket uses a rocket engine that employs liquid propellants. Liquids are desirable propellants because they have a reasonably high density and a high specific impulse (Isp). This allows the volume of the propellant tanks to be relatively low. It is also possible to use lightweight centrifugal turbopumps to pump the propellant from the tanks into the combustion chamber, which means the propellants can be kept under low pressure in light tanks.

Propellant combinations

Common bipropellant combinations include liquid oxygen (LOX) with kerosene (RP-1), LOX with liquid hydrogen, and storable hypergolic combinations such as nitrogen tetroxide with monomethylhydrazine (MMH) or unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine (UDMH). Monopropellants such as hydrazine are decomposed over a catalyst in small thrusters. The choice of propellant trades performance against storability, toxicity, and cost.

History

The first liquid-propellant rocket was launched by Robert H. Goddard on 16 March 1926 in Auburn, Massachusetts. Liquid rockets were developed extensively in Germany with the V-2, and later became the basis for most orbital launch vehicles, including the Saturn V, the Space Shuttle main engines, and modern vehicles such as Falcon 9.

Advantages and disadvantages

Compared with solid rockets, liquid rockets offer higher specific impulse, the ability to throttle, shut down, and restart, but at the cost of greater mechanical complexity, and in the case of cryogenic propellants, difficult storage and handling.
