Sledding

Sledding is a winter activity in which participants ride a sled, sledge or toboggan down a snow-covered slope, typically for recreation. It is one of the most widespread winter pastimes in cold climates, enjoyed informally on hills, parks and even streets after snowstorms. Related competitive sports include luge, bobsleigh and skeleton, as well as the North American toboggan championships.

Equipment

Traditional sleds include the wooden toboggan (derived from Indigenous North American designs, a flat board with a curled front), runner sleds such as the Flexible Flyer with steel runners and a steerable front axle, and the simple wooden sledge common in Europe. Modern recreational sleds are usually moulded plastic — including disc or saucer sleds, foam sliders and inflatable snow tubes. Very steep or dangerous hills are sometimes closed by authorities, and riders traditionally steer with their feet or by shifting weight, braking by dragging heels in the snow.

History

Sledges are among humanity's oldest transport technologies, used for hauling loads over snow and ice long before the wheel was practical in northern regions. Recreational sledding grew popular in the 19th century; organised toboggan runs were built at resorts such as St. Moritz, Switzerland — where the Cresta Run (1884) remains famous — and in North America, where elaborate toboggan chutes were erected in cities such as Montreal and Chicago. The U.S. National Toboggan Championships have been held in Camden, Maine since 1991, using a wooden chute originally built in the 1930s.

Safety

Sledding injuries — from collisions with trees, fences and vehicles, and from falls — send thousands of people, mostly children, to emergency departments each winter in the United States alone. Safety advice includes choosing slopes free of obstacles and roads, sledding feet-first or sitting up rather than head-first, wearing helmets, and supervising children.
