Mariachi

OriginWestern Mexico, especially Jalisco
Typical instrumentsViolins, trumpets, Spanish guitar, vihuela, guitarrón, harp
RecognitionUNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, 2011

A mariachi band performing in traditional charro dress.

Mariachi is a genre of Mexican folk music and the ensemble that performs it, originating in western Mexico, particularly the state of Jalisco, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Mariachi groups perform at celebrations, serenades, weddings, funerals and public festivals, and the music has become one of the most recognisable symbols of Mexican culture worldwide.

Instrumentation and dress

A full mariachi typically includes violins, two trumpets, a Spanish guitar, a high-pitched five-string vihuela, and the deep-bodied acoustic bass guitarrón, which together form the rhythmic foundation; some regional groups retain the harp. Musicians traditionally wear the ornate embroidered traje de charro — fitted suits with silver buttons, wide bow ties and broad-brimmed sombreros.

Repertoire and history

The repertoire spans sones, rancheras, corridos, boleros, polkas and huapangos, including standards such as "La Bamba", "El Son de la Negra" and "Cielito Lindo". Originally a rural string tradition, mariachi was urbanised in the early twentieth century as groups migrated to Mexico City, and it was popularised nationally through radio, cinema and the recordings and films of singers such as Jorge Negrete and Pedro Infante. Plaza Garibaldi in Mexico City remains a famous gathering place for mariachi musicians.

Recognition

In 2011 UNESCO inscribed mariachi on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, citing its role in transmitting history, language and cultural values. The tradition is also deeply rooted in the Mexican-American communities of the US Southwest, where school and university mariachi programmes are common.
