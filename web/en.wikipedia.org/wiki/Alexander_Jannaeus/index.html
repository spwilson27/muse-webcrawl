Alexander Jannaeus (c. 127 – 76 BCE) was the second king of the Hasmonean dynasty, reigning over an expanding Judean kingdom from 103 to 76 BCE. The third son of John Hyrcanus, he succeeded his brother Aristobulus I as both king and high priest, and under his rule the Hasmonean state reached its greatest territorial extent.

Reign

Released from prison by his brother's widow Salome Alexandra, whom he then married in accordance with levirate custom, Jannaeus pursued an aggressive policy of conquest. He campaigned in the coastal plain, Transjordan and the south, besieging Acre (Ptolemais) and Gaza; where his armies succeeded, populations were in many cases forcibly converted to Judaism, continuing the policy begun by his father.

Civil war

Jannaeus favoured the Sadducees and clashed bitterly with the Pharisees. At a feast he was publicly insulted over a ritual dispute concerning the water libation during Sukkot, and the crowd pelted him with citrons. The conflict escalated into a full-scale civil war lasting about six years, in which, according to Josephus, some 50,000 Jews died. The rebels invited the Seleucid king Demetrius III to intervene; though Demetrius defeated Jannaeus near Shechem, most of his Jewish allies then deserted him. The war ended with Jannaeus besieging the remaining rebels at Bethoma, bringing them to Jerusalem and — again according to Josephus — crucifying some 800 of them while feasting with his concubines as their families were slaughtered before their eyes.

Later years and death

Jannaeus continued campaigning in Transjordan and fell ill of fever, aggravated, Josephus says, by heavy drinking. He died in 76 BCE while besieging the fortress of Ragaba. On his deathbed he reportedly advised Salome Alexandra to make peace with the Pharisees, and she succeeded him, reversing his religious policy.

Coinage and sources

Jannaeus issued the most abundant coinage of any Hasmonean ruler, bearing bilingual Greek and Hebrew (or Aramaic) inscriptions with anchor, star, wheel and lily designs. The principal literary source for his reign is Josephus's Jewish War and Antiquities of the Jews; he is also mentioned in the Dead Sea Scrolls, where the Pesher Nahum's "Lion of Wrath" is widely identified with him.
