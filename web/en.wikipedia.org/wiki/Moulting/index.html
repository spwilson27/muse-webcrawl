Moulting

A cicada moulting (ecdysis)

In biology, moulting (or molting), also known as sloughing, shedding, or for many invertebrates ecdysis, is the manner in which an animal routinely casts off a part of its body — often an outer layer or covering — either at specific times of the year or at points in its life cycle.

In different animals

Birds periodically replace their feathers, usually once a year, shedding old feathers as new ones grow; some species, such as penguins, undergo a "catastrophic moult", replacing all feathers at once while fasting ashore. Mammals such as deer and many northern species shed and regrow their coats seasonally. Reptiles shed their skin — snakes often in a single piece — and amphibians shed and commonly eat theirs. Arthropods, including insects, spiders and crustaceans, must moult their rigid exoskeleton in order to grow, a process controlled by the hormone ecdysone; the stages between moults are called instars.

Process

In ecdysis, the old cuticle separates from the underlying epidermis (apolysis), a new cuticle is secreted beneath it, and the old covering is split and cast off. The animal is typically soft-bodied and vulnerable until the new covering hardens.
