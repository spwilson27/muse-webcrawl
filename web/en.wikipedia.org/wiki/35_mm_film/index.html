35 mm film is a film gauge used in filmmaking and still photography. It is a film stock with a width of 34.98 mm (1.377 in), originally created by William Kennedy Dickson and Thomas Edison using film stock supplied by George Eastman. The 35 mm width, with four perforations per frame along both edges, became the dominant motion-picture format for over a century and, in its still-photography form (135 film), the most popular photographic format of the twentieth century.

History

Dickson, working in Edison's laboratory, cut 70 mm Eastman roll film in half to produce the 35 mm width around 1892, adding the sprocket holes that allowed a camera mechanism to advance the film precisely. Edison's patents on the format were litigated for years. Oskar Barnack at Leitz later turned the movie gauge sideways for a compact still camera, the Leica (1925), doubling the motion-picture frame to 24 × 36 mm and establishing the "full-frame" still format. Kodak's 135 cartridge (1934) made the format universal for amateurs and professionals alike.

Motion pictures

In cinema, the standard 35 mm frame runs vertically through the camera with four perforations per frame ("4-perf"). Academy ratio (1.37:1) was standardised in 1932; anamorphic CinemaScope and Super 35 variants followed. Although digital projection has displaced it in most theatres, 35 mm prints remain in repertory use and some directors continue to shoot on the gauge.

Still photography

135 film is supplied in cartridges for 12, 24 or 36 exposures of 36 × 24 mm. Half-frame cameras used 18 × 24 mm. The format's lens ecosystem, and the equivalence of digital "full-frame" sensors to its frame size, keep 35 mm terminology central to photography.
