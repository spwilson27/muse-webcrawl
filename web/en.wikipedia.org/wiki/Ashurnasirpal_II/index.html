Ashurnasirpal II (Akkadian: Aššur-nāṣir-apli, "Ashur is guardian of the heir") was king of Assyria from 883 to 859 BC. He succeeded his father, Tukulti-Ninurta II, and is regarded as the founder of the Neo-Assyrian Empire in its fully developed form: through relentless campaigning he restored Assyrian control over northern Mesopotamia and built a new imperial capital at Kalhu.

Campaigns

Ashurnasirpal's annals describe annual campaigns to the west, reaching the Mediterranean and extracting tribute from Phoenician cities, and to the north and east against the Nairi lands and Urartu's neighbours. His inscriptions boast, in graphic detail, of exemplary cruelty toward rebels — flaying, impalement and mass deportation — intended to terrorise subjects into submission, a signature of Assyrian imperial propaganda.

Kalhu and the Northwest Palace

In 879 BC Ashurnasirpal moved the capital from Ashur to Kalhu (modern Nimrud), which he rebuilt on a grand scale. His Northwest Palace was decorated with carved stone reliefs of the king in war, hunting and ritual, flanked by colossal human-headed winged bulls (lamassu). He celebrated the inauguration with a ten-day banquet for nearly 70,000 guests, recorded on the Banquet Stele. The reliefs, excavated by Austen Henry Layard in the 1840s, are now divided among museums including the British Museum and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Legacy

Ashurnasirpal was succeeded by his son Shalmaneser III. His reign set the template — administrative, military and artistic — for the Assyrian empire that would dominate the Near East for two centuries.
