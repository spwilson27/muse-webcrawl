Freedmen's Town

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freedmen's Town is a historic African American community in the Fourth Ward of Houston, Texas. Founded in 1865 by formerly enslaved people who settled on the southern bank of Buffalo Bayou after emancipation, it became the centre of Black economic, religious, and cultural life in Houston — home to churches, schools, businesses, and professionals — and is regarded as the city's most important African American heritage district.

History

After the Emancipation Proclamation was enforced in Texas on June 19, 1865 (Juneteenth), hundreds of freed men and women migrated to Houston and purchased small lots — often through the assistance of ministers such as the Reverend Jack Yates — building a self-sufficient town of some 400 families by 1870. Residents paved streets with bricks they made and bought themselves, and founded institutions that survive today, including Antioch Missionary Baptist Church (1866) and the Gregory School (1876), the first school for Black children in Houston, now the African American Library at the Gregory School.

Decline and preservation

In the twentieth century the community was eroded by discriminatory planning: the city sited public housing and offices in the ward, Interstate 45 cut through the neighbourhood in the 1950s, and from the 1990s gentrification and townhouse development in the Midtown area displaced residents and demolished most historic housing. Only a fraction of the original housing stock survives. Freedmen's Town was designated a historic district and listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1985, and preservation campaigns have fought to protect the remaining brick streets — hand-laid by residents in the early 1900s — and landmark churches. In 2021 the community gained wider attention when proposed infrastructure projects threatened the historic brick streets, prompting renewed preservation efforts by the Houston Freedmen's Town Conservancy.
