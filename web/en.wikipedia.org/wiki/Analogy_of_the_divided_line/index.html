Analogy of the divided line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The analogy of the divided line is a thought experiment presented by the Greek philosopher Plato in Book VI of the Republic. Socrates asks his interlocutor to imagine a line divided into two unequal parts, representing the visible world and the intelligible world, and then to subdivide each part in the same proportion, yielding four segments corresponding to four states of mind and their objects.

The four segments

  • Eikasia (imagination or illusion): images, shadows, and reflections of visible things.
  • Pistis (belief): the visible things themselves — animals, plants, and artefacts.
  • Dianoia (thought): mathematical and hypothetical reasoning, which uses visible diagrams as images of intelligible objects.
  • Noesis (understanding): dialectical knowledge of the Forms themselves, culminating in the Form of the Good.

Interpretation

The line maps degrees of clarity and truth: as one ascends, both the objects known and the cognition of them become more real and more exact. Together with the allegory of the cave and the analogy of the Sun, which flank it in the Republic, the divided line is central to Plato's epistemology and metaphysics, and its proportional structure — in which the middle segments are equal in length — has been debated by commentators since antiquity.
