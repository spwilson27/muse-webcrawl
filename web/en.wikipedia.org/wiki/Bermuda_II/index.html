Bermuda II was a bilateral air services agreement between the governments of the United Kingdom and the United States, signed on 23 July 1977. It regulated scheduled air services between the two countries for more than three decades, replacing the original Bermuda Agreement of 1946, and was itself superseded by the EU–US Open Skies agreement, which took effect in 2008.

Background

The 1946 Bermuda Agreement had been the model for bilateral air service agreements worldwide, balancing market access with government oversight of capacity and fares. By the 1970s the United Kingdom had become dissatisfied with the arrangement, arguing that American carriers enjoyed a disproportionate share of the lucrative transatlantic market, particularly at London Heathrow. In June 1976 the British government gave notice of denunciation of the 1946 agreement, triggering a year of renegotiation.

Provisions

Bermuda II was considerably more restrictive and protectionist than its predecessor. It limited the number of designated carriers on each route, controlled capacity, and required government approval of fares. Access to Heathrow was confined to two British carriers (British Airways and, until its demise in 1991, British Caledonian, whose rights passed to Virgin Atlantic) and two American carriers (Pan Am and TWA, whose Heathrow rights were sold to American Airlines and United Airlines in 1991). Other services were directed to Gatwick or Stansted.

Effects and replacement

The agreement's restrictions on Heathrow access became a persistent source of friction and a major obstacle in wider aviation liberalisation talks. Following the United Kingdom's membership of the European Union, the European Court of Justice ruled national bilateral agreements incompatible with EU law, paving the way for the EU–US Open Skies agreement signed in 2007. When it came into force on 30 March 2008, Bermuda II ceased to apply, and Heathrow was opened to additional American and European carriers.
