Chroma key

Chroma key compositing, commonly known as green screen or blue screen, is a visual-effects technique for layering two or more images or video streams together. A subject is filmed against a uniformly coloured background, and that colour is then made transparent in post-production (or live), allowing the subject to be placed over a different background.

Technique

The process works by identifying pixels within a selected colour range — the "key" — and replacing them with another image. Green is the most common colour in digital production because image sensors are most sensitive to green, clothing rarely contains it, and it is far from human skin tones. Blue was standard in the film era, when blue-sensitive emulsion layers and optical compositing favoured it; blue screens are still preferred for blond hair, dark scenes, and subjects wearing green.

Successful keying requires even lighting of the screen to avoid shadows and wrinkles, sufficient separation between subject and screen to prevent colour spill, and avoidance of key-coloured wardrobe or props, which would become transparent along with the background.

History

Precursors of the technique include travelling mattes and the sodium vapour process used at Disney in films such as Mary Poppins (1964). Petro Vlahos developed refined colour-difference keying for motion pictures, earning an Academy Award; his Ultimatte system became an industry standard. Digital keying in software such as After Effects, Nuke, and real-time production tools has since made the technique ubiquitous.

Uses

Chroma key is used in film and television for weather forecasts, news virtual sets, music videos, and large-scale effects shots, and increasingly in live streaming and video conferencing, where presenters composite themselves over gameplay or presentation graphics.
