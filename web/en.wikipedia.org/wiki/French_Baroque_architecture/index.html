French Baroque architecture, sometimes called French classicism, was the style that dominated French building during the reigns of Louis XIII (1610–1643), Louis XIV (1643–1715) and Louis XV (1715–1774). It combined elements of the Italian Baroque with French classical restraint, expressing the power and grandeur of the Bourbon monarchy. Its supreme achievements are the Palace of Versailles and the royal squares and churches of Paris.

Early Baroque (c. 1600–1643)

Under Marie de' Medici and Louis XIII, architects such as Salomon de Brosse — designer of the Luxembourg Palace in Paris (begun 1615), modelled on Florence's Palazzo Pitti — introduced rusticated masonry, steep slate roofs and pavilioned plans that fused Italian palazzo forms with French château traditions. Jacques Lemercier built the Sorbonne church and, for Cardinal Richelieu, the Palais Cardinal and the planned town of Richelieu. François Mansart perfected an elegant, disciplined classicism in works such as the Château de Maisons (1642) and gave his name to the mansard roof.

The age of Louis XIV

After 1661 Louis XIV's minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert centralised artistic production under the Crown. Louis Le Vau, Charles Le Brun and André Le Nôtre collaborated on the château of Vaux-le-Vicomte (1656–1661) for finance minister Nicolas Fouquet — a synthesis of architecture, painting and landscape that the king then commandeered, team and all, for the transformation of Versailles. Le Vau enlarged Versailles from 1668; Jules Hardouin-Mansart added the Hall of Mirrors (1678–1684) and the vast north and south wings, making it the largest palace in Europe. In Paris the period produced the east facade of the Louvre (the Colonnade, designed 1667 by a committee including Claude Perrault), the dome of Les Invalides (Hardouin-Mansart, 1677–1706), and the royal squares Place des Victoires and Place Vendôme.

Late Baroque and the Régence

In the early eighteenth century the style grew lighter and more intimate, anticipating the Rococo: smaller rooms, curving forms and refined ornament appear in Parisian hôtels particuliers by architects such as Pierre Bullet and Germain Boffrand. Planning achievements continued with the Place de la Concorde (begun 1755, Ange-Jacques Gabriel) and Gabriel's Petit Trianon at Versailles (1762–1768), which points toward Neoclassicism.

Characteristics

French Baroque buildings favour symmetry, axial planning, classical orders used with relative sobriety, rustication, steep pavilioned roofs, and the integration of architecture with formal gardens — the jardin à la française of Le Nôtre, with its parterres, canals and radiating avenues. The style was widely imitated by courts across Europe.
