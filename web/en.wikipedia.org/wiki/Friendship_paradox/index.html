Friendship paradox

In most networks, a typical person's friends have more friends than the person does.

The friendship paradox is the phenomenon, first formally stated by sociologist Scott L. Feld in 1991, that on average, an individual's friends have more friends than the individual does. The result is counterintuitive but follows from a statistical sampling bias: people with many friends appear in many other people's friend lists, so a random person's friends are disproportionately drawn from the well-connected.

Mathematical explanation

In a social network, the average degree (number of friends) of a randomly chosen person is the mean of all degrees. The average degree of a randomly chosen friend, however, is weighted by degree: each person of degree d contributes d times to the count of friends, so the average becomes the mean of the squares divided by the mean. By Cauchy's inequality this is always greater than or equal to the mean, with equality only when every person has the same number of friends. The gap is largest when friendship counts vary widely.

Applications and generalisations

The paradox generalises to other attributes: your friends are likely to be more active, happier or wealthier than you if those traits correlate with having more friends. It has practical uses in epidemiology and public health: vaccinating the friends of randomly chosen people reaches more-connected individuals and slows outbreaks more effectively than vaccinating random individuals — a strategy called acquaintance immunisation. The paradox also underlies much social-media envy: feeds are dominated by the most active and popular users, distorting perceptions of ordinary life.
