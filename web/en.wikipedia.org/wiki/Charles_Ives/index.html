Charles Edward Ives (20 October 1874 – 19 May 1954) was an American modernist composer, one of the first American composers of international renown. His music was largely ignored during his early career, and most of his major works were performed only decades after they were written. Ives combined the American popular and church-music traditions of his youth with radical innovations — polytonality, polyrhythm, tone clusters and quarter tones — anticipating techniques later associated with European modernism.

Early life and double career

Ives was born in Danbury, Connecticut, the son of George Ives, a U.S. Army bandleader in the Civil War who encouraged his son's experiments with simultaneous keys and marching bands playing different tunes. Charles became a church organist as a teenager and studied composition at Yale under Horatio Parker. Concluding that he could not support a family by experimental music, Ives went into the insurance business, co-founding the agency Ives & Myrick in 1907 and pioneering concepts of estate planning — while composing prolifically in his spare time.

Major works

His output includes four symphonies (the Fourth, 1910–16, requires multiple conductors), the orchestral sets Three Places in New England and Holidays Symphony, the tone poem The Unanswered Question (1908, revised 1930s), the Central Park in the Dark, and the Piano Sonata No. 2, "Concord, Mass., 1840–60" (1920) — with movements named for Emerson, Hawthorne, the Alcotts and Thoreau — published at his own expense together with his prose Essays Before a Sonata. He also wrote some 200 songs drawing on hymns, parlor songs and patriotic tunes.

Recognition

After a heart attack in 1918 Ives composed little. Recognition came late: John Kirkpatrick championed the Concord Sonata from 1939, Lou Harrison conducted the premiere of the Third Symphony in 1946 (it won the Pulitzer Prize for Music in 1947), and Leonard Bernstein conducted the premiere of the Second Symphony in 1951, fifty years after its composition. Ives died in New York in 1954. Stravinsky later praised him as a pioneer who had explored the future "with the naïveté of a child and the power of a genius".
