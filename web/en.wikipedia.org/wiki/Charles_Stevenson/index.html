Charles Leslie Stevenson (June 27, 1908 – March 14, 1979) was an American analytic philosopher best known for his work in ethics and aesthetics. He was a leading exponent of emotivism, the theory that moral judgments do not state facts but express attitudes and seek to influence the attitudes of others.

Education and career

Stevenson studied at Yale University (B.A. 1930) and Harvard University, where he earned his Ph.D. in 1935, and also studied at Cambridge, where he was influenced by G. E. Moore, C. D. Broad, and I. A. Richards. He taught at Yale before moving to the University of Michigan in 1946, where he remained until 1977. His most influential book, Ethics and Language (1944), was awarded the Prix de l'Institut de France attention in philosophical circles and became a standard text in Anglo-American metaethics.

Emotivism and emotive meaning

Stevenson distinguished the descriptive meaning of a statement from its emotive meaning — the tendency of a word to evoke attitudes. Ethical disputes, he argued, involve both disagreement in belief and disagreement in attitude; purely verbal disputes can arise when parties attach different emotive meanings to the same term. His account of persuasive definitions — definitions that preserve a term's emotive meaning while altering its descriptive content in order to redirect attitudes — became a standard tool in philosophy and rhetoric.

In Facts and Values (1963), a collection of essays, Stevenson extended his analysis to aesthetics and the philosophy of science, defending a form of non-cognitivism while insisting that reasons remain relevant to moral judgment.

Selected works

Ethics and Language (1944); "The Emotive Meaning of Ethical Terms" (Mind, 1937); Facts and Values: Studies in Ethical Analysis (1963); papers on interpretation and evaluation in aesthetics.
