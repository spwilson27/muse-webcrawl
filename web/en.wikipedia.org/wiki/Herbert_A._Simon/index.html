Herbert A. Simon

Herbert Alexander Simon (15 June 1916 – 9 February 2001) was an American political scientist and scholar whose work spanned economics, cognitive psychology, computer science and artificial intelligence. He is best known for the theory of bounded rationality — the idea that decision makers, limited in information and cognitive capacity, "satisfice" rather than optimise — for which he received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1978. With Allen Newell he founded much of artificial intelligence, receiving the Turing Award in 1975.

Life and career

Simon was born in Milwaukee to a German immigrant father, an electrical engineer, and an accomplished pianist mother. He studied at the University of Chicago (BA 1936, PhD in political science 1943), worked on municipal administration research, and in 1949 joined the Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon University) in Pittsburgh, where he remained for the rest of his career, helping to found its business school and later its psychology and computer science programmes.

Decision making and organisations

His dissertation, published as Administrative Behavior (1947), attacked the classical "principles of administration" as mere proverbs and recast organisations as systems of decision making. In papers of the 1950s Simon formalised bounded rationality: because humans cannot enumerate all alternatives or consequences, they search until they find an option that is good enough — satisficing. The theory reshaped economics, organisation theory, and later behavioural economics.

Artificial intelligence and cognition

At RAND and Carnegie Tech, Simon and Allen Newell (with Cliff Shaw) built the Logic Theorist (1956), which proved theorems from Principia Mathematica, and the General Problem Solver (1957), founding the physical symbol system approach to AI and cognitive simulation. Simon predicted in 1965 that "machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work a man can do". His later work The Sciences of the Artificial (1969) developed a general theory of design, and his research on expertise — including chess — established the role of chunked pattern recognition in expert intuition.
