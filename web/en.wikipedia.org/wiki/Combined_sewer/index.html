Combined sewer

A combined sewer is a type of gravity sewer with a system of pipes, tunnels, and other infrastructure that carries both sewage and stormwater together to a sewage treatment plant or disposal site. Modern separate sewer systems, by contrast, use one pipe network for sanitary sewage and a distinct network for stormwater runoff.

History

Combined systems were standard in the 19th and early 20th centuries, when cities such as London, Paris, and New York built vast networks of brick sewers — London's system, designed by Joseph Bazalgette after the Great Stink of 1858, is a famous example. Most older cities in Europe and the northeastern and Great Lakes United States retain combined sewers in their historic cores.

Combined sewer overflows

During heavy rain or snowmelt, the volume of water in a combined system can exceed the capacity of the treatment plant. The excess mixture of stormwater and untreated sewage is then discharged directly into rivers, lakes, or harbors at designed overflow points — events known as combined sewer overflows (CSOs). CSOs are a significant source of water pollution, carrying pathogens, debris, and chemicals into receiving waters.

Mitigation

Cities address CSOs through storage tunnels that hold storm flows for later treatment (such as Chicago's Tunnel and Reservoir Plan, or "Deep Tunnel"), sewer separation projects, real-time control of gates and pumps, and green infrastructure — permeable pavement, rain gardens, and green roofs — that keeps stormwater out of the sewer in the first place. In the United States, the Environmental Protection Agency's CSO Control Policy of 1994 requires affected cities to implement long-term control plans.
