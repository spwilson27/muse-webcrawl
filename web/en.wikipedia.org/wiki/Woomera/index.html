A woomera is an Australian Aboriginal wooden spear-throwing device. Similar to an atlatl, it serves as a lever that greatly increases the velocity and range of a spear. The name woomera comes from the Dharug language of the Eora people of the Sydney region, and has been widely adopted in English and anthropological literature for spear-throwers across Aboriginal Australia, though each language group has its own names for the implement.

Description

The woomera is typically a flat, elongated piece of hardwood, often mulga, between 60 and 90 cm long, with a cup or shallow trough along its length. At one end is a hook (spur) of wood, bone, or a stone flake set in resin, against which the butt of the spear rests; the other end is held in the hand. Many examples have a flaked stone adze (tula) mounted in resin at the handle end.

Use

To throw, the user holds the woomera with the spear lying along its groove and butt seated on the hook. The throw is made with an overarm motion; because the woomera effectively lengthens the throwing arm, it multiplies the leverage and can propel a spear at speeds well over 100 km/h with ranges exceeding 100 metres. Skilled hunters used it for kangaroo and emu hunting and in warfare.

Multifunctionality

The woomera is a classic multi-tool. Its trough serves as a dish for mixing ochre pigments or carrying water-soaked materials; it can be used as a fire-saw for making fire by friction, as a small shield in ritual fighting, and—via the attached adze—for woodworking tasks such as shaping bowls and shields. In some regions designs were incised on its surface, and woomeras feature in ceremonial exchange.

History

Spear-throwers are ancient in Australia; evidence from rock art and archaeology suggests use for many thousands of years. Unlike the bow, which was adopted only in Cape York through Torres Strait contact, the woomera was the principal projectile launcher across most of the continent. Ethnographic collections in museums worldwide hold large numbers of woomeras, many decorated regionally distinctive forms.
