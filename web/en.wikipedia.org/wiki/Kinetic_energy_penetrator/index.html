Kinetic energy penetrator

A kinetic energy penetrator is an ammunition type used primarily by tanks to defeat modern armoured targets. Unlike explosive shells, it uses no explosive charge; instead the projectile damages the target purely through its kinetic energy. Modern penetrators are long, thin rods of dense material such as tungsten alloy or depleted uranium, fired at very high velocity.

Design

The most common form is the armour-piercing fin-stabilised discarding sabot (APFSDS) round: a sub-calibre long-rod penetrator held in the gun barrel by a sabot that separates after leaving the muzzle, with fins stabilising the rod in flight. Penetration depends mainly on the length and density of the rod and on impact velocity; modern rods may be 600 mm or more long and strike at around 1,700 m/s.

Mechanism

At impact, the enormous pressure generated causes both the penetrator and the armour to behave like fluids, the rod eroding as it bores through the target — a regime described by hydrodynamic penetration theory. Behind-armour effects include spall and fragments of the rod itself, which incapacitate the crew and equipment inside.

History

Kinetic penetrators evolved from solid shot and armour-piercing capped shells through armour-piercing discarding sabot (APDS) rounds of the Second World War era to the long-rod APFSDS ammunition introduced from the 1960s onward, which remains the standard anti-tank round for main battle tanks.
