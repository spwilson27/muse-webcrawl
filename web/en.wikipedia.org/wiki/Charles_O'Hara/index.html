Charles O'Hara (c. 1740 – 25 February 1802) was a British Army officer of Irish descent who served in the Seven Years' War, the American War of Independence and the French Revolutionary Wars. He is remembered for formally surrendering the British sword at the Siege of Yorktown in 1781 and, legend aside, for the singular distinction of having surrendered to both George Washington and Napoleon Bonaparte.

Early life

O'Hara was the illegitimate son of James O'Hara, 2nd Baron Tyrawley, a distinguished soldier and governor of Gibraltar. Educated at Westminster School, he was commissioned as a cornet in the Coldstream Guards in 1752 and served as his father's aide-de-camp, campaigning in Germany during the Seven Years' War and later in Senegal and Gibraltar.

American war

Serving under Lord Cornwallis in the southern campaign, O'Hara fought at Guilford Court House in 1781, where he was wounded. At the surrender of Yorktown, Cornwallis — pleading illness — delegated O'Hara to carry out the capitulation. O'Hara first attempted to present the sword to the French commander, the Comte de Rochambeau, who directed him to Washington; Washington in turn directed him to his subordinate Benjamin Lincoln. O'Hara was held prisoner and exchanged in 1782.

Later career

Promoted major general, O'Hara commanded British forces at Toulon in 1793, where he was captured during a French counterattack led in part by the young Napoleon Bonaparte — hence the claim that he surrendered to both future leaders. Exchanged in 1795, he became Governor of Gibraltar, serving until his death there in 1802. The tower known as O'Hara's Tower, and O'Hara's Battery, on the Rock of Gibraltar bear his name.
