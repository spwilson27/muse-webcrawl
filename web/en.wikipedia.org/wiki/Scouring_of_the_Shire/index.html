"The Scouring of the Shire" is the eighth chapter of Book VI of J. R. R. Tolkien's The Return of the King (1955), the third volume of The Lord of the Rings. Returning home from the War of the Ring, the hobbits Frodo Baggins, Sam Gamgee, Merry Brandybuck and Pippin Took find the Shire industrialised, polluted and tyrannised by the wizard Saruman, operating under the name "Sharkey" through his agent Lotho Sackville-Baggins. The four travellers raise the Shire, defeat Sharkey's ruffians at the Battle of Bywater, and confront Saruman at Bag End, where he is killed by his own servant, Gríma Wormtongue, who is in turn shot by hobbit archers. Scholars consider the chapter structurally and thematically central to the novel.

Plot summary

The hobbits discover that in their absence Lotho has used his money to buy up land and impose rules and rationing, backed by Men ("ruffians"), while the real power is the exiled Saruman. The Travellers rally the Shire-folk; nineteen hobbits are killed at Bywater but the ruffians are routed. At Bag End, Frodo offers Saruman mercy, but Wormtongue murders him; Frodo forbids revenge, yet the hobbits shoot Wormtongue. The Shire is then restored, aided by Sam's gift of Galadriel's earth, and the Party Tree is replaced by a mallorn sapling.

Themes and analysis

Tolkien denied that the chapter was an allegory of post-war Britain, though he acknowledged it drew on his experience of the industrial despoliation of rural England. Critics have read the Scouring as the moral climax of the book: the hobbits apply what they have learned abroad to save their home themselves, without great armies; Frodo's refusal of violence reflects his growth and his war-weariness. The chapter also completes Saruman's arc of diminishment, from the White Wizard to a petty tyrant murdered by his own creature.

Adaptations

The Scouring was omitted from Peter Jackson's film trilogy; its key beats were redistributed (Saruman dies at Isengard in the extended edition of The Return of the King, and the Mirror of Galadriel scene in The Fellowship of the Ring shows a vision of the enslaved Shire). Jackson considered the chapter anti-climactic for film, a decision much discussed by Tolkien scholars.
