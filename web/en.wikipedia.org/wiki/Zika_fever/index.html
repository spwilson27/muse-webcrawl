Zika fever, also known as Zika virus disease or simply Zika, is an infectious disease caused by the Zika virus. Most cases have no symptoms, but when present they are usually mild — including fever, rash, red eyes, and joint pain — and typically last less than a week. The virus is spread primarily by Aedes mosquitoes, notably Aedes aegypti. Zika gained worldwide attention during the 2015–16 outbreak in the Americas, when infection during pregnancy was linked to microcephaly and other severe birth defects.

Signs and symptoms

Only about one in five infected people develops symptoms. When symptoms occur, they begin 3 to 14 days after the bite of an infected mosquito and include low-grade fever, a maculopapular rash, conjunctivitis (red eyes), muscle and joint pain, headache and malaise. Symptoms are generally mild and resolve within two to seven days. Rarely, Zika infection in adults is associated with Guillain–Barré syndrome, a condition of muscle weakness caused by immune damage to the nervous system.

Cause and transmission

The Zika virus is a mosquito-borne flavivirus, related to the viruses that cause dengue, yellow fever and West Nile fever. It was first isolated in 1947 from a rhesus macaque in the Zika Forest of Uganda. Besides mosquito bites, the virus can be transmitted from mother to fetus during pregnancy, sexually, and through blood transfusion.

Diagnosis and prevention

Diagnosis is confirmed by laboratory testing of blood or other body fluids for viral RNA or antibodies, though cross-reactivity with dengue complicates serological tests. There is no specific treatment or approved vaccine; management is supportive, with rest, fluids and paracetamol. Prevention relies on mosquito control and personal protection — insect repellent, protective clothing, window screens and eliminating standing water — and on precautions against sexual transmission.

Epidemiology

After decades of sporadic cases in Africa and Asia, outbreaks occurred in Yap Island (2007) and French Polynesia (2013). The virus reached Brazil in 2015 and spread rapidly across the Americas, prompting the World Health Organization to declare a Public Health Emergency of International Concern in February 2016. Case numbers fell sharply after 2017, though the virus remains present in many tropical regions.
