Perfect tender rule

The perfect tender rule is a doctrine of United States contract law for the sale of goods, codified in Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC § 2-601). It provides that, in a single-delivery contract, a buyer may reject goods if they "fail in any respect to conform to the contract" — that is, the seller's tender of delivery must be exactly as promised, and any defect, however minor, in principle justifies rejection of the whole shipment.

Qualifications

The rule's apparent harshness is softened by several doctrines. The seller generally has a right to cure a defective tender within the contract time, and sometimes beyond it if the seller reasonably believed the goods would be acceptable (UCC § 2-508). Rejection must be timely and followed by notice, and a buyer who keeps or resells the goods may be held to have accepted them. Acceptance can be revoked only where a nonconformity "substantially impairs" the value of the goods and was difficult to discover (§ 2-608) — a stricter standard than for initial rejection. For instalment contracts, § 2-612 replaces perfect tender with a substantial-impairment test for each instalment and for the whole contract.

Comparison with other doctrines

The perfect tender rule contrasts with the common-law doctrine of substantial performance, which governs services and construction contracts and allows recovery where performance is substantially, though not perfectly, complete. It also differs from the approach of the UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG), under which avoidance generally requires a "fundamental breach". Courts and commentators have debated whether the rule invites opportunistic rejection when market prices move against a buyer, though UCC obligations of good faith limit such conduct.
