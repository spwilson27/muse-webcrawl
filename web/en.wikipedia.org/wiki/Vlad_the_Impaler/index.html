Vlad III, commonly known as Vlad the Impaler (Romanian: Vlad Țepeș) or Vlad Dracula (Romanian: Vlad Drăculea), was Voivode (Prince) of Wallachia three times between 1448 and his death in 1476/77. He is often considered one of the most important rulers in Wallachian history and a national hero in Romania.

He was the second son of Vlad Dracul, who became the ruler of Wallachia in 1436. Vlad and his younger brother, Radu, were held as hostages in the Ottoman Empire in 1442 to secure their father's loyalty. Vlad's eldest brother Mircea and their father were murdered after John Hunyadi, regent-governor of Hungary, invaded Wallachia in 1447. Hunyadi installed Vlad's second cousin, Vladislav II, as the new voivode.

Early life

Vlad was born in Sighișoara, Kingdom of Hungary (today in Romania), in the winter of 1428–31. His father, Vlad Dracul, had previously been a member of the Order of the Dragon, a military order founded by Sigismund, Holy Roman Emperor, to protect Christianity in Eastern Europe against the Ottoman Empire; the order's insignia, a dragon, gave rise to the family name "Dracul" ("the Dragon"), and Vlad III's patronymic "Drăculea" ("son of the Dragon").

In the spring of 1442, Vlad and Radu were summoned to meet Sultan Murad II in Gallipoli and were taken into custody as political hostages. They were held at Eğrigöz (now Doğrugöz) in Anatolia. Their captivity shaped Vlad's lifelong hostility toward the Ottomans and toward Radu, who converted to Islam and entered Ottoman service.

Reigns

Vlad first took the throne in October 1448 with Ottoman support after Vladislav II left Wallachia to fight the Ottomans, but he was deposed within two months when Vladislav returned with Hungarian backing. After years of exile and maneuvering, Vlad killed Vladislav in hand-to-hand combat and seized the throne for his main reign in 1456.

During his second reign (1456–1462), Vlad consolidated power, purging boyars he blamed for his father's and brother's deaths — according to tradition, at an Easter feast. He strengthened the army and the state, patronized Orthodox monasteries, and promoted trade while punishing foreign merchants who violated Wallachian commercial rules.

Ottoman campaigns

Vlad refused to pay the annual tribute to Sultan Mehmed II and raided Ottoman territory south of the Danube in the winter of 1461–62, reportedly killing more than 23,000 Turks and Bulgarians. In June 1462 he launched the famous Night Attack at Târgoviște, an attempted assassination of Mehmed himself that narrowly failed. As the Ottoman army advanced on Târgoviște, it encountered a "forest" of thousands of impaled prisoners — a sight said to have shaken even Mehmed. Though the sultan withdrew, Vlad was betrayed by boyars and his brother Radu, was arrested by his ally Matthias Corvinus of Hungary on charges of treason, and spent years in Hungarian captivity.

Impalement and reputation

Vlad's reputation for cruelty rests on his signature punishment of impalement, applied to Ottoman soldiers, criminals, corrupt officials, and disloyal boyars alike. German pamphlets printed in the late 15th century — among the first best-sellers of the printing press — sensationalized his atrocities, while Russian tales portrayed him as a stern but just ruler enforcing order against wicked boyars. Estimates of his victims range from tens of thousands upward; historians treat the most lurid figures with caution while accepting that his reign was exceptionally brutal even by the standards of the age.

Death and legacy

Restored to the throne a third time in 1476 with Hungarian and Moldavian support, Vlad was killed in battle against the Ottomans near Bucharest in December 1476 or January 1477. His head was reportedly sent to Constantinople and displayed on a stake.

In the 19th century, Romanian nationalism recast Vlad as a hero who defended his country's independence. His name and legend became internationally famous through Bram Stoker's 1897 novel Dracula, although Stoker's vampire count drew on Vlad mainly for the name "Dracula" and a Transylvanian setting rather than for the historical prince's biography.
