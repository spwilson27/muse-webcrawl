The Eskimo–Aleut languages (also known as Eskaleut) are a language family native to Alaska, northern Canada (Inuit Nunangat), Greenland, and the far east of Siberia (the Chukchi Peninsula). The family is usually divided into two branches: the Eskimo languages, themselves split into the Inuit and Yupik groups, and the Aleut language, spoken in the Aleutian Islands and the Pribilof Islands.

History

The Eskimo–Aleut language family is believed to have spread across the Arctic relatively recently in prehistory. The divergence between Aleut and the Eskimo languages is estimated at roughly 4,000 years ago, while the Inuit dialect continuum spread eastward from Alaska across Canada to Greenland within roughly the last thousand years, associated with the Thule culture. Because of this recent expansion, the Inuit varieties from Alaska to Greenland remain closely related and in part mutually intelligible.

Characteristics

Eskimo–Aleut languages are polysynthetic, building very long words from a root followed by numerous suffixes; a single word can express what English requires a whole sentence to say. The Eskimo languages are ergative–absolutive in alignment. The family has relatively few consonant and vowel phonemes, and vocabulary shared between Aleut and Eskimo is limited, though certain core words for items of Arctic material culture are cognate. Contact with neighbouring families, such as the Chukotko-Kamchatkan languages in Siberia, has been a subject of scholarly discussion; proposed wider groupings such as Uralo-Siberian remain unproven.

Status

Several languages of the family, including Greenlandic (Kalaallisut), Inuktitut and Central Alaskan Yup'ik, have tens of thousands of speakers, while many others, including several Yupik languages and most Aleut dialects, are severely endangered. Revitalisation efforts are under way in Alaska, Canada, Greenland and Russia.
