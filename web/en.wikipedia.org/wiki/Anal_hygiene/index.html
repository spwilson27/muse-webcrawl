Anal hygiene

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anal hygiene, or anal cleansing, refers to the hygienic practices performed on a person's anus and perineum after defecation, and in some cases before or after other activities. The core purpose is to remove faeces and prevent irritation, odour, and the spread of pathogens. Practices vary widely by culture: water-based washing is standard in much of Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Europe and South America, while dry wiping with toilet paper predominates in the Anglophone world.

Methods

Water washing

Washing with water, using a bidet, a handheld bidet sprayer ("bum gun"), a lota, or a jug, is the normative practice in Muslim-majority countries, where ritual washing (istinja) is prescribed after using the toilet; in India and Southeast Asia; and in countries such as Japan, where washlet toilet seats are common. Water is generally considered more effective and gentler than dry wiping.

Paper and wiping

Toilet paper was mass-produced commercially from the mid-19th century in the United States and became the standard in many Western countries. Before paper, materials such as leaves, corncobs, rags, and the Roman xylospongium (sponge on a stick) were used. Wet wipes are also marketed for this purpose, though they have been criticised for contributing to sewer blockages ("fatbergs").

Health considerations

Inadequate cleansing can cause irritation, itching (pruritus ani), and contribute to infections such as urinary tract infections; excessively vigorous wiping can also damage the sensitive perianal skin. Health professionals generally advise gentle cleansing, and washing with water is frequently recommended for people with haemorrhoids or after childbirth or anal surgery.

History

Evidence of deliberate anal cleansing extends to antiquity: the ancient Romans used the communal tersorium, the Greeks used clay scraper shards (pessoi), and paper was used for hygiene in China by the 6th century AD, centuries before its adoption in Europe.
