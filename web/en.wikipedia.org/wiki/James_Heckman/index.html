James Joseph Heckman (born 19 April 1944) is an American economist at the University of Chicago. He shared the 2000 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Daniel McFadden for his development of theory and methods for analyzing selective samples.

Work on selection bias

Heckman's central methodological contribution is the Heckman correction (the "Heckit" procedure), a two-step statistical method that corrects for non-random sample selection — for example, the fact that observed wages are available only for people who choose to work. The approach transformed empirical research in labor economics and program evaluation.

Early childhood and human capital

In later decades Heckman has become the leading economist of early-childhood intervention, re-analyzing experiments such as the Perry Preschool Program and the Abecedarian Project to argue that high-quality early education for disadvantaged children yields high rates of return through improved cognitive and non-cognitive ("character") skills. His "Heckman Curve" is widely cited in education policy debates.

Honours

Beyond the Nobel, Heckman received the John Bates Clark Medal in 1983 and is among the most cited economists in the world. He directs centers at Chicago devoted to the economics of human development and inequality.
