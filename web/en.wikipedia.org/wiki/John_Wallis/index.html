John Wallis

Born: 3 December 1616, Ashford, Kent, England
Died: 8 November 1703 (aged 86), Oxford, England
Fields: Mathematics, cryptography, phonetics
Known for: Wallis product; introducing the symbol ∞ for infinity

John Wallis (3 December 1616 – 8 November 1703) was an English clergyman and mathematician who is given partial credit for the development of infinitesimal calculus. Between 1643 and 1689 he served as chief cryptographer for Parliament and, later, the royal court. He is credited with introducing the symbol ∞ to represent the concept of infinity. He similarly used 1/∞ for an infinitesimal.

Life

Wallis was born in Ashford, Kent, the third of five children of the Reverend John Wallis. He was educated at Emmanuel College, Cambridge, where he took a degree in divinity. During the English Civil War he deciphered Royalist messages for the Parliamentarians, a skill he later exercised for the restored monarchy. In 1649 he was appointed Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford, a post he held for over fifty years despite having published little mathematics beforehand.

Mathematics

Wallis's most important works include the Arithmetica Infinitorum (1656), in which he developed methods of indivisibles into a systematic theory that influenced Isaac Newton, and the Mathesis Universalis (1657). He derived the celebrated Wallis product for π:

π/2 = (2/1)·(2/3)·(4/3)·(4/5)·(6/5)·(6/7)⋯

He also wrote on conic sections, algebra—his Treatise of Algebra (1685) gave the first published history of the subject in English—and was a founding member of the Royal Society. In phonetics, his Grammatica Linguae Anglicanae (1653) and work on teaching deaf people to speak were pioneering.
