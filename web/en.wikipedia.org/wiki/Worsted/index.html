Worsted cloth is smooth and fine, with a clear woven pattern.

Worsted is a high-quality type of wool yarn and the lightweight, smooth fabric made from it. Worsted yarn is spun from long-staple wool fibres that have been combed to remove short fibres and lay the remaining fibres parallel, producing a dense, even, hard-wearing thread. The name derives from Worstead, a village in Norfolk, England, which with nearby Norwich became a centre of the trade after Flemish weavers settled there in the twelfth century.

Manufacture

In worsted spinning the raw wool is scoured, then combed — traditionally with heated combs, later by machine — so that only long fibres remain, aligned in a parallel "top". Short fibres removed in combing (noils) are diverted to the woollen trade. The combed sliver is drawn out through successive gilling and roving stages and spun with a firm twist. Because the fibres lie parallel, worsted yarn is smooth and lustrous, with little of the fuzziness of woollen-spun yarn, and it resists pilling and abrasion.

Worsted versus woollen

The distinction between worsted and woollen cloth is fundamental in textiles. Woollen yarns use shorter fibres that are carded rather than combed, trapping air and producing soft, lofty, fuzzy fabrics such as flannel and tweed, prized for warmth. Worsted fabrics by contrast are crisp, smooth and durable, hold a sharp crease, and show their weave pattern clearly; they are generally lighter and cooler. Classic worsted fabrics include suiting cloth, gabardine, serge, challis and barathea, used for tailored suits, uniforms, trousers and dresses. "Worsted weight" is also a standard category of medium-thickness knitting yarn, although in knitting the term refers to thickness rather than strictly to the combing process.

History

Worstead cloth was a major English export in the Middle Ages, and the Norfolk worsted industry reached its height in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries before competition and mechanisation shifted production to the mills of Yorkshire's West Riding, where towns such as Bradford became the world centre of the worsted trade in the nineteenth century. Worsted suiting remains the standard cloth for fine tailoring today.
