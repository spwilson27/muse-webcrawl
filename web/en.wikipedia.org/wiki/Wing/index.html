Wing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An airliner wing seen from a passenger window above clouds at sunset]
Over the wing at cruising altitude.

A wing is a fin-shaped structure that produces lift when moved through air, the defining component of fixed-wing aircraft. Wing design — planform (straight, swept, delta), airfoil section, aspect ratio, dihedral and devices such as flaps, slats, spoilers and winglets — determines an aircraft\'s speed, efficiency, stability and low-speed handling. Birds\' and insects\' wings solve the same physics by different means.

How wings work

An airfoil\'s shape and angle of attack deflect air downward (and accelerate it over the upper surface), generating lift proportional to the square of speed. Induced drag arises from wingtip vortices; long, slender (high aspect-ratio) wings minimise it — sailplanes exceed 30:1 — while fighters accept drag for strength and manoeuvre.

Design evolution

Biplanes gave way to cantilever monoplanes in the 1930s; swept wings (from German wartime research) delayed transonic drag rise for jets; delta wings served supersonic types (Concorde, Mirage); supercritical airfoils (NASA, 1960s–70s) improved transonic efficiency. Winglets (blended, sharklets, split scimitars) cut fuel burn by several percent.

Structures and systems

Wings are usually aluminium or composite torsion boxes whose interior doubles as fuel tanks; they carry engines, landing gear (often), ailerons, flaps and fuel pumps. Icing protection (hot bleed air or electrothermal mats) guards leading edges in cloud.
