Film base

A film base is a transparent substrate which acts as a support medium for the emulsion that lies atop it. Photographic and motion picture film consists of a photosensitive emulsion layer coated onto this base, along with various supplementary layers such as antihalation and antistatic coatings.

Materials

Nitrate base

The earliest practical film base was cellulose nitrate, introduced by George Eastman in 1889. Nitrate film offered excellent clarity and flexibility but was dangerously flammable — it can burn even underwater and decomposes into toxic gases — and it decays chemically over time. Its use for motion pictures ended in the early 1950s.

Acetate base

Cellulose acetate "safety film" replaced nitrate for most purposes. Variants include diacetate and triacetate. Acetate is much safer but suffers from vinegar syndrome, the hydrolysis of the base that releases acetic acid and causes shrinkage and brittleness.

Polyester base

Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) polyester base, sold under names such as Estar, is extremely strong, dimensionally stable, and chemically inert. It is preferred for archival film and for applications such as X-ray film, though its strength means a jam in a camera or projector can damage the equipment rather than tearing the film.
