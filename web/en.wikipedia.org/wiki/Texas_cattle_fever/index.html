Texas cattle fever
Other namesBovine babesiosis, redwater fever
CauseBabesia bigemina and B. bovis
VectorCattle fever ticks (Rhipicephalus spp.)

Texas cattle fever, also called bovine babesiosis, is a tick-borne disease of cattle caused by the protozoan parasites Babesia bigemina and Babesia bovis, which invade and destroy red blood cells. Southern cattle that carried the infection without serious illness spread the disease to susceptible northern herds along the great cattle trails, causing devastating losses in the 19th century.

The cattle fever tick, vector of the disease

Scientific discovery

In 1893, Theobald Smith and F. L. Kilborne of the US Bureau of Animal Industry demonstrated that the disease was transmitted by the cattle fever tick — the first demonstration that an arthropod could transmit an infectious disease, a landmark in parasitology and public health that foreshadowed discoveries about mosquitoes and malaria and yellow fever. Cattle that survived infection remained carriers, immune themselves but dangerous to naive herds.

Eradication

A federal–state eradication program begun in 1906 used quarantine lines, systematic dipping of cattle in arsenical vats and pasture vacation to eliminate the fever tick. By 1943 the tick was declared eradicated from the United States except for a permanent quarantine buffer zone along the Rio Grande, where reinfestations from Mexico are still intercepted.

Signs

Affected cattle develop high fever, anaemia, weakness and red-coloured urine ("redwater") from haemoglobin released by destroyed red blood cells; death rates in susceptible adult cattle can be very high.
