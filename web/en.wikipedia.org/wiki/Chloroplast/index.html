Chloroplast

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A chloroplast is an organelle in the cells of plants and algae where photosynthesis takes place. Chloroplasts are lens-shaped, bounded by a double membrane, and contain an internal system of flattened sacs (thylakoids, stacked into grana) floating in the stroma. The green pigment chlorophyll, embedded in the thylakoid membranes, captures light energy.

In the light-dependent reactions, water is split — releasing the oxygen of the air — and light energy is stored as ATP and NADPH. In the Calvin cycle, in the stroma, that energy fixes carbon dioxide into sugars (see cellular respiration for the reverse process). A leaf cell may contain dozens of chloroplasts, which can move within the cell to optimise light capture.

Chloroplasts have their own small circular genome and divide by fission — evidence, with their double membrane, that they descend from free-living cyanobacteria engulfed by an ancestral eukaryote, the endosymbiotic origin they share with mitochondria. Plant cells also use chloroplast relatives (plastids) for storage and colour, as in the chromoplasts that ripen fruit (see plant).
