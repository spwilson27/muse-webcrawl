Anti-miscegenation laws in the United States were state laws that prohibited marriage — and in some states sexual relations — between people of different races. Enacted first in the colonial era, such laws defined much of the legal architecture of American racial separation until the U.S. Supreme Court invalidated them unanimously in Loving v. Virginia (1967).

The word "miscegenation" was coined in an 1863 hoax pamphlet published during the Civil War. Earlier statutes spoke instead of "intermarriage" between whites and Negroes, Native Americans, or, in western states, Asians.

History

Colonial and early republic

Maryland passed the first colonial ban in 1664, followed by Virginia in 1691, which also banished white partners in interracial marriages. After independence most southern and several northern and western states maintained or enacted bans. California and other western states extended prohibitions to marriages between whites and Chinese, Japanese, Filipino and other Asian partners.

Expansion and challenges

During the Jim Crow era bans were strengthened; Virginia's Racial Integrity Act of 1924, drafted under registrar Walter Plecker, imposed a strict "one-drop rule". In Pace v. Alabama (1883) the Supreme Court upheld harsher penalties for interracial adultery. The first successful legal challenge in the twentieth century came in California, whose supreme court struck down the state ban in Perez v. Sharp (1948). Several states repealed their laws in the 1950s and early 1960s, but sixteen southern and border states retained them in 1967.

Loving v. Virginia

Mildred Loving, a Black and Native American woman, and Richard Loving, a white man, married in Washington, D.C. in 1958 and were arrested on returning to Virginia, pleading guilty to violating the state's ban. Their case reached the Supreme Court, which ruled on 12 June 1967 that laws prohibiting interracial marriage violate the Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote that "the freedom to marry, or not marry, a person of another race resides with the individual and cannot be infringed by the State."

Aftermath

Although unenforceable after 1967, some state statutes and constitutional provisions remained on the books for decades; South Carolina removed its constitutional ban in 1998 and Alabama in 2000, the last state to do so. Public approval of interracial marriage, measured by Gallup at 4% in 1958, rose to over 90% by the 2020s. The Respect for Marriage Act of 2022 added federal statutory protection for interracial marriages.
