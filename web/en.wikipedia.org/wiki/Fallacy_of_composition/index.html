Fallacy of composition

What is true of the parts need not be true of the whole

The fallacy of composition is an informal fallacy that arises when one infers that something is true of the whole from the fact that it is true of some part of the whole, or of every part. It is related to the mereological fallacy and is the reverse of the fallacy of division, which infers that what is true of the whole must be true of its parts.

Examples

  • "Each player on the team is excellent, so the team must be excellent" — the players may not work well together.
  • "This machine is made of lightweight parts, so the machine is lightweight."
  • "If one person stands up at a concert they can see better, so if everyone stands, everyone will see better."
  • In economics, the paradox of thrift: saving is prudent for an individual, but if everyone saves more, aggregate demand and income may fall.

Discussion

The inference is not always fallacious: some properties do transfer from parts to wholes — a wall made entirely of red bricks is red, and a body's mass is the sum of the masses of its parts. The fallacy lies in assuming transfer in cases where the property depends on the arrangement of parts or on interactions among them, as with emergent properties. Philosophers distinguish harmless composition inferences from genuine fallacies by asking whether there is a reason to think the property is preserved under aggregation.
