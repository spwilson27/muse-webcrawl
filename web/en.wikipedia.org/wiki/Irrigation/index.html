Irrigation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Irrigation is the artificial application of water to land or soil to assist in growing crops — the agricultural technology, older than the plough in some regions, that converts rainfall's lottery into controlled supply and made civilisation possible in dry lands. Irrigated land is only about a fifth of the world's cropland but produces roughly 40% of its food, and irrigation accounts for about 70% of all human freshwater withdrawals — the single largest claim on the planet's fresh water.

History and methods

Basin irrigation from the Nile's annual flood fed Egypt for five millennia; Mesopotamia's canals (and their salinisation, which helped fell Sumer) taught the technology's dark side; Persia's underground qanats, Sri Lanka's tank cascades, China's Dujiangyan system (256 BC, still working) and the floating chinampas of Mexico spread the art worldwide. Methods form an efficiency ladder: surface (flood and furrow) irrigation, still dominant worldwide at 40–60% efficiency; sprinkler systems (the centre-pivot circles green in the Great Plains from the Ogallala Aquifer); and drip irrigation, pioneered in Israel from the 1960s, delivering water to roots at up to 90%+ efficiency. Where rivers end in closed basins, irrigation writes the sharpest environmental stories — the diverted Amu and Syr Darya and the death of the Aral Sea in Central Asia, the drained wetlands of Mesopotamia, falling aquifers from Punjab to Kansas. Salinity, waterlogging and equity between upstream and downstream users ("hydraulic civilisations" were an early theory of despotism) remain irrigation's permanent politics, now sharpened by climate change and groundwater depletion measured from satellites.
