Cha-cha-cha (dance)

The cha-cha-cha (also called cha-cha) is a dance of Cuban origin. It is danced to the music of the same name introduced by the Cuban composer and violinist Enrique Jorrín in the early 1950s. This rhythm was developed from the danzón-mambo. The name of the dance is an onomatopoeia derived from the shuffling sound of the dancers' feet when they dance two consecutive quick steps (correctly, actually three) that characterize the dance.

History

In the early 1950s, Enrique Jorrín worked as a violinist and composer with the Orquesta América charanga in Havana. When Jorrín composed a new danzón-mambo around 1951, he noticed that many dancers had difficulty with the syncopated rhythms of mambo. He therefore wrote melodies that emphasised the first downbeat and simplified the syncopation; the resulting composition, "La engañadora", is generally regarded as the first cha-cha-cha. The dance that evolved to accompany the music added a triple step — the "cha-cha-cha" — into the basic box pattern of the mambo.

By 1954 the cha-cha-cha had reached the United States and Europe, where it became one of the most popular social dances of the decade. Dance teachers such as Pierre Margolie codified the steps, and the rhythm was standardized for ballroom competition.

Technique

In both international and American ballroom styles the cha-cha-cha is danced in 4/4 time at about 30 measures per minute. The basic figure involves a checked forward step, a weight transfer back, and a chasse of three steps — side-close-side, forward or back — from which the dance takes its name. Counting is typically "two, three, cha-cha-cha" or "two, three, four-and-one". Steps are taken with the ball of the foot first, and the dance is characterised by sharp hip action, straightening of the legs, and a playful, flirtatious character in the choreography.

Styles

Two main competitive styles exist. In International Latin, the dance uses a smaller step, pronounced Cuban hip motion, and figures such as the fan, hockey stick, alemana and New York. In American Rhythm, the technique and figure vocabulary differ somewhat, with a stronger emphasis on open breaks and underarm turns. Socially, the dance is also performed to a wide range of popular music beyond its Cuban repertoire.
