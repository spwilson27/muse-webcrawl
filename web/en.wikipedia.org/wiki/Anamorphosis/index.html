Anamorphosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anamorphosis is a distorted projection or perspective requiring the viewer to occupy a specific vantage point, use special tools, or both to view a recognisable image. The word derives from the Greek ana (again) and morphe (shape, form). It is used in painting, photography, sculpture, film, and architecture.

Types

In perspective anamorphosis (also called oblique anamorphosis), the image resolves when seen from a particular oblique angle; the most famous example is the smeared skull in Hans Holbein the Younger's The Ambassadors (1533), which becomes a recognisable skull viewed from the side. In mirror anamorphosis (catoptric anamorphosis), a cylindrical or conical mirror placed on the painting restores the distorted drawing. Leonardo da Vinci recorded early experiments with the technique in his notebooks.

Later uses

Anamorphic techniques flourished in Baroque ceiling painting and resurfaced in modern street art and pavement chalk drawings, where images are stretched across pavements to appear three-dimensional from one viewpoint. Anamorphic widescreen cinematography uses cylindrical lenses to squeeze a wide image onto standard film, unsqueezing it at projection.
