Spinal fusion

Spinal fusion
Other namesSpondylodesis, spondylosyndesis
SpecialtyOrthopaedic surgery, neurosurgery
PurposeJoin two or more vertebrae to eliminate painful motion or stabilise the spine

Spinal fusion, also known as spondylodesis, is a surgical procedure in which two or more vertebrae are permanently joined together so that they heal as a single solid bone. By eliminating motion at a painful or unstable spinal segment, fusion aims to relieve pain, correct deformity, and protect the spinal cord and nerves.

Indications

Fusion is used for conditions including spondylolisthesis, spinal stenosis with instability, severe degenerative disc disease, spinal fractures, tumours, infections, and deformities such as scoliosis and kyphosis. It is frequently combined with decompression of neural structures (laminectomy) or removal of a damaged disc (discectomy).

Technique

The surgeon places bone graft — taken from the patient's own pelvis (autograft), from a donor (allograft), or a synthetic substitute — between the vertebrae or across their posterior elements. Metal instrumentation such as pedicle screws, rods, plates, and interbody cages holds the segment rigid while the graft incorporates over several months. Approaches are named by their direction: posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF), transforaminal (TLIF), anterior (ALIF), and lateral (XLIF/LLIF). Cervical fusion is commonly performed from the front of the neck (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, ACDF).

Outcomes and risks

Many patients obtain substantial relief of leg pain and instability-related back pain, though results for fusion performed solely for chronic low back pain from disc degeneration remain debated. Risks include infection, bleeding, nerve injury, failure of the bones to fuse (pseudarthrosis — more common in smokers), hardware problems, and accelerated degeneration at adjacent unfused levels, known as adjacent segment disease. Recovery typically involves weeks to months of restricted activity, sometimes with a brace.

History

The first spinal fusions were reported in 1911 by Fred Albee and Russell Hibbs, originally for tuberculosis of the spine and scoliosis. Internal fixation with screws and rods, introduced in the mid-twentieth century and refined with pedicle-screw systems in the 1980s, greatly increased fusion rates and made the procedure far more common.
