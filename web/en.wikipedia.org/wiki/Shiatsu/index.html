Shiatsu

Shiatsu (指圧, from Japanese shi "finger" and atsu "pressure") is a form of Japanese bodywork based on concepts in traditional Chinese medicine such as qi meridians. In the Japanese language, shiatsu means "finger pressure." In practice, shiatsu techniques include massages with fingers, palms, elbows, and feet, assisted stretching, and joint manipulation, usually applied to a clothed client lying on a floor mat (futon) or a low table.

Shiatsu is usually given on a futon with the client fully clothed.

History

Shiatsu grew out of Japanese anma massage and was systematised in the early 20th century. Tokujiro Namikoshi (1905–2000) is credited with founding modern shiatsu; he established the Japan Shiatsu College in 1940 and combined anatomical knowledge with pressure technique. In 1955 the Japanese Ministry of Health recognised shiatsu as a distinct therapy, defining it in 1964 separately from anma and massage. In the West, Shizuto Masunaga developed "Zen Shiatsu," which extended the meridian system of traditional Chinese medicine and emphasised diagnosis through the hara (abdomen) and psychologically oriented treatment.

Theory and practice

Practitioners assess the flow of qi (Japanese ki) along meridians, treating states described as kyo (empty, deficient) and jitsu (full, excess). Pressure is applied perpendicular to the body with relaxed body weight rather than muscular force, moving through a sequence (kata) according to the school. Schools include Namikoshi shiatsu, Masunaga's Zen Shiatsu, and barefoot and macrobiotic variants.

Evidence and regulation

Systematic reviews find limited high-quality clinical evidence for shiatsu beyond possible short-term relief of pain and stress; it is classified as a complementary and alternative medicine. Regulation varies by country: in Japan shiatsu practitioners are licensed by the state, while elsewhere practice is covered by voluntary professional registers.
