Suleiman the Magnificent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Tughra ( imperial calligraphic seal) of Suleiman the Magnificent in gold and blue]

Suleiman I (6 November 1494 – 6/7 September 1566), commonly called Suleiman the Magnificent in the West and 'the Lawgiver' (Kanuni) among the Ottomans, was the tenth and longest-reigning sultan of the Ottoman Empire (r. 1520–1566). Under him the empire reached its territorial and cultural apogee: he took Belgrade (1521), Rhodes (1522) and most of Hungary after Mohács (1526), besieged Vienna (1529), annexed Iraq and fought Persia, while Ottoman fleets contested the Mediterranean and reached the Indian Ocean. At home he codified law, rebuilt the administration, and patronised the architect Sinan and the poet Bâkî in a golden age of Ottoman civilisation.

Conquests

Suleiman personally led thirteen campaigns. In Europe his armies shattered Hungary at Mohács (King Louis II died in the rout), partitioned it with Transylvania as a vassal, and twice threatened Vienna (1529, and the campaign of 1532). Against Safavid Persia he took Baghdad (1534) and fixed a frontier (Amasya, 1555) close to today's Iran–Iraq border. His admirals — Hayreddin Barbarossa above all — made the Ottoman fleet dominant in the Mediterranean (Preveza, 1538), though Malta held out in 1565.

Lawgiver and court

Suleiman's kanunnames systematised taxation, land tenure and criminal law alongside the sharia, earning his Turkish epithet. His reign is also the story of Hürrem Sultan (Roxelana), the slave-concubine he controversially married, whose political influence opened the 'Sultanate of Women'; of his grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha, executed in 1536; and of succession tragedy: he had his son Mustafa strangled (1553) and Bayezid executed (1561), leaving the throne to Selim II. Suleiman died on campaign before Szigetvár in 1566, his death concealed for weeks; the fortress fell the day after.
