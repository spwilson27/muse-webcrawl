Lake stratification

Lake stratification is the tendency of lakes to form separate and distinct thermal layers during warm weather. It arises because water changes density with temperature, reaching maximum density at about 4 °C (39 °F), and is a central topic of limnology.

The three layers

A thermally stratified lake typically has three layers:

  • The epilimnion — the warm, well-mixed upper layer, in contact with the atmosphere.
  • The metalimnion (or thermocline) — the middle layer where temperature falls rapidly with depth.
  • The hypolimnion — the cold, dense, quiet bottom layer, cut off from surface mixing.

Seasonal mixing regimes

Lakes are classified by how often they mix: dimictic lakes mix twice a year (in spring and autumn turnover), monomictic lakes once, polymictic lakes frequently, and meromictic lakes almost never — their deep layers remain permanently stratified, sometimes by salinity differences. In dimictic temperate lakes, ice cover in winter produces inverse stratification, with 0 °C water under the ice and 4 °C water at the bottom.

Ecological consequences

Stratification governs oxygen and nutrient distribution: the hypolimnion may become anoxic during summer, while autumn turnover replenishes nutrients to surface waters. Climate warming is lengthening stratification periods in many lakes, with consequences for water quality and aquatic life.
