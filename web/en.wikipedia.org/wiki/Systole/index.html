Systole

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Systole (pronounced /ˈsɪstəli/) is the part of the cardiac cycle during which some chambers of the heart contract after refilling with blood. The term originates from Greek συστολή (systolē), meaning "a drawing together or a contraction". Originally, "systole" referred only to the contraction of the ventricles, though usage has since extended the term to atrial contraction as well.

Types of systole

Atrial systole

Atrial systole occurs towards the end of ventricular diastole, completing the filling of the ventricles; it contributes the final "atrial kick" of blood volume. It is triggered by depolarisation spreading from the sinoatrial node across the atria, and the associated wave of the electrocardiogram is the P wave.

Ventricular systole

Ventricular systole follows the depolarisation of the ventricles (the QRS complex) and is conventionally divided into isovolumetric contraction, in which ventricular pressure rises with all valves closed, and the ejection phase, in which the aortic and pulmonary valves open and blood is expelled into the great arteries. At a resting heart rate of 70 beats per minute, ventricular systole lasts roughly 0.3 seconds. The first heart sound ("lub") marks the closure of the atrioventricular valves at its onset.

Clinical significance

Systolic blood pressure — the first number in a blood-pressure reading — is the peak arterial pressure during ventricular systole. Abnormalities of systolic function characterise heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and murmurs arising from stenotic semilunar valves or regurgitant atrioventricular valves are characteristically heard during systole.
