Newton's rings

Newton's rings are a pattern of concentric coloured or dark-and-light rings produced by the interference of light reflected between two surfaces — typically a convex lens placed on a flat glass plate, creating a thin, wedge-like air film of varying thickness.

Explanation

Light reflected from the top of the air film and from the plate beneath travels different path lengths. Where the film thickness satisfies the condition for constructive or destructive interference, bright or dark rings appear. Because the air gap increases with radius, the rings are concentric and become more closely spaced outward. In monochromatic light the rings are sharp; in white light they are coloured.

History and uses

Robert Hooke described the phenomenon, and Isaac Newton studied it extensively, reporting it in his Opticks (1704), though he interpreted it within his corpuscular theory of light. The wave explanation came with Thomas Young and Augustin-Jean Fresnel. Measuring ring diameters allows determination of a lens's radius of curvature or the wavelength of light, and the effect is used in optical testing of lens surfaces — an undesirable version, “Newton's rings,” also appears when scanning film in contact with glass.
