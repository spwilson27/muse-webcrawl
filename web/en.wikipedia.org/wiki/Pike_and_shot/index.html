Pike and shot

Pike and shot was a historical infantry tactical combination that featured in European warfare roughly from the late 15th to the late 17th century. It consisted of formations combining pikemen — foot soldiers armed with very long spears, typically 4–6 metres — with infantry equipped with firearms, initially the arquebus and later the heavier musket (the "shot"). The pikemen protected the vulnerable, slow-reloading shot from cavalry and enemy infantry, while the shot provided the killing firepower that pikes lacked at range.

Development

The system grew out of the late-medieval revival of massed pike infantry by the Swiss, whose dense squares defeated mounted knights, and the spread of handheld gunpowder weapons. Spanish commanders in the Italian Wars, above all Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba, combined arquebusiers with pikemen and sword-and-buckler men; the Spanish tercio — a large square of up to 3,000 men with a core of pikes and sleeves of shot at its corners — dominated European battlefields for much of the 16th century.

In the 1590s Maurice of Nassau reformed Dutch infantry into smaller, shallower battalions drilled to deliver volley fire by ranks (the countermarch), making firepower more continuous and the formations more flexible. Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden carried reform further in the Thirty Years' War: thinner lines, integrated light artillery, salvo fire, and pikemen used offensively, famously triumphing at Breitenfeld in 1631.

Decline

As flintlock muskets, paper cartridges and platoon firing increased the rate and reliability of fire, the proportion of shot to pikes rose steadily — from rough parity around 1600 to two or three musketeers per pikeman by the late 1600s. The invention of the socket bayonet around the 1690s finally let every musketeer double as a pikeman, and by about 1700–1710 the pike had disappeared from European armies, replaced by line infantry tactics.
