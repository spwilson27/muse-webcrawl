Persistent vegetative state

A persistent vegetative state (PVS), also called unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, is a disorder of consciousness in which patients with severe brain damage have progressed from a coma to a state of wakefulness without detectable awareness of themselves or their environment. Reflex movements such as eye opening, sleep–wake cycles, and spontaneous breathing are preserved, but there is no evidence of purposeful behaviour or language comprehension.

Definition and terminology

The term "persistent vegetative state" was introduced by Bryan Jennett and Fred Plum in 1972. The condition is termed persistent when it lasts more than one month, and is historically described as permanent after three months following non-traumatic brain injury or twelve months after traumatic injury, although late recoveries, while rare, have been documented. Modern guidelines increasingly favour the term "prolonged disorder of consciousness" and caution against premature judgements of permanence.

Causes

PVS most commonly results from traumatic brain injury or from non-traumatic causes such as cardiac arrest causing hypoxic-ischaemic brain damage, stroke, or severe infection. The cerebral cortex is extensively damaged or disconnected while the brainstem, which governs arousal and autonomic functions, remains relatively intact.

Diagnosis and recovery

Diagnosis rests on repeated standardised behavioural assessment (such as the Coma Recovery Scale–Revised) to distinguish the vegetative state from the minimally conscious state; studies suggest a substantial misdiagnosis rate when assessment is informal. Neuroimaging and electrophysiology may reveal covert awareness in a minority of patients. Recovery of consciousness becomes progressively less likely with time, and most survivors who do emerge remain severely disabled.

Legal and ethical issues

The condition has been at the centre of prominent legal cases over the withdrawal of artificial nutrition and hydration, including those of Karen Ann Quinlan, Terri Schiavo, and Tony Bland, which established in several jurisdictions that artificial nutrition and hydration constitute medical treatment that may lawfully be withdrawn when it is no longer in the patient's best interests.
