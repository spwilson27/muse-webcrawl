Acrylic paint

Acrylic paint
Type: Water-based paint
Binder: Acrylic polymer emulsion
Invented: 1930s–1950s

Acrylic paint is a fast-drying paint made of pigment suspended in acrylic polymer emulsion and plasticizers, silicon oils, defoamers, stabilizers, or metal soaps. Most acrylic paints are water-based, but become water-resistant when dry. Depending on how much the paint is diluted with water, or modified with acrylic gels, mediums, or pastes, the finished acrylic painting can resemble a watercolor, a gouache, or an oil painting, or have other characteristics not attainable with other media.

Watercolor and oil painters also use acrylic underpainting. Commercial acrylic paints were first developed in the mid-20th century and were widely adopted by artists including Andy Warhol, David Hockney, and Helen Frankenthaler.

History

Otto Röhm invented acrylic resin, which was quickly transformed into acrylic paint. In 1934, the first usable acrylic resin dispersion was developed by German chemical company BASF. Between 1946 and 1949, Leonard Bocour and Sam Golden invented a solution acrylic paint under the brand Magna. These were mineral spirit-based paints. Water-based acrylic paints were later introduced as house paints, and artists adopted them from the 1950s and 1960s, notably after Liquitex and other brands marketed them widely.

Properties

Acrylic paint can be diluted with water up to a point; excessive dilution weakens adhesion. It dries quickly, can be painted over without disturbing underlying layers, and cleans from brushes with soap and water while wet. Once dry it is flexible and relatively durable, though it can soften in heat and harden in cold.

Techniques

Artists use impasto with heavy-body paints and gels, glazing with transparent mediums, pouring, and mixed media. Acrylics adhere to many surfaces including canvas, paper, wood, and fabric, often with gesso priming.
