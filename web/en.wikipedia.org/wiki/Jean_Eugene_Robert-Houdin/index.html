Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin (December 7, 1805 – June 13, 1871) was a French clockmaker, inventor, and magician widely regarded as the father of the modern style of conjuring. He transformed stage magic by abandoning the wizard's robes and occult trappings of earlier performers in favor of elegant evening dress and salon-style illusions built on ingenious mechanical apparatus of his own design. The escape artist Erik Weisz later paid homage to him by adopting the stage name Harry Houdini.

Early life

Born Jean-Eugène Robert in Blois, the son of a watchmaker, he trained in his father's trade and married Josèphe Cécile Houdin, adding her surname to his own. A set of books on conjuring, sent to him in error in place of works on clockmaking, kindled his interest in magic; he studied with the local conjurer Maître Torrini (the self-styled Comte de Grisy) and built automata and mystery clocks that won medals at the French Industrial Exposition of 1844.

The Soirées fantastiques

In July 1845 Robert-Houdin opened his own small theatre in the Palais Royal in Paris, presenting his Soirées fantastiques. His signature illusions included the "Ethereal Suspension," in which his young son appeared to float in the air after inhaling ether; the "Marvellous Orange Tree," a mechanical tree that blossomed and produced fruit; an automaton trapeze artist; and feats of apparent mind-reading performed with his son Émile as the "second sight" act. His clockmaking skill made the apparatus exceptionally refined and precise.

Mission to Algeria and later years

In 1856 the French government sent Robert-Houdin to Algeria, where marabout religious leaders were using conjuring tricks to encourage resistance to colonial rule; his superior illusions, including catching a bullet between his teeth, were intended to demonstrate French "magic" as the stronger power. He retired to Saint-Gervais, near Blois, where he wrote his celebrated memoirs, Confidences d'un prestidigitateur (1858), and works explaining magic methods, dying there in 1871.

Legacy

Robert-Houdin's influence on magic is foundational: he established the magician as a gentleman performer, insisted on polished presentation and original apparatus, and inspired generations of successors, most famously Houdini, who took his name in tribute — though Houdini later turned against his idol in print, disputing several of Robert-Houdin's claims to original inventions. The Maison de la Magie museum in Blois now stands opposite his former home.
