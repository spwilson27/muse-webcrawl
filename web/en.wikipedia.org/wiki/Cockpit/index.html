Cockpit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An airliner glass cockpit with multiple glowing displays at night]
A modern flight deck.

A cockpit or flight deck is the area, usually near the front of an aircraft, from which pilots control it. Modern airliner cockpits are \'glass cockpits\': electronic flight instrument displays replace banks of analogue dials, presenting attitude, navigation, engine and system data, with flight-management computers and autopilot controls on the glareshield. Historically open to the weather in early aircraft, the cockpit became enclosed, then pressurised, then fortified after 2001 with reinforced, locked doors.

Layout and instruments

Each pilot has primary flight and navigation displays; central screens show engine and system synoptics (EICAS/ECAM); the pedestal holds throttles, radios and the FMS keypad. Overhead panels manage electrics, hydraulics, fuel and pressurisation. Standby instruments and checklists back up the electronics.

Crew roles and automation

The pilot flying (PF) and pilot monitoring (PM) divide tasks; automation policy encourages manual flying practice to preserve skills. Voice recorders (CVR) and flight data recorders (FDR) — the \'black boxes\', painted orange — are crash-protected recorders in the tail.

History

The word derives from cock-fighting pits via shipboard usage. Steam-gauge cockpits needed a flight engineer (third crew member) into the 1980s; two-pilot glass cockpits (A320, 737NG) became standard, and single-pilot airliner concepts are studied but face public and regulatory resistance.
