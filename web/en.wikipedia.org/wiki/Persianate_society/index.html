Persianate society

Persianate society
Concept byMarshall Hodgson (1974)
RegionIran, Central Asia, Anatolia, South Asia, Caucasus
HallmarksPersian language and literature, Perso-Islamic court culture

A Persianate society is a society that is based on or strongly influenced by the Persian language, culture, literature, art and identity. The term, coined by historian Marshall G. S. Hodgson in his 1974 work The Venture of Islam, describes the vast zone — stretching at its height from the Balkans and Anatolia through Iran and Central Asia to Bengal and the Deccan — in which Persian served as the language of administration, diplomacy and high culture, regardless of the ethnicity of rulers or the vernacular of the population.

Characteristics

Persianate societies shared a repertoire of courtly etiquette (adab), poetic canons (Rumi, Saadi, Hafez), historiography, miniature painting, garden design, architecture and administrative practice, fused with Islam in what Hodgson called the "Perso-Islamic" tradition. Turkic dynasties — Seljuks, Ghaznavids, Timurids, Ottomans (in part) and Mughals — were among the chief patrons of this culture, making "Persianate" a cultural-linguistic category rather than an ethnic one.

Extent and decline

Persian was the court language of the Mughal Empire until the 19th century and of many Central Asian khanates. Its sphere contracted from the 18th century onward under pressure from vernacular nationalism, Russian and British colonial administration, and language reforms in Turkey and Iran, though Persian literary culture remains influential in Iran, Afghanistan and Tajikistan.
