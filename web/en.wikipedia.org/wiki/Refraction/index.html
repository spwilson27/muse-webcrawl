Refraction

Refraction
FieldOptics, wave physics
Governing lawSnell's law
Key quantityRefractive index (n)
Related phenomenaReflection, diffraction, dispersion, total internal reflection

Refraction is the redirection of a wave as it passes from one medium to another. The redirection can be caused by the wave's change in speed or by a change in the medium. Refraction of light is the most commonly observed phenomenon, but other waves such as sound waves and water waves also experience refraction. How much a wave is refracted is determined by the change in wave speed and the initial direction of wave propagation relative to the direction of change in speed.

For light, refraction follows Snell's law, which states that, for a given pair of media, the ratio of the sines of the angle of incidence and angle of refraction is equal to the ratio of phase velocities in the two media, or equivalently, to the reciprocal of the ratio of the indices of refraction:

sin θ1 / sin θ2 = v1 / v2 = n2 / n1

Explanation

Refraction occurs because waves travel at different speeds in different media. When a wavefront crosses a boundary at an angle, the part of the wave that enters the new medium first changes speed first, causing the whole wavefront to pivot. This is analogous to a line of marching soldiers wheeling as they pass from pavement onto mud. At the boundary, some of the wave's energy is also typically reflected, a phenomenon described by the Fresnel equations.

Refractive index

The refractive index of a medium is a dimensionless number that describes how fast light travels through it relative to vacuum. Typical values are about 1.0003 for air, 1.333 for water, and around 1.5 for common glass. The index generally depends on wavelength, which causes dispersion: white light entering a prism is split into its constituent colours because each colour is refracted by a slightly different amount.

Total internal reflection

When light travels from a denser medium to a rarer one at a sufficiently steep angle of incidence, it cannot escape and is entirely reflected back into the denser medium. This occurs above the critical angle and is the operating principle of optical fibres.

Applications and natural phenomena

Refraction is exploited in lenses for eyeglasses, cameras, telescopes, and microscopes. In nature it produces mirages, the apparent displacement of submerged objects, the twinkling of stars, and rainbows (in combination with internal reflection and dispersion in raindrops). Continuous refraction in the atmosphere also slightly alters the observed positions of celestial objects and extends daylight by a few minutes at sunrise and sunset.
