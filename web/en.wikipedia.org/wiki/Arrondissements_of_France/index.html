Arrondissements of France

An arrondissement (French: [aʁɔ̃dismɑ̃]) is the third-level administrative division of France, below the region and the department and above the canton and commune. As of recent counts there are 332 arrondissements in France (including overseas departments). Each is headed by a subprefect (sous-préfet), who assists the prefect of the department; the capital of an arrondissement is called its subprefecture (sous-préfecture), except where it is also the departmental prefecture.

Role

Arrondissements have no elected assembly of their own (unlike departments and communes); they serve as territorial units of state administration, for the organisation of subprefectural services, and as a framework for some statistics and elections (legislative constituencies are drawn within or across them). They should not be confused with the municipal arrondissements of Paris, Lyon, and Marseille, which are subdivisions of those cities with elected councils and mayors.

History

The arrondissements were created by the law of 28 Pluviôse Year VIII (17 February 1800), replacing the districts of the Revolutionary period. Their number has been adjusted repeatedly: suppressed in budget cuts in the 1920s (some later restored), and periodically redrawn to follow population changes — a notable redrawing took effect in 2017, aligning arrondissement boundaries with those of intercommunal bodies.

Statistics

Arrondissements vary greatly in size and population: the most populous, such as Lille or Lyon, contain well over a million inhabitants, while rural arrondissements in mountainous departments may have fewer than 15,000. The commune of Paris constitutes its own arrondissement-level unit within the capital.
