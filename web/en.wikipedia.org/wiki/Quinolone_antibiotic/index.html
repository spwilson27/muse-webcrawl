Quinolone antibiotic

Quinolone antibiotics constitute a large group of broad-spectrum bacteriocidals that share a bicyclic core structure related to the compound 4-quinolone. They are used to treat a wide variety of infections in humans and animals. Nearly all quinolone antibiotics in use are fluoroquinolones, which contain a fluorine atom in their chemical structure and are effective against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. Examples include ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and moxifloxacin.

Mechanism of action

Quinolones kill bacteria by inhibiting the enzymes DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV, which are required for bacterial DNA replication, transcription, and repair. By trapping these enzymes on DNA, the drugs cause double-stranded DNA breaks and rapid bacterial death. Human topoisomerases are structurally different and much less affected, giving the class its selective toxicity.

Generations

  • First generation (e.g., nalidixic acid, 1962): narrow coverage of Gram-negative urinary pathogens.
  • Second generation (e.g., ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin): broader Gram-negative coverage, including Pseudomonas, and some Gram-positive activity.
  • Third generation (e.g., levofloxacin): enhanced Gram-positive (pneumococcal) activity—"respiratory fluoroquinolones".
  • Fourth generation (e.g., moxifloxacin): added anaerobic coverage.

Adverse effects and regulation

Fluoroquinolones carry risks of tendinitis and tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, central nervous system effects, QT prolongation, dysglycaemia, and aortic aneurysm or dissection. Since 2008 the US Food and Drug Administration has added a series of boxed warnings, and in 2016 it advised reserving fluoroquinolones for conditions without alternative treatments, such as uncomplicated urinary tract infections, sinusitis, and bronchitis. The European Medicines Agency likewise restricted their use in 2018–2019. Widespread use has also driven antimicrobial resistance, including MRSA and multidrug-resistant gonorrhoea and tuberculosis strains.

Uses

Indications include complicated urinary tract and kidney infections, prostatitis, anthrax exposure, certain sexually transmitted infections, community-acquired pneumonia (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), and drug-resistant tuberculosis regimens. Ciprofloxacin is a standard prophylactic for exposure to Bacillus anthracis.
