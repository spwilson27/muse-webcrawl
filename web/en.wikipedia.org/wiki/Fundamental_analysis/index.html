Fundamental analysis

Fundamental analysis
FieldFinance, investing
GoalEstimating a security's intrinsic value
Key inputsFinancial statements, economic indicators, industry conditions

Fundamental analysis, in accounting and finance, is the analysis of a business's financial statements (usually to analyse the business's assets, liabilities, and earnings), health, and competitors and markets. It also considers the overall state of the economy and factors including interest rates, production, earnings, employment, GDP, housing, manufacturing and management. The goal is to estimate a security's intrinsic value and compare it with its market price to make buy, hold or sell decisions.

There are two basic approaches: top-down analysis, which begins with the global economy, then narrows to sectors and companies, and bottom-up analysis, which begins with a specific business regardless of its industry or region.

Methods

Analysts examine a company's income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement, computing ratios such as price-to-earnings (P/E), return on equity, and debt-to-equity. Valuation models include discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis, dividend discount models, and comparisons of multiples with peer companies. The approach is associated with Benjamin Graham and David Dodd, whose 1934 book Security Analysis founded the discipline, and with value investors such as Warren Buffett.

Contrast with technical analysis

Fundamental analysis differs from technical analysis, which forecasts price movements from historical trading data and chart patterns rather than from underlying economic and financial factors. Critics influenced by the efficient-market hypothesis argue that publicly available fundamental information is already reflected in prices.
