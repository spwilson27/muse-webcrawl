History of the telescope

The history of the telescope begins with the first practical refracting telescopes constructed in the Netherlands in 1608. Spectacle maker Hans Lipperhey applied for a patent for a device "for seeing things far away as if they were nearby", and Jacob Metius and Zacharias Janssen were also associated with early designs.

Galileo and the refracting telescope

Hearing of the invention, Galileo Galilei built improved versions in 1609 with magnifications of up to about 20× and turned them on the heavens. His observations — the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, lunar mountains, and sunspots, published in Sidereus Nuncius (1610) — provided powerful support for the Copernican system. Johannes Kepler proposed an improved two-convex-lens design, the Keplerian telescope.

Reflecting telescopes

To avoid chromatic aberration, Isaac Newton built the first practical reflecting telescope in 1668, using a curved mirror. Reflectors dominated large-instrument astronomy thereafter. William Herschel built enormous reflectors, including a 40-foot telescope (1789), and discovered Uranus in 1781. In the nineteenth century, Lord Rosse's 72-inch "Leviathan of Parsonstown" revealed the spiral structure of galaxies.

Modern telescopes

The 100-inch Hooker telescope at Mount Wilson (1917) enabled Edwin Hubble's discovery of the expanding universe. The 200-inch Hale Telescope at Palomar followed in 1948. Space observatories such as the Hubble Space Telescope (1990) escaped atmospheric distortion, while ground-based giants like the Keck telescopes used segmented mirrors and adaptive optics. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, observes in the infrared.
