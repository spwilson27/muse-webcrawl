Tepal

A tepal is one of the outer parts of a flower (collectively the perianth) when the petals and sepals are not clearly differentiated — that is, when the flower's undifferentiated perianth segments are neither distinctly petal-like nor sepal-like, or when the two whorls closely resemble one another in colour and form.

Usage

The term is used for flowers in which the perianth consists of two similar whorls, as in lilies, tulips, and many other monocotyledons, and in some basal angiosperms such as magnolias. In a tulip, for example, the six coloured perianth segments are all tepals, there being three outer and three inner. Where the segments differ, the outer whorl consists of sepals (forming the calyx) and the inner whorl of petals (forming the corolla).

The word is an anagram of "petal" and was introduced into botanical usage in the 19th century (often attributed to Augustin Pyramus de Candolle). Some botanists distinguish between sepaloid and petaloid tepals when one whorl is greener or smaller than the other, and older terminology described such flowers with "perianth segments" or "perigone" leaves.
