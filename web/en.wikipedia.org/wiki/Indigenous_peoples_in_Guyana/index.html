Indigenous peoples in Guyana

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Indigenous peoples in Guyana, commonly called Amerindians, are the original inhabitants of the territory of present-day Guyana. They number roughly 78,000–90,000 people, about 10 per cent of the national population, and belong to nine recognised nations. Amerindians live predominantly in the forested interior and savannah regions, in more than 160 titled villages and communities, as well as in coastal towns and the capital, Georgetown.

The nine nations

The nine recognised peoples are the Arawak (Lokono), Wai-Wai, Macushi, Patamona, Arecuna (Pemon), Akawaio, Wapishana, Warrau and Carib (Kalina). The Macushi, Wapishana and Patamona are prominent in the Rupununi savannahs of the south; the Warrau are associated with the northwestern wetlands; the Wai-Wai live in the far south near the Brazilian border.

Land rights

Land titling is governed by the Amerindian Act of 2006, which provides for village lands to be held communally under the leadership of elected councils headed by a Toshao. Titling has been controversial: the Amerindian Peoples Association and village councils have long sought recognition of customary territories larger than the areas titled, and disputes over mining, logging and protected areas on customary lands are recurrent. The National Toshaos Council is the national representative body of village leaders.

Culture and contemporary life

Amerindian Heritage Month is celebrated each September, with a designated heritage village hosting national festivities. Traditional livelihoods include rotational farming (cassava is the staple), fishing, hunting and craftwork such as hammocks, baskets and pottery; many communities now also engage in eco-tourism, notably around the Iwokrama forest and the giant otter and arapaima conservation projects of the North Rupununi. Stephen Campbell, elected in 1957, was the first Amerindian member of the national legislature.
