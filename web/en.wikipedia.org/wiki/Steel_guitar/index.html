Steel guitar is a type of guitar and a method of playing it, in which the pitch is changed by pressing a polished steel bar against the strings rather than by fretting with the fingers. The technique originated in Hawaii in the late 19th century — tradition credits Joseph Kekuku — and spread worldwide in the early 20th century through touring Hawaiian troupes and recordings, becoming a defining sound of country music.

Types

  • Lap steel guitar — held flat on the lap or on a stand, with a raised nut; may be acoustic or electric.
  • Console steel guitar — a multi-neck instrument on legs, without pedals, used in western swing and Hawaiian music.
  • Pedal steel guitar — developed in the 1950s (with key contributions by Bud Isaacs, whose 1954 playing on Webb Pierce's "Slowly" popularised the sound, and by Buddy Emmons and Jimmy Day); foot pedals and knee levers change the tuning of individual strings mid-note, enabling the instrument's characteristic crying, bending chords.
  • Resonator guitar (e.g. Dobro) — played steel-style in bluegrass, using a metal cone for acoustic amplification.

Technique and use

The player stops the strings with a solid bar (the "steel" or tone bar) in one hand and plucks with fingerpicks on the other, producing smooth glissandi impossible on a conventionally fretted guitar. Steel guitar is central to Hawaiian music, country, western swing, bluegrass (as Dobro), gospel ("sacred steel") and has been used in rock by players such as David Gilmour and in the lap steel work of Ben Harper. Noted pedal steel players include Buddy Emmons, Lloyd Green and Robert Randolph.
