Good Friday Agreement

The Good Friday Agreement (GFA) or Belfast Agreement (Irish: Comhaontú Bhéal Feirste; Ulster Scots: Guid Friday Greeance or Bilfawst Greeance) is a pair of agreements signed on 10 April 1998 (Good Friday) that ended most of the violence of the Troubles, a political conflict in Northern Ireland that had prevailed since the late 1960s. It was a major development in the Northern Ireland peace process of the 1990s.

Provisions

The agreement rested on the principle of consent: Northern Ireland would remain part of the United Kingdom unless majorities in both Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland voted for a united Ireland. It created a devolved Northern Ireland Assembly with a power-sharing executive drawn from unionist and nationalist parties; a North/South Ministerial Council for cross-border cooperation; and a British–Irish Council. It also provided for police reform, the decommissioning of paramilitary weapons, prisoner releases, and incorporation of the European Convention on Human Rights into Northern Ireland law. Ireland amended its constitution to drop its territorial claim to Northern Ireland.

Referendums and aftermath

The agreement was approved by referendums in both jurisdictions on 22 May 1998 — by 71% in Northern Ireland and 94% in the Republic. Power-sharing institutions have since been suspended and restored several times, but the agreement is widely regarded as having ended large-scale political violence in Northern Ireland. Its consent principle and cross-border arrangements became central to debates after the United Kingdom's withdrawal from the European Union.
