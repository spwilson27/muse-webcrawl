Free-trade zone

A free-trade zone (FTZ), also known as a foreign-trade zone in the United States, is a geographic area within a country where goods may be imported, stored, handled, manufactured, or re-exported without being subject to customs duties or certain other taxes and regulations. Duties typically become payable only if the goods enter the domestic market of the host country.

Features and purposes

Governments establish free-trade zones — often at ports, airports, or border regions — to attract foreign investment, promote exports, create employment, and encourage technology transfer. Firms operating in a zone may benefit from duty exemption or deferral, simplified customs procedures, and sometimes tax holidays and relaxed regulations. Free-trade zones are one type of special economic zone, a broader category that also includes export processing zones and free ports.

History and examples

Free ports such as Singapore and Hong Kong historically operated as large-scale free-trade areas. The United States created its Foreign-Trade Zones program under the Foreign-Trade Zones Act of 1934. China's special economic zones, beginning with Shenzhen in 1980, used FTZ-type incentives on a large scale and are credited with driving rapid industrialization and export growth. Today thousands of zones operate worldwide, particularly in developing and emerging economies.

Criticism

Critics argue that zones can cause revenue losses, encourage a "race to the bottom" in labor and environmental standards, and facilitate smuggling, money laundering, or counterfeit trade where controls are lax. Supporters counter that well-run zones generate jobs and integrate host economies into global supply chains.
