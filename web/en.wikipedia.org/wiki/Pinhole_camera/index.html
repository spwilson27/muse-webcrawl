Pinhole camera
TypeCamera (lensless)
PrincipleCamera obscura — light through a small aperture
RelatedCamera obscura, zone plate camera

A pinhole camera is a simple camera without a lens but with a tiny aperture — a pinhole. Light from a scene passes through the aperture and projects an inverted image on the opposite side of the box, a phenomenon known as the camera obscura effect. Exposure times are typically long, from fractions of a second in bright light to hours in dim conditions.

Principle

Because the aperture is very small, the camera has effectively infinite depth of field: everything from near to far appears in acceptable focus, though images are soft because of diffraction. The optimal pinhole size is proportional to the square root of the focal length (the distance from aperture to film). A pinhole camera's shutter is usually manually operated, and images can be captured on film, photographic paper, or digital sensors.

History

The pinhole phenomenon was described in antiquity — in the Chinese Mozi text (5th century BC) and by Aristotle — and was analysed by the Arab scholar Ibn al-Haytham. Artists used room-sized camera obscuras as drawing aids from the Renaissance onward. Pinhole photography on light-sensitive materials began in the 19th century after the invention of photography, and the technique enjoyed revivals as an art form in the 20th century.

Modern use

Pinhole cameras are popular for education and artistic photography, valued for their soft images and extremely long exposures that blur moving subjects. They are also used in solargraphy — exposures lasting months that record the sun's path across the sky — and in simple X-ray and gamma-ray imaging where lenses cannot be used.
