First Aliyah

The First Aliyah (Hebrew: העלייה הראשונה, HaAliyah HaRishona), also known as the Farmers' Aliyah, was a major wave of Jewish immigration (aliyah) to Ottoman Syria (later Mandatory Palestine), which lasted from 1882 to 1903. Jews who migrated in this wave came mostly from Eastern Europe and from Yemen. An estimated 25,000 to 35,000 Jews immigrated in this period, though a large proportion later left.


First Aliyah pioneers in an agricultural settlement, 1880s.

Background

The immigration was driven by antisemitic pogroms in the Russian Empire following the assassination of Tsar Alexander II in 1881, by restrictive legislation such as the May Laws of 1882, and by the rise of the Hovevei Zion ("Lovers of Zion") movement, which promoted Jewish settlement in the Land of Israel. Leon Pinsker's pamphlet Auto-Emancipation (1882) argued that Jews could achieve dignity only through national self-determination. Yemenite Jews also arrived beginning in 1881–82, motivated by messianic expectation and worsening conditions.

Settlement

The immigrants founded agricultural colonies (moshavot), many of which survive as Israeli towns: Rishon LeZion, Rosh Pinna, Zikhron Ya'akov, Petah Tikva (re-established), Rehovot, Gedera, Hadera, and Nes Tziona, among others. The Bilu movement, a small group of idealistic young pioneers founded in Kharkov in 1882, symbolised the new settlement ethos, though its members were few.

The colonies struggled with malaria, lack of agricultural experience, Ottoman restrictions on immigration and land purchase (notably the 1882 restrictions and the "red slip" entry permits), and financial difficulty. Many survived only through the subsidies of Baron Edmond de Rothschild, whose administration bought land, planted vineyards and built wineries at Rishon LeZion and Zikhron Ya'akov, but whose paternalistic management also created tensions with the settlers. Eliezer Ben-Yehuda's revival of Hebrew as a spoken language also took root in this period.

Legacy

Although many First Aliyah immigrants left, those who remained established the agricultural and institutional foundations later built upon by the Second Aliyah (1904–1914). The period is regarded as the beginning of modern Zionist settlement in Palestine.
