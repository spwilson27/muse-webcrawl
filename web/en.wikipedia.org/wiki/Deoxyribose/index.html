Deoxyribose, or more precisely 2-deoxyribose, is a monosaccharide with the formula C5H10O4. Its name indicates that it is a deoxy sugar, meaning that it is derived from the sugar ribose by loss of an oxygen atom. Since the pentose sugars arabinose and ribose differ only in stereochemistry at C2′, deoxyribose and deoxyarabinose are equivalent, although the latter term is rarely used because ribose, not arabinose, is the precursor to deoxyribose.

Structure

In aqueous solution, deoxyribose exists predominantly as a mixture of cyclic forms: the five-membered furanose ring (as found in DNA) and the six-membered pyranose ring. The absence of the 2′ hydroxyl group is a defining feature; in DNA it makes the polymer far more chemically stable than RNA, because there is no neighbouring hydroxyl to attack the phosphodiester backbone.

Biological importance

Deoxyribose is a key component of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA): alternating deoxyribose and phosphate groups form the sugar–phosphate backbone, with the nucleobases adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine attached to the 1′ carbon of each sugar. Phoebus Levene identified deoxyribose in 1929 as a component of nucleic acids.
