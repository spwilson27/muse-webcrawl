Salience network

The salience network (also called the midcingulo-insular network) is a large-scale network of the human brain, anchored in the anterior insula and the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, with subcortical nodes in the amygdala, substantia nigra/ventral tegmental area and thalamus. First delineated from resting-state functional MRI studies in 2007, it is thought to detect behaviourally relevant ("salient") stimuli and to switch the brain between the internally oriented default mode network and the task-focused central executive network.

Anatomy and function

The anterior insula — particularly the right fronto-insular cortex, which contains large spindle-shaped von Economo neurons — is regarded as the network's hub, integrating interoceptive, emotional and cognitive information. According to the influential "network switching" model, the salience network identifies the most relevant internal and external stimuli and initiates the engagement of executive resources while disengaging the default mode network.

Clinical significance

The salience network was identified in studies of frontotemporal dementia, in which the anterior insula and anterior cingulate degenerate early — the same regions that atrophy in that disease. Abnormal salience-network connectivity has since been reported in schizophrenia (where it may contribute to misattribution of salience to irrelevant stimuli), anxiety disorders, depression and autism, making it a major focus of research network neuroscience.
