Hong Kong Basic Law

The Basic Law of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region of the People's Republic of China is a national law of China that serves as the constitutional document of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR). It was adopted on 4 April 1990 by the National People's Congress and came into effect on 1 July 1997, when sovereignty over Hong Kong was transferred from the United Kingdom to China.

Background

The Basic Law implements the Sino-British Joint Declaration of 1984, in which China pledged that Hong Kong's capitalist system and way of life would remain unchanged for 50 years under the principle of "one country, two systems." It was drafted between 1985 and 1990 by a committee including both mainland and Hong Kong members; the drafting process was shaken by the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests, after which several Hong Kong drafters were expelled and the final text included provisions—such as national security legislation under Article 23—reflecting Beijing's concerns.

Contents

The Basic Law has 160 articles in nine chapters, plus three annexes. It guarantees Hong Kong a high degree of autonomy in all matters except foreign affairs and defence; preserves the common law legal system, an independent judiciary with the power of final adjudication, free markets, freedom of speech, press, assembly, and religion; and states that the socialist system shall not be practised in the HKSAR. Article 45 and Article 68 name the "ultimate aim" of selecting the Chief Executive and all Legislative Council members by universal suffrage. Article 158 vests the power of final interpretation in the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress, which has issued several controversial interpretations, and Article 159 governs amendment.

Debates and developments

Disputes over the pace of democratisation, NPCSC interpretations, and national security have dominated Hong Kong politics since 1997. After the 2019–2020 protests, Beijing imposed the Hong Kong National Security Law in 2020 by adding it to Annex III of the Basic Law, bypassing Hong Kong's own legislature. Critics argue such measures have eroded the autonomy the Basic Law promised; Beijing maintains they fall within its constitutional authority.
