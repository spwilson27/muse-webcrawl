Jean Moulin

Jean Pierre Moulin (20 June 1899 – 8 July 1943) was a French civil servant and hero of the French Resistance in World War II. As prefect of Chartres he refused German orders in 1940, then, as Charles de Gaulle's delegate, unified the Resistance movements of the southern zone and chaired the first meeting of the National Council of the Resistance in 1943. Arrested and tortured by Gestapo officer Klaus Barbie, he died without betraying his comrades.

Jean Moulin
Born: 20 June 1899, Béziers, France
Died: 8 July 1943, near Metz (on a train to Germany)
Known for: Unifying the French Resistance

Early career

Born in Béziers, son of a professor, Moulin served briefly in 1918, studied law, and rose rapidly in the prefectoral corps, becoming France's youngest prefect, at Chartres in Eure-et-Loir in 1939. He aided Spanish Civil War refugees and worked with minister Pierre Cot.

Resistance

In June 1940 Moulin refused to sign a German document falsely blaming Senegalese troops for atrocities; beaten and imprisoned, he attempted suicide by cutting his throat — thereafter wearing a scarf to hide the scar — and was dismissed by Vichy. In London in late 1941 de Gaulle charged him (code names Rex, Max) with coordinating the Resistance. Parachuted into Provence in January 1942, he founded the Mouvements Unis de la Résistance and in May 1943 presided over the Conseil National de la Résistance in Paris, uniting movements, parties and unions behind de Gaulle.

Arrest and death

Betrayed at a meeting in Caluire on 21 June 1943, Moulin was tortured by Klaus Barbie in Lyon and Paris and died on a train bound for Germany. In 1964 his ashes were transferred to the Panthéon in a ceremony immortalised by André Malraux's oration. He remains the emblematic martyr of the French Resistance.
