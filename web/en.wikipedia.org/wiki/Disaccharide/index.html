Disaccharide

A disaccharide (also called a double sugar or biose) is the sugar formed when two monosaccharides are joined by a glycosidic bond. Like monosaccharides, disaccharides are simple sugars soluble in water. Three common examples are sucrose, lactose, and maltose.

Disaccharide
Type: Carbohydrate
Examples: Sucrose, lactose, maltose
Consists of: Two monosaccharide units

Classification

There are two different types of disaccharides: reducing disaccharides, in which one monosaccharide retains a free hemiacetal unit that can act as a reducing aldehyde group; and non-reducing disaccharides, in which the components bond through an acetal linkage between their anomeric centres, leaving neither with a free hemiacetal unit.

Cellobiose and maltose are examples of reducing disaccharides; sucrose and trehalose are examples of non-reducing disaccharides.

Formation

The formation of a disaccharide from two monosaccharides proceeds by a dehydration (condensation) reaction in which a molecule of water is removed. Hydrolysis, for example by disaccharidase enzymes or dilute acid, breaks the glycosidic bond and releases the constituent monosaccharides, which cells can use for energy.

Common disaccharides

  • Sucrose — glucose + fructose; table sugar, found in sugar cane and sugar beet.
  • Lactose — glucose + galactose; the sugar in milk.
  • Maltose — glucose + glucose; formed during starch digestion and germination of grains.
  • Cellobiose — glucose + glucose with a β(1→4) linkage; a repeating unit of cellulose.
  • Trehalose — glucose + glucose; found in fungi, insects and some plants.

Properties and digestion

Disaccharides are generally sweet, crystalline and water-soluble. In the human digestive system, specific enzymes in the small intestine — sucrase, lactase and maltase — hydrolyse them before absorption. Lactose intolerance results from deficiency of lactase.
