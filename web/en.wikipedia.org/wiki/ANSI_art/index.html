ANSI art

Classic ANSI artwork built from the IBM code page 437 character set.

ANSI art is a computer art form that emerged in the 1980s and flourished on dial-up bulletin board systems (BBSes) in the late 1980s and early 1990s. It is created from a set of 256 characters in the code page 437 character set, combined with ANSI escape sequences — chiefly ANSI.SYS under MS-DOS — that control text color, blinking, and cursor position.

Characteristics

ANSI art uses the block and box-drawing characters of code page 437 (such as ░ ▒ ▓ █ and ═ ║) together with sixteen foreground colors and eight background colors to produce shading, logos for BBSes and art groups, and elaborate full-screen pictures. The standard canvas was 80 columns wide, with height theoretically unlimited, enabling huge "scrolling" works. Files carried the extension .ANS, often accompanied by SAUCE metadata records describing title, author, and group.

Scene

ANSI artists organized into rival art groups that released monthly "artpacks". The most famous groups included ACiD Productions (founded 1990), iCE Advertisements, and later Blocktronics. Tools such as ACiDDraw, TheDraw, and PabloDraw were used to draw and animate ANSI screens. As the World Wide Web displaced BBSes in the mid-1990s, the ANSI scene declined, but it survives as part of the text art and demoscene communities, with new packs still released and ANSI art displayed via modern viewers and BBS revivals.

Related forms

ANSI art is closely related to ASCII art, which uses only printable ASCII characters, and to PETSCII art on Commodore machines and Amiga ASCII/ANSI styles, which used different fonts and shading conventions.
