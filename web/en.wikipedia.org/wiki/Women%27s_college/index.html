Women's college

A women's college is an institution of higher education where enrollment is primarily or exclusively composed of women. Such colleges were founded in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries to provide women with access to higher education when most universities excluded them, and a number continue to operate today, particularly in the United States.

History

Early women's colleges included Mount Holyoke (founded 1837) and the institutions that became known as the "Seven Sisters": Barnard, Bryn Mawr, Mount Holyoke, Radcliffe, Smith, Vassar and Wellesley. In Britain, Girton and Newnham at Cambridge and Lady Margaret Hall and Somerville at Oxford were founded for women in the late nineteenth century.

Today

From the 1960s onward, many formerly single-sex institutions became coeducational; Vassar admitted men in 1969 and Radcliffe merged into Harvard. Remaining women's colleges argue that they offer distinctive leadership opportunities and supportive environments, and many participate in consortia with nearby coeducational universities.
