First Report on the Public Credit

The First Report on the Public Credit was a report issued by Alexander Hamilton, the first United States Secretary of the Treasury, on 14 January 1790, as part of the Hamiltonian economic program. The First Report dealt with public debt and other matters related to public finance, and addressed the way in which both the foreign and domestic debts of the United States and its states should be satisfied. In it, Hamilton proposed funding the national debt at par, assumption by the federal government of the states' war debts, and refinancing through new bonds backed by federal tax revenues.

Background

At the time of the report, the United States owed roughly $54 million (including interest) from the Revolutionary War, and the individual states owed about $25 million more. Hamilton argued that "a national debt, if it is not excessive, will be to us a national blessing", tying the creditworthiness of the new nation to its willingness to honor obligations.

Proposals and controversy

Key elements included:

  • Funding the foreign debt in full.
  • Redeeming domestic debt securities at face value — controversial because many original holders, including soldiers, had sold their certificates to speculators at steep discounts. James Madison proposed "discrimination" between original and current holders; Congress rejected it.
  • Federal assumption of state debts, which passed only as part of the Compromise of 1790, in exchange for situating the permanent capital on the Potomac River.

The program, together with the excise tax and Hamilton's later reports on a national bank and manufactures, established American public credit, but its favoritism toward creditors and the Northeast deepened sectional and partisan divisions that produced the first American party system.
