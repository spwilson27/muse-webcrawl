Challenger expedition

The Challenger expedition of 1872–1876 was a scientific programme that made many of the discoveries that laid the foundation of oceanography. The expedition was named after the mother vessel, HMS Challenger.

Background

Prompted by the success of earlier dredging work by Charles Wyville Thomson and William Benjamin Carpenter, the Royal Society of London obtained the use of Challenger, a 2,306-ton steam-assisted corvette, from the Royal Navy. The ship was refitted with laboratories, dredging platforms and extensive stores of specimen jars and alcohol for preservation.

Voyage

The expedition travelled nearly 70,000 nautical miles, crossing the Atlantic, Pacific and Southern Oceans. At 354 stations the crew took soundings, dredged the sea floor, trawled for specimens and recorded temperatures, currents and water chemistry. The deepest sounding, near the Mariana Islands, found depths of about 4,475 fathoms (8,184 m), in the area now known as the Challenger Deep.

Results

The expedition catalogued over 4,000 previously unknown species and collected some 13,000 specimens. The fifty-volume Report on the Scientific Results of the Voyage of H.M.S. Challenger, edited after Thomson's death by John Murray, was published between 1880 and 1895 and remains a landmark of marine science. The expedition disproved the "azoic theory" that life could not exist below 300 fathoms, and dredged up the manganese nodules later of great commercial interest.

Legacy

The Challenger expedition is regarded as the founding voyage of modern oceanography. Many oceanographic institutions, ships and features — including the Space Shuttle Challenger — have been named in its honour.
