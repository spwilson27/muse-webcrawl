Distant reading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Distant reading is an approach to literary studies that applies computational and quantitative methods to large corpora of texts, in contrast to close reading of individual works. Instead of examining a small canon intensively, distant reading studies thousands or millions of books at once using graphs, maps, trees, and statistical models, seeking patterns in literary history that are invisible at the scale of a single text.

Origins

The term was coined by the Italian literary scholar Franco Moretti, notably in his 2000 essay "Conjectures on World Literature" and the book Graphs, Maps, Trees (2005). Moretti argued that literary history had focused on a tiny fraction of published literature – the canon – while the "great unread" majority of books went unexamined; distance, he wrote, is "a condition of knowledge". At Stanford he founded the Literary Lab, whose pamphlets applied quantitative methods to questions such as the evolution of novel genres and the geography of narrative.

Methods

Distant reading draws on the digital humanities: text mining, topic modelling, stylometry, word-frequency analysis, network analysis of characters and places, and visualisation. Large digitised collections, such as Google Books and HathiTrust, made corpora of unprecedented size available for analysis.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether patterns extracted by counting can answer interpretive questions, whether the corpora are representative (they over-represent canonical, Anglophone, and male authors), and whether graphs and models can obscure as much as they reveal. Defenders reply that distant and close reading are complementary, and that quantitative evidence can confirm, complicate, or refute claims long made on impressionistic grounds.
