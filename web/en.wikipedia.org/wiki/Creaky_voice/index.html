Creaky voice

Image: Spectrogram showing the irregular pulses of creaky phonation (placeholder)

Creaky voice (also known as vocal fry, laryngealisation, or pulse phonation) is the lowest vocal register, produced when the vocal folds are relaxed and pressed close together so that air bubbles through them with a low-frequency, irregular popping or rattling sound. In phonetics, creaky voice is one of the phonation types, situated below modal voice on the scale of glottal opening, and it can serve as a contrastive feature in some languages.

Phonetics and physiology

During creaky phonation the arytenoid cartilages are drawn together, the vocal folds are slack and compact, and only short segments of the folds vibrate. Fundamental frequency is very low and irregular, often around 20–70 pulses per second. Creakiness is represented in the International Phonetic Alphabet by a subscript tilde (◌̰) and the feature can spread over vowels, sonorants or entire syllables.

In languages

Creaky voice is phonemically contrastive in a number of languages, including Zapotec languages of Mexico, Hmong, Jalapa Mazatec, and Danish, where the related stød prosody involves laryngealisation. It is also an allophonic and sociolinguistic feature in many varieties of English, where it frequently appears at the ends of utterances.

Sociolinguistics

In contemporary English, "vocal fry" became a widely discussed speech trait in the 2010s, associated in the media particularly with young women in North America. Studies have found that creak occurs in speakers of all genders and has long existed in various dialects (for example among some British male speakers). The public debate around vocal fry — including criticism that it harms speakers' perceived authority and counter-arguments that such criticism is discriminatory — has made it a touchstone in discussions of linguistic prejudice.
