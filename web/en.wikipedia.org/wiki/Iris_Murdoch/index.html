Dame Jean Iris Murdoch (15 July 1919 – 8 February 1999) was an Irish-born British novelist and philosopher. She is best known for her novels about good and evil, sexual relationships, morality and the power of the unconscious. Her first published novel, Under the Net (1954), was selected in 1998 for the Modern Library's list of the 100 best English-language novels of the 20th century.

Her 1978 novel The Sea, the Sea won the Booker Prize. In 1987 she was made a Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire. In 2008, The Times ranked Murdoch twelfth on a list of "The 50 greatest British writers since 1945".

Life

Murdoch was born in Dublin to Irish parents and raised in London. She studied classics, ancient history and philosophy at Somerville College, Oxford, and philosophy as a postgraduate at Newnham College, Cambridge, where she encountered Ludwig Wittgenstein's influence through her teachers. During the Second World War she worked for the Treasury and afterwards for the UN relief agency UNRRA in Europe.

From 1948 to 1963 she taught philosophy at St Anne's College, Oxford. Her philosophical works include Sartre: Romantic Rationalist (1953), The Sovereignty of Good (1970) and Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals (1992). Her moral philosophy emphasises attention — the just and loving perception of other people — drawing on Simone Weil.

Novels

Murdoch wrote 26 novels, including The Flight from the Enchanter (1956), The Bell (1958), A Severed Head (1961), The Italian Girl (1964), The Black Prince (1973), The Sacred and Profane Love Machine (1974), The Sea, the Sea (1978) and The Book and the Brotherhood (1987). Her fiction frequently features intricate plots, enclosed communities, power-seeking "enchanter" figures and philosophical dialogue.

Later life and legacy

Murdoch married the literary critic John Bayley in 1956. She was diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease in the 1990s and died in Oxford in 1999. Bayley's memoir Elegy for Iris (1998) was adapted into the film Iris (2001).
