Pope John XXII (Latin: Ioannes XXII; 1244 – 4 December 1334), born Jacques Duèze, was head of the Catholic Church from 7 August 1316 until his death in December 1334. He was the second of the Avignon popes, and his long reign was marked by administrative and financial reform, a bitter conflict with the Holy Roman Emperor Louis IV, the controversy over Franciscan poverty, and the dispute over the Beatific Vision.

Early life and election

Jacques Duèze was born at Cahors in southwestern France, the son of a shoemaker or banker, and studied law at Montpellier and Orléans. He rose through the Church under the Angevin kings of Naples, becoming bishop of Fréjus and then of Avignon, chancellor of Naples, and, in 1312, cardinal-bishop of Porto. After the death of Clement V in 1314, the conclave at Carpentras broke down in discord for more than two years. Finally, through the efforts of Philip, Count of Poitiers (later Philip V of France), the cardinals were assembled at Lyon, where on 7 August 1316 they elected the elderly Duèze, reportedly as a compromise candidate expected to live only a short time. He instead reigned for more than eighteen years.

Administration and finance

John settled the papal court permanently at Avignon and organised the curia into an efficient administrative machine. He systematised papal taxation, expanded the system of reservations and annates, founded the papal treasury's accounting procedures, and accumulated a large reserve — his treasury was estimated at his death at sums that made him one of the richest rulers in Europe. He reorganised dioceses in southern France, founded universities' faculties, patronised canon lawyers, and issued the collection of canon law known as the Extravagantes Ioannis XXII. He also canonised Thomas Aquinas in 1323.

Conflict with Louis IV

John claimed the right to approve the election of the Holy Roman Emperor and to administer the empire during a vacancy. When Louis of Bavaria defeated his rival Frederick of Austria at Mühldorf in 1322 without seeking papal approval, John excommunicated him. Louis responded in the Sachsenhausen Appeal and marched on Rome, where in 1328 he had the antipope Nicholas V installed. Thinkers at Louis's court, notably Marsilius of Padua (in the Defensor pacis, 1324) and William of Ockham, produced radical arguments limiting papal power. The antipope eventually submitted to John in 1330, but Louis remained excommunicate.

The poverty controversy

John came into conflict with the Franciscan order over the doctrine of the absolute poverty of Christ and the apostles. Radical "Spiritual" Franciscans who defied papal authority were condemned, and four were burned at Marseille in 1318. In the bulls Ad conditorem canonum (1322) and Cum inter nonnullos (1323), John rejected the order's arrangement of corporate poverty and declared heretical the claim that Christ and the apostles owned nothing. The Franciscan minister general Michael of Cesena fled Avignon to Louis's court, carrying the dispute's theological and political stakes across Europe.

Beatific Vision controversy

In sermons delivered in 1331–1332, John proposed the opinion that the souls of the just do not enjoy the full Beatific Vision of God until after the Last Judgment. The opinion provoked a storm of criticism, especially from the University of Paris and King Philip VI of France. On his deathbed, John submitted his view to the judgment of the Church; his successor Benedict XII defined the orthodox doctrine in the bull Benedictus Deus (1336). John died at Avignon on 4 December 1334, aged about ninety — among the oldest popes in history.
