Hedonism

Hedonism refers to a family of views about what is good for a person, centred on pleasure and the absence of pain. In philosophy, axiological hedonism holds that pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic bad; prudential hedonism applies this to human welfare; motivational hedonism (psychological hedonism) is the descriptive claim that people in fact act only to seek pleasure and avoid pain; and normative (ethical) hedonism claims that pleasure is what we ought to pursue. In ordinary speech, "hedonism" often denotes the unreflective pursuit of sensual pleasures.

Ancient philosophy

The earliest systematic hedonists were the Cyrenaics, followers of Aristippus of Cyrene (c. 435–356 BCE), who taught that immediate bodily pleasure is the goal of life. Epicurus (341–270 BCE) developed a subtler version: because overindulgence produces pain, the wise person seeks ataraxia (tranquillity) and aponia (absence of pain) through moderation, friendship and philosophy. Epicureanism was long caricatured as crude indulgence, a reading Epicurus's surviving letters contradict.

Modern developments

Hedonism underlies classical utilitarianism: Jeremy Bentham proposed a "hedonic calculus" for weighing pleasures and pains, while John Stuart Mill distinguished higher (intellectual) from lower (bodily) pleasures. In the 20th century, hedonism about well-being was challenged by Robert Nozick's experience machine thought experiment, which asks whether we value only pleasant experiences or also contact with reality.

Psychology

Related concepts in psychology include the pleasure principle of Freudian theory and the distinction between hedonic well-being (positive affect and life satisfaction) and eudaimonic well-being (meaning and self-realisation), both studied in positive psychology.
