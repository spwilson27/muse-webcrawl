Altmetrics

Altmetrics are alternative metrics for assessing the impact of scholarly works, proposed as a complement or alternative to traditional citation-based measures such as the impact factor and h-index. The term, a contraction of "alternative metrics", was coined around 2010 and popularised by the Altmetrics Manifesto (Priem et al., 2010). Altmetrics track attention to research outputs in sources such as social media, news media, policy documents, blogs, reference managers (e.g. Mendeley), Wikipedia, and online recommendation systems.

Indicators and providers

Well-known aggregators include Altmetric (whose multicoloured "donut" badge and Altmetric Attention Score appear on many publisher pages), Plum Analytics (part of Elsevier, with PlumX metrics), and Crossref Event Data. Indicators are often grouped into captures (saves, downloads), mentions (news, blogs, tweets), social media activity, and citations in non-traditional sources such as patents and policy documents.

Uses and criticism

Supporters argue that altmetrics give faster feedback than citations (which take years to accumulate), capture societal as well as academic impact, and can cover outputs beyond journal articles, such as datasets, software, and presentations. Critics note that online attention is easily gamed, varies greatly in quality (a retweet is not an endorsement), is biased toward topics with popular appeal, and depends on commercial platforms whose data and algorithms may change. Consequently, funders and institutions generally treat altmetrics as complementary "attention" data rather than measures of research quality, consistent with initiatives such as the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA) and the Leiden Manifesto.
