Class struggle

In political philosophy, the class struggle is the conflict of interests and political tension that exists in a society divided into social classes. The concept is central to Marxism: Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels famously opened The Communist Manifesto (1848) with the declaration that "the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."

Marxist theory

In Marxist theory, society is divided by relations of production into classes with opposing interests — for example, slaves and masters in antiquity, serfs and lords under feudalism, and the bourgeoisie and proletariat under capitalism. Because the exploiting class appropriates surplus value produced by the exploited class, their interests are irreconcilable, and conflict — through strikes, revolts, and revolutions — is the motor of historical change. Marxists hold that class struggle under capitalism will ultimately lead to the overthrow of the bourgeoisie and a classless, communist society.

Earlier and other views

Historians before Marx, including French liberal historians of the Restoration such as François Guizot and Augustin Thierry, analysed history in terms of conflict between social orders. Anarchists such as Mikhail Bakunin also placed class struggle at the centre of their thought, while later sociologists debated how far class conflict explains social change compared with factors such as status, party, and culture.

Criticism

Critics argue that the concept oversimplifies social divisions, that class identities compete with national, religious, and ethnic loyalties, and that the predicted intensification of class struggle under capitalism did not occur in the form Marx expected. Nonetheless, the term remains widely used in political discourse to describe conflicts over wages, welfare, taxation, and inequality.
