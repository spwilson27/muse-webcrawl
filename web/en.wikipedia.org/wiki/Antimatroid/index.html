Antimatroid

In mathematics, an antimatroid is a formal system that describes the ordering in which a set of elements can be built up one element at a time, by a process in which each new element to be added has certain prerequisites that must be satisfied first. Antimatroids were introduced by Edsger Dijkstra in the context of his analysis of a system for authoring documents, and were subsequently studied in combinatorics and discrete mathematics in connection with partially ordered sets, greedoids, and convex geometries.

Feasible sets of an antimatroid form a lattice under union and intersection

Definition

An antimatroid can be defined as a family of sets, called feasible sets, satisfying two axioms: the family is closed under union (the union of any two feasible sets is feasible), and it satisfies an accessibility property—every nonempty feasible set has an element whose removal leaves a feasible set. Equivalently, an antimatroid can be described by a set of finite sequences, called words, closed under taking prefixes and satisfying a property that allows the orderings to be extended step by step.

Characterizations

Sequences in the sequence-based formulation are required to have no repeated symbols, to be closed under prefixes, and to satisfy the property that if two sequences contain elements not shared by the other, either sequence can be extended by the first unshared element of the other. The maximal words of an antimatroid, those that cannot be extended, are permutations of the ground set, and the feasible sets are exactly the sets of elements appearing as prefixes of these words.

Antimatroids are equivalent to convex geometries, a dual notion defined by families of "convex" sets closed under intersection, in much the same way that matroids relate to other closure systems.

Examples

The feasible orderings generated by a partial order—linear extensions respecting given precedence constraints—form an antimatroid. Other examples arise from shelling orderings in computational geometry, from tree search processes, and from chordal graphs, where perfect elimination orderings define antimatroids.
