Aspergillosis

Cause: Aspergillus species, usually A. fumigatus
Specialty: Infectious disease, pulmonology
Risk factors: Immunosuppression, chronic lung disease, asthma, cystic fibrosis

Aspergillus hyphae invading tissue, as seen under the microscope.

Aspergillosis is a group of infections caused by fungi of the genus Aspergillus, most commonly Aspergillus fumigatus. The mould is ubiquitous in soil, decaying vegetation and indoor environments, and its airborne spores (conidia) are inhaled daily by most people without ill effect. Disease occurs mainly in people with weakened immune systems or pre-existing lung damage, though allergic forms affect people with asthma or cystic fibrosis.

Forms of disease

  • Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) — an allergic reaction in the airways causing wheeze, cough and worsening asthma control.
  • Aspergilloma — a "fungus ball" growing within a pre-existing lung cavity, which may cause coughing of blood.
  • Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis — slowly progressive lung destruction, often following tuberculosis.
  • Invasive aspergillosis — a rapidly progressive, often fatal infection of the lungs and other organs in severely immunocompromised patients, such as those undergoing chemotherapy, organ transplantation or treatment with high-dose corticosteroids.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines imaging (chest CT often shows nodules or a "halo sign" in invasive disease), culture and microscopy of respiratory samples, detection of the fungal cell-wall component galactomannan in blood or bronchoalveolar fluid, and sometimes biopsy.

Treatment

Triazole antifungals, particularly voriconazole, are the mainstay of treatment for invasive disease; alternatives include isavuconazole, liposomal amphotericin B and echinocandins. Aspergillomas may require surgical resection if they cause serious bleeding. Rising azole resistance in A. fumigatus, linked in part to agricultural fungicide use, is an increasing clinical concern.
