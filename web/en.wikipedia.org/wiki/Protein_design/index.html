Protein design is the rational design of new protein molecules to perform a target function. It spans modifying existing proteins to designing entirely new proteins from scratch (de novo design). Protein design is closely related to, but distinct from, protein engineering, and can be approached computationally — predicting a sequence that will fold into a desired structure — or experimentally, for example through directed evolution.

Rational and computational design

In computational protein design, a target backbone structure is specified and algorithms search sequence space for amino-acid combinations predicted to stabilize that structure, using an energy function to score van der Waals packing, hydrogen bonding, electrostatics and solvation. The Rosetta software suite, developed in David Baker's laboratory, became a standard tool. Early successes included Top7 (2003), the first protein designed to adopt a fold not seen in nature.

Deep learning methods

From about 2020, deep learning transformed the field. Structure-prediction systems such as AlphaFold2 solved the forward folding problem, while inverse-folding networks such as ProteinMPNN generate sequences for a given backbone far more successfully than earlier physics-based methods. Diffusion-based tools such as RFdiffusion generate new backbones, including symmetric oligomers and binders shaped complementary to a chosen target. These tools have produced designed enzymes, self-assembling nanoparticles and minibinder proteins against viral targets.

Applications

Designed proteins are used as vaccine components (including nanoparticle vaccines for respiratory viruses), as enzymes for green chemistry and plastic degradation, as biosensors, as components of protein nanomaterials, and as potential therapeutics such as de novo cytokine mimetics and designed binding proteins. David Baker shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for computational protein design.

Challenges

Accurate modelling of dynamics, catalysis, allostery and binding specificity remains difficult, and experimentally validating designs is essential because computational predictions are imperfect. Designs must also avoid aggregation and, for therapeutics, immunogenicity.
