Myth of the clean Wehrmacht

The myth of the clean Wehrmacht (German: Mythos der sauberen Wehrmacht) is the false notion that the regular German armed forces of Nazi Germany, the Wehrmacht, were not involved in the Holocaust or other war crimes, and fought an honourable war, with atrocities attributed solely to the Waffen-SS and other Nazi organizations. Historians have thoroughly debunked the myth: the Wehrmacht's leadership planned and, together with the SS, implemented the criminal "war of annihilation" (Vernichtungskrieg) against the Soviet Union, participated in mass murder of Jews and other civilians, and was responsible for the deaths of millions of Soviet prisoners of war.

Origins

The myth was constructed after 1945 by former senior Wehrmacht officers. At the Nuremberg trials, generals blamed Hitler and the SS for criminal orders, and memoirs by figures such as Erich von Manstein (Lost Victories, 1955) and Heinz Guderian presented the army as apolitical and honourable. The 1950 Himmerod memorandum, written by former officers for West German rearmament, demanded public rehabilitation of the Wehrmacht's honour as a condition of creating a new German army, and senior Wehrmacht figures cooperated with the US Army Historical Division, shaping early Cold War historiography of the Eastern Front.

The historical record

Research — especially from the 1970s onward, drawing on German military archives — established the Wehrmacht's complicity in detail:

  • The Barbarossa Decree (1941) removed military jurisdiction over crimes against Soviet civilians.
  • The Commissar Order directed the summary execution of captured Soviet political officers.
  • Army units cooperated closely with the Einsatzgruppen in mass shootings of Jews, including logistical support and participation.
  • Some 3.3 million of 5.7 million captured Soviet POWs died in Wehrmacht custody from starvation, exposure, and shooting.
  • The "anti-partisan" war in the Balkans and the USSR killed hundreds of thousands of civilians in reprisals.

Public confrontation

The myth persisted in West German public memory for decades. Its decisive public collapse came with the Hamburg Institute for Social Research's exhibition Vernichtungskrieg. Verbrechen der Wehrmacht 1941 bis 1944 ("War of Annihilation: Crimes of the Wehrmacht 1941 to 1944"), opened in 1995. Although criticism of some photo captions forced its temporary withdrawal and revision, the revised exhibition (2001) confirmed the core findings, and the exhibition drew hundreds of thousands of visitors and extensive debate.
