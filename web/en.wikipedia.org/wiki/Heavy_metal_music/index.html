Heavy metal music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A heavy metal guitarist on a festival stage in a wall of amplifier light, long hair down, mid-riff]

Heavy metal (or simply metal) is a genre of rock music that developed in the late 1960s and early 1970s, largely in the industrial English Midlands and the United States, from blues rock and psychedelia thickened into a massive sound: distorted guitars, emphatic beats, extended solos, and lyrics and imagery of power, fantasy and darkness.

Black Sabbath (formed in Birmingham in 1968; debut 1970) are the genre's founding band; Led Zeppelin and Deep Purple complete the usual unholy trinity of origins. Judas Priest stripped the blues away and added twin guitars and leather; Iron Maiden and the New Wave of British Heavy Metal (c. 1979–83) globalised it; Metallica and thrash, death and black metal, nu metal and metalcore mark the main subsequent waves.

Culture

Metal sustains the most loyal subculture in popular music — the denim-and-patches uniform, the 'metal horns' hand sign (popularised by Ronnie James Dio), headbanging, and festivals like Wacken Open Air that draw tens of thousands to a German village. Its history carries recurring moral panics (the 1980s PMRC hearings in the US, satanic-panic trials) that research has never substantiated and fans wear as battle honours.

Musicianship

Beneath the volume sits conservatory-grade technique: classical influences from Ritchie Blackmore through Yngwie Malmsteen to progressive metal's odd metres, and a guitar culture that prizes virtuosity as heavy rock's original audiences never demanded. Metal is now among the world's most global genres, with major scenes from Indonesia to Botswana.
