London's lost rivers

The subterranean rivers of London are the tributaries of the River Thames and the River Lea that were built over during the growth of the city. Known as London's lost rivers, they now flow through underground culverts, many of them incorporated into London's combined sewer system, though their courses are still traced by street patterns, place names and the occasional dip in the road.

Principal rivers

The largest is the River Fleet, which rises on Hampstead Heath and flows via Camden Town, King's Cross and Farringdon to join the Thames at Blackfriars; the valley of the Fleet gave its name to Fleet Street and the former Fleet Prison. The Walbrook ran through the City of London itself, dividing it into two hills, Cornhill and Ludgate Hill. West of the City, the Tyburn flowed through Marylebone — the area around Tyburn Road (now Oxford Street) took its name from it — and the Westbourne ran from Hampstead through Hyde Park, where it fed the Serpentine, to Chelsea. South of the Thames, the Effra flowed from Norwood through Brixton to Vauxhall, and the Neckinger ran through Bermondsey.

Covering

As London expanded, the rivers became open sewers and were progressively arched over from the seventeenth century onward; the Fleet was culverted in stages after the Great Fire era and finally covered in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. In the 1860s Joseph Bazalgette's intercepting sewer network absorbed most of their flows. Several "lost rivers" survive partly in the open in suburban reaches, and there have been campaigns to "daylight" sections of rivers such as the Effra and the Quaggy.

In culture

The hidden rivers have inspired psychogeographical writing and urban exploration; Nicholas Barton's The Lost Rivers of London (1962) remains the standard survey, and the rivers feature in works by Iain Sinclair and in Ben Aaronovitch's Rivers of London novels.
