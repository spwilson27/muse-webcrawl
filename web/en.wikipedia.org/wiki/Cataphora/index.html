Cataphora

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, cataphora is the use of a word or phrase that refers to a word or phrase used later in a sentence. It is a kind of endophora, and stands in contrast to anaphora, in which the referring expression points backwards to an antecedent that has already appeared. The later expression to which a cataphor points is known as its postcedent.

Examples

A classic example is: "When he arrived home, John went to bed." Here the pronoun he precedes the name John to which it refers. Other examples include "Because he was tired, John went to bed early" and "If you want it, you can buy the car."

Comparison with anaphora

Anaphora is far more common in most languages: the antecedent normally appears before the referring expression. Cataphora is more restricted and often produces a marked or literary effect, frequently used for suspense in narrative writing: "Out of the darkness stepped he — the stranger everyone had been waiting for." Cataphoric structures are also common in subordinate clauses placed before main clauses, where the pronoun in the subordinate clause refers forward to a noun phrase in the main clause.

Constraints

Cataphora is subject to structural constraints. A pronoun cannot generally refer forward to a noun phrase that structurally dominates it; for example, "He said that John was tired" cannot have he referring to John in the way anaphora would allow. Such restrictions are studied under binding theory in generative grammar.
