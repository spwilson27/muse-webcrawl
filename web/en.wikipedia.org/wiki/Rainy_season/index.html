The rainy season (also known as the wet season) is the time of year when most of a region's average annual rainfall occurs. Generally, the season lasts at least one month. Areas with a pronounced rainy season are found mainly in the tropics and subtropics, where seasonal shifts of prevailing winds and the migration of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) bring alternating wet and dry periods.

Causes

Rainy seasons arise where the ITCZ moves over a region, where monsoon circulations develop — as in South and Southeast Asia, West Africa, northern Australia, and the American Southwest — or where seasonal changes in wind direction bring moist maritime air inland. In the Asian monsoon, the rainy season typically runs from June to September, delivering the bulk of the Indian subcontinent's annual rainfall and filling rivers and reservoirs on which agriculture depends.

Effects

Rainy seasons replenish water supplies and support rain-fed agriculture, but they also bring flooding, landslides, road washouts, and increased incidence of water-borne and mosquito-borne diseases such as malaria and dengue. In savanna regions, the rains trigger the growth of grasses and the seasonal migrations of herbivores. The break or failure of a rainy season can cause drought, crop failure, and food insecurity for hundreds of millions of people.

Terminology

In some cultures and languages the rainy season has a specific name: tsuyu in Japan, changma in Korea, and meiyu in China describe the East Asian early-summer rains, while the North American monsoon is locally called the monzón. Mediterranean climates invert the pattern, with a winter rainy season and dry summers.
