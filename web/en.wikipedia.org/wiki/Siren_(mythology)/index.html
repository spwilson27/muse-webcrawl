Siren (mythology)

In Greek mythology, the Sirens (Greek: Σειρῆνες, Seirênes) were dangerous creatures who lured nearby sailors with their enchanting music and singing voices to shipwreck on the rocky coast of their island. In early Greek art they were represented as birds with large women's heads, bird feathers and scaly feet, though later they were often depicted as beautiful women, and in Roman and medieval traditions they merged with mermaids.

Mythology

The Sirens' most famous appearance is in Homer's Odyssey, where Odysseus, warned by Circe, had his crew plug their ears with wax and ordered himself to be bound to the mast so that he alone could hear their song safely as the ship passed. In the Argonautica of Apollonius of Rhodes, Jason and the Argonauts escaped the Sirens because the musician Orpheus drowned out their song with more beautiful music. Later traditions gave the Sirens names — among them Parthenope, Ligeia, and Leucosia — and various parentages, usually as daughters of the river god Achelous and a Muse. One myth held that they were companions of Persephone punished for failing to prevent her abduction by Hades.

Interpretation

Ancient and modern commentators have treated the Sirens as symbols of dangerous temptation, forbidden knowledge, or the perils of the sea. Their island was variously located near Capri and the Sirenuse islands off the coast of Italy. The word entered later languages in the phrase "siren song", meaning an irresistible but dangerous appeal, and lent its name to the sirenians (manatees and dugongs) through an association with mermaids.
