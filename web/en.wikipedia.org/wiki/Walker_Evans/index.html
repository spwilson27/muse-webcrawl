Walker Evans

Walker Evans (November 3, 1903 – April 10, 1975) was an American photographer best known for his work for the Farm Security Administration (FSA) documenting the effects of the Great Depression. Much of Evans's work from the FSA period uses the large-format, 8×10-inch camera. He is celebrated for a detached, "documentary style" that influenced generations of photographers.

Career

Evans dropped out of Williams College, spent a year in Paris intending to write, and took up photography in New York around 1928. Early subjects included New York skyscrapers and Victorian architecture. From 1935 he worked for the Resettlement Administration / Farm Security Administration under Roy Stryker, travelling through the American South.

In 1936 Evans and writer James Agee spent a summer with three sharecropper families in Hale County, Alabama; the resulting book, Let Us Now Praise Famous Men (1941), contains some of the most famous portraits in photography, including that of Allie Mae Burroughs. His 1938 Museum of Modern Art exhibition American Photographs was the museum's first devoted to a single photographer. From 1938 to 1941 he secretly photographed passengers on the New York subway (published in 1966 as Many Are Called). He later worked for Fortune magazine and taught at Yale, experimenting in his final years with the Polaroid SX-70.
