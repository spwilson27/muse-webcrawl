Cushing's syndrome is a collection of signs and symptoms caused by prolonged exposure to high levels of the hormone cortisol. It is named after neurosurgeon Harvey Cushing, who first described a case in 1932.

Signs and symptoms

Characteristic features include rapid weight gain concentrated in the trunk and face ("moon face" and a "buffalo hump" between the shoulders), thinning skin that bruises easily, purple stretch marks (striae) on the abdomen, muscle weakness, hypertension, elevated blood sugar or diabetes, osteoporosis, and mood changes such as depression and irritability.

Causes

The most common cause overall is the long-term use of glucocorticoid medicines such as prednisone. Endogenous Cushing's syndrome arises when the body itself produces too much cortisol: most often from a pituitary adenoma secreting ACTH (in which case it is called Cushing's disease), less often from an adrenal tumour or from ectopic ACTH production by a tumour elsewhere, such as a small-cell lung cancer.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis uses tests such as the overnight dexamethasone suppression test, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, and late-night salivary cortisol, followed by imaging to locate the source. Treatment usually aims to remove or shrink the responsible tumour surgically; medications that block cortisol synthesis, and sometimes radiotherapy, are alternatives. Untreated, the syndrome carries serious cardiovascular and metabolic risks.
