The Rainbow Serpent or Rainbow Snake is a common deity in the mythology and art of Aboriginal Australian peoples. It is named after the obvious identification between the shape of a rainbow and the shape of a snake. The serpent is also part of the beliefs of some peoples in West Africa (in the form of the loa Aida-Weddo) and has parallels elsewhere, but this article concerns the Australian being.

The Rainbow Serpent is one of the most important ancestral beings of the Dreaming (Tjukurrpa). Typically a creator god, the serpent travelled across the primordial landscape, shaping mountains, valleys and waterways, creating waterholes and establishing law, language and ceremony. In many stories the serpent is associated with water, rain, rivers, fertility and the cycle of the seasons, and can be both a benevolent protector and a dangerous, punishing force when sacred law is broken.

Names and regional forms

Because Aboriginal Australia comprises hundreds of language groups, the Rainbow Serpent is known by many names. In Arnhem Land the Kunwinjku call the female serpent Ngarrny, while the Yolngu know the serpent Witj, the great olive python associated with the Lardil and the Wawilak sisters myth. In the Kimberley the being is associated with Wunggurr; among the Warumungu of Central Australia it is Wollunqua. Other names include Julunggul, Galeru, Yurlungur, Eingana and Almudj.

Mythology

In many Dreaming stories the Rainbow Serpent emerged from beneath the earth at the beginning of time and created the features of the land as it moved. A widespread cycle, recorded in Arnhem Land, tells of the Wawilak (Wawalag) sisters, whose pollution of the serpent's waterhole provoked it to swallow them and their children; the serpent then rose into the sky and became the first rainbow. Similar swallowing-and-regurgitation myths link the serpent to initiation rites, in which novices are symbolically swallowed and reborn.

The serpent is closely bound to water: it dwells in deep permanent waterholes, guards them from pollution, and its movement is seen in storms, floods and the rainbow that follows rain. Sorcerers and healers in some traditions were said to obtain quartz crystals and power from the serpent.

Rock art

Depictions of serpent-like figures in the rock art of Arnhem Land date back as far as 6,000 years or more, and some scholars have suggested that the imagery developed as the climate changed after the last Ice Age and the rainbow became a more prominent symbol of the wet season. Rainbow Serpent imagery remains central to contemporary Aboriginal art.
