Fungus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fungus (plural fungi) is any member of the kingdom Fungi: eukaryotic organisms that include yeasts, moulds, rusts, mushrooms and toadstools. Fungi digest food externally, secreting enzymes and absorbing the products; their cell walls contain chitin, and most grow as networks of thread-like hyphae (a mycelium). They are more closely related to animals than to plants, despite long being classified with plants.

Fungi are the planet's great decomposers and recyclers: without them, dead wood and leaves would accumulate indefinitely. Described species number around 150,000, but estimates of the true total run to 2–5 million.

Roles

Ecologically, fungi form mycorrhizal partnerships with most land plants, create lichens with algae, and cause diseases of plants (rusts, blights — the Irish potato famine was a fungus-like oomycete) and animals (chytrid fungi devastating amphibians; Candida and Aspergillus in weakened human patients).

Uses

Humans use fungi directly as food (mushrooms, truffles), in fermentation (yeast for bread and beer, moulds for cheese and soy sauce), and as sources of medicines — penicillin and other antibiotics, statins, ciclosporin. Industrial mycology also produces enzymes, citric acid and biofuels.
