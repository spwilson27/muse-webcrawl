Monogram Pictures Corporation was an American film studio that operated between 1931 and 1953, producing low-budget films — often called "Poverty Row" productions. Monogram specialised in Westerns, action pictures, mysteries and series films, including the Charlie Chan, Bowery Boys, Cisco Kid and Bomba the Jungle Boy series. In 1953 its parent reorganised its prestige output under the name Allied Artists Pictures Corporation, and the Monogram name was retired in 1979.

History

Monogram was formed in 1931 by W. Ray Johnston from earlier companies, and merged briefly into Republic Pictures in 1935 before being re-established independently in 1937. During the 1940s the studio released hundreds of inexpensive features shot in a week or two, providing the second half of double bills. Stars included John Wayne in his early Western period, Boris Karloff in the Mr. Wong series, and Leo Gorcey and Huntz Hall of the Bowery Boys, whose 48 films (1946–1958) were among the studio's most profitable.

Allied Artists

Seeking higher-budget prestige pictures, the company created the Allied Artists label, which released such films as Friendly Persuasion (1956) and Love in the Afternoon (1957), while Monogram-branded B-pictures continued until 1953. Allied Artists later produced Cabaret (1972), Papillon (1973) and The Man Who Would Be King (1975) before financial decline and bankruptcy in 1979–80.
