The Tal Committee was an Israeli public committee appointed in 1999 by Prime Minister Ehud Barak and headed by retired Supreme Court Justice Zvi Tal. It was charged with finding a legal arrangement for the long-standing exemption of Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) yeshiva students from compulsory military service—an arrangement dating to the state's founding, when about 400 students were exempted, but which by the 1990s covered tens of thousands and had become one of Israel's most contentious issues of equality in the burden of national service.

Recommendations and the Tal Law

The committee reported in April 2000, recommending that the deferral of service for full-time yeshiva students continue but be codified in statute, with changes to encourage eventual enlistment or civilian national service: at age 23, a student could take a "year of decision" to work without losing his status, then choose between returning to study, shortened military service, or civilian service. The Knesset enacted the recommendations as the Tal Law in July 2002, as a temporary measure renewable every five years.

Criticism and invalidation

The law was criticised by secular Israelis and reservists' movements as entrenching inequality, and enrolment in the alternative service tracks remained low. In February 2012 the High Court of Justice ruled the Tal Law unconstitutional, holding that it failed to meet the standard of equality and had not achieved its aims; it expired in August 2012. The problem of Haredi conscription has remained unresolved through successive governments, commissions (such as the Plesner Committee) and laws, and the High Court ruled in 2024 that the state must enforce conscription of Haredi men in the absence of a valid exemption law.
