State religion

A state religion (also called an established religion, state church, or official religion) is a religion or creed officially endorsed by a sovereign state. A state with an official religion, while not necessarily a theocracy, may restrict or privilege religious practice, fund the favoured church through taxation, and give its clergy a formal role in government. The degree of establishment varies widely, from purely ceremonial recognition to legal systems substantially shaped by religious law.

Christian establishments

The Church of England is the established church of England: the monarch is its Supreme Governor, bishops sit in the House of Lords, and its measures require parliamentary approval. The Church of Scotland is Scotland's national church, though with recognised spiritual independence. Lutheran churches were long established in Scandinavia: Denmark retains the Evangelical Lutheran Church as its official church (Folkekirken), Norway loosened its establishment in 2012–2017, and Sweden disestablished the Church of Sweden in 2000. Greece's constitution recognises Eastern Orthodoxy as the "prevailing religion", Iceland maintains the Evangelical Lutheran Church as its state church, and Roman Catholicism is the official religion of countries such as Costa Rica, Liechtenstein, Malta and Monaco, and holds special status in several others.

Other religions

Islam is the state religion of many countries in the Middle East and North Africa and of Malaysia, Pakistan and Bangladesh, with varying consequences for law and public life; Judaism is constitutionally bound up with the identity of Israel as a Jewish state, though without a single formally "established" denomination. Buddhism holds official or privileged status in Bhutan (as spiritual heritage), Cambodia, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Myanmar. Some states combine official recognition with guarantees of religious freedom, while officially secular states — such as France under laïcité or the United States under the Establishment Clause — constitutionally forbid an established religion.

History

State religions are as old as organised states: cults of civic gods characterised ancient Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece and Rome, and the Christianisation of the Roman Empire under Theodosius I (380 AD) made Nicene Christianity the imperial religion. The Reformation entrenched the principle cuius regio, eius religio — the ruler's religion determined the subject's. Since the 18th century the global trend has been toward disestablishment and constitutional religious freedom, though established churches persist in many democracies.
