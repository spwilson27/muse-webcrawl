Lipid raft

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lipid rafts are small (10–200 nm), heterogeneous, highly dynamic, sterol- and sphingolipid-enriched domains within the plasma membrane that compartmentalise cellular processes. They can be visualised as "rafts" of tightly packed, more ordered lipids floating in a sea of more loosely packed, disordered membrane lipids, and they concentrate particular proteins while excluding others.

Properties and composition

Rafts are enriched in cholesterol and sphingolipids such as sphingomyelin and glycosphingolipids, which pack together into a liquid-ordered phase. Resident proteins often carry lipid anchors — glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors on the outer leaflet, or palmitoyl and myristoyl groups on the inner leaflet. Caveolae, the flask-shaped invaginations built around the protein caveolin, are considered a specialised, stabilised form of raft.

Function

By corralling receptors and signalling molecules, rafts serve as organising centres for signal transduction — for example in T-cell and B-cell receptor signalling, growth-factor pathways and neurotransmitter systems. They also matter in membrane trafficking and as entry or exit portals for pathogens: influenza, HIV and SARS-CoV-2 interact with raft domains, and the cholera toxin binds the raft ganglioside GM1.

History and controversy

The raft model grew out of the fluid mosaic model of the membrane and was articulated by Kai Simons and Elina Ikonen in 1997. Because resting rafts are below the resolution of light microscopy and very short-lived, their existence in living cells was long debated; evidence from super-resolution microscopy, single-molecule tracking and lipidomics now supports small, transient rafts that can be stabilised into larger platforms during signalling.
