Christianity in Japan is among the nation's minority religions, practised by roughly one percent of the population. Christianity was introduced by Portuguese Jesuit missionaries in 1549, suppressed and driven underground during the Edo period, and re-established after Japan reopened in the 19th century.

[Hero image: Ōura Church in Nagasaki, Japan's oldest surviving Christian church — image placeholder]

Early missions and persecution

Francis Xavier arrived at Kagoshima in 1549, and missions won converts including daimyō in Kyushu; by 1600 there may have been 300,000 Japanese Christians. Toyotomi Hideyoshi issued an expulsion edict in 1587, and in 1597 twenty-six Christians were crucified at Nagasaki (the Twenty-six Martyrs of Japan). Under the Tokugawa shogunate Christianity was banned outright; suspected believers were forced to trample on images of Christ or Mary (fumi-e), and after the Shimabara Rebellion (1637–38) the faith was driven underground. "Hidden Christians" (Kakure Kirishitan) preserved baptised, disguised practices for over two centuries.

Reopening and growth

When Japan reopened in the 1850s, Catholic missionaries built the Ōura Church in Nagasaki (1864), where hidden Christians revealed themselves in 1865. The ban was lifted in 1873. Protestant missionaries founded schools and universities, and figures such as Uchimura Kanzō (founder of the Nonchurch movement) and Nitobe Inazō shaped Meiji intellectual life. The Russian Orthodox mission of St Nicholas of Japan built the Tokyo Resurrection Cathedral ("Nikolai-dō").

Present day

Today most Japanese Christians are Protestant, with substantial Catholic and smaller Orthodox communities. Christian influence exceeds the numbers of adherents through Christian schools and universities, and customs such as Christmas celebrations and Western-style weddings are widely adopted culturally. Notable Japanese Christians include the novelist Endō Shūsaku, whose novel Silence explores the persecution era.
