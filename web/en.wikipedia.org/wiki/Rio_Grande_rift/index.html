The Rio Grande rift is a north–south trending continental rift zone in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico, extending roughly 1,000 km from central Colorado through New Mexico to the El Paso region and into Chihuahua. It separates the Colorado Plateau on the west from the interior of the North American craton (the Great Plains) on the east, and its chain of deep sedimentary basins channels the Rio Grande, which flows southward through the rift axis.

Geology

Extension began around 30 million years ago, during the mid-Cenozoic ignimbrite flare-up, and proceeded in phases, producing a series of asymmetric half-grabens — including the San Luis, Española, Albuquerque and Socorro basins — filled with several kilometres of sediment and volcanic rock of the Santa Fe Group. Flanking mountain ranges such as the Sangre de Cristo, Sandia and Franklin Mountains are the uplifted footwalls of rift faults. Volcanism associated with the rift includes the Jemez lineament and the Valles Caldera, a major supervolcano-forming caldera; the crust beneath the rift is thinned and underlain by hot, buoyant mantle, and the Socorro area hosts a mid-crustal magma body. Modest extension and seismicity continue today, monitored by GPS.

Human significance

The rift's basins hold the region's aquifers and most of New Mexico's population, including Albuquerque and Santa Fe, as well as geothermal resources. Its long record of sedimentation preserves important fossil mammals and a history of the ancestral Rio Grande.
