Industrial democracy

Industrial democracy is an arrangement in which workers participate in decision-making, share responsibility, and hold authority within their place of work. The term covers a wide spectrum of practices, from consultation and works councils to full worker self-management and worker cooperatives, all intended to give employees a voice in decisions that affect their working lives — counterbalancing the authority of owners and managers.

Forms

The most institutionalised form is codetermination (German: Mitbestimmung), under which employees elect representatives to company supervisory boards and works councils; it is legally established in Germany and several other European countries. Other forms include collective bargaining through trade unions, employee ownership and share schemes, participative management and quality circles, and, at the strongest end, self-management in which workers elect and can recall their managers, as practised historically in Yugoslavia and in some cooperative firms.

History

Demands for democracy in industry grew with the labour movement of the 19th and early 20th centuries. Thinkers such as Beatrice and Sidney Webb popularised the term, guild socialists proposed worker-controlled industries, and after the Second World War codetermination was adopted in West Germany, first in coal and steel (1951) and later across larger companies. Debates continued in Britain in the 1970s, notably around the Bullock Report of 1977, which proposed worker directors but was never implemented.

Arguments

Supporters argue that participation improves morale, productivity, and the quality of decisions, and that democratic rights should not stop at the factory gate. Critics argue that it blurs managerial accountability, slows decision-making, and conflicts with owners' property rights. Research generally finds modest positive effects of participation on satisfaction and productivity, depending heavily on how genuine the participation is.
