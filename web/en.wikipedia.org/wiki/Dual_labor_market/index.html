Dual labour market

A dual labour market (also called a segmented labour market) is the division of the labour market into two largely non-competing sectors: a primary sector of relatively well-paid, secure jobs with good working conditions and prospects for advancement, and a secondary sector of low-paid, insecure jobs with poor conditions and little mobility into the primary sector.

FieldLabour economics, sociology
Key proponentsPeter Doeringer, Michael Piore
Key workInternal Labor Markets and Manpower Analysis (1971)

Theory

The theory was developed around 1970 by economists including Peter Doeringer and Michael Piore. It holds that labour markets do not operate as a single competitive market in which workers move freely to better jobs. Instead, primary-sector employment is organised through internal labour markets within firms, with ports of entry, promotion ladders and job security, while secondary jobs — in small firms, casual work and low-wage services — offer little training or progression.

Evidence and implications

Segmentation is associated with persistent wage inequality that cannot be explained by education or skill alone: similar workers earn very different wages depending on the sector and employer. Access to primary jobs can be rationed by credentials, discrimination, geography and social networks, which helps explain the concentration of women and minority workers in secondary employment.

Criticism

Critics argue that the boundary between sectors is blurred, that human capital and compensating differentials explain much of the observed pattern, and that the "gig economy" has produced intermediate forms of work that fit neither category neatly.
