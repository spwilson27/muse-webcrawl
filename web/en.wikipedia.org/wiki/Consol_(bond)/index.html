Consol (bond)

[Hero image placeholder: engraved British government consol certificate]

In the United Kingdom, consols (originally short for consolidated annuities) are a form of government bond, redeemable at the option of the government, dating originally from the 18th century. The first consolidated annuities were issued in 1751. They consolidated the government's many debts into a single perpetual annuity paying interest indefinitely.

History

Consols originated in 1751 as the Consolidated Fund, replacing earlier annuities issued during the War of the Austrian Succession. For much of the 18th and 19th centuries, consols were the principal vehicle of British public debt and the benchmark security of the London capital market. In the Victorian era, the interest rate was reduced several times: William Ewart Gladstone's conversion of 1853 and George Goschen's conversion of 1888 reduced the coupon from 3% to 2.75% and then to 2.5%.

Valuation

Because consols are perpetual bonds with no maturity date, their value depends only on the coupon and the prevailing long-term interest rate: the price of a consol paying an annual coupon c when the market yield is r is c/r. Consols are often used as textbook examples of perpetual cash flows and form part of the classic formula for valuing annuities in perpetuity.

Redemption

In 2014–2015, the British government redeemed all remaining consols and other undated gilts, ending more than 260 years of history. The final outstanding issues, including the 2.75% consols and other Victorian-era undated stock, were redeemed at par by the UK Debt Management Office.
