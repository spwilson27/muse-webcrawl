Expropriative anarchism

Expropriative anarchism (Spanish: anarquismo expropiador) is the name given to a practice of theft, robbery, and fraud carried out by anarchist affinity groups in Argentina and Spain during the early 20th century, justified as a form of direct expropriation of the bourgeoisie and as a means of financing revolutionary activity. Its most famous practitioner was the Spanish-Argentine anarchist Severino Di Giovanni, and groups such as Los Solidarios in Spain, which included Buenaventura Durruti and Francisco Ascaso, engaged in bank robberies attributed to this tendency.

History

The practice emerged in the 1920s amid intense labour conflict and state repression. In Argentina, expropriators robbed banks, payrolls, and businesses, funding anarchist newspapers, prisoner aid, and strikes; some proceeds were also used for personal survival under conditions of clandestinity. In Spain, Los Solidarios carried out the famous 1923 robbery of the Bank of Spain in Gijón. The tactic was controversial within the anarchist movement itself: many syndicalists condemned it as banditry that discredited the cause, while its defenders argued that reclaiming wealth from capitalists was legitimate revolutionary expropriation.

Legacy

Expropriative anarchism influenced later currents of insurrectionary anarchism and illegalism, and its episodes—such as Di Giovanni's campaign and execution in 1931—remain prominent in the historiography of anarchism in the Spanish-speaking world.
