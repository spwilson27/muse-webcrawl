Stellar classification

In astronomy, stellar classification is the classification of stars based on their spectral characteristics. Electromagnetic radiation from the star is analyzed by splitting it with a prism or diffraction grating into a spectrum exhibiting the rainbow of colors interspersed with spectral lines. Each line indicates an ion of a certain chemical element, with variation in line strength indicating the temperature of the star.

Morgan–Keenan classification

The modern Morgan–Keenan (MK) system assigns stars to spectral classes using the letters O, B, A, F, G, K, and M, a sequence from the hottest (O type) to the coolest (M type). Each class is subdivided by a numeric digit, 0 being hottest and 9 coolest (so A8, A9, F0, F1 form a sequence from hotter to cooler). A luminosity class (I through V) is appended to indicate size and brightness.

History

The scheme descends from the Harvard classification developed at Harvard College Observatory in the late 19th and early 20th centuries by Edward Pickering and the "Harvard Computers," including Williamina Fleming, Antonia Maury, and Annie Jump Cannon, who classified hundreds of thousands of stars for the Henry Draper Catalogue. The order O B A F G K M is remembered by mnemonics such as "Oh, Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me."

Extended classes

Additional classes include L, T, and Y for brown dwarfs cooler than M stars; W for Wolf–Rayet stars; and C and S classes for carbon-rich and related evolved stars. The Sun is classified as a G2 V main-sequence star.
