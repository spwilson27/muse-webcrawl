The Dilbert Principle is a 1996 book by Scott Adams, creator of the comic strip Dilbert. A satirical examination of modern business management, the book was a number-one New York Times bestseller and one of the most popular business humor books of the 1990s.

The book expands on a concept Adams first articulated in a 1995 Wall Street Journal article: the Dilbert Principle holds that companies tend to systematically promote incompetent employees to management positions in order to limit the damage they can do to actual productive work. Adams presents it as an inversion of the Peter Principle, in which employees are promoted to their level of incompetence; under the Dilbert Principle, incompetence is the very qualification for promotion, because managers were never good at the jobs below them in the first place.

Contents

Combining prose essays, Dilbert cartoons, and emails from readers describing real workplace absurdities, the book mocks management fads including business process re-engineering, ISO 9000 certification, mission statements, team building, and downsizing. Adams proposes principles such as "the most ineffective workers are systematically moved to the place where they can do the least damage: management" and advises readers on strategies for coping with office politics.

Reception

The book spent many weeks atop bestseller lists and helped make Adams a sought-after speaker on workplace culture. It was followed by sequels including The Dilbert Future (1997) and The Joy of Work (1998).
