Treaty 8 is one of the Numbered Treaties between the Crown in Canada and First Nations. First signed on 21 June 1899 at Lesser Slave Lake, it covers approximately 840,000 square kilometres — parts of present-day northern Alberta, northeastern British Columbia, northwestern Saskatchewan and the southwest corner of the Northwest Territories. By area it is the largest of all the Numbered Treaties, and more than thirty First Nations are signatories or adherents.

Background

The treaty was prompted by the Klondike Gold Rush of 1897–98, which sent miners streaming through the northern interior, and by official concern that conflict with Indigenous peoples could follow. A government commission travelled through the region in the summer of 1899, signing the treaty at Lesser Slave Lake and taking adhesions from Cree, Dane-zaa (Beaver), Chipewyan, Slavey and other bands at posts across the north; further adhesions followed in 1900 and into the twentieth century.

Terms

The written terms broadly followed those of Treaty 6: surrender of land title in exchange for reserves (smaller per capita than in the southern treaties), annuities, agricultural assistance, and continued rights to hunt, fish and trap throughout the surrendered territory except on lands taken up for settlement, mining or other purposes. First Nations oral tradition emphasizes verbal assurances that their way of life would not be interfered with "as long as the sun shines, the grass grows and the rivers flow".

Legacy

The taking-up clause has been at the heart of major litigation over resource development — including oil sands and hydroelectric projects in northeastern British Columbia — and the Supreme Court of Canada's decisions on Treaty 8 rights have been foundational to modern Aboriginal law. In 2023 British Columbia reached a landmark agreement with Treaty 8 nations on cumulative effects and land restoration.
