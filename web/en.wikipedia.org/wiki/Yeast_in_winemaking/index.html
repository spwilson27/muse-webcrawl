Yeast in winemaking

Yeast in winemaking
Principal speciesSaccharomyces cerevisiae
RoleConverts grape sugars to ethanol and carbon dioxide (alcoholic fermentation)

The role of yeast in winemaking is the most important element distinguishing wine from grape juice. Through alcoholic fermentation, yeasts convert the sugars of grape must — chiefly glucose and fructose — into ethyl alcohol and carbon dioxide, while also producing many secondary compounds that shape a wine's aroma, flavour, and mouthfeel. The dominant species is Saccharomyces cerevisiae, prized for its alcohol tolerance, but many other "non-Saccharomyces" yeasts present in the vineyard and winery participate, especially early in fermentation.

History

Humans made wine for millennia without knowing the agent of fermentation. Louis Pasteur's Études sur le vin (1866) demonstrated that yeast drives alcoholic fermentation and that other microbes spoil wine, laying the foundations of modern oenology. In 1883 Emil Christian Hansen isolated pure yeast cultures at the Carlsberg laboratory, and by the 1890s Hermann Müller-Thurgau had introduced the practice of inoculating musts with selected pure cultures, allowing winemakers predictable fermentations.

Cultured versus ambient fermentation

Most commercial wine is made by inoculating must with a selected dried strain of S. cerevisiae chosen for traits such as alcohol tolerance, temperature range, low hydrogen sulfide production, and aroma enhancement (for example, strains that release varietal thiols in Sauvignon blanc). Alternatively, winemakers may rely on ambient or "wild" fermentation, in which indigenous yeasts on the grapes and cellar equipment begin the ferment; species such as Hanseniaspora, Metschnikowia, and Torulaspora act first before Saccharomyces takes over as alcohol rises. Wild ferments are often credited with greater textural and aromatic complexity but carry higher risk of sluggish or stuck fermentations and off-flavours.

Yeast nutrition and management

Healthy fermentation requires yeast assimilable nitrogen (YAN), vitamins, and oxygen at the outset. Nutrient additions such as diammonium phosphate are common. Temperature control — cooler for whites, warmer for reds — steers both fermentation kinetics and aroma retention. After fermentation, contact with spent yeast (lees) in practices such as sur lie ageing and bâtonnage contributes texture, and in sparkling wines made by the traditional method a second fermentation by yeast in the bottle creates the carbonation, followed by autolysis that gives brioche-like aromas.

Spoilage yeasts

Not all yeasts are welcome: Brettanomyces produces volatile phenols perceived as barnyard or medicinal notes, and Pichia and film-forming yeasts can oxidise wine surfaces. Sulphur dioxide, hygiene, and temperature are the main controls.
