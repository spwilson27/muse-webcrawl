The Itô integral is a fundamental concept in stochastic calculus, introduced by Japanese mathematician Kiyoshi Itô in the 1940s. It defines integration of a stochastic process with respect to Brownian motion (or, more generally, a semimartingale), where the ordinary Riemann–Stieltjes integral fails because Brownian paths have unbounded variation. The integral is constructed by evaluating the integrand at the left-hand endpoint of each subinterval, which makes the integrand non-anticipating — a convention that produces results different from classical calculus and underpins modern mathematical finance.

Construction

For a step process H taking the value Hk on each interval [tk, tk+1), the integral over [0,T] is defined as the sum of Hk(Wtk+1 − Wtk). The definition is extended to general predictable, square-integrable processes by approximation in the L² sense.

Properties

  • Itô isometry: the expected square of the integral equals the expected integral of the squared integrand — E[(∫H dW)²] = E[∫H² dt].
  • Martingale property: with suitable conditions the Itô integral is a martingale, with zero expectation; this reflects the left-endpoint rule, which forbids using future information.
  • Itô's lemma: the chain rule acquires a second-order correction term because Brownian motion has nonzero quadratic variation, [W]t = t; for a smooth f, df(W) = f′(W) dW + ½ f″(W) dt.

Comparison with the Stratonovich integral

The Stratonovich integral evaluates the integrand at interval midpoints, obeys the ordinary rules of calculus, but is not a martingale and corresponds to anticipating half a step ahead. The two are related by a correction involving the quadratic covariation of integrand and integrator; physics and engineering applications often favour Stratonovich, while finance uses Itô because portfolio strategies must be non-anticipating.

Applications

The Itô integral is the building block of Itô stochastic differential equations, used to model diffusions in physics, biology and — most famously — mathematical finance, where the Black–Scholes framework expresses a self-financing trading strategy exactly as an Itô integral with respect to a Brownian-driven stock price.
