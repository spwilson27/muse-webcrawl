The Friedel–Crafts reactions are a set of reactions developed by Charles Friedel and James Mason Crafts in 1877 to attach substituents to an aromatic ring. The two principal types are Friedel–Crafts alkylation and Friedel–Crafts acylation, both proceeding by electrophilic aromatic substitution, typically catalysed by a strong Lewis acid such as aluminium chloride (AlCl₃).

Alkylation

In alkylation, an alkyl halide reacts with an arene in the presence of a Lewis acid to form an alkylbenzene. Limitations include carbocation rearrangements, polyalkylation (because the product is more reactive than the starting material), and failure on strongly deactivated rings such as nitrobenzene.

Acylation

In acylation, an acyl chloride or anhydride introduces a ketone group onto the ring. The acylated product is deactivated, so multiple acylation is avoided, and the intermediate acylium ion does not rearrange — making acylation followed by reduction (e.g. Clemmensen or Wolff–Kishner) the standard route to straight-chain alkylbenzenes.

Importance

Friedel–Crafts chemistry underpins industrial production of ethylbenzene and cumene (feedstocks for styrene and phenol) and remains a fundamental carbon–carbon bond-forming method in organic synthesis.
