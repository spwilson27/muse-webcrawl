Leitmotif

A recurring musical theme associated with a character or idea
OriginGerman: Leitmotiv, "leading motif"
Associated withRichard Wagner
Related termsMotif, idée fixe, theme

A leitmotif (from German Leitmotiv, "leading motif") is a short, recurring musical phrase or melody associated with a particular character, place, object, emotion, or idea in a dramatic work, most famously in opera. Each time the associated element appears or is referred to, the motif is heard, often varied in harmony, rhythm, or orchestration to reflect the dramatic situation.

Although earlier composers used recurring themes, the technique was brought to its fullest development by Richard Wagner in his mature operas, above all the four-opera cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen (1876), which contains dozens of interrelated motifs that combine and transform across some fifteen hours of music.

History

Precursors of the leitmotif appear in the operas of Carl Maria von Weber and in Hector Berlioz's idée fixe in the Symphonie fantastique (1830), a melody that recurs throughout the work representing the beloved. Wagner himself initially resisted the term; the word was popularised by his followers and by the thematic guides of Hans von Wolzogen, who catalogued the motifs of the Ring.

In Wagner's system the motifs are not mere labels. They accumulate meaning through dramatic context, can be combined in counterpoint to suggest relationships, and undergo symphonic development in the orchestra, which functions as a kind of commentator on the action. Well-known examples include the Valhalla motif, the Sword motif, and the motif of Siegfried's horn call.

Use in later music and film

The leitmotif technique passed into late-Romantic opera and the symphonic poem, and from there into film music. Composers such as Max Steiner, Erich Wolfgang Korngold, and later John Williams used leitmotifs extensively; Williams's scores for Star Wars attach themes to characters such as Luke Skywalker, Darth Vader ("The Imperial March"), and the Force itself. Howard Shore's music for The Lord of the Rings films is likewise built on an extensive network of motifs.

Criticism

Critics have sometimes accused mechanical leitmotif usage of reducing music to a labelling system, and the philosopher Theodor W. Adorno mocked the motifs as "calling cards." Defenders reply that in the best hands the technique creates a web of musical memory that deepens dramatic meaning beyond what words alone can say.
