Sandite

Sandite is a substance used on railways in the United Kingdom, Ireland, the Netherlands and Belgium to combat leaves on the line, which can cause train wheels to slide and lose adhesion on the rails. It consists of a mixture of sand, antifreeze and steel shot.

A railhead treatment train applying Sandite

Application

Sandite is applied by special trains — railhead treatment trains — and in some cases by converted passenger trains fitted with dispensing equipment. It is laid on the railhead in autumn, when fallen leaves are crushed by passing wheels into a slippery, low-friction layer. The steel shot in the mixture helps trains to complete track circuits, ensuring correct signalling operation, while the sand improves traction for accelerating and braking trains.

History

Sandite was originally developed and applied by British Rail. After privatisation, its application in Great Britain passed to Network Rail, which operates a fleet of railhead treatment trains each autumn. The operation is part of the annual industry effort to mitigate the disruption caused by leaf fall, which costs the British railway tens of millions of pounds annually in delays and wheel damage (wheel flats) caused by sliding.

Related methods

Other adhesion measures include traditional sandboxes on trains, which drop sand directly in front of the wheels, high-pressure water jetting of the railhead, and laser cleaning systems trialled in the Netherlands and elsewhere.
