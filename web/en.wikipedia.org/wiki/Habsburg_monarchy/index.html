The Habsburg monarchy, also known as the Danubian monarchy or the Austrian monarchy, was the collection of empires, kingdoms, duchies and other polities ruled by the Austrian branch of the House of Habsburg (and, from 1740, by the House of Habsburg-Lorraine). Conventionally dated from 1526, when Ferdinand I acquired Bohemia and Hungary after the Battle of Mohács, the monarchy was not a single unified state but a composite monarchy: a personal — later dynastic — union of territories each retaining its own laws, diets and privileges, bound together by the person of the ruler, the court at Vienna, and common institutions for war and foreign policy.

Formation

The foundations were laid when Rudolf I gained Austria in 1276–1282 and when the family's hereditary lands expanded across the Austrian duchies, Carinthia, Carniola, Tyrol and the Vorlande. The decisive enlargement came in 1526: after Louis II of Hungary and Bohemia died at Mohács fighting the Ottomans, Ferdinand of Austria — brother of Emperor Charles V, and married to Louis's sister Anne — was elected king of Bohemia and of a rump Hungary. The Habsburg, Ottoman and Transylvanian contest for Hungary would define the monarchy's eastern frontier for 170 years.

Confessional age and the Thirty Years' War

The monarchy was a leading power of the Counter-Reformation. Rudolf II moved the imperial court to Prague (1583–1611), making it a centre of art and science. Conflict between the Catholic Habsburgs and the Protestant estates of Bohemia erupted in the Defenestration of Prague (1618), igniting the Thirty Years' War. Imperial victory at White Mountain (1620) was followed by harsh re-Catholicisation of Bohemia and the Renewed Land Ordinance (1627), which made the Bohemian crown hereditary in the Habsburg line.

Baroque empire and the Ottoman wars

The failed Ottoman siege of Vienna (1683) and the ensuing Great Turkish War, led militarily by Prince Eugene of Savoy, brought the reconquest of Hungary and Transylvania, confirmed by the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) and Passarowitz (1718). The monarchy thereby became a great power spanning central and southeastern Europe, ruling peoples speaking German, Czech, Hungarian, Croatian, Italian, Polish, Romanian, Serbian and other languages.

Reform and enlightened absolutism

Charles VI secured international recognition of the Pragmatic Sanction (1713) to allow his daughter to succeed. Maria Theresa (1740–1780), though she lost Silesia to Prussia, centralised administration, reformed taxation, the army and education, and founded a modern bureaucracy. Her son Joseph II (1780–1790) pushed enlightened absolutism further: toleration patents, the abolition of serfdom (1781), and administrative and ecclesiastical reforms, several of which were rolled back after his death.

End

Under pressure from Napoleonic France, Francis II proclaimed the hereditary Austrian Empire in 1804 and dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in 1806. From 1804 historians usually speak of the Austrian Empire, and after the Compromise of 1867 of Austria-Hungary, rather than of the Habsburg monarchy as such; the term nevertheless remains in use for the whole 1526–1918 span of Habsburg rule in central Europe.
