Halophyte

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Halophyte
DefinitionPlant adapted to grow in high-salinity environments
HabitatsSalt marshes, mangrove swamps, salt deserts, coasts
ExamplesSalicornia, mangroves, saltbush, sea beet

A halophyte is a salt-tolerant plant that grows in soil or waters of high salinity, coming into contact with saline water through its roots or by salt spray, such as in saline semi-deserts, mangrove swamps, marshes, and seashores. The word derives from Ancient Greek halas ("salt") and phyton ("plant"). Only a small proportion of plant species — around 0.25% of flowering plants — are halophytes, but they are disproportionately important in coastal and arid ecosystems. Plants that cannot tolerate salt are called glycophytes, which includes almost all crop plants.

Adaptations

Halophytes cope with salt through several strategies. Salt excluders filter out most salt at the roots. Salt accumulators take up salt and sequester it in cell vacuoles, away from sensitive enzymes, often becoming succulent as they dilute the accumulated salt with water. Some species, such as many mangroves and the saltbushes (Atriplex), possess specialised salt glands or bladder hairs that excrete excess salt onto the leaf surface. Other adaptations include reducing leaf area, thick waxy cuticles, sunken stomata, and shedding salt-laden leaves.

Classification

Halophytes are often divided into obligate halophytes, which require saline conditions for optimal growth (many actually grow better with moderate salt), and facultative halophytes, which tolerate salt but grow best without it. Salicornia (glasswort or samphire) species are among the most salt-tolerant land plants known, thriving in seawater-strength salinity.

Uses

Because soil salinisation renders increasing areas of farmland unproductive, halophytes attract interest as alternative crops irrigated with seawater or brackish water. Salicornia bigelovii produces oil-rich seeds and has been trialled as a biofuel feedstock grown in coastal deserts. Some halophytes are eaten directly — samphire is a traditional delicacy — and others serve as forage (saltbush in Australia), in land reclamation, phytoremediation of salt-affected soils, and coastal erosion control (mangroves and Spartina grasses).
