Vascular cambium

The vascular cambium is a lateral meristem in the stems and roots of vascular plants — principally the woody seed plants — that produces secondary xylem (wood) to its inside and secondary phloem (inner bark) to its outside. It is the main growth tissue responsible for the thickening of stems and roots in dicotyledons and gymnosperms, and its seasonal activity produces annual growth rings used in dendrochronology.

Cross-section of a woody stem: the cambium lies between xylem and phloem.

Structure and activity

The cambium is a cylinder of thin-walled meristematic cells of two types: long, spindle-shaped fusiform initials, which give rise to the axial system of vessels, tracheids, fibres and sieve elements, and small ray initials, which produce the radial parenchyma rays that store and transport materials across the stem. In temperate climates the cambium is dormant in winter and reactivates in spring, producing wide, thin-walled earlywood cells and later narrower, thick-walled latewood — the contrast forming a visible annual ring. As secondary xylem accumulates, the cambium is displaced outward; its derivatives also generate the secondary protective tissues indirectly by stimulating formation of the cork cambium (phellogen) further out.

Importance

Nearly all commercial timber is secondary xylem produced by the vascular cambium. Grafting in horticulture succeeds only when the cambial layers of scion and rootstock are aligned so they can fuse. Hormones — especially auxin produced in buds and young leaves — regulate cambial activity, linking shoot growth to wood formation.
