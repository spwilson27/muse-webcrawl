OrderCarnivora
FamilyCanidae

GenusVulpes Frisch, 1775

Vulpes is a genus of the subfamily Caninae, comprising the "true foxes". The best known and most widely distributed member is the red fox (Vulpes vulpes). Species of Vulpes are generally smaller than other canids such as wolves, dogs, and jackals, with longer legs, narrower snouts, bushy tails typically tipped with white, and vertical-slit pupils — features associated with their largely solitary, small-prey hunting lifestyle.

Species

Twelve living species are generally recognised:

  • Red fox (V. vulpes) — the largest and most widespread, native across the Northern Hemisphere
  • Arctic fox (V. lagopus) — circumpolar tundra species (sometimes placed in its own genus Alopex)
  • Fennec fox (V. zerda) — the smallest fox, of the Sahara
  • Kit fox (V. macrotis) and swift fox (V. velox) — North American grassland and desert species
  • Corsac fox (V. corsac) — Central Asian steppes
  • Rüppell's fox (V. rueppellii), pale fox (V. pallida), Blanford's fox (V. cana), and Cape fox (V. chama)
  • Tibetan fox (V. ferrilata) and Bengal fox (V. bengalensis)

Fossil Vulpes species extend the genus back several million years in both the Old and New Worlds.

Behaviour and ecology

Unlike pack-hunting canids, Vulpes foxes typically forage alone, relying on acute hearing to locate rodents beneath grass or snow, which they catch with a characteristic mousing pounce. They are opportunistic omnivores, eating small mammals, birds, invertebrates, fruit, and carrion, and most species cache surplus food. Foxes usually breed once a year, raising litters in underground dens (earths). The name Vulpes is simply the Latin word for "fox".
