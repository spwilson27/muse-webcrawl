Lapita culture

The Lapita culture is the name given to a Neolithic Austronesian people and their material culture, which spread from Island Southeast Asia through Near Oceania into Remote Oceania between about 1600 and 500 BC. The Lapita are widely regarded as the ancestors of the modern Polynesian peoples and of many coastal populations of Melanesia.

Material culture

The culture is named after the site of Lapita in New Caledonia and is recognised archaeologically by its distinctive dentate-stamped pottery — fine earthenware decorated with geometric designs made by pressing toothed implements into the clay — together with shell ornaments, stone adzes and long-distance exchange of obsidian. Lapita settlers carried a portable horticultural economy of taro, yam, banana and breadfruit, and domesticated pigs, dogs and chickens.

Expansion

From an origin generally placed in the Bismarck Archipelago around 1500 BC, Lapita voyagers in outrigger canoes rapidly colonised the Reef/Santa Cruz islands, Vanuatu, New Caledonia, Fiji, Tonga and Samoa by roughly 900–800 BC. After a long pause in West Polynesia, their descendants, the Polynesians, settled the rest of the Pacific. Genetic and linguistic studies associate the initial Lapita expansion with Austronesian-speaking seafarers from Taiwan and the northern Philippines, with later admixture from Papuan-speaking populations in Near Oceania.
