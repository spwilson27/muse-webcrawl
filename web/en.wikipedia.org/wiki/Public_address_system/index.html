Public address system

A public address system (or PA system) is an electronic system comprising microphones, amplifiers, loudspeakers and related equipment used to amplify and distribute sound — typically the human voice, announcements or music — to a large or distant audience. PA systems are installed in schools, railway stations, airports, sports venues, houses of worship, factories and public buildings, and portable systems are used for meetings, performances and rallies.

History

Electrical public address became practical in the 1910s and 1920s with the development of the carbon microphone, vacuum-tube amplifiers and moving-coil loudspeakers. Early large-scale systems carried speeches to crowds of tens of thousands, and by the 1930s PA was standard at political rallies, sporting events and railway stations. The horn loudspeaker, efficient and weatherproof, became the characteristic fitting of outdoor systems, while the "Tannoy" brand name (from a British manufacturer) became a generic term in Britain for a PA system.

Components and design

A basic system consists of one or more input transducers (microphones or line-level sources), a mixer to combine and adjust them, a power amplifier, and loudspeakers arranged to cover the listening area. Design challenges include achieving intelligibility in reverberant spaces, avoiding acoustic feedback between loudspeakers and microphones, and providing even coverage. Distributed systems in large buildings often use high-impedance "70 V" or "100 V line" wiring to feed many ceiling or horn speakers from one amplifier. Modern systems add digital signal processing, automatic mixing, paging zones, and integration with emergency voice-alarm systems, which must remain intelligible and operational during fires.

Applications

  • Announcements — transport hubs, schools and workplaces use paging for information and emergencies.
  • Sound reinforcement — concerts and events use large-scale PA (often called line-array systems) to amplify live performance.
  • Background music and voice alarm — retail and public buildings combine music with evacuation messages.

Feedback

The characteristic squeal of a PA system is acoustic feedback, occurring when amplified sound from a loudspeaker re-enters a microphone and is re-amplified in a loop. It is controlled by microphone and loudspeaker placement, directional pickup patterns and equalisation.
