Extensible Messaging and Presence Protocol

XMPP is an open standard for instant messaging and presence

Former nameJabber

Standards bodyIETF; XMPP Standards Foundation

The Extensible Messaging and Presence Protocol (XMPP, originally named Jabber) is an open communication protocol designed for instant messaging, presence information and contact-list maintenance. Based on XML, it enables the near-real-time exchange of structured data between two or more network entities and, unlike most proprietary messaging platforms, uses a decentralised, federated architecture similar to email.

History

Jeremie Miller began the Jabber project in 1998, releasing the first server in 1999. The protocol was standardised by the IETF under the XMPP name in 2004 (RFC 3920 and RFC 3921), with revised core specifications published as RFC 6120 and RFC 6121 in 2011. The XMPP Standards Foundation coordinates hundreds of extension proposals (XEPs) covering multi-user chat, publish-subscribe, file transfer and voice and video signalling.

Architecture

XMPP networks are decentralised: anyone may run a server, and users are identified by Jabber IDs of the form user@domain, modelled on email addresses. Clients connect to their home server, and servers connect to one another for federation. The three basic stanza types are message, presence and iq (info/query). Security features include TLS encryption and SASL authentication, with end-to-end encryption available through extensions such as OMEMO.

Adoption

XMPP powered early Google Talk and Facebook Chat (with federation later withdrawn) and remains in use in open-source messaging, the Internet of Things, online gaming and military and financial messaging systems that favour interoperable, self-hosted infrastructure.
