Mono Basin

The Mono Basin is an endorheic drainage basin located east of the Sierra Nevada in Mono County, California, United States. It is best known for Mono Lake, a large, shallow saline soda lake at its centre, famous for its limestone tufa towers and its highly productive ecosystem of brine shrimp and alkali flies that supports millions of migratory birds. The basin is one of the longest-lived lake systems in North America, with Mono Lake estimated to be at least 760,000 years old.

Geography and geology

The basin is bounded by the Sierra Nevada to the west, the Bodie Hills to the north, and volcanic highlands to the east and south. It formed by faulting associated with Basin and Range extension. Volcanism has been prominent: the Mono–Inyo Craters chain runs through the basin, and Panum Crater and the lake's islands, Negit and Paoha, are volcanic features. The Long Valley Caldera lies just to the south.

Hydrology

Streams including Rush Creek, Lee Vining Creek, and Mill Creek drain Sierra snowmelt into Mono Lake, which has no outlet; water leaves only by evaporation, concentrating salts and minerals. Beginning in 1941, the city of Los Angeles diverted much of the basin's stream flow into the Los Angeles Aqueduct, causing the lake level to fall by about 45 feet (14 m) and exposing the tufa towers. A landmark 1994 decision by the California State Water Resources Control Board, following the public trust litigation of the Mono Lake Committee, ordered the lake level raised and partially restored the basin's streams.

Ecology

Despite its salinity — roughly two and a half times that of the ocean — Mono Lake supports huge populations of brine shrimp (Artemia monica) and alkali flies, feeding nesting California gulls and migratory phalaropes and grebes. The surrounding basin includes sagebrush steppe, Jeffrey pine forest, and freshwater marsh habitats.
