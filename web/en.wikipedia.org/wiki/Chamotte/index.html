Chamotte

Chamotte granules used as a refractory aggregate

Chamotte (also called grog in English) is a ceramic raw material produced by firing refractory clay to a high temperature and then crushing and grinding it into granules of controlled size. Because the material has already been fired, it shrinks very little when incorporated into a new ceramic body and fired again. Chamotte is widely used in the manufacture of refractories, firebricks, kiln furniture, and studio pottery.

Production

Chamotte is made by calcining fireclay — a clay rich in kaolinite and alumina — in rotary kilns or shaft kilns at temperatures typically between 1,200 °C and 1,500 °C. The fired material is then crushed, milled, and sieved into fractions ranging from fine dust to coarse grains several millimetres across. Depending on the clay source, the alumina content of chamotte ranges roughly from 30% to over 45%.

Properties

The addition of chamotte to a clay body:

  • Reduces drying and firing shrinkage, because the grog particles are already fired and dimensionally stable.
  • Opens the texture of the body, improving drying behaviour and resistance to thermal shock.
  • Increases mechanical strength and abrasion resistance in refractory products.
  • Adds a characteristic speckled texture valued in sculptural and rustic pottery.

Uses

In industry, chamotte is the principal aggregate in fireclay bricks used to line furnaces, kilns, incinerators, and fireplaces, and in refractory castables and mortars. In studio ceramics, potters add grog to throwing and sculptural bodies to improve workability and reduce cracking in thick work. Chamotte is also used in the manufacture of sanitaryware and as a component of some proppants and foundry moulding materials.

Etymology

The word chamotte comes from French and became standard in German and other European ceramics terminology in the 19th century. The English term grog originally referred to crushed, already-fired ceramic material recycled into new bodies.
