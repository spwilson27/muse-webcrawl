Louis, Duke of Burgundy

Louis, Duke of Burgundy (16 August 1682 – 18 February 1712), styled Petit Dauphin (Little Dauphin), was the eldest son of Louis, Grand Dauphin, and the grandson of King Louis XIV of France. He was second in line to the French throne and in 1711, upon the death of his father, became Dauphin of France himself. He died of measles the following year, a few days after his wife, predeceasing his grandfather and leaving his youngest son to inherit the throne as Louis XV.

Early life and education

Louis was born at the Palace of Versailles, the eldest of three sons of the Grand Dauphin. His younger brothers were Philip, who later became King Philip V of Spain, and Charles, Duke of Berry. As a child he was educated under the direction of François Fénelon, Archbishop of Cambrai, who wrote his famous work The Adventures of Telemachus partly for the prince's instruction. Louis was intelligent and pious but could be arrogant and bad-tempered as a youth; under Fénelon's influence he matured into a serious and devout prince, interested in reform.

Marriage

On 7 December 1697 Louis married his second cousin Marie Adélaïde of Savoy, the eldest daughter of Victor Amadeus II, Duke of Savoy, and Anne Marie d'Orléans. The marriage, arranged by the Treaty of Turin which ended Savoy's participation in the Nine Years' War, proved to be a love match. Marie Adélaïde was lively and charming and became a great favourite of Louis XIV. The couple had three sons, of whom only the youngest survived infancy.

Military career

During the War of the Spanish Succession, Louis was given command of the army in Flanders in 1708, with the experienced Duke of Vendôme as his subordinate. The two men disagreed bitterly over strategy, and their quarrels contributed to the French defeat at the Battle of Oudenarde and the subsequent loss of Lille. The episode damaged the prince's reputation, though his supporters attributed the failure to Vendôme.

Death

Louis's father, the Grand Dauphin, died of smallpox in April 1711, making Louis the new Dauphin of France. In February 1712 an epidemic of measles struck the court. Marie Adélaïde died on 12 February, and Louis, who had refused to leave her side, contracted the disease and died six days later at the Château de Marly, aged 29. Their elder surviving son, Louis, Duke of Brittany, died of the same disease the following month. The succession thus passed to their two-year-old younger son, Louis, Duke of Anjou, who became Louis XV in 1715.

Legacy

The Duke of Burgundy was associated with a circle of reform-minded nobles, sometimes called the Bourguignon faction, who hoped that when he became king he would curb the absolute monarchy and restore power to the aristocracy and provincial estates. His early death ended those plans.
