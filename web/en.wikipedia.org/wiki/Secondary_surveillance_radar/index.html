Secondary surveillance radar (SSR) is a radar system used in air traffic control (ATC) that relies on a transponder aboard the aircraft to reply to interrogations from the ground station. Unlike primary radar, which merely detects reflections from the aircraft's skin, SSR obtains identity and altitude information directly from the aircraft, and requires far less transmitted power for a given range.

Operation

The ground interrogator transmits pulse pairs at 1030 MHz. The aircraft transponder replies at 1090 MHz with a train of pulses encoding either a four-digit octal identity code (Mode A — the "squawk"), or the aircraft's pressure altitude in 100-foot increments (Mode C). The antenna's rotation gives bearing, and the reply delay gives range. The system descends from the wartime Identification friend or foe (IFF) equipment of the Second World War, civilised by ICAO standardisation in the 1950s.

Mode S

Conventional SSR suffers from garbling (overlapping replies), spurious replies to sidelobes, and a limited pool of 4,096 identity codes. Mode S (Selective) addresses each aircraft individually using a unique 24-bit ICAO address, permits data-link messages, and reduces interference. Mode S transponders also broadcast ADS-B position reports ("Mode S ES"), which increasingly supplement radar surveillance entirely.

Advantages and limitations

SSR returns are stronger and cleaner than primary echoes, identify individual flights, and provide altitude automatically; but the system depends entirely on functioning airborne equipment — a non-transponding aircraft is invisible to SSR, so controllers retain primary radar for non-cooperative targets.
