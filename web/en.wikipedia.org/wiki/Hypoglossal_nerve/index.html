Hypoglossal nerve

The hypoglossal nerve, also known as the twelfth cranial nerve (CN XII), is a purely motor nerve that innervates all the extrinsic and intrinsic muscles of the tongue, except for the palatoglossus, which is innervated by the vagus nerve. It enables the movements involved in speech, swallowing and manipulating food in the mouth.

The hypoglossal nerve emerges from the medulla and passes through the hypoglossal canal.

Course

The nerve arises from the hypoglossal nucleus in the medulla oblongata, emerges as a series of rootlets between the pyramid and the olive, and leaves the skull through the hypoglossal canal. It descends in the neck between the internal carotid artery and internal jugular vein, loops forward across the carotid arteries, and runs on the surface of the hyoglossus muscle to enter the tongue. Along its course it is joined by fibres from C1 that supply the thyrohyoid and geniohyoid muscles and form the descendens hypoglossi.

Clinical significance

Damage to the nerve causes paralysis of the tongue on the same side; on protrusion, the tongue deviates toward the side of the lesion, because the intact genioglossus pushes it forward unopposed. Lower motor neurone lesions produce wasting and fasciculations. Causes include tumours of the skull base, carotid surgery or dissection, and motor neurone disease, in which tongue fasciculations are a characteristic sign.
