A deacon is a member of the diaconate, an office in Christian churches that is generally associated with service of some kind, but which varies among theological and denominational traditions. The word derives from the Greek diakonos, meaning "servant" or "messenger." In many traditions the diaconate is the first of the three major orders of ministry, followed by priest (presbyter) and bishop.

Biblical origins

The office is traditionally traced to Acts 6, in which the apostles appoint seven men, including Stephen and Philip, to oversee the daily distribution of food to widows so that the apostles can devote themselves to preaching. Paul's letters mention deacons alongside overseers (bishops), and the First Epistle to Timothy lists qualifications for deacons. Phoebe is described in Romans 16 as a deacon (or servant) of the church at Cenchreae.

Role in different traditions

In the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Oriental Orthodox churches, deacons are ordained clergy who assist priests and bishops, proclaim the Gospel, preach, baptize, witness marriages, and conduct funerals, though they cannot celebrate the Eucharist or hear confessions. Catholic deacons may be "transitional" (proceeding to the priesthood) or "permanent." Anglican and Lutheran churches also ordain deacons, while in many Baptist, Methodist, and other Protestant churches deacons are elected lay leaders who handle administration, finance, and congregational care.

Vestments and insignia

The deacon's distinctive vestment in Western and Eastern rites is the dalmatic, and the deacon wears the stole diagonally over the left shoulder rather than around the neck as priests do. This visual distinction dates to the early centuries of the Church.

Deaconesses

Women served as deaconesses in the early church and in some later traditions. The question of restoring or expanding a female diaconate has been studied and debated in the Roman Catholic Church and others in recent decades, while some Orthodox, Anglican, and Protestant bodies ordain women as deacons.
