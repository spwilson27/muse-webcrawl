Simón José Antonio de la Santísima Trinidad Bolívar y Ponte Palacios y Blanco (24 July 1783 – 17 December 1830), known as Simón Bolívar or El Libertador, was a Venezuelan military and political leader who led what are currently the countries of Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Panama and Bolivia to independence from the Spanish Empire.

Early life

Bolívar was born into a wealthy criollo family in Caracas. Educated in Spain and influenced by Enlightenment thought, he swore in Rome in 1805 to devote himself to the liberation of his homeland. He married María Teresa Rodríguez del Toro in 1802; she died of yellow fever the following year, and Bolívar never remarried.

Independence campaigns

After the Venezuelan declaration of independence in 1811, Bolívar fought through a series of defeats and exiles, issuing the Cartagena Manifesto (1812) and the controversial Decree of War to the Death (1813). Following the llanero campaigns and his alliance with José Antonio Páez, he crossed the Andes in 1819 and defeated the Spanish at Boyacá, liberating New Granada. Victories at Carabobo (1821) secured Venezuela, and his forces under Antonio José de Sucre won at Pichincha (1822) and Ayacucho (1824), ending Spanish rule in South America. Bolívar drafted constitutions and served as president of Gran Colombia, Peru, and Bolivia (named in his honour).

Later years and death

Bolívar's dream of a united Spanish America collapsed amid regional separatism; Gran Colombia dissolved, he resigned the presidency in 1830, survived an assassination attempt, and died of tuberculosis at Santa Marta on 17 December 1830. His remains were returned to Caracas in 1842.
