Water wheel

A water wheel is a machine for converting the energy of flowing or falling water into useful power, usually by means of a set of paddles or buckets mounted around a rotating wheel. For most of history it was the principal prime mover of industry, driving mills, fulling stocks, forge hammers, sawmills and pumps.

Types

In an undershot wheel the current strikes paddles at the bottom — simple but inefficient. An overshot wheel receives water over the top into buckets, using the weight of the water and reaching efficiencies of 60–85 percent, but requires a head of water. A breastshot wheel is struck at mid-height and combines features of both. Horizontal wheels, such as the Greek or Norse mill, drive a millstone directly without gearing.

History

Water wheels were described by Vitruvius in the 1st century BC and were widespread in the Roman world — the Barbegal complex in Gaul used sixteen overshot wheels to mill grain on an industrial scale. They remained central to European and Asian industry until steam power displaced them in the 19th century, and they are the direct ancestors of the modern water turbine.
