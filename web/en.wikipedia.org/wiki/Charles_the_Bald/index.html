Charles II, known as Charles the Bald (French: Charles le Chauve; 823–877), was King of West Francia from 843 and Holy Roman Emperor from 875 until his death. The youngest son of Louis the Pious and grandson of Charlemagne, he received the western third of the Frankish Empire under the Treaty of Verdun (843), laying the foundation of the medieval kingdom of France.

Struggle for a kingdom

Charles was the son of Louis the Pious by his second wife, Judith of Bavaria, whose efforts to secure a share of the empire for her son helped provoke civil wars among Louis's older sons. After Louis's death in 840, Charles allied with his half-brother Louis the German against their elder brother Lothair I, defeating him at Fontenoy in 841 and sealing the alliance with the Oaths of Strasbourg (842), the earliest surviving text in a Romance vernacular ancestral to French. The Treaty of Verdun (843) gave Charles the west: roughly modern France west of the Scheldt, Meuse, Saône, and Rhône.

Reign in West Francia

Charles's reign was tested by Viking raids deep into his kingdom, Breton and Aquitanian separatism, and aristocratic revolts, including invasions by Louis the German. He responded with fortified bridges (Edict of Pîtres, 864), currency reform, and payments of tribute. He was also a notable patron: his court supported scholars such as John Scotus Eriugena, and lavish illuminated manuscripts including the Codex Aureus of St. Emmeram and the Vivian Bible were produced for him. His capitularies, notably the Capitulary of Quierzy (877), dealt with the heritability of counties and benefices.

Emperor

On the death of Emperor Louis II of Italy in 875, Charles raced into Italy and was crowned emperor by Pope John VIII on Christmas Day 875. His authority remained contested, and after Louis the German's death he failed to secure the east. Summoned back to Italy in 877 to aid the Pope against Saracens, Charles fell ill crossing the Alps and died at Avrieux in October 877 — according to hostile sources, poisoned by his physician. His son Louis the Stammerer succeeded him in West Francia.

Name and legacy

The epithet "the Bald" may have been ironic — contemporary images show him with abundant hair — or may refer to his early landlessness (calvus in the sense of "bare" of territory). His reign is central to the emergence of France as a distinct kingdom.
