Rubiaceae

Rubiaceae
Kingdom:Plantae
Order:Gentianales
Family:Rubiaceae Juss.

Rubiaceae is a family of flowering plants, commonly known as the coffee, madder, or bedstraw family. It consists of terrestrial trees, shrubs, lianas, or herbs that are recognizable by simple, opposite leaves with interpetiolar stipules. The family contains about 14,000 species in about 580 genera, which makes it the fourth-largest angiosperm family. Rubiaceae has a cosmopolitan distribution; however, the largest species diversity is concentrated in the (sub)tropical regions.

Economically important species include Coffea, the source of coffee; Cinchona, the source of the antimalarial alkaloid quinine; and some timber species. Ornamental cultivars include Gardenia, Ixora, Pentas and Mussaenda.

Characteristics

The Rubiaceae are morphologically easily recognizable by a combination of characters: opposite or whorled leaves, interpetiolar stipules, tubular sympetalous corollas, and an inferior ovary. The flowers are usually bisexual and actinomorphic, often heterostylous. Fruits are berries, capsules, drupes or schizocarps.

Distribution

Rubiaceae have a cosmopolitan distribution and are found in nearly every region of the world, except Antarctica. The highest diversity is found in humid tropical and subtropical regions, particularly in northern South America and Southeast Asia.

Taxonomy

The family is usually divided into three subfamilies: Cinchonoideae, Ixoroideae and Rubioideae. The name Rubiaceae derives from the genus Rubia, the madder genus, named by Linnaeus. Antoine Laurent de Jussieu formally described the family in 1789.

Uses

Coffee is the most economically important member of the family, one of the world's most traded commodities. Quinine from Cinchona bark was historically the principal treatment for malaria. Madder (Rubia tinctorum) was an important source of red dye.
