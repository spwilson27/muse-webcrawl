British Isles naming dispute

The British Isles naming dispute concerns the term "British Isles", widely used in geography to describe the archipelago of Great Britain, Ireland and their surrounding islands off the north-western coast of continental Europe. Many people in Ireland regard the term as objectionable because it implies a British political claim over Ireland, and it is not used by the Irish government. Alternative names proposed or used include "Britain and Ireland", "the Atlantic Archipelago", "the Anglo-Celtic Isles", "these islands", and, in Irish, Éire agus an Bhreatain.

Background

The name derives from classical geographical terms (such as the Greek Prettanikai nesoi) applied to the islands by writers including Ptolemy. Its modern usage became established in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, coinciding with English and later British political dominance over Ireland. Following Irish independence in 1922, the term fell out of official Irish use; Irish legislation, state documents and the Irish diplomatic service avoid it. In 2006 the Irish foreign minister confirmed that the term "British Isles" had no official status in Ireland and was not used by the government.

Usage

In the United Kingdom and in international geographical usage the term remains common and is generally understood as purely geographical. However, some publishers and media outlets favour "Britain and Ireland". The British and Irish governments jointly have used formulas such as "these islands" in official documents including the 1998 Good Friday Agreement. The dispute is an example of the wider sensitivity of terminology in British–Irish relations, alongside issues such as the names of Derry/Londonderry and the use of "the mainland" for Great Britain.

Alternative terms

Alternatives proposed include "Atlantic Archipelago", suggested by the historian J. G. A. Pocock, "Islands of the North Atlantic" (echoed in the acronym IONA — also the name of the island of Iona), and simply "Britain and Ireland". None has displaced "British Isles" in general British or international usage, and the issue remains a matter of diplomatic and cultural sensitivity rather than formal dispute.
