Droste effect

Droste effect
TypeRecursive visual effect
Named afterDroste cocoa brand
Related termMise en abyme

The Droste effect (Dutch pronunciation: [ˈdrɔstə]) is the effect of a picture recursively appearing within itself, in a place where a similar picture would realistically be expected to appear. This produces a loop which in theory could go on forever, but in practice continues only as far as the image's resolution allows.

Origin

The effect is named after a 1904 design for the Dutch cocoa brand Droste, showing a nurse carrying a serving tray holding a box of Droste cocoa, which itself bears an image of the nurse carrying the tray. The design was drawn by Jan Musset and remained in use for decades, with minor variations. The effect was already known in medieval art: Giotto's 1320 Stefaneschi Triptych shows Cardinal Stefaneschi offering the triptych itself, which contains the scene, to St Peter.

In art and popular culture

The recursive image is a visual example of a strange loop, a concept explored by M. C. Escher in his 1956 lithograph Print Gallery, which depicts a man in a gallery viewing a print that contains the gallery itself. Mathematician Hendrik Lenstra and colleagues analysed the Escher print and completed its missing centre using conformal mapping. The French term for the technique is mise en abyme. The effect is also an early example of self-reference and is closely related to recursion and to fractals.
