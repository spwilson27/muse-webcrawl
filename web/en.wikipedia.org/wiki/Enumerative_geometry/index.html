Enumerative geometry

Enumerative geometry is the branch of algebraic geometry concerned with counting the number of solutions to geometric questions, mainly by means of intersection theory. Typical questions ask, for example, how many lines meet four given lines in space, or how many conic sections pass through five given points.

History

The problem of Apollonius — counting circles tangent to three given circles — is an ancient example. The subject took modern form in the 19th century with the work of Hermann Schubert, whose calculus of enumerative geometry produced celebrated counts such as the 27 lines on a smooth cubic surface and the 3,264 conics tangent to five given conics. Schubert's methods lacked rigorous foundations, and making them rigorous was the substance of Hilbert's fifteenth problem. The modern rigorous framework uses the Chow ring of moduli spaces and intersection multiplicities.

Modern developments

In the 1990s, enumerative geometry was transformed by ideas from string theory: physicists using mirror symmetry predicted counts of rational curves on Calabi–Yau threefolds that mathematicians verified and extended using new invariants such as Gromov–Witten invariants. These invariants count curves on algebraic varieties with prescribed incidence conditions and have deep connections to quantum cohomology.
