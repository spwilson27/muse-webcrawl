Dutch Brazil

Dutch Brazil (Dutch: Nederlands-Brazilië), also known as New Holland (Nieuw-Holland), was a colony of the Dutch Republic in northeastern Brazil, controlled by the Dutch West India Company from 1630 to 1654. At its greatest extent it stretched along the coast from Maranhão to Sergipe, centred on the captured Portuguese captaincies of Pernambuco, Paraíba, Rio Grande do Norte, and Ceará, with its capital at Recife — rebuilt by the Dutch as Mauritsstad.

History

The Dutch invasion formed part of the Dutch–Portuguese War and the wider struggle against Habsburg Spain. After an initial conquest of Recife and Olinda in 1630, the colony's most notable governor was Johan Maurits, Prince of Nassau-Siegen (1637–1644), who promoted religious tolerance, urban development, and scientific and artistic documentation of Brazil — his entourage included the painters Frans Post and Albert Eckhout and the naturalists Willem Piso and Georg Marcgraf. Recife under Mauritsstad became home to the first synagogue in the Americas, Kahal Zur Israel.

After Johan Maurits was recalled, Portuguese planters rose in revolt in 1645 (the Pernambucan Insurrection), winning the Battles of Guararapes in 1648 and 1649. Cut off by war in Europe and the First Anglo-Dutch War, the Dutch capitulated at Recife in January 1654. The Treaty of The Hague (1661) formalised Portugal's recovery of the territory in exchange for an indemnity. The episode left lasting marks on the region's culture and on the Caribbean, as many Dutch and Jewish settlers relocated to colonies such as Curaçao and Suriname, carrying sugar expertise with them.
