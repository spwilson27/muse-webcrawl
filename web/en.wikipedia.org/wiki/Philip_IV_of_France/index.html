Philip IV (1268 – 29 November 1314), called Philip the Fair (French: Philippe le Bel), was King of France from 1285 until his death, and King of Navarre (as Philip I) through his marriage to Joan I. Renowned for his striking appearance, rigid piety and autocratic rule, he greatly extended royal power and administrative centralisation in France.

Reign

Philip's reign is famous for his conflicts with the papacy and the military religious orders. His quarrel with Pope Boniface VIII over clerical taxation culminated in the outrage at Anagni (1303), after which the papacy fell under French influence and moved to Avignon. In 1307, driven by the crown's debts and a desire to seize their wealth, Philip ordered the arrest of the Knights Templar; under torture many confessed to heresy, the order was dissolved in 1312, and its last Grand Master, Jacques de Molay, was burned at the stake in Paris in 1314 — according to legend, cursing the king and pope to die within the year, as both did.

Philip also expelled the Jews from France in 1306, confiscating their property, repeatedly debased the coinage, and summoned the first Estates General in 1302 to rally support against the pope. His marriage to Joan of Navarre brought Champagne and Navarre to the crown. His three sons — Louis X, Philip V and Charles IV — succeeded him in turn but all died without surviving male heirs, ending the direct Capetian line in 1328 and setting up the succession crisis behind the Hundred Years' War.
