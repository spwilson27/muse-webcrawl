Pilum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The pilum (plural pila) was a javelin commonly used by the Roman army in ancient times. It was around 2 metres (6 ft 7 in) long overall, consisting of a wooden shaft from which projected an iron shank about 7 millimetres (0.28 in) in diameter and 60 centimetres (24 in) long, ending in a small pyramidal point. The pilum's design focused the weight of the weapon so that it could penetrate shields and armour, and its shank could bend on impact, weighing down an enemy's shield and preventing the weapon from being thrown back.

Design

The iron shank was joined to the wooden shaft by a socket or a flat tang riveted into a pyramidal block. Two broad types are recognised: a heavier pilum and a lighter one. Where the tang was fixed with rivets, one rivet was sometimes made of wood or soft metal so that the joint would break or bend under load. The total weight of surviving examples suggests a range from about 1 to 3 kilograms depending on type.

Use in battle

Legionaries typically carried two pila. As the lines closed, usually at a range of 15 to 30 metres, the Romans threw their pila in volleys before charging with the gladius. Because the long shank made the pilum difficult to extract, an enemy struck in the shield often had to abandon it; if the point pierced armour it could wound directly. Ancient authors, including Vegetius and Plutarch, describe how Marius is said to have modified the rivets so the shaft would break and drag, further encumbering enemy shields.

History

The pilum was adopted by the Romans probably from Iberian or Italic precedents in the 4th or 3rd century BC and became the signature heavy javelin of the legions of the Republic and early Empire. Its use declined during the 3rd century AD as infantry equipment and tactics changed, with lighter javelins such as the spiculum and plumbatae taking its place.
