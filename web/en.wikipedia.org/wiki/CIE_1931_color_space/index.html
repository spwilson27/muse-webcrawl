CIE 1931 color space

The CIE 1931 color spaces are the first defined quantitative links between distributions of wavelengths in the electromagnetic visible spectrum, and physiologically perceived colors in human color vision. The mathematical relationships that define these color spaces are essential tools for color management, important in many industries including printing, manufactured paints, and displays.

The standard observer

The CIE 1931 space rests on color-matching experiments conducted in the late 1920s by W. David Wright (with ten observers) and John Guild (with seven observers), in which subjects adjusted the intensities of three monochromatic primary lights to match a test color. Averaging these results produced the RGB color-matching functions, which the CIE transformed mathematically into the all-positive XYZ color-matching functions x̄(λ), ȳ(λ), z̄(λ), describing the response of the "standard observer" over a 2° field of view.

Tristimulus values and the chromaticity diagram

Any color's XYZ tristimulus values are obtained by integrating the product of the spectral power distribution of the light and each color-matching function over wavelength. Normalizing X, Y, Z by their sum gives chromaticity coordinates x and y, plotted on the famous horseshoe-shaped CIE xy chromaticity diagram: the curved boundary is the spectrum locus of monochromatic lights, and the straight bottom edge is the "line of purples". The Y value corresponds to luminance, which is why the ȳ function was chosen to equal the photopic luminosity function.

Legacy

A supplementary 10° standard observer was defined in 1964 for larger fields of view. Although newer models exist, the 1931 XYZ system remains the foundation of colorimetry: the CIELAB and CIELUV spaces are nonlinear transformations of XYZ, and RGB working spaces such as sRGB are defined by their relationship to it.
