Baudouin (Dutch: Boudewijn; 7 September 1930 – 31 July 1993) was King of the Belgians from 1951 until his death in 1993, the longest reign of any Belgian monarch at the time of his death. He succeeded his father, Leopold III, whose abdication followed the controversy of the Royal Question.

Early life

Baudouin was born at Laeken, the elder son of Prince Leopold and Princess Astrid of Sweden. His mother died in a car accident in Switzerland in 1935. During the Second World War the royal family were deported to Germany and then Austria after the Normandy landings, before being liberated in 1945 and living in exile in Switzerland while Belgium debated the Royal Question.

Reign

A referendum in 1950 narrowly favoured Leopold's return, but unrest — including a general strike and deaths of demonstrators at Grâce-Berleur — led Leopold to delegate his powers to Baudouin in August 1950; Baudouin became king in his own right on Leopold's abdication on 17 July 1951.

Baudouin's reign saw the independence of the Belgian Congo in 1960, at whose ceremony his speech praising Leopold II's colonial achievement was answered by Patrice Lumumba's famous rejoinder. In Belgium he presided over the transformation of the unitary state into a federal one. A devout Catholic, Baudouin in 1990 refused to sign a law liberalising abortion; the government declared him temporarily unable to reign for one day, promulgated the law, and Parliament reinstated him — the so-called "one-day king" episode.

Marriage and death

In 1960 Baudouin married the Spanish noblewoman Fabiola de Mora y Aragón. The couple had no children, despite several pregnancies ending in miscarriage. Baudouin died suddenly of heart failure on 31 July 1993 while holidaying at Motril in Spain. He was succeeded by his younger brother, Albert II. An extraordinary period of national mourning followed, and he was buried in the Royal Crypt at Laeken.
