Positivism

Founder: Auguste Comte
Key idea: Knowledge from empirical observation
Related: Logical positivism, empiricism

Positivism is a philosophical school holding that all genuine knowledge is either true by definition (as in logic and mathematics) or derived from sensory experience and empirical evidence, and that knowledge claims beyond experience — theological and metaphysical speculation — are meaningless or unknowable. The term and system were created by the French philosopher Auguste Comte (1798–1857).

Comte's system

Comte proposed a "law of three stages" of human thought: the theological stage, explaining the world by gods; the metaphysical stage, explaining it by abstract forces; and the positive (scientific) stage, which seeks observed laws of phenomena. He founded sociology as the science of society, coined the term "altruism", and proposed a secular "Religion of Humanity". Positivism influenced republican movements — the motto "Order and Progress" on the flag of Brazil derives from Comte.

Later developments

In the 20th century, the Vienna Circle developed logical positivism (logical empiricism), combining empiricism with mathematical logic and proposing the verification principle of meaning. Karl Popper criticised verificationism and proposed falsifiability instead; later critics such as W. V. O. Quine and Thomas Kuhn further undermined the logical positivist programme. Positivist assumptions — value-free inquiry, observable variables, hypothesis testing — nonetheless remain influential in the social sciences, and "positivism" is often contrasted with interpretivist and critical approaches.
