Aestheticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A languid Pre-Raphaelite-style portrait with sunflowers and peacock feathers, emblems of the aesthetic movement]
A languid Pre-Raphaelite-style portrait with sunflowers and peacock feathers, emblems of the aesthetic movement

Aestheticism was an art and literary movement of the late nineteenth century that championed 'art for art's sake' — the autonomy of beauty from moral, didactic or utilitarian purpose. Rooted in Théophile Gautier's preface to Mademoiselle de Maupin (1835) and gathering force in Britain from the 1860s, it united Walter Pater, Oscar Wilde, James McNeill Whistler and the later Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood circle, and fed into Decadence and Symbolism.

Doctrine

Against Victorian moralism, aesthetes held that art needs no justification beyond beauty: 'all art is quite useless', as Wilde's preface to The Picture of Dorian Gray put it, meaning useless as sermon or lesson. Pater's Studies in the History of the Renaissance (1873) urged burning with a 'hard, gem-like flame' — intensity of aesthetic experience as life's aim. The movement prized Japanese art, medievalism, peacock feathers, sunflowers and blue-and-white porcelain, all satirised in Gilbert and Sullivan's Patience (1881).

Art and design

Whistler's nocturnes and his 1878 libel trial against John Ruskin (who had accused him of 'flinging a pot of paint in the public's face'; Whistler won a derisory farthing) became the movement's public theatre. The Grosvenor Gallery (opened 1877) was its showcase; aesthetic design — William Morris's arts and crafts in parallel, Liberty's fabrics, spare 'artistic' furniture — redesigned the Victorian interior around beauty.

Legacy

Aestheticism collapsed as a public movement with Wilde's trials (1895) but its DNA runs through modernism: the autonomy of art, the dandy as artist, and the elevation of style over subject prepared the ground for Virginia Woolf, abstraction and the modern art market's cult of the artist-personality.
