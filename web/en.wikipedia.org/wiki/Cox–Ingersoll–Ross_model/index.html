Cox–Ingersoll–Ross model


CIR short-rate paths

In mathematical finance, the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) model describes the evolution of interest rates. It is a type of "one-factor model" (short-rate model) as it describes interest rate movements as driven by only one factor of market risk. The model can be used in the valuation of interest rate derivatives. It was introduced in 1985 by John C. Cox, Jonathan E. Ingersoll, and Stephen A. Ross as an extension of the Vasicek model.

The model

The CIR model specifies that the instantaneous interest rate follows the stochastic differential equation
drt = a(b − rt)dt + σ√rt dWt,
where Wt is a Wiener process. The drift factor a(b − rt) is the same as in the Vasicek model, pulling the rate toward the long-term mean b at speed a; but the standard deviation factor σ√rt is proportional to the square root of the rate, which prevents the rate from becoming negative (provided the Feller condition 2ab ≥ σ² holds) and gives larger volatility when rates are high.

Bond pricing

Like the Vasicek model, CIR is an affine term-structure model: zero-coupon bond prices have a closed-form exponential-affine expression, making the model tractable for calibration to yield-curve data. The related CIR process is also widely used in credit-risk and stochastic-volatility modelling (as in the Heston model).
