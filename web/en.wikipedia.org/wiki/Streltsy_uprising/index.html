Streltsy uprising

The Streltsy, Moscow's standing musketeer regiments, rose in revolt in 1682 and again in 1698.

The Streltsy uprisings were rebellions by the Streltsy — the standing infantry regiments of musketeers who formed the guard of Moscow — against the Tsarist government of Russia. The term most often refers to the Moscow uprising of 1682 and the Streltsy uprising of 1698.

Background

The Streltsy were created by Ivan the Terrible in the 1540s–1550s as Russia's first standing army. By the late 17th century the Moscow Streltsy, numbering over 20,000, had become a hereditary caste whose members combined military service with trade and crafts, resented the growing use of "new formation" regiments officered by foreigners, and suffered from arrears of pay and abuses by their colonels. They were also sympathetic to the Old Believer schism in the Russian Orthodox Church.

Uprising of 1682

On the death of Tsar Feodor III in April 1682, a succession dispute arose between the families of the ten-year-old Peter (the future Peter the Great) and his half-brother Ivan V. Exploiting rumours that Ivan had been murdered, the Streltsy stormed the Kremlin on 15 May 1682, killed leading boyars of the Naryshkin faction — including Artamon Matveyev — and demanded that Ivan and Peter reign jointly, with their sister Sophia Alekseyevna as regent. The Streltsy effectively dictated terms for months; an associated religious revolt by Old Believers (the Khovanshchina, named after the Streltsy commander Prince Ivan Khovansky) ended when Sophia had Khovansky executed in September 1682.

Vasily Surikov's painting The Morning of the Streltsy Execution depicts the aftermath of the later revolt.

Uprising of 1698

In 1698, while Peter I was abroad on his Grand Embassy to Western Europe, four Streltsy regiments ordered to the western frontier mutinied and marched on Moscow, complaining of service conditions and foreign officers. They were defeated by government troops under Patrick Gordon and Aleksey Shein at the New Jerusalem Monastery in June 1698. Peter hurried home and conducted a brutal investigation: over a thousand Streltsy were executed, their bodies displayed in Moscow, and the Moscow Streltsy corps was disbanded. Regent Sophia, whom Peter suspected of inspiring the revolt, was forced to become a nun.

Consequences

The destruction of the Streltsy removed a conservative military caste and an obstacle to Peter's reforms; the Streltsy regiments elsewhere in Russia were gradually phased out in the early 18th century as Peter built a regular conscript army on the Western model.
