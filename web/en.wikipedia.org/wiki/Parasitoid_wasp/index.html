Parasitoid wasp

OrderHymenoptera
Major groupsIchneumonidae, Braconidae, Chalcidoidea, and many others
Estimated speciesHundreds of thousands described and undescribed

Parasitoid wasps are a large group of hymenopteran insects whose larvae develop on or within the bodies of other arthropods, eventually killing the host. Unlike true parasites, parasitoids invariably sterilize or kill their hosts, a life history that has made them one of the most species-rich and ecologically important groups of insects.

Most parasitoid wasps attack the eggs, larvae, pupae or adults of other insects, especially Lepidoptera, Coleoptera and Diptera. The female wasp locates a host using chemical cues, then uses her ovipositor to lay one or more eggs in or on it. The wasp larva feeds on the host's tissues, often consuming non-vital organs first so the host stays alive until the wasp completes development.

Defensive chemistry

Many parasitoid wasps inject venoms and symbiotic polydnaviruses along with their eggs. These viruses suppress the host's immune system, preventing encapsulation of the egg, and can alter host development and behaviour to benefit the developing wasp.

Biological control

Parasitoid wasps are widely used in biological pest control. Species of Trichogramma, Encarsia and braconids such as Cotesia are mass-reared and released to control crop pests including whiteflies, aphids and caterpillars, reducing the need for chemical insecticides.

Evolutionary significance

Charles Darwin famously wrote that the gruesome habits of ichneumon wasps shook his belief in a beneficent designer: he could not persuade himself that a beneficent and omnipotent God would have designedly created the Ichneumonidae with the express intention of their feeding within the living bodies of caterpillars. Parasitoid wasps may account for a substantial fraction — perhaps 10% or more — of all animal species.
