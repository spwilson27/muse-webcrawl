Australian architecture

The architecture of Australia has developed gradually from the shelters and structures of Indigenous Australians, through the Georgian and Victorian styles of the colonial period, to the distinctive vernacular houses of the 20th century — the Queenslander and the fibro cottage — and the modern landmarks of Sydney and Canberra.

Indigenous and colonial architecture

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples built a range of semi-permanent and permanent structures, from stone houses in western Victoria to the aquaculture structures of Budj Bim. European settlement from 1788 brought Georgian convict-built cottages, military and civic buildings such as Francis Greenway's Sydney churches and Hyde Park Barracks, and later Victorian terraces, Gothic Revival churches, and grand boom-era commercial buildings in gold-rich Melbourne.

The Australian house

Responding to climate, Australian domestic architecture developed the encircling verandah, and in Queensland the elevated timber "Queenslander" with corrugated-iron roof. The Federation style (c. 1890–1915) combined Queen Anne and bungalow elements; the interwar period added Californian bungalows; postwar austerity produced the fibro and brick-veneer suburban home that came to dominate Australian cities.

Modernism and after

Walter Burley Griffin's plan for Canberra (1912) introduced large-scale designed landscape urbanism. Postwar modernism, led by architects such as Harry Seidler and Robin Boyd — whose book The Australian Ugliness (1960) attacked suburban design — reshaped city skylines. Jørn Utzon's Sydney Opera House (opened 1973) became the nation's most recognisable building. Late-20th and 21st-century work by Glenn Murcutt (Pritzker Prize, 2002) emphasised lightweight, climate-responsive design "touching the earth lightly".
