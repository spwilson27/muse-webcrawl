Holy Roman Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Imperial Crown of the Holy Roman Empire, octagonal gold crown from the 10th century]

The Holy Roman Empire was a multi-ethnic complex of territories in western and central Europe that developed during the early Middle Ages and continued until its dissolution in 1806 during the Napoleonic Wars. Conventionally dated from Otto I's imperial coronation in 962 (or from Charlemagne's in 800), it comprised the Kingdoms of Germany, Italy and Burgundy and hundreds of principalities, duchies, counties, prince-bishoprics and free imperial cities. Its emperors, elected by the prince-electors, claimed the inheritance of Rome; its real power was always negotiated with its princes — Voltaire's quip that it was 'neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire' captures its later fragmentation.

Medieval empire

The Ottonian and Salian emperors made the empire the strongest power in Latin Christendom, but the Investiture Controversy with the papacy (1075–1122) — crystallised in Henry IV's penance at Canossa (1077) — broke imperial control of the church. Under the Hohenstaufen (Frederick Barbarossa, Frederick II), the empire fought Italian cities and popes for Italy while German princes consolidated hereditary territories. The Golden Bull of 1356 fixed the seven electors and the electoral procedure for centuries.

Early modern empire and end

The Habsburgs held the imperial crown almost continuously from 1438. The empire survived the Reformation — at the price of the Peace of Augsburg (1555) and the catastrophe of the Thirty Years' War, ended by the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which confirmed the near-sovereignty of its 300-plus states. Prussia and Austria dominated its last century as rivals. In 1806, after Austerlitz and the formation of Napoleon's Confederation of the Rhine, Francis II abdicated the imperial crown, ending the thousand-year empire.
