Self-organization

Self-organization, also called spontaneous order, is a process where some form of overall order arises from local interactions between parts of an initially disordered system. The process can be spontaneous when sufficient energy is available, not needing control by any external agent. It is a core concept in the study of complex systems, from physics and chemistry to biology, economics, and computer science.

Overview

Self-organizing systems typically exhibit emergence: global patterns arise that cannot be straightforwardly predicted from the components alone. Examples include convection cells in a heated fluid (Bénard cells), the Belousov–Zhabotinsky chemical reaction, flocking behaviour in birds, ant colony foraging trails, the formation of crystals and snowflakes, and market prices in economics.

History

The idea has roots in the philosophy of Immanuel Kant and was developed scientifically in cybernetics by W. Ross Ashby (1947) and Heinz von Foerster, in thermodynamics by Ilya Prigogine (who coined "dissipative structures" for order arising far from equilibrium), and in Hermann Haken's synergetics. The term is also central to theories of autopoiesis proposed by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela.

Characteristics

Self-organization generally requires a system that is open to flows of energy or matter, nonlinear interactions among components, positive and negative feedback, and the absence of a central controller. Order is maintained by the continual dissipation of energy.
