Janissaries

The Janissaries (Ottoman Turkish: Yeniçeri, "new soldier") were an elite infantry corps that formed the household troops and bodyguards of the Ottoman Sultan from the late 14th century until 1826. Originally recruited through the devşirme system — a levy of Christian boys from the Balkans who were converted to Islam and trained for imperial service — the Janissaries became one of the first modern standing armies, among the earliest forces to make extensive use of firearms, and a decisive political power in the Ottoman Empire.

Origins and recruitment

The corps is traditionally dated to the reign of Sultan Murad I (1362–1389). Under the devşirme, Ottoman officials periodically levied boys, mostly from Christian peasant families in the Balkans, who were converted to Islam, taught Turkish and given years of rigorous training in Istanbul. The most talented entered palace service or the scribal administration; the rest joined the Janissary corps. Janissaries wore a distinctive tall felt cap (the börk), were paid regular salaries from the treasury, and were at first forbidden to marry or engage in trade. Their soup cauldron, the kazan, was the symbol of the regiment; "overturning the cauldron" meant mutiny.

Military role

Janissaries fought at the centre of Ottoman armies in battles from Nicopolis (1396) and Ankara (1402) to the conquest of Constantinople (1453), Mohács (1526) and the sieges of Vienna. They were among the first infantry in Europe to adopt firearms in mass — muskets, supported by their field fortifications of wagons and trenches — and their discipline made them feared opponents for two centuries. Many followed the Bektashi Sufi order, whose patron Haji Bektash was the corps' spiritual protector.

Decline and abolition

From the late 16th century the corps' discipline eroded: recruitment by heredity and purchase replaced the devşirme, membership rolls swelled with tradesmen seeking tax privileges, and the Janissaries became a conservative political bloc that deposed sultans — including Osman II (1622) and Selim III (1807) — and repeatedly blocked military reform. When Sultan Mahmud II ordered the formation of a new-style army in 1826, the Janissaries revolted; loyal artillery bombarded their barracks in Istanbul, killing thousands. The corps was abolished in the event officially remembered as the Auspicious Incident (Vaka-i Hayriye), clearing the way for Ottoman military modernisation.

Legacy

The Janissaries' military bands (mehter) gave the Ottoman world the "Turkish music" of bass drums, cymbals and crescents that influenced European composers from Lully to Mozart and Beethoven. The corps remains a potent symbol in Turkish and Balkan historical memory — of imperial might, of the devşirme's human cost, and of institutional conservatism.
