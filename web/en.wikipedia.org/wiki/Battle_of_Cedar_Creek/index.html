Battle of Cedar Creek

The Battle of Cedar Creek, fought on 19 October 1864 near Middletown, Virginia, was the climactic engagement of the Valley Campaigns of 1864 in the American Civil War. A surprise Confederate dawn attack routed much of the Union Army of the Shenandoah, but Major General Philip Sheridan's dramatic arrival from Winchester rallied his troops, and a Union counterattack in the afternoon turned the battle into a decisive Confederate defeat.

"Sheridan's Ride" to the battlefield, 19 October 1864.

Background

Lieutenant General Jubal Early's Confederate Army of the Valley, though outnumbered, had survived Sheridan's autumn campaign in the Shenandoah Valley. Desperate to change the situation, Early planned a risky night march and dawn assault on the Union camps along Cedar Creek, south of Middletown, while Sheridan was away in Washington and Winchester.

The battle

Attacking through fog at dawn on 19 October, Early's men overwhelmed two Union corps and captured over 1,300 prisoners and some 18 cannon. But the Confederate troops, hungry and exhausted, stopped to loot the abandoned Union camps, and the attack stalled against the unbroken Union VI Corps and cavalry. Sheridan, hearing the guns, rode 20 miles from Winchester — a ride immortalised in the popular poem "Sheridan's Ride" — re-formed his army, and at about 4 p.m. launched a general counterattack that shattered Early's line and recaptured all the lost guns and more.

Significance

Cedar Creek ended effective Confederate resistance in the Shenandoah Valley. Coming weeks before the 1864 presidential election, the victory — with those at Atlanta and Mobile Bay — bolstered Northern morale and Abraham Lincoln's re-election. Casualties were roughly 5,700 Union and 2,900 Confederate.
