Polish cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polish cuisine (Polish: kuchnia polska) is a style of cooking and food preparation originating in or widely popular in Poland. Polish cuisine has evolved over the centuries to become very eclectic because of Poland's history, and it shares many similarities with other West Slavic, German, Austrian and Jewish culinary traditions.

Polish-styled cooking in other cultures is often referred to as à la polonaise. The cuisine is rich in meat, especially pork, chicken and game, in addition to a wide range of vegetables, spices, fungi and herbs. It is also characterised by the use of various kinds of noodles as well as cereals and grains, and by hearty dishes such as pierogi (filled dumplings), kiełbasa (sausage), bigos (hunter's stew) and żurek (soured rye soup).

History

Medieval Polish cuisine was based on dishes made of agricultural produce and cereal crops, as well as domesticated and wild animals. The many wars, shifting borders and trade links of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth brought influences from Lithuanian, Tatar, Armenian, Jewish, French and Italian cooking. In the 19th and 20th centuries, cookbooks such as Lucyna Ćwierczakiewiczowa's 365 obiadów za pięć złotych codified a national repertoire, and after 1945 mass catering and later post-1989 restaurant culture reshaped everyday eating.

Typical dishes

  • Pierogi — dumplings filled with potato and cheese, sauerkraut and mushroom, minced meat, or fruit.
  • Bigos — a stew of sauerkraut, fresh cabbage and assorted meats, considered a national dish.
  • Żurek — a sour rye soup often served in a bread bowl with sausage and egg.
  • Kotlet schabowy — a breaded pork cutlet similar to a schnitzel.
  • Gołąbki — cabbage rolls stuffed with meat and rice in tomato sauce.
  • Pączki — filled doughnuts traditionally eaten before Lent.
