The Second French Intervention in Mexico (Spanish: Segunda Intervención Francesa en México; 1861–1867) was an invasion of the Second Mexican Republic by the Second French Empire, supported by Mexican conservatives, that installed the Austrian archduke Maximilian as Emperor of Mexico. It ended in a Mexican republican victory and Maximilian's execution.

Background

After the Reform War, President Benito Juárez suspended payments on Mexico's foreign debt in 1861. France, Britain and Spain landed forces at Veracruz under the Treaty of London to force repayment; when Napoleon III's imperial ambitions became clear, Britain and Spain withdrew in April 1862.

Course of the war

On 5 May 1862, Mexican forces under Ignacio Zaragoza defeated the French at the Battle of Puebla — the victory commemorated by Cinco de Mayo. Reinforced, the French took Puebla after a siege in 1863 and occupied Mexico City; a conservative Assembly of Notables proclaimed a monarchy and offered the crown to Ferdinand Maximilian of Habsburg, who arrived in 1864 with his wife Carlota and ruled the Second Mexican Empire. Juárez's republican government retreated north and waged guerrilla resistance. Maximilian, a liberal by temperament, alienated his conservative backers by retaining Juárez's reforms while failing to win over republicans.

Collapse and aftermath

With the American Civil War over, the United States pressed France under the Monroe Doctrine and supplied the republicans; facing war with Prussia, Napoleon III withdrew French troops in 1866–67. Maximilian refused to abdicate, was captured at Querétaro, and was executed by firing squad on 19 June 1867, together with generals Miramón and Mejía — the scene immortalised in Édouard Manet's paintings. Juárez re-entered Mexico City and restored the Republic.
