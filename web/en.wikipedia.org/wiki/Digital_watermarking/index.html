Digital watermarking

Digital watermarking is the process of hiding digital information in a carrier signal; the hidden information should, but does not need to be, related to the carrier signal. Digital watermarks may be used to verify the authenticity or integrity of the carrier signal or to show the identity of its owners. They are prominently used for tracing copyright infringements and for banknote authentication.

Like traditional watermarks on paper, digital watermarks may be perceptible or imperceptible. A visible watermark can be used to mark previews of images so that they cannot be mistaken for final products, while a robust invisible watermark is designed to survive transformations such as compression, cropping, and format conversion. Fragile watermarks, by contrast, are meant to break under any modification, thereby signalling that the content has been altered.

History

The term "digital watermarking" was coined by R. G. van Schyndel, A. Z. Tirkel and C. F. Osborne at the 1994 IEEE International Conference on Image Processing. Techniques grew out of the older field of steganography, and interest surged in the 1990s as digital media distribution raised concerns about piracy.

Applications

Watermarking is used for copyright protection, source tracking (fingerprinting), broadcast monitoring, data authentication, and transaction tracking. In fingerprinting, a different watermark identifying the recipient is embedded in each copy so that a leaked copy can be traced back to its source.

Properties

Key properties include robustness (resistance to removal), imperceptibility, payload capacity, and security. These properties trade off against one another: increasing robustness generally reduces imperceptibility or capacity.
