Art auction


An evening sale at a major auction house.

An art auction is the sale of works of art by competitive bidding, usually conducted by an auction house on behalf of consignors. Art auctions form the core of the "secondary market" for art — the resale of works after their first sale by the artist or a dealer — and provide the most visible public prices in the art world. The market is dominated by the international houses Christie's and Sotheby's, founded in London in 1766 and 1744 respectively, followed by firms such as Phillips and Bonhams, and, in China, China Guardian and Poly Auction.

How sales work

Works are consigned to the auction house under contract, catalogued, researched for provenance and condition, and published in a sale catalogue with a pre-sale estimate. Sales are typically organised by category — Old Masters, Impressionist and Modern, Post-war and Contemporary — and the most important "evening sales" are held during the spring and autumn seasons in New York, London and Hong Kong. Bidding may take place in the room, by telephone through a member of staff, by written ("absentee") bid, or online.

The winning bid, the "hammer price", is supplemented by a buyer's premium — a percentage fee charged by the house — so the total price paid exceeds the hammer price. If bidding fails to reach a confidential minimum, the reserve price, the lot is "bought in" and remains unsold.

Record prices

The highest price ever paid at auction for a work of art is for Leonardo da Vinci's Salvator Mundi, sold at Christie's New York in November 2017 for $450.3 million including premium. Other landmark results include Picasso's Les Femmes d'Alger (Version 'O') ($179.4 million, 2015) and Modigliani's Nu Couché ($170.4 million, 2015). Auction records are frequently used as benchmarks for the value of an artist's market as a whole.

Practices and criticism

The art auction trade has been criticised for opacity: guarantees and irrevocable bids provided by third parties, "chandelier bidding" (fictitious bids called by the auctioneer below the reserve), and the potential for money laundering and market manipulation. Since the early 2000s, online bidding platforms and the growth of the Chinese market have substantially reshaped the business.
