Siege warfare

Siege warfare is a form of constant, low-intensity conflict characterised by one party holding a strong, static fortified position while the other attempts to take it. Sieges involve surrounding the target and blocking the reinforcement or escape of troops as well as the supply of weapons or food.

History

Sieges were a constant feature of warfare from antiquity through the medieval period, when walled cities and castles resisted attack. Famous sieges include the siege of Tyre by Alexander the Great, the siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE, and the long siege of Constantinople in 1453.

Methods

Attackers used siege towers, battering rams, catapults, trebuchets, mining (digging tunnels beneath walls), and later artillery and starvation blockades. Defenders countered with fortifications, sorties, and stockpiled supplies. The development of gunpowder artillery and star forts changed siege warfare in the early modern era, and by the twentieth century sieges had largely given way to encirclements of cities, as at Leningrad and Stalingrad during the Second World War.
