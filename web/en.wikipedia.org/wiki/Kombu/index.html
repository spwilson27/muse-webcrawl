Kombu (Japanese: 昆布, romanized: konbu) is a family of edible kelp (seaweed) widely eaten in East Asia, especially Japan, Korea and China. Most kombu comes from the species Saccharina japonica (formerly Laminaria japonica), a brown alga of the family Laminariaceae, harvested mainly from the cold waters around Hokkaido, Japan's northern island, which accounts for the great majority of production.

Culinary use

Kombu is best known as the principal ingredient, together with katsuobushi (dried bonito flakes), of dashi, the fundamental stock of Japanese cooking. Dried kombu is steeped in water to release flavour compounds; it is also pickled, simmered in dishes such as kobumaki (rolled kombu), shaved into oboro kombu and tororo kombu, and candied as a tea accompaniment. Kombu should generally be removed before the water boils, as prolonged boiling produces bitterness and sliminess.

Umami

Kombu is one of the richest natural sources of glutamic acid. In 1908 the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda extracted glutamate from kombu dashi and identified it as the source of a distinct savoury taste, which he named umami — leading to the development of monosodium glutamate (MSG). The white powdery coating that appears on dried kombu is largely mannitol and is a sign of quality; it should not be washed off, only lightly wiped.

Nutrition

Kombu is a good source of dietary fibre and minerals including iodine — of which it contains very high levels, leading health authorities to caution against excessive consumption — as well as calcium, iron and potassium, and the polysaccharide fucoidan. Varieties prized in Japanese cuisine include ma-kombu, Rausu kombu, Rishiri kombu, Hidaka kombu and naga-kombu, each with distinct flavour and uses in regional dashi traditions, notably in Kyoto and Osaka cooking and in Okinawan cuisine, where per-capita consumption is famously high.
