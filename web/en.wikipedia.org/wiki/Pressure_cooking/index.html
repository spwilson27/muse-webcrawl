Pressure cooking

Pressure cooking is the process of cooking food under high-pressure steam in a sealed vessel, which raises the boiling point of water above 100°C and greatly speeds cooking. The vessel used is a pressure cooker; modern electric multi-cookers such as the Instant Pot have popularized the method.

Principle

As water in the sealed pot boils, the trapped steam increases the internal pressure — typically by about 1 atmosphere (15 psi) above ambient in high-pressure cookers — raising the boiling point to approximately 120°C (250°F). At this temperature, chemical reactions in cooking proceed much faster, so foods such as dried beans, tough cuts of meat, and stocks cook in a fraction of the usual time, with reduced water and energy loss.

History

The French physicist Denis Papin invented the "steam digester" in 1679, a sealed vessel with a safety valve intended to extract fats and gelatin from bones. Domestic pressure cookers were developed in the early 20th century and became widespread after the Second World War. Early models gained a reputation for occasional explosions, spurring improved safety mechanisms: modern cookers include multiple redundant valves, locking lids that cannot open under pressure, and pressure-release devices.

Methods of releasing pressure

Pressure may be released naturally (letting the pot cool), by quick release (venting the steam manually), or by running cold water over a stovetop cooker (cold water release). Recipes specify the method because the continued cooking during natural release affects the result. Pressure cooking is less effective at high altitude, where lower ambient pressure reduces the achievable temperature, and some foods, such as certain foamy or frothing items, require care to avoid blocking the vent.
