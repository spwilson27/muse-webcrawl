George A. Olah

Born: 22 May 1927, Budapest, Hungary

Died: 8 March 2017, Beverly Hills, California, USA

Awards: Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1994)

George Andrew Olah (born Oláh György; 22 May 1927 – 8 March 2017) was a Hungarian-American chemist. He received the 1994 Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for his contribution to carbocation chemistry", and later promoted the methanol economy as an alternative to fossil fuels.

Life

Olah studied chemical engineering at the Budapest University of Technology and Economics and taught there before fleeing Hungary after the 1956 revolution. He worked for Dow Chemical in Canada and the United States, then held professorships at Case Western Reserve University and from 1977 at the University of Southern California, where he founded the Loker Hydrocarbon Research Institute.

Superacids and carbocations

Using superacids — acid systems stronger than concentrated sulfuric acid, such as "magic acid" (a mixture of fluorosulfuric acid and antimony pentafluoride) — Olah stabilised carbocations long enough to study them directly by spectroscopy. His work settled the long-running norbornyl cation controversy in favour of non-classical, bridged structures and overturned the classical rules of carbon bonding.

The methanol economy

In later life Olah advocateded producing methanol from carbon dioxide and hydrogen as a recyclable fuel and chemical feedstock, developing direct methanol fuel cells and a process for capturing atmospheric CO₂. He co-authored the book Beyond Oil and Gas: The Methanol Economy (2006).
