Jewish delicatessen

Inside a traditional Jewish delicatessen.

A Jewish delicatessen, also known as a Jewish deli, is a retailer and restaurant specializing in the traditional foods of Ashkenazi Jewish cuisine, particularly cured and smoked meats and fish, pickles, and baked goods. The classic Jewish deli arose in the United States, especially New York City, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries with waves of Jewish immigration from Eastern Europe.

Foods

Signature items include pastrami, corned beef, brisket, and tongue, typically served in oversized sandwiches, often on rye bread; smoked fish such as lox, whitefish, and sable; bagels with cream cheese; knishes; matzah ball soup; chopped liver; kosher dill pickles; and desserts such as black-and-white cookies, rugelach, and New York-style cheesecake. Many traditional delis were kosher or "kosher-style," with later establishments often serving meat and dairy together, which strict kosher law forbids.

History and decline

New York once had an estimated 1,500 Jewish delis in the 1930s; changing tastes, assimilation, rising costs, and the decline of kosher observance reduced their number dramatically over the following decades. Surviving landmarks and a revival of interest in traditional deli food have kept the institution alive, with famous examples including Katz's Delicatessen in New York and Schwartz's in Montreal (a related smoked-meat tradition).
