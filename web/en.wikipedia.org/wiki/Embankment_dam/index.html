Embankment dam

An embankment dam is a large artificial dam made from the compaction of a complex semi-plastic mound of various compositions of soil, sand, clay, or rock. It has a semi-permeable waterproof natural covering for its surface and a dense, impervious core. The two main types are earth-fill dams and rock-fill dams.

Construction

Embankment dams are built by placing and compacting material in layers with heavy machinery. A typical zoned dam has an impermeable clay core that blocks seepage, surrounded by filter and drain zones that control water movement and by shells of compacted earth or rock that provide stability. Because they rely on their great weight and broad base, embankment dams can be built on weaker foundations than concrete dams and can use material available near the site.

Advantages and failure modes

Embankment dams are the most common type of large dam worldwide and include some of the largest structures ever built, such as the Tarbela Dam in Pakistan. Their principal vulnerabilities are overtopping by floodwater, which rapidly erodes the downstream face, and internal erosion or "piping" of core material along cracks or conduits. Earthquakes can also damage them through settlement and liquefaction, so they require careful design, filter protection, and long-term monitoring.
