The Sinclair C5 is a small one-person battery electric vehicle, technically an "electrically assisted pedal cycle", designed by Sir Clive Sinclair and launched in the United Kingdom on 10 January 1985. The driver sits in a recumbent position in an open three-wheeled polypropylene body, steered by a handlebar beneath the knees, with a 250-watt electric motor supplementing pedal power.

Design and launch

Sinclair, whose Sinclair Research had made a fortune from home computers such as the ZX Spectrum, believed electric transport was the future and had worked on electric vehicle concepts since the 1970s. The C5 was legally classed as an electrically assisted pedal cycle, so it could be driven on the road by anyone aged 14 or over without a licence, tax, insurance or helmet. Its manufacture was contracted to Hoover's factory at Merthyr Tydfil in Wales.

Reception and failure

The launch, held in mid-winter, was widely mocked. Journalists criticised the C5's low driving position and vulnerability in traffic, its limited range — the claimed 20 miles could fall drastically in cold weather, which also sapped the lead-acid battery — lack of weather protection, and modest hill-climbing ability. Sales fell far short of hopes: around 5,000 were sold before Sinclair Vehicles went into receivership in October 1985, with total production commonly estimated at some 14,000 units. The C5 became a byword in Britain for a high-profile business failure.

Legacy

In later years the C5 has been reassessed as a forerunner of today's e-bikes and micro-mobility vehicles, handicapped chiefly by the heavy lead-acid battery technology of its day. Surviving examples are collected and modified by enthusiasts, and the vehicle has become a cult object; in 2017 Sinclair's nephew Grant Sinclair launched a modernised successor, the IRIS eTrike.
