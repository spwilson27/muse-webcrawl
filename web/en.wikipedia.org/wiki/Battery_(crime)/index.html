Battery (crime)

Battery is a criminal offence involving unlawful physical contact. Under common law it is usually defined as the intentional application of force to another person without lawful justification, causing harmful or offensive contact. Battery is frequently charged together with assault, which in its narrow legal sense is the threat or attempt to commit battery, though many modern statutes merge the two.

Common law elements

Traditionally, prosecutors must prove (1) an act by the defendant that (2) causes harmful or offensive touching of another, (3) done with intent to cause the contact (or, in some jurisdictions, criminal negligence). No actual injury is required: spitting on someone or grabbing an object they are holding can constitute battery, because the contact need only be offensive to a reasonable sense of dignity. Defences include consent, lawful authority (such as a lawful arrest), and self-defence.

Statutory variations

Many U.S. states have codified battery with graded offences: simple battery is often a misdemeanour, while aggravated battery — involving a deadly weapon, serious bodily injury, or a victim in a protected class such as a police officer, child or elderly person — is a felony. In England and Wales the common-law offence survives as "assault by beating" under section 39 of the Criminal Justice Act 1988, with more serious wounding and grievous bodily harm charged under the Offences against the Person Act 1861. In Scotland the equivalent conduct is prosecuted as assault.

Battery as a tort

In civil law, battery is an intentional tort: a plaintiff may sue for damages for intentional, unconsented harmful or offensive contact, even without physical injury. The criminal and civil wrongs share a name but proceed independently, under different burdens of proof.
