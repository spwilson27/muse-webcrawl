Preliminary reference

[Image: A national courtroom; through the preliminary reference procedure national courts ask the Court of Justice to interpret EU law]

A preliminary reference (or preliminary ruling) is a procedure in European Union law by which a court or tribunal of a member state asks the Court of Justice of the European Union to interpret a provision of EU law, or to rule on the validity of an act of the EU institutions, where that question arises in proceedings before the national court. The national proceedings are suspended while the Court of Justice answers; the national court then applies the ruling to the facts before it. The procedure is governed by Article 267 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union and is regarded as the principal mechanism ensuring the uniform interpretation and application of EU law across the member states.

Who may refer

Any "court or tribunal of a Member State" may refer a question if it considers that a decision on the point is necessary to enable it to give judgment. Courts against whose decisions there is no judicial remedy under national law — typically supreme courts — are in principle obliged to refer, unless the point is irrelevant, has already been decided by the Court (acte éclairé), or the correct application of EU law is so obvious as to leave no scope for reasonable doubt (acte clair), exceptions developed in the CILFIT judgment (1982). The Court of Justice decides for itself whether the referring body qualifies as a court or tribunal, applying criteria such as legal origin, permanence, compulsory jurisdiction and independence.

Procedure

The national court frames the questions referred; the Court of Justice answers them in the abstract, reformulating where necessary, and does not decide the national case itself. Written observations may be submitted by the parties, the member states and the EU institutions, and the case usually proceeds with an opinion from an advocate general followed by a judgment. Proceedings typically take well over a year, though expedited and urgent procedures (the latter for cases in the area of freedom, security and justice, often involving detained persons) can deliver answers in a matter of weeks or months. In 2024, part of the Court's preliminary-reference jurisdiction in specific technical areas (such as VAT, excise duties and the customs code) was transferred to the General Court to ease the Court of Justice's workload.

Effects

The ruling binds the referring court, and its interpretation is authoritative for all national courts. Through references the Court of Justice has delivered many of its most consequential judgments, from Van Gend en Loos and Costa v ENEL onwards — both were preliminary references — to modern case law on data protection, consumer rights and the rule of law. The procedure is often described as a dialogue between national courts and the Court of Justice rather than an appeal, and it has been the channel through which individuals, via their national courts, can effectively challenge state and EU action.
