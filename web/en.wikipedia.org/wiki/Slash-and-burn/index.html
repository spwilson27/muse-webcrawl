Slash-and-burn

Slash-and-burn agriculture (also called swidden cultivation, shifting cultivation, or milpa in Mesoamerica and jhum in South Asia) is a farming method that involves cutting and burning plants in a forest or woodland to create a field called a swidden. The ash provides a nutrient-rich layer that helps fertilize crops, and the clearing temporarily suppresses weeds and pests.

After a few years of cultivation, yields decline as nutrients are exhausted, and the plot is abandoned to fallow; the farmers move to a new clearing while the old one regrows. With long fallow periods and low population density, the system can be sustainable and supports high agrobiodiversity. With shortened fallows, however, it leads to soil erosion, deforestation, and loss of forest carbon.

History and distribution

Swidden is one of humanity's oldest agricultural techniques, practised from the Neolithic onward. Today it remains widespread in the Amazon Basin, Central and West Africa, and Southeast Asia, supporting an estimated 200–500 million people in some form. In historical northern Europe it survived into the 20th century as Finnish svedjebruk.

Environmental debate

Slash-and-burn is frequently blamed for tropical deforestation, though studies distinguish smallholder swidden with long rotations from large-scale clearing for pasture and plantations. Alternatives promoted to farmers include Inga alley farming and other agroforestry systems designed to maintain fertility without burning.
