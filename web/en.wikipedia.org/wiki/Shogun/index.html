Shogun (将軍, shōgun), officially Sei-i Taishōgun (征夷大将軍, "Commander-in-Chief of the Expeditionary Force Against the Barbarians"), was the title of the military dictators of Japan during most of the period from 1185 to 1868. Nominally appointed by the Emperor, shoguns were usually the de facto rulers of the country, although during part of the Kamakura period they were themselves subject to the rule of the Hōjō regents.

History

The title was originally granted to imperial generals sent to subdue the Emishi peoples of northern Honshu in the early Heian period; Sakanoue no Tamuramaro was among its most famous early holders. It acquired its lasting meaning when Minamoto no Yoritomo, having defeated the Taira in the Genpei War, was appointed shogun in 1192 and established the Kamakura shogunate — a military government (bakufu) that ruled alongside, and above, the imperial court in Kyoto.

After the fall of Kamakura in 1333 and a brief imperial restoration, Ashikaga Takauji founded the Ashikaga (Muromachi) shogunate in 1336/1338. Its weakening grip in the 15th century ushered in the Sengoku period of civil war. Japan was reunified by Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi — neither of whom took the title — and in 1603 Tokugawa Ieyasu became shogun, founding the Tokugawa shogunate, which ruled from Edo for over 260 years of relative peace.

End of the office

The arrival of Commodore Perry's ships in 1853 and the ensuing crisis eroded the shogunate's authority. In 1867 the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, formally returned governing power to the Emperor (Taisei Hōkan), and the office was abolished with the Meiji Restoration in 1868.

The shogunate

The shogun's administration, the bakufu ("tent government"), controlled the military, foreign relations, and much of the land, while the Emperor retained ritual and legitimating functions. In the Tokugawa system the shogunate directly governed the great cities and the shogun's own vast domains, and kept the regional daimyō in check through the sankin-kōtai system of alternate attendance at Edo.
