Popped rice

Popped rice is a type of popped grain made from rice. It is a traditional food in Southeast Asia and the Indian subcontinent, where it has been produced for centuries using simple methods such as heating rice kernels in sand or oil. Popped rice can be eaten as a snack on its own or used to make sweet treats and breakfast cereals.

Production

Traditionally, rice is parboiled, dried, and then heated rapidly in hot sand, salt or oil, causing the moisture inside each grain to flash into steam and puff the kernel. In industrial production, rice flour dough may instead be extruded or rice grains are puffed in pressure guns similar to those used for other puffed cereals. Unlike popcorn, which pops from a hard, sealed hull, rice puffs because steam generated inside the grain expands its starchy interior.

Regional varieties

  • Muri / murmura — puffed rice in India and Bangladesh, used in street snacks such as bhel puri and jhal muri, served with spiced mixtures, and eaten with milk or curry.
  • Phaen / khai — popped rice in Thai and Lao traditions, including ceremonial uses.
  • Ampaw — sweetened popped rice cakes in the Philippines, often bound with syrup and served at festivals.
  • Puffed rice cereal — in Western countries, popped rice is sold as a breakfast cereal (such as Rice Krispies, introduced in 1928) and bound into crispy rice treats with marshmallow.

Cultural significance

In parts of India, popped rice is offered in religious rituals and is a staple festive food; in Bengal, the larger popped grains called khoi are used in ceremonies, while in Maharashtra puffed rice (churmure) is common street food. Popped rice is light, cheap and shelf-stable, making it a widespread poor person's snack as well as a festival food.
