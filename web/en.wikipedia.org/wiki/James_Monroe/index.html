James Monroe (April 28, 1758 – July 4, 1831) was an American statesman, lawyer, and diplomat who served as the fifth president of the United States from 1817 to 1825. The last president who was a Founding Father, and the last of the "Virginia dynasty," he is best known for the Monroe Doctrine and for presiding over the "Era of Good Feelings."

Early life and Revolutionary War

Monroe was born in Westmoreland County, Virginia, and studied at the College of William & Mary. He left college to fight in the Continental Army, was wounded at the Battle of Trenton (1776), and served as an aide to Lord Stirling. He later studied law under Thomas Jefferson, whose protégé he became.

Political career

Monroe served in the Virginia Assembly, the Continental Congress, and the U.S. Senate, and as minister to France and Britain. As a special envoy he helped negotiate the Louisiana Purchase in 1803. He was governor of Virginia and, under James Madison, Secretary of State (1811–1817) and briefly Secretary of War during the War of 1812.

Presidency

Monroe won easy election in 1816 and near-unanimous re-election in 1820 amid one-party dominance. His administration settled disputes with Britain (the Rush–Bagot Agreement and the Convention of 1818), acquired Florida from Spain by the Adams–Onís Treaty (1819), and managed the Missouri Compromise (1820) over the extension of slavery. In his annual message of 2 December 1823 he articulated the Monroe Doctrine: the Americas were closed to further European colonisation, and European intervention in the hemisphere would be viewed as a threat to the United States. His presidency also saw the Panic of 1819, the first major peacetime economic crisis.

Later life and legacy

Monroe retired to his Oak Hill estate in Loudoun County, Virginia, burdened by debts, and presided over the Virginia Constitutional Convention of 1829–1830. He died in New York City on 4 July 1831 — the third president to die on Independence Day, after John Adams and Jefferson (both 1826). The capital of Liberia, Monrovia, is named for him, in recognition of his support for the American Colonization Society.
