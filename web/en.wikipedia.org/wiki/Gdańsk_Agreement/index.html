Gdańsk Agreement

The Gdańsk Agreement (Polish: Porozumienia sierpniowe, the "August Agreements") was an accord signed on 31 August 1980 between the communist government of Poland and the striking workers of the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk, represented by the Inter-Enterprise Strike Committee led by Lech Wałęsa. The agreement legalised independent, self-governing trade unions — the first in a Soviet-bloc country — and led directly to the founding of Solidarity (Solidarność).

Background

In July 1980, price rises triggered strikes across Poland. On 14 August, workers at the Lenin Shipyard struck over the dismissal of crane operator Anna Walentynowicz; electrician Lech Wałęsa climbed the shipyard fence to lead the protest. The strike committee issued 21 demands, headed by the right to form free trade unions independent of the party and employers, the right to strike, freedom of speech, and the release of political prisoners.

The agreement

Negotiations were broadcast over the shipyard loudspeakers to thousands of workers. On 31 August, Wałęsa and Deputy Prime Minister Mieczysław Jagielski signed the agreement; Wałęsa famously used an oversized souvenir pen bearing the image of Pope John Paul II. Parallel agreements were concluded at Szczecin (30 August) and Jastrzębie (3 September). The government accepted free trade unions, limits on censorship, wage increases, and the broadcasting of Sunday Mass on state radio.

Consequences

On 17 September 1980 the national union Solidarity was founded, soon counting some 10 million members — nearly a third of Poland's population. Sixteen months of uneasy coexistence ended when General Wojciech Jaruzelski imposed martial law on 13 December 1981 and outlawed the union. Solidarity survived underground, was re-legalised in 1989, and its victory in the June 1989 elections set in motion the collapse of communist rule across Eastern Europe.
