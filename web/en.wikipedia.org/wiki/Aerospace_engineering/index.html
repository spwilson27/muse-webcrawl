Aerospace engineering

[Image: a jet airliner and a launching rocket side by side, illustrating the two branches of aerospace engineering]

Aerospace engineering is the field of engineering concerned with the design, development, testing and production of Aircraft and spacecraft. It is conventionally divided into aeronautical engineering, dealing with vehicles that fly within Earth's atmosphere, and astronautical engineering, dealing with vehicles that operate beyond it.

The discipline applies fluid mechanics, thermodynamics, materials science, structural analysis, guidance and control, and propulsion to vehicles ranging from gliders and jet airliners to rockets, satellites and interplanetary probes.

History

Aerospace engineering emerged from the pioneering aviation work of the early twentieth century — the Wright brothers' first powered flight in 1903 and the rapid development of aircraft in the First World War. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, Robert Goddard and Hermann Oberth laid the theoretical and practical foundations of rocketry in the same period.

The field expanded dramatically during the Space Race: the Soviet launch of Sputnik 1 in 1957, the creation of NASA, and the Apollo Moon landings transformed astronautical engineering and the industry's scale.

Branches and practice

Key sub-disciplines include aerodynamics, aeroelasticity, avionics, propulsion (jet engines, rocket motors), structures and materials such as composites and titanium alloys, and flight dynamics and control. Modern practice relies heavily on computational fluid dynamics and computer-aided design, verified by wind-tunnel and flight testing.

Major employers include aircraft manufacturers such as Boeing and Airbus, space agencies such as NASA and the European Space Agency, and private spaceflight companies.
