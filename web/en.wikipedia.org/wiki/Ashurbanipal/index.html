Ashurbanipal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Ashurbanipal hunting lions, from the carved reliefs of the North Palace at Nineveh]

Ashurbanipal (r. 669–631 BC, sometimes given as 668–627 BC) was the king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire at its greatest territorial extent — and its last great king. A scholar-king who boasted of his literacy ('I read the cuneiform script... I understand the pre-flood tablets' lore'), he is remembered above all for the Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, the systematic collection of Mesopotamian literature and scholarship whose excavation recovered the Epic of Gilgamesh and tens of thousands of tablets. His reign combined brilliant brutality — the sack of Thebes (663 BC), the destruction of Elam and Babylon's revolt — with civil wars that left the empire exhausted at his death.

The scholar-king's empire

Designated crown prince by Esarhaddon (whose succession treaty bound the empire to him), Ashurbanipal ruled Egypt briefly through vassals until Psamtik I asserted independence, campaigned in the Levant and Arabia, and made Nineveh the centre of a court of scholars, astrologers and scribes instructed to hunt down and copy tablets from every temple library in Babylonia. The royal reliefs of his palaces — the famous lion hunts, in which the king kills lions released from cages in ritualised mastery over chaos — are masterpieces of Assyrian art.

Wars and aftermath

His brother Shamash-shum-ukin's revolt in Babylon (652–648 BC) ended with Babylon starved and burned and the brother dead in his palace fire. Ashurbanipal then annihilated Elam (646 BC), destroying Susa and desecrating its kings' tombs — vengeance he described with relish. Within twenty years of his death the empire he left at its widest extent had collapsed entirely: Nineveh fell in 612 BC. Whether overextension, succession strife between his sons, or simple exhaustion caused the speed of the fall remains debated.
