Voyage data recorder

The fixed protective capsule of a ship's voyage data recorder

PurposeAccident investigation; recording of voyage data

A voyage data recorder (VDR) is a data recording system designed for merchant vessels, analogous to the flight recorder ("black box") of an aircraft. Collecting data from the ship's sensors and bridge audio, it preserves a record of the vessel's final hours for use in accident investigations. Under the International Maritime Organization's SOLAS convention, VDRs are mandatory for passenger ships and for cargo ships of 3,000 gross tonnage and over on international voyages.

Recorded data

A VDR continuously records, among other items:

  • Date and time, and ship's position (from GPS)
  • Speed through the water and over the ground
  • Heading, course over ground, and rudder orders and responses
  • Bridge audio from microphones, and VHF radio communications
  • Radar and ECDIS (electronic chart) images
  • Depth under the keel, main alarms, and status of watertight and fire doors
  • Wind speed and direction, engine orders, and hull stresses on some vessels

Protective capsule

The data are stored in a crash-protected capsule, designed to withstand the extreme shock, fire and deep-sea pressure following a sinking. Modern standards require both a fixed capsule and a float-free capsule fitted with an emergency position-indicating radio beacon (EPIRB), so that data can be recovered even if the ship is lost. A long-term recording medium inside the ship preserves 30 days (720 hours) of data.

Simplified VDR

Existing cargo ships could be fitted with a simplified voyage data recorder (S-VDR), which records a reduced set of data items where full interfacing with all bridge systems would be impracticable.

Investigations

VDR data has played a central role in investigations of collisions, groundings and sinkings, including those of the Costa Concordia (2012) and El Faro (2015), where the recovered recorder was retrieved from the seabed by remotely operated vehicle.
