Batrachotoxin

Batrachotoxin (BTX) is an extremely potent steroidal alkaloid neurotoxin, among the most powerful non-peptide neurotoxins known. It was first isolated from the skin secretions of the golden poison frog (Phyllobates terribilis) of Colombia — the name derives from the Greek batrachos, "frog". Indigenous Emberá people of the Chocó region have used secretions of this frog to poison blowgun darts for hunting. Related compounds occur in certain other poison frogs and, remarkably, in the feathers and skin of some New Guinea birds such as the hooded pitohui.

Mechanism and toxicity

Batrachotoxin binds to voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve and muscle membranes and locks them open, preventing inactivation. The resulting massive sodium influx causes irreversible depolarisation, paralysis, convulsions and death from respiratory or cardiac failure. The estimated lethal dose in mice is about 2 micrograms per kilogram by injection, making it roughly an order of magnitude more potent than curare. There is no known antidote; treatment is supportive, and tetrodotoxin — which blocks the same channel from the other side — can antagonise its effects experimentally.

Chemistry and research

Batrachotoxin was characterised by John Daly and colleagues at the National Institutes of Health in the 1960s; the frogs lose their toxicity in captivity, indicating the toxin ultimately derives from dietary arthropods, probably melyrid beetles. Because wild supplies are scarce and protected, total syntheses have been pursued for decades; a practical asymmetric synthesis was reported by the Baran laboratory in 2016. Batrachotoxin remains a valuable pharmacological probe of sodium-channel function.
