Coal mining is the extraction of coal from the ground. Coal has been mined since ancient times, but the industry expanded enormously with the Industrial Revolution, when coal fuelled steam engines, iron and steel production, and later electricity generation. Today coal remains a major global source of electricity and is essential to steelmaking, though its use is declining in many countries because of air pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions.

Methods

Surface mining — strip mining, opencast mining, and mountaintop removal — is used when seams lie near the surface; giant draglines and shovels strip away overburden to expose the coal. Underground mining reaches deeper seams by shafts and tunnels: in room-and-pillar mining, coal is cut from "rooms" leaving pillars to support the roof, while longwall mining shears coal from a long face and lets the roof collapse behind — the most productive underground method.

History

Coal was mined in Britain from Roman times and in China for centuries, but mechanisation in the 18th and 19th centuries — steam pumps, winding engines, safety lamps (after the explosions that killed many miners) — transformed output. Coal regions such as South Wales, the Ruhr, Appalachia, and Silesia became centres of industry and of labour movements. Peak employment passed in the mid-20th century in most Western countries as mechanisation rose and markets contracted; Britain's last deep mine closed in 2015.

Safety and environment

Mining hazards include roof falls, explosions of methane and coal dust, flooding, and long-term lung disease (pneumoconiosis, "black lung"). Disasters such as Monongah (1907) and Soma, Turkey (2014) killed hundreds. Environmental impacts include subsidence, acid mine drainage, landscape destruction from surface mining, and the carbon dioxide released when coal is burned — the largest single source of global CO₂ emissions from energy.
