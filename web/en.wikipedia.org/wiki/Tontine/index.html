Tontine

A tontine is an investment scheme in which subscribers contribute to a common fund and receive dividends from it; as members die, their shares are forfeited to the survivors, so each remaining member's income grows, until the last survivor receives everything. It is named after Lorenzo de Tonti, a Neapolitan banker who proposed the scheme to the French government in the 17th century.

History

Governments in France, England, and elsewhere used tontines to raise revenue, and in the 19th century tontine life insurance policies became hugely popular in the United States. The system fell into disrepute because it created a financial interest in other members' deaths — a theme exploited in fiction, notably the novel and television serial The Wrong Box — and because of abuses in fund management. Tontines were largely banned or heavily regulated in the early 20th century, though similar arrangements survive in some pension and communal savings schemes today, and economists periodically propose reviving them as retirement products.
