Homotopy groups of spheres

FieldAlgebraic topology
Notationπn(Sk)
Key resultsFreudenthal suspension theorem; Serre's finiteness theorem; Hopf invariant one theorem

In the mathematical field of algebraic topology, the homotopy groups of spheres describe how spheres of various dimensions can wrap around one another. They are examples of topological invariants, taking values in algebraic structures such as groups, that capture information about the maps from an n-dimensional sphere to a k-dimensional sphere up to continuous deformation (homotopy classes of maps).

Despite the elementary statement of the problem, most homotopy groups of spheres remain unknown in general. The subject has driven much of the development of modern algebraic topology, including spectral sequences, stable homotopy theory, and cobordism theory.

Background

The group πn(Sn) is the infinite cyclic group ℤ, its generator detected by degree; the Hopf map of 1931 showed that π3(S2) is also ℤ, generated by the Hopf fibration — a surprising result showing that maps from a sphere to a lower-dimensional sphere can be nontrivial. Freudenthal's suspension theorem implies that the groups stabilize: for n sufficiently large relative to k, πn(Sk) becomes constant, giving rise to the stable homotopy groups of spheres.

Structure

Jean-Pierre Serre proved in the early 1950s that, apart from the cases πn(Sn) and the Hopf-invariant groups π4n−1(S2n), all homotopy groups of spheres are finite abelian groups. Computations proceed stem by stem: the stable groups begin ℤ (the 0-stem), ℤ/2, ℤ/2, ℤ/24 (the 3-stem, related to the J-homomorphism), and so on. In the 2010s and 2020s, large-scale computations using motivic and synthetic methods — notably by Daniel Isaksen, Guozhen Wang, and Zhouli Xu — extended knowledge of the stable stems to around the 90-stem, resolving long-standing questions such as the Kervaire invariant one problem in most dimensions.

Methods

Principal tools include the long exact sequence of a fibration, the Serre spectral sequence, the Adams spectral sequence and its motivic and synthetic variants, Pontryagin–Thom theory relating homotopy groups to cobordism classes of framed manifolds, and Toda brackets. The Hopf invariant one theorem of J. Frank Adams restricts the Hopf-invariant-one elements to dimensions 1, 2, 4, and 8, corresponding to the real division algebras.
