Aquavit (also spelled akvavit; Danish: akvavit, Norwegian: akevitt, Swedish: akvavit) is a distilled spirit produced principally in Scandinavia, where it has been made since the 15th century. Its name derives from the Latin aqua vitae, "water of life", the same phrase that gives rise to the Irish uisce beatha (whiskey) and French eau de vie.

Aquavit is distilled from grain or potatoes and must be flavoured such that the dominant taste comes from caraway and/or dill seed. Many brands add other herbs and spices — such as anise, fennel, coriander, citrus peel and cumin — and the spirit is commonly matured in oak casks, which give Norwegian styles their characteristic golden colour and fuller flavour.

History

The earliest known reference to aquavit-like spirit in Scandinavia is a 1531 letter from the Danish lord Eske Bille, who sent a sample to Olav Engelbrektsson, the last Archbishop of Norway, praising its supposed medicinal properties. Production spread through Denmark, Norway and Sweden, and by the 19th century aquavit had become the standard festive schnapps of the region.

Styles

Danish aquavits are typically clear and strongly caraway-flavoured, drunk ice-cold in a single shot. Swedish akvavit is similar and is a fixture of the midsummer and crayfish-party tables. Norwegian akevitt is usually barrel-aged — most famously Linie aquavit, which is shipped across the equator and back in sherry casks, the rocking of the voyage and temperature changes mellowing the spirit. Iceland produces a related caraway spirit called brennivín.

Drinking culture

Aquavit is traditionally served chilled in small stemmed glasses alongside hearty, fatty foods — pickled herring, smoked salmon, roast pork and smorgasbord dishes — and is inseparable from celebratory toasts sung to tunes such as "Helan går". Under European Union rules, aquavit must contain at least 37.5% alcohol by volume and be predominantly flavoured with caraway or dill.
