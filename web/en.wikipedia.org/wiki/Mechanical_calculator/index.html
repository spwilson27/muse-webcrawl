Mechanical calculator

A mechanical calculator, or calculating machine, is a mechanical device used to perform the basic operations of arithmetic automatically. Most mechanical calculators were comparable in size to small desktop computers and have been rendered obsolete by the advent of the electronic calculator.

History

The first mechanical calculator was the Pascaline, designed by Blaise Pascal in 1642 to help his father, a tax collector, and capable of addition and subtraction with automatic carries. In 1672–73 Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz designed the Stepped Reckoner, introducing the stepped drum (Leibniz wheel), which allowed all four operations; only two were built. In the 1820s–1850s Charles Xavier Thomas de Colmar's arithmometer became the first mass-produced calculating machine, widely used in offices for a century.

Later developments

  • The Odhner pinwheel calculator (1873) miniaturised arithmetic machines; millions of clones were made.
  • W. T. Odhner, Dorr E. Felt's Comptometer (1887) and William Seward Burroughs's printing adding machine (1888) commercialised fast key-driven machines.
  • The Curta calculator (1948), designed by Curt Herzstark, was a small hand-cranked cylinder that could perform all four operations and remained in production until 1970.
  • Electromechanical desk calculators of the 1950s–60s (Friden, Marchant, Monroe) were displaced by electronic calculators in the early 1970s.

Mechanisms

Core techniques include the Leibniz stepped drum, the pinwheel, proportional levers, and the carry mechanisms (such as Pascal's sautoir) that propagate tens between digit wheels. Input was by sliding levers, keyboards, or cranks; output appeared on digit wheels or, on printing models, on paper tape.
