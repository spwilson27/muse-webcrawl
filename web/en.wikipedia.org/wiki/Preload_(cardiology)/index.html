Preload (cardiology)

In cardiac physiology, preload is the end-diastolic volume (EDV) that stretches the right or left ventricle of the heart to its greatest dimensions under variable physiologic demand. In simplified terms, preload is the stretching of the cardiac myocytes prior to contraction, related to the sarcomere length. Since sarcomere length cannot easily be measured in the intact heart, preload is usually estimated from indices such as end-diastolic volume or end-diastolic pressure.

Definition and determinants

Preload is affected by venous return, blood volume, heart rate, and ventricular compliance. An increase in venous return — for example from a blood transfusion or sympathetic venoconstriction — increases preload. Conversely, haemorrhage, dehydration, or venodilator drugs reduce it.

Frank–Starling law

According to the Frank–Starling law of the heart, an increase in preload increases stroke volume: the greater the stretch of the ventricular wall during filling, the more forceful the contraction, up to a physiological limit. This mechanism matches output of the two ventricles and adapts cardiac output to changes in venous return.

Clinical relevance

Estimating preload helps guide fluid therapy in shock and heart failure. Central venous pressure and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure have historically been used as surrogates, though dynamic measures such as stroke volume variation are now often preferred in critically ill patients.
