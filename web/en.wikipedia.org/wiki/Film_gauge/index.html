Film gauge

Film gauge is a physical property of photographic or motion picture film stock which defines its width. The most commonly used gauges in cinema history are 8 mm, 16 mm, 35 mm, and 70 mm, measured across the width of the film strip including its sprocket holes.

Main gauges

35 mm became the standard for theatrical cinema in the early twentieth century and remained dominant for a hundred years. 16 mm, introduced in 1923, served educational, documentary, television, and independent production. 8 mm and Super 8 were designed for home movies. 70 mm (shot on 65 mm stock) offered a larger image area for prestige roadshow releases and formats such as Todd-AO and IMAX.

Characteristics

A larger gauge provides a bigger frame area, giving finer grain, greater resolution, and more light for projection, at higher cost in stock and equipment. Gauges are further characterized by perforation pitch and shape, the number of perforations per frame (the "pulldown"), and the space left for soundtracks. Though digital cinematography has displaced film in most production, the traditional gauges remain in use by some filmmakers and for archival purposes.
