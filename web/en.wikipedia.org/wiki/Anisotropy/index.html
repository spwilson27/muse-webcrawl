Anisotropy
Properties that vary with direction
OppositeIsotropy
FieldsPhysics, materials science, geology, cosmology

Anisotropy is the property of a material or system whose characteristics differ depending on the direction in which they are measured. It is the opposite of isotropy, in which properties are the same in all directions. The word derives from Greek an- (not), isos (equal) and tropos (direction).

Examples in materials

Wood is a familiar anisotropic material: it is far stronger and stiffer along the grain than across it. Single crystals are anisotropic in properties such as refractive index (giving birefringence in calcite), thermal expansion, electrical conductivity and elastic stiffness. Composite materials are deliberately made anisotropic — fibre-reinforced polymers are stiffest along their fibres. In geology, seismic anisotropy in the Earth's mantle records the alignment of olivine crystals by mantle flow.

In physics and cosmology

The cosmic microwave background is almost isotropic, and its tiny anisotropies — temperature fluctuations of about one part in 100,000 measured by the COBE, WMAP and Planck satellites — carry information about the early universe and the seeds of galaxies. Magnetic materials show anisotropy in their magnetisation energy, which governs the behaviour of permanent magnets and magnetic recording media.

Mathematical description

Direction-dependent properties are generally described by tensors rather than single numbers: for example, conductivity and elasticity in anisotropic media are represented by second- and fourth-rank tensors respectively, whose components change with the choice of axes.
