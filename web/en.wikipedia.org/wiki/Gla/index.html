Gla

Gla (Greek: Γλα), also called Ghlas (Γλᾶς), was an important fortified site of the Mycenaean world in Boeotia, Greece, built on a low rocky outcrop in the former Lake Copais. Dating to the 13th century BC, Gla is remarkable for the scale of its fortifications: its Cyclopean walls enclose an area far larger than that of other Mycenaean citadels such as Mycenae or Tiryns, yet contain comparatively few buildings.

Site and remains

The fortress occupies a flat-topped limestone outcrop roughly 700 metres long and up to 250 metres wide. Its circuit wall, about 3 kilometres in length and up to 5 metres thick, is built in the massive "Cyclopean" masonry typical of Mycenaean fortification and is pierced by four gates. Inside the walls were a small number of structures, including a central complex often interpreted as a palace or administrative building (sometimes called the Melathron), storerooms and an agora-like enclosure.

Function

Gla is generally connected with the great Mycenaean drainage works that reclaimed Lake Copais for agriculture: massive dykes and canals diverted the rivers of the basin to sinkholes (katavothres) at its edge. The fortress probably served as an administrative and storage centre overseeing the reclaimed land and its produce, and perhaps as a refuge. The site was destroyed by fire around the end of the 13th century BC and never reoccupied on the same scale, as the drainage system failed and the lake returned.

Excavations

The site was explored in the late 19th century and excavated by Greek archaeologists, notably in campaigns led by Ioannis Threpsiadis in the 1950s and later by Spyros Iakovidis, whose work clarified the plan of the central complex.
