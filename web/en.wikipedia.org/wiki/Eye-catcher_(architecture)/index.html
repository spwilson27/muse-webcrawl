Eye-catcher (architecture)

A hilltop folly built to catch the eye and terminate a vista

In landscape architecture and garden design, an eye-catcher is a structure or feature placed at a prominent point — often at the end of a vista or on a skyline — in order to draw and reward the gaze and to give a designed landscape a focal point or sense of destination. Eye-catchers are a characteristic device of the eighteenth-century English landscape garden.

Forms

Eye-catchers take many forms: a folly tower or sham ruin, a temple, an obelisk, a triumphal arch, a distant church spire deliberately incorporated into the view, or even a neighbouring landowner's monument framed by planting. Unlike most garden buildings they were intended to be seen from afar rather than visited; many are mere façades, hollow shells or ruins by design, built cheaply of local materials because only their silhouette was meant to be read. Famous examples include the Broadway Tower in Worcestershire, the sham ruined arch at Kew, and numerous estate temples aligned on the principal axis of a great house.

Function and use

The eye-catcher organised the experience of a landscape: it marked the boundary of an estate, suggested greater extent or antiquity than the property possessed, and gave walkers and riders a goal. The term is also used more loosely in architecture and urban design for any striking building intended to arrest attention and anchor a view corridor.
