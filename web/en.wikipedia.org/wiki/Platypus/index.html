Platypus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The platypus (Ornithorhynchus anatinus) is a semiaquatic, egg-laying mammal (a monotreme) endemic to eastern Australia and Tasmania — one of only five living monotreme species, with the echidnas. Its mosaic anatomy — a duck-like bill, beaver-like tail, otter-like feet and venomous ankle spurs in males — so baffled European naturalists that the first specimens sent to Britain (1799) were suspected of being a taxidermist's hoax.

The bill is not a beak but a soft, electro-sensitive organ: the platypus hunts underwater with eyes, ears and nostrils shut, detecting the electric fields of prey muscle contractions — the only mammal to forage primarily by electroreception. Females lay leathery eggs and nurse their young on milk secreted onto skin patches, having no nipples. The platypus genome, sequenced in 2008, is equally mosaic — mammal, reptile and bird-like features side by side, recording its deep divergence (~166 million years) from other mammals.

Venom and oddities

Male platypus venom, though not lethal to humans, causes intense pain and swelling; it shares gene families with reptile venoms by convergence. Platypus fur fluoresces blue-green under ultraviolet light (discovered 2020) — a trait of unknown function shared with some other mammals.

Conservation

Listed Near Threatened, the platypus declines with river degradation, drought, dams and bycatch in fishing nets; it is a flagship for Australian freshwater conservation.
