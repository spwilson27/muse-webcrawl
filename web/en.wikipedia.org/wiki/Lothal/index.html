Lothal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lothal Location: Gujarat, India
Culture: Indus Valley Civilisation
Period: c. 3700–1500 BCE
Excavated: 1955–1960

Lothal (IPA: [loˑtʰəl]) was one of the southernmost cities of the ancient Indus Valley Civilisation, located in the modern state of Gujarat, India, near the Gulf of Khambhat. Construction of the city is believed to have begun around 3700 BCE, and it became an important port trading in beads, gems, copper and ivory with Mesopotamia, Egypt and Persia. The name Lothal, in Gujarati, means "the mound of the dead".

Excavation

The site was discovered in 1954 and excavated between 1955 and 1960 by S. R. Rao of the Archaeological Survey of India. Its most famous structure is a large trapezoidal basin lined with fired bricks, interpreted by Rao as a dockyard — among the earliest known in the world — which would have been connected by a channel to the Sabarmati river and the sea. Some archaeologists have disputed the dock interpretation, arguing the basin was a reservoir for irrigation or drinking water, but the discovery of anchors, seals and a warehouse beside the basin supports its role in maritime trade.

The city

Lothal was planned on a grid, with an upper town or acropolis for the ruler and merchants, and a lower town of workshops and residences. A bead-making factory, with furnaces and unfinished carnelian beads, indicates specialised craft production for export. The city was twice destroyed by floods and rebuilt; it declined after about 1900 BCE as trade patterns changed, and was finally abandoned around 1500 BCE.
