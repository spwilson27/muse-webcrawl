Jaggery

Jaggery is a traditional non-centrifugal cane sugar consumed widely in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and parts of Africa and the Caribbean. It is made by boiling raw, concentrated sugarcane juice — or sap from palm trees such as date palm, coconut palm, or palmyra — until it solidifies. Unlike refined sugar, jaggery is not separated from the molasses and retains trace minerals and a deep golden-brown color.

Production

Sugarcane juice is extracted by crushing, clarified by boiling (traditionally with natural clarificants), and concentrated in large shallow pans over a furnace fired with dried cane bagasse. When the syrup thickens, it is poured into moulds to set as blocks, or granulated. Palm jaggery is made by tapping palm inflorescences, collecting the sweet sap, and boiling it down the same day before it ferments.

Uses

Jaggery is used in sweet and savory dishes throughout the Indian subcontinent: in sweets such as payasam and laddoo, in lentil and vegetable dishes in Gujarati and Maharashtrian cooking, and dissolved in tea and coffee in place of sugar. It is valued in Ayurvedic tradition and used in offerings and festivals. Well-known regional forms include Indian gur, Bangladeshi gur, Sri Lankan hakuru (coconut jaggery used in treacle), and Burmese palm jaggery.
