Dome

A dome (from Latin domus, "house") is an architectural element similar to the hollow upper half of a sphere. There is significant overlap with the term cupola, which may also refer to a dome or a structure on top of a dome. Domes can be built of masonry, concrete, metal, glass or other materials, and they transfer their loads efficiently through compression, allowing large interior spaces to be covered without internal supports.

History

Corbelled domes were built by prehistoric peoples, but the form was perfected by Roman engineers, whose unreinforced concrete dome of the Pantheon in Rome (completed c. 125 CE), at 43.3 metres in diameter, remained the world's largest for well over a millennium. Byzantine architects, notably at Hagia Sophia (537 CE), placed domes on pendentives, an innovation imitated throughout the Islamic world and Renaissance Europe. Brunelleschi's double-shelled dome for Florence Cathedral (1436) revived large-scale dome building in the West, followed by Michelangelo's dome of St Peter's Basilica. In the twentieth century, geodesic domes popularized by Buckminster Fuller and thin-shell concrete domes extended the form to stadiums and exhibition halls.

Structural behaviour

A dome behaves like an arch rotated about a vertical axis: meridional forces are compressive, while hoop forces near the base become tensile and must be resisted by chains, rings or buttressing — the problem that produced cracks in many historic domes and inspired structural analysis from Hooke's hanging-chain analogy onward.
