Stratovolcano

A stratovolcano, also known as a composite volcano, is a conical volcano built up by many alternating layers (strata) of hardened lava and tephra. Unlike shield volcanoes, stratovolcanoes are characterized by a steep profile with a summit crater and explosive eruptions, with periodic intervals of quiet or effusive eruptions.

Stratovolcanoes are among the most common type of volcano and include many of the Earth's best-known examples, such as Mount Fuji in Japan, Mount St. Helens and Mount Rainier in the United States, Mount Vesuvius and Mount Etna in Italy, and Cotopaxi in Ecuador. They typically form at subduction zones, where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another, and are common around the Pacific Ring of Fire.

Formation

Stratovolcanoes form from viscous, slow-moving lava — typically andesite, dacite, or rhyolite — that cools and hardens before spreading far. Alternating layers of lava flows, volcanic ash, cinders, and pyroclastic material accumulate over many eruptions, building the tall, layered cone. The high viscosity of the magma, combined with dissolved gases, makes eruptions prone to be explosive.

Hazards

Stratovolcano eruptions can be highly destructive. Hazards include pyroclastic flows, lahars (volcanic mudflows), ashfall, lava flows, and landslides from flank collapse. The 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens and the AD 79 eruption of Vesuvius, which destroyed Pompeii and Herculaneum, are famous examples of stratovolcano destructive power.
