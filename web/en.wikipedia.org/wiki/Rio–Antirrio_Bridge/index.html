The Rio–Antirrio Bridge (Greek: Γέφυρα Ρίου–Αντιρρίου), officially the Charilaos Trikoupis Bridge, is one of the world's longest multi-span cable-stayed bridges, crossing the Gulf of Corinth near Patras in Greece. It links the town of Rio on the Peloponnese to Antirrio on the Greek mainland, completing a fixed connection long envisioned since the 19th century.

Design and construction

The bridge is 2,880 metres long overall, with a continuous cable-stayed deck of 2,252 metres carried on four pylons. Its foundations rest on a seabed up to 65 metres deep in a seismically active zone where the gulf is widening by up to about 30 mm per year; the piers stand on gravel layers laid on the seabed and are designed to slide during earthquakes, while the deck is suspended from the pylons so it can accommodate tectonic movement.

Construction by the French-led Gefyra consortium, designed by architect Berdj Mikaelian, began in 1998. The bridge was completed ahead of schedule and opened to traffic on 7 August 2004, shortly before the Athens Olympic Games; the Olympic flame crossed over it. Its cost was approximately €839 million, financed partly by the European Investment Bank.

Significance

Before the bridge, crossing depended on ferries taking about 45 minutes; the bridge reduced the crossing to roughly five minutes. It carries the Ionia Odos (A5) motorway and forms part of European route E55. The bridge is named for Charilaos Trikoupis, the 19th-century Greek prime minister who first proposed a bridge at the site in 1889.
