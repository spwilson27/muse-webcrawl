Arithmetic logic unit

An arithmetic logic unit (ALU) is a combinational digital circuit that performs arithmetic and bitwise operations on integer binary numbers. It is a fundamental building block of the central processing unit (CPU) of many computers. The control unit tells the ALU which operation to perform on the available data; when the operation is finished, the result is stored in an output register, and status bits record information such as carry, overflow, zero, and sign.

Functions

Typical ALU operations include addition, subtraction (usually by two's-complement addition), bitwise AND, OR, XOR, and NOT, shifts and rotations, and comparisons (effectively subtraction with the result discarded in favor of the status flags). Many ALUs also generate increment, decrement, and pass-through results. Multiple ALUs, or multi-cycle units, handle multiplication and division in most designs.

History

Mathematician John von Neumann proposed the ALU as part of the stored-program computer architecture in his 1945 First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC. In 1946 he and colleagues elaborated the design at the Institute for Advanced Study. Early implementations ranged from the single-bit serial ALU of the Manchester Baby to the 74181, a 4-bit ALU slice introduced by Texas Instruments in 1970 that was widely used in minicomputers and early microprocessors and could be cascaded for wider words.

Implementation

An ALU is built from logic gates: a chain of full adders performs addition, with carry-lookahead logic added in faster designs, and multiplexers select among the arithmetic and logic results. Floating-point arithmetic is generally performed by a separate floating-point unit (FPU). Modern processors contain several ALUs so that superscalar pipelines can execute multiple integer operations per clock cycle.
