Social housing

Notable systemsVienna (Gemeindebau), Netherlands housing associations, UK council housing, Singapore HDB

Social housing is housing provided at below-market rents by public authorities or non-profit organisations and allocated according to need rather than ability to pay. It is distinct from purely private rental housing and from owner-occupation, and overlaps with the broader category of public housing, though in many countries most social housing is now provided by independent housing associations rather than directly by the state.

Models around the world

In Vienna, roughly 60 per cent of residents live in municipally owned or subsidised housing, including the famous interwar Gemeindebauten such as Karl-Marx-Hof; because eligibility extends well into the middle class, the stock is socially mixed and politically well supported. In the Netherlands, housing associations own around 29 per cent of all homes, the largest social sector in Europe. In the United Kingdom, council housing built after 1919 housed a third of the population at its late-1970s peak, but the Right to Buy policy from 1980 sold much of the stock to tenants, and provision shifted to housing associations. In Singapore, the Housing and Development Board houses the great majority of the population, though most flats are sold on long leases rather than rented.

Allocation and rents

Social homes are typically allocated through waiting lists assessed on need — homelessness, overcrowding, disability or local connection — although in high-demand areas waits can run to years. Rents are usually set by formula, often linked to tenants' incomes or to historic costs rather than local market levels, which keeps housing affordable but also constrains providers' investment. Tenancies in social housing are usually more secure than in the private sector.

Debates

Supporters argue social housing provides stability, mixed communities and insulation from housing-market volatility. Critics in some countries associate large estates with concentrated poverty and social stigma, and argue for demand-side subsidies instead. Since the 2008 financial crisis and amid rising housing costs, many governments have revived social housebuilding programmes, while debates continue over land value, funding models and the balance between targeted welfare housing and universal provision.
