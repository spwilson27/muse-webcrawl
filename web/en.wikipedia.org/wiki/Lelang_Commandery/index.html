Lelang Commandery

Lelang Commandery (Chinese: 樂浪郡; Korean: 낙랑군, Nangnang-gun) was one of the Chinese commanderies established by Emperor Wu of the Han dynasty after his conquest of Wiman Joseon in 108 BC. Centred near modern Pyongyang on the Taedong River, it was the most durable of the Four Commanderies of Han, administering territory in the northern Korean Peninsula for more than four centuries, until it was conquered by Goguryeo in AD 313.

History

After the Han conquest, Lelang became a hub of trade and cultural exchange between China, the Korean Three Kingdoms' predecessors and the Japanese archipelago; its census of AD 2 recorded over 400,000 inhabitants in 25 counties. In AD 82 the commandery was reduced in size as outlying counties were abandoned. In the early third century the Gongsun warlords of Liaodong split off its southern districts as Daifang Commandery. Lelang thereafter passed to Cao Wei and then the Jin dynasty, whose weakening grip allowed Goguryeo under King Micheon to overrun the commandery in 313.

Culture and archaeology

Excavations around Pyongyang, especially during the Japanese colonial period, uncovered hundreds of Han-period tombs with lacquerware, mirrors, coins and painted brick chambers, including the famous Anak Tomb No. 3 in some Korean accounts assigned to the later Goguryeo period. The painted "basket image" lacquerware from Lelang tombs is a highlight of collections in the National Museum of Korea. Lelang served as a conduit through which Chinese writing, iron technology, coinage and administrative practices reached the peninsula and, via trade, Japan.

Historiography

The exact location and significance of Lelang are sensitive subjects in Korean historiography, because the commandery's location at Pyongyang implies a long period of direct Chinese administration on the peninsula; nationalist scholarship in both Koreas has at times disputed its siting, though the mainstream view accepts the Pyongyang location on archaeological grounds.
