A maze is a path or collection of paths, typically from an entrance to a goal. The word is used both for branching puzzle structures in which a solver must find a route, and for unicursal designs that lead unambiguously through a convoluted path to the centre; in modern usage, maze generally denotes the branching (multicursal) type, while labyrinth is reserved for the unicursal type. Mazes have been built with walls of stone, hedge, turf and corn (maize), on floors and pavements, and on paper as puzzles.

History

The most famous maze of legend is the Cretan Labyrinth of Greek mythology, built by Daedalus for King Minos to imprison the Minotaur, and penetrated by Theseus with the help of Ariadne's thread. Egypt's great mortuary complex at Hawara was described by Herodotus as a labyrinth surpassing the pyramids. In the Middle Ages, unicursal labyrinths were laid in the floors of cathedrals such as Chartres, serving as symbolic pilgrimages.

Branching hedge mazes became fashionable in the formal gardens of Renaissance and Baroque Europe; the maze at Hampton Court Palace, planted around 1690 for William III, is the oldest surviving hedge maze in Britain and remains famous. Turf mazes and "mizmaze" sites survive in England, and in the late twentieth century large-scale maize ("corn") mazes became a popular seasonal attraction, especially in North America, cut into elaborate pictorial designs.

Solving

The most elementary solving method is the wall follower rule (the "hand-on-wall" method): by keeping one hand continuously on a wall, a solver is guaranteed to reach the exit of a simply-connected maze, though possibly not by the shortest route. More systematic methods from graph theory — treating junctions as nodes and paths as edges — include depth-first search, Trémaux's algorithm, dead-end filling, and the A* algorithm used in computer pathfinding.

Mazes in science

Mazes are standard apparatus in animal psychology and behavioural neuroscience: the radial arm maze, Morris water maze and Barnes maze are used to study spatial learning and memory in rodents, a research tradition going back to early twentieth-century experiments with rats in small-scale replicas of the Hampton Court maze.

Generation

Computer maze generation is a classic application of graph algorithms: a grid of cells is treated as a graph and a spanning tree is carved out using methods such as randomised depth-first search, Kruskal's algorithm, Prim's algorithm, or Wilson's algorithm, each producing mazes with different textures and bias.
