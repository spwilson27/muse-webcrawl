Chacmool

A chacmool is a distinctive form of Mesoamerican stone sculpture depicting a reclining human figure with its head turned to one side, knees drawn up, and a bowl or disc held on the stomach, apparently as a receptacle for offerings. Chacmools are found at Toltec, Maya, Aztec, and other pre-Columbian sites in Mexico and Central America, dating roughly from the 9th to the 16th centuries.

History and interpretation

The name was coined in 1875 by the explorer Augustus Le Plongeon, who excavated an example at Chichén Itzá and gave it a fanciful Yucatec Maya name meaning "thundering paw". Well-known examples stand before the Temple of the Warriors at Chichén Itzá, at Tula, and in the Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan, where one retained traces of polychromy and a sacrificial bowl. Scholars generally interpret the figures as intermediaries between worshippers and the gods, receiving offerings — possibly including sacrificial hearts — though their exact identity remains debated.
