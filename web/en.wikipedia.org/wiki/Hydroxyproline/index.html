Hydroxyproline

Hydroxyproline, or (2S,4R)-4-hydroxypyrrolidine-2-carboxylic acid, is a non-proteinogenic amino acid that is a major component of the protein collagen, comprising roughly 13.5 percent of mammalian collagen. It is produced by hydroxylation of the amino acid proline by the enzyme prolyl hydroxylase following protein synthesis (post-translational modification), a reaction that requires vitamin C. Hydroxyproline stabilizes the collagen triple helix and its deficiency contributes to the symptoms of scurvy.

Molar mass131.13 g/mol

Synthesis and function

Hydroxyproline is not incorporated into proteins directly from the genetic code; instead, specific proline residues in procollagen chains are hydroxylated by prolyl 4-hydroxylase in the endoplasmic reticulum, using ascorbic acid (vitamin C) as a cofactor. The hydroxyl group strengthens the collagen triple helix through stereoelectronic effects and hydrogen-bonding networks involving bridging water molecules. Another form, 3-hydroxyproline, occurs in smaller amounts.

Occurrence

Besides collagen and the closely related connective-tissue proteins, hydroxyproline occurs in elastin (in small amounts), in plant cell-wall proteins such as extensin, and in some antibiotics and fungal toxins. Gelatin, produced from collagen, is correspondingly rich in hydroxyproline, and the amino acid is used as a marker for collagen content in foods and tissues.

Clinical significance

Breakdown of collagen releases hydroxyproline, which is excreted in urine or metabolized in the liver; urinary hydroxyproline was historically used as a marker of bone resorption and of disorders such as Paget's disease of bone. Defects in hydroxyproline metabolism are associated with hyperhydroxyprolinemia. Measurement of hydroxyproline in tissue samples remains a standard quantitative assay for fibrosis in research.
