Grunge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Seattle band on stage in ripped jeans and flannel shirts under harsh lights]
A Seattle band on stage in ripped jeans and flannel shirts under harsh lights

Grunge is a rock music genre and subculture that emerged during the mid-1980s in the Pacific Northwest, primarily Seattle, fusing Punk rock's rawness with heavy metal's weight and indie rock's songcraft. In the early 1990s, led by Nirvana's Nevermind (1991), it became the dominant form of rock worldwide — before the death of Kurt Cobain in 1994 marked its end as a movement.

Origins

Seattle's isolation bred a local sound: Green River, Soundgarden, the Melvins and Mudhoney on the independent label Sub Pop, whose publicists coined and marketed 'grunge' (the word had been used dismissively since the 1970s). Songs alternated quiet verses with explosive choruses over down-tuned, fuzz-heavy guitars; thrift-store flannel and apathy became the anti-fashion uniform.

Breakthrough

Nevermind — and its single 'Smells Like Teen Spirit' — knocked Michael Jackson from number one in January 1992, opening the floodgates: Pearl Jam's Ten, Soundgarden's Badmotorfinger and Alice in Chains defined the 'Seattle four', while major labels strip-mined the scene and fashion houses sold designer grunge. Cobain's discomfort with fame, and his suicide in April 1994, fixed the genre's myth of authenticity consumed by its own success.

Legacy

Grunge killed hair metal, normalised alternative rock's dominance of the 1990s and left a permanent template of loud–quiet dynamics and confessional songwriting — inherited, diluted or fought against by post-grunge, Britpop's rivals and every guitar generation since.
