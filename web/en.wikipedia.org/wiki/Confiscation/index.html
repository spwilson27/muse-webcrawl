Confiscation

Confiscation (from the Latin confiscatio, "joining to the fiscus", i.e. transfer to the treasury) is a legal form of seizure by a government or other public authority. The penalty can involve the seizure of specific property, or general assets of a person or organization. It differs from forfeiture in some legal traditions, and from eminent domain, which involves compensation.

History

Confiscation has been used since antiquity — in Rome against condemned persons, in medieval and early modern Europe against traitors and religious dissidents, and during revolutions, as in the seizure of church and émigré property during the French Revolution. The American Civil War saw the Confiscation Acts of 1861 and 1862 directed against property used in support of the Confederacy.

Modern usage

Modern states confiscate property in contexts including customs enforcement, proceeds-of-crime legislation, sanctions against foreign nationals and entities, and penalties for regulatory offences. Such powers are constrained in many jurisdictions by constitutional protections of property and due process; in the United States, the Excessive Fines Clause has been applied to limit punitive forfeitures. Asset recovery of looted state funds is a major theme of international anti-corruption law, including the UN Convention against Corruption.
