Tankette

Tankette
Type: Light armoured fighting vehicle
Crew: 1–2
Era: Interwar period and Second World War

A tankette is a tracked armoured fighting vehicle that resembles a small tank, roughly the size of a car. It is mainly intended for light infantry support or reconnaissance. Colloquially it may also simply mean a small tank. Tankettes were most popular in the interwar period, when several countries produced them in large numbers, but their thin armour and light armament proved inadequate in the Second World War.

Origins

The concept emerged in Britain in the 1920s, when Major Giffard Le Quesne Martel built a one-man "tank" in his garage, and the Carden Loyd tankette — widely exported and copied under licence — became the archetype of the class. Major-General J. F. C. Fuller and others envisioned swarms of cheap one- or two-man vehicles, though in practice tankettes were most useful as machine-gun carriers and reconnaissance vehicles.

Notable types

Poland's TKS, Italy's L3/33 and L3/35, Japan's Type 94 and Type 97 Te-Ke, France's Renault UE Chenillette, and the Soviet T-27 were all produced in substantial numbers. Several of these were license developments of the Carden Loyd design.

Combat record

Tankettes saw combat in the Second Sino-Japanese War, the Spanish Civil War, the Polish campaign of 1939 and elsewhere. Their speed could not compensate for armour that could be defeated by heavy machine guns and anti-tank rifles, and the class was largely abandoned in favour of light tanks and armoured cars, though the idea reappeared in later unmanned and ultra-light vehicles such as the German Wiesel.
