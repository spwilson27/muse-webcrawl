Powhatan

The Powhatan people — also spelled Powatan, and known as the Virginia Algonquians — are a Native American people who formed a powerful chiefdom, the Powhatan Confederacy, in the tidewater region of what is now Virginia. They spoke an Eastern Algonquian language and were the people encountered by English colonists who founded Jamestown in 1607.

The paramount chiefdom

At the time of English contact, the paramount chief (mamanatowick) was Wahunsenacawh, known to the English as Chief Powhatan. He had built, by inheritance and conquest, a confederacy of some 30 tribes and roughly 150 settlements between the Potomac River and the Virginia Capes, with a population estimated at 14,000 to 21,000 people. His capital was at Werowocomoco on the York River.

Relations with Jamestown

Initial relations mixed trade, diplomacy and conflict. The English account of Captain John Smith being saved from execution by Powhatan's daughter Pocahontas (Matoaka) became one of the most famous stories of early America, though historians debate its literal truth. War broke out in 1609–1614 (the First Anglo-Powhatan War), ending with the capture of Pocahontas and her marriage to tobacco planter John Rolfe, which brought several years of peace.

Powhatan died in 1618 and was succeeded by his brother Opechancanough, who led major attacks on the colony in 1622 and 1644. After the second attack, Opechancanough was captured and killed, and the treaty of 1646 confined the Powhatan people to reserved lands, beginning centuries of pressure on their territory and identity.

Descendants today

Several Powhatan-descendant tribes in Virginia, including the Pamunkey and Mattaponi, retain reservations established in the colonial era — among the oldest in the United States. In 2018, six Virginia tribes, including the Pamunkey (recognised earlier, in 2015), received United States federal recognition.
