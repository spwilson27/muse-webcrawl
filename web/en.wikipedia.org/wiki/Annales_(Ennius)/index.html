Ennius's Annales was the national epic of Rome until it was superseded by Virgil's Aeneid.

The Annales was a Latin epic poem by Quintus Ennius (239–169 BC) recounting the history of Rome from the fall of Troy and the wanderings of Aeneas down to events of the poet's own lifetime. Composed in eighteen books, it was the first major Roman epic written in dactylic hexameter, the metre of Homer, replacing the native Saturnian verse of earlier poets such as Naevius.

Content and structure

The poem opened with Ennius's famous dream in which the soul of Homer appeared to him and declared that it had passed, through Pythagoras and a peacock, into Ennius himself — a claim that Ennius was Homer reborn. The books covered the founding of Rome by Romulus, the kings, the wars against Pyrrhus, the Punic Wars with Carthage, and Rome's wars in the Greek east, ending around 171 BC. The title recalls the annales maximi, the yearly chronicles kept by the pontifex maximus.

Survival

Only about 600 lines of the poem survive, preserved as quotations in later authors such as Cicero, the grammarian Festus, and Macrobius. Famous fragments include the line on Fabius Maximus Cunctator, "unus homo nobis cunctando restituit rem" ("one man, by delaying, restored the state to us"), quoted by Virgil in the Aeneid, and the description of Romulus and Remus watching for omens on the Aventine and Palatine hills.

Influence

The Annales established the hexameter as the standard metre of Latin epic and supplied later poets with a storehouse of archaic diction and patriotic exempla. Lucretius, Cicero and Horace all admired Ennius; Horace reported that critics called him a "second Homer." Although Virgil's Aeneid displaced it as Rome's national epic, Virgil borrowed whole lines and scenes from Ennius, and the Annales remained a school text into late antiquity. Modern editions of the fragments include those of Johannes Vahlen and Otto Skutsch, whose 1985 edition is standard.
