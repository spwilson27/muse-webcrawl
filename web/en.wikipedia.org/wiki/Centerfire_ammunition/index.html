Centerfire ammunition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Centerfire ammunition is a type of metallic cartridge used in firearms, where the primer is located at the center of the base of the cartridge case. Unlike rimfire ammunition, where the primer is spun into the rim of the case, the centerfire primer is a separate, replaceable component seated in a pocket in the case head. Most modern rifles, shotguns and handguns use centerfire ammunition, with the notable exception of small-calibre rimfire rounds such as .22 Long Rifle.

History

Early forms of centerfire priming appeared in the 1840s–1860s in the work of inventors including Pauly, Pottet, and Schneider. The practical modern systems were developed in the 1860s: the Boxer primer by Colonel Edward Boxer of the Royal Laboratory at Woolwich, and the Berdan primer by U.S. Army officer Hiram Berdan. Centerfire cartridges made it possible to reload spent cases, a major advantage for military and civilian shooters.

Primer types

Berdan primers use an anvil formed as part of the cartridge case, with two small flash holes; they are common in military surplus ammunition outside the United States. Boxer primers contain their own anvil within the primer cup and a single central flash hole, which makes the case easy to decap and reload; they predominate in the United States and in commercial ammunition generally. Primers come in standard sizes (small and large, for pistol and rifle) and in magnum variants.

Advantages

Centerfire ignition is more reliable than rimfire, particularly in larger calibres and at higher pressures; the cartridge case is stronger because the rim need not be crushed by the firing pin; and cases can be reloaded many times. These qualities made centerfire ammunition the standard for military service cartridges and for virtually all hunting and defensive firearms above small-calibre target rounds.
