Khosrow II (Middle Persian: Khusrō; c. 570 – 25 February 628), known as Khosrow Parviz, "Khosrow the Victorious," was the last great king of the Sasanian Empire, reigning from 590 to 628, with an interruption in 591. His reign saw Persia reach its greatest territorial extent in the war against the Byzantine Empire, and then collapse into chaos, leaving the empire fatally weakened a decade before the Arab conquest.

Accession and restoration

Khosrow succeeded his father Hormizd IV in 590 but was almost immediately driven from the throne by the general Bahram Chobin. Fleeing to the Byzantine Empire, he was restored in 591 with the military help of the emperor Maurice, in return for territorial concessions in Armenia and Iberia. The alliance with Maurice defined the first decade of his reign.

War with Byzantium, 602–628

The murder of Maurice by the usurper Phocas in 602 gave Khosrow his pretext for war, claiming to act as Maurice's avenger. Persian armies under Shahrbaraz and Shahin conquered Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine—capturing Jerusalem in 614 and carrying off the True Cross—and Egypt, and by 626 stood at Chalcedon opposite Constantinople while their Avar allies besieged the city. The tide turned under the emperor Heraclius, whose daring campaigns through Armenia and Azerbaijan culminated in the Battle of Nineveh (627) and the sack of Khosrow's palace at Dastagerd.

Overthrow and death

Defeat, taxation, and Khosrow's refusal to negotiate sparked a coup in February 628: his son Kavad II seized the throne, executed his brothers, and had Khosrow tried and executed. The peace with Heraclius restored the old frontiers, but the empire descended into civil war and rapid royal turnover that destroyed Sasanian power within a generation.

Legacy

Khosrow's court at Ctesiphon was famed for its luxury—the great iwan of Taq Kasra, the legendary spring carpet, and patronage of music under Barbad—and his romance with the Christian princess Shirin became one of the great love stories of Persian literature, retold by Ferdowsi and Nizami.
