The Aire and Calder Navigation is the system of navigable sections of the River Aire and the River Calder in West Yorkshire, England. Authorised by an Act of Parliament in 1699, it was one of the earliest river navigations created by statute in England and later became one of the most commercially successful inland waterways in the country, linking the industrial towns of Leeds and Wakefield to the Humber Estuary and the port of Goole.

The navigation is unusual in that it remained a busy commercial waterway well into the late twentieth century, carrying coal, oil and aggregates in large barges long after most British canals had declined. Much of the main line was enlarged in stages so that it could take compartment boats and, later, barges of several hundred tonnes.

History

Before the navigation, the Aire was navigable only to Leeds in favourable conditions and the Calder only as far as Wakefield. Merchants in Leeds, Wakefield and Halifax promoted a scheme to improve both rivers. The Act of 1699 empowered undertakers to make the Aire navigable from the Ouse at Airmyn up to Leeds, and the Calder from its junction with the Aire at Castleford up to Wakefield. Work proceeded slowly because of disputes with mill owners, but the route to Leeds was substantially complete by about 1704.

The navigation prospered with the growth of the West Riding woollen industry. A further Act of 1774 authorised a new canal, the Selby Canal, which gave a shorter route towards the Humber. In the 1820s the company built an entirely new cut from Ferrybridge to Goole, together with docks at Goole, which opened in 1826 and developed into a company town and a significant port for the export of coal.

Compartment boats

In the 1860s the engineer William H. Bartholomew introduced the famous "Tom Pudding" compartment boats: trains of unpowered tub-like compartments, each carrying around 40 tons of coal, which were pushed or hauled to Goole and there hoisted bodily by great hydraulic hoists so their contents could be tipped into sea-going ships. The system survived, in modernised form, until the 1980s.

Modernisation

Between the wars and after nationalisation in 1948 the main line was progressively deepened and widened, locks were lengthened, and the navigation became part of the British Waterways network. A modernised freight service using large push-towed barges carried coal to Ferrybridge power station until the early twenty-first century. The navigation is now managed by the Canal & River Trust and is used by leisure craft, with residual commercial traffic.

Route

From Goole, the navigation follows the Dutch River and then the New Junction Canal area waterways north-westwards through Knottingley to Ferrybridge, then along the improved Aire through Castleford — where the Calder joins — past Woodlesford to Leeds, where it connects with the Leeds and Liverpool Canal. A branch follows the Calder from Castleford through Altofts and Methley to Wakefield, meeting the Calder and Hebble Navigation. At Castleford the cut also gives access, via the Aire, towards Selby and York.
