Japanese art

Japanese art covers a wide range of art styles and media, including ancient pottery, sculpture, ink painting and calligraphy on silk and paper, ukiyo-e paintings and woodblock prints, ceramics, origami, and in more recent times manga and anime. It has a long history, ranging from the beginnings of human habitation in Japan, sometime in the 10th millennium BC, to the present day.

Japan has been subject to sudden invasions of new ideas followed by long periods of minimal contact with the outside world. Over time the Japanese developed the ability to absorb, imitate, and finally assimilate those elements of foreign culture that complemented their aesthetic preferences. The earliest complex art in Japan was produced in the 7th and 8th centuries in connection with Buddhism. In the 9th century, as the Japanese began to turn away from China and develop indigenous forms of expression, the secular arts became increasingly important; until the late 15th century, both religious and secular arts flourished.

History

Jōmon and Yayoi periods

The first settlers of Japan were the Jōmon people (c. 10,500 – c. 300 BC), named for the cord markings that decorated the surfaces of their clay vessels. Their pottery is among the oldest in the world. The Yayoi period (c. 300 BC – c. AD 300) brought wet-rice agriculture and bronze and iron technology from the Asian mainland, along with bronze mirrors, bells (dōtaku), and more refined wheel-made pottery.

Asuka and Nara periods

With the introduction of Buddhism from Korea and China in the mid-6th century, religious sculpture and temple architecture flourished. The Hōryū-ji temple complex near Nara preserves some of the oldest wooden buildings in the world and masterpieces of early Buddhist sculpture such as the Shaka Triad by Tori Busshi. During the Nara period (710–794), sculpture in bronze, clay, and dry lacquer reached a high level of realism under Tang Chinese influence.

Heian period

In the Heian period (794–1185), esoteric Buddhism inspired mandalas and fierce deity sculpture, while the imperial court at Kyoto fostered distinctly Japanese forms: yamato-e painting, narrative handscrolls (emaki) such as the Tale of Genji Scroll, and refined calligraphy in the kana syllabary.

Kamakura and Muromachi periods

The Kamakura period (1185–1333) saw vigorous, realistic sculpture by the Kei school, including Unkei and Kaikei. Under the Ashikaga shogunate in the Muromachi period (1336–1573), Zen Buddhism shaped ink painting (suiboku-ga) by masters such as Sesshū Tōyō, the dry landscape gardens of Kyoto, the tea ceremony, and Noh theatre.

Azuchi-Momoyama and Edo periods

The Azuchi-Momoyama period (1568–1600) produced bold decorative screens (byōbu) by the Kano and Rinpa schools, with gold-leaf backgrounds. In the Edo period (1603–1868), the Rinpa school of Tawaraya Sōtatsu and Ogata Kōrin flourished, and the urban merchant class patronized ukiyo-e woodblock prints by artists such as Hokusai and Hiroshige, whose landscapes later influenced European Impressionism and Post-Impressionism (Japonisme).

Modern and contemporary art

After the Meiji Restoration (1868), Japan adopted Western oil painting (yōga) while preserving traditional nihonga. In the postwar era, the Gutai group pioneered performance and installation art, and artists such as Yayoi Kusama and Takashi Murakami gained international prominence. Manga and anime have become globally influential forms of Japanese visual culture.
