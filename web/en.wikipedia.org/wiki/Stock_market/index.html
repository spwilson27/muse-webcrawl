Stock market

[Image: the floor of a stock exchange with traders and large electronic quotation boards]

A stock market is a marketplace where shares of publicly listed companies are issued and traded. Through the primary market, companies sell new shares to investors in an initial public offering (IPO), raising capital; in the secondary market investors trade existing shares among themselves on exchanges such as the New York Stock Exchange, Nasdaq and the London Stock Exchange, with prices set continuously by supply and demand.

Indices and participants

Market performance is summarised by indices — the S&P 500 (500 large US companies), the Dow Jones Industrial Average (30 blue-chip industrials), the FTSE 100 in London and the Nikkei 225 in Tokyo. Participants include retail investors, pension funds and other institutional investors, market makers, and mutual funds and hedge funds pursuing active strategies; much trading is now electronic and algorithmic, including high-frequency trading. Total global market capitalisation is on the order of one hundred trillion US dollars, with the United States accounting for the largest share.

Booms, crashes and regulation

Stock markets channel savings to investment and provide liquidity and price signals, but they are prone to bubbles and crashes: the 1929 Wall Street crash that heralded the Great Depression, the one-day 1987 "Black Monday" fall, the dot-com collapse of 2000–02, the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID crash of 2020. Markets are overseen by regulators such as the US Securities and Exchange Commission, which police disclosure, insider trading and market manipulation. Long-run returns to diversified equities have historically exceeded those on bonds and cash, with higher volatility.
