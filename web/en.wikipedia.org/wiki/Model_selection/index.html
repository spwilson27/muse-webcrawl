Model selection

Model selection balances fit against complexity to avoid under- and overfitting

Model selection is the task of choosing a statistical (or machine learning) model from a set of candidate models, given data. The central difficulty is the trade-off between goodness of fit and simplicity: a more complex model fits the observed data better but risks overfitting — capturing noise rather than the underlying pattern, and therefore predicting new data poorly.

Information criteria

Widely used criteria score models by their likelihood penalised for the number of estimated parameters. The Akaike information criterion (AIC) estimates relative information loss, while the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) applies a stronger complexity penalty and approximates a Bayes factor. Deviance information criteria and minimum description length approaches pursue similar goals from different foundations.

Cross-validation

Cross-validation estimates predictive performance directly by fitting the model on part of the data and testing it on held-out parts, rotating the split. It is the standard approach in machine learning for choosing hyperparameters and architectures, at the cost of additional computation.

Other approaches

Stepwise regression, regularisation (which embeds selection within estimation), likelihood-ratio tests for nested models, and Bayesian model averaging are also used. The choice of method depends on whether the goal is prediction or identifying a "true" model, a distinction emphasised in Leo Breiman's essay on the "two cultures" of statistical modelling.
