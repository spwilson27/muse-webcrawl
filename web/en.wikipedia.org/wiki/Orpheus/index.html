Orpheus (Ancient Greek: Ὀρφεύς) is a legendary musician, poet, and prophet in ancient Greek religion. Some sources credit Orpheus as the founder and prophet of the so-called "Orphic mysteries". He was unique in being the son of a Muse (Calliope) and sometimes of the god Apollo. The major stories about him are centered on his ability to charm all living things (and even stones) with his music, his attempt to retrieve his wife Eurydice from the underworld, his voyage with the Argonauts, and his death at the hands of the Thracian Maenads.

The descent for Eurydice

In the most famous myth, Orpheus's wife Eurydice died from a snakebite on their wedding day. Orpheus descended alive into Hades and, by the power of his song, persuaded Hades and Persephone to release her, on condition that he walk ahead of her and not look back until both had reached the upper world. At the threshold, fearing deception, he turned — and Eurydice vanished forever. The story, told by Virgil in the Georgics and by Ovid in the Metamorphoses, became one of the most retold myths in Western art, inspiring operas by Monteverdi (L'Orfeo, 1607), Gluck, and Offenbach, as well as plays, poems, and films.

Death and legacy

After his loss, Orpheus withdrew from the company of women and was torn to pieces (sparagmos) by Maenads, in some versions because he spurned Dionysus for Apollo. His head and lyre floated down the Hebrus to Lesbos, where the head continued to sing and prophesy. The Muses placed his lyre among the stars as the constellation Lyra. Orphic religion attributed to him sacred writings (the Orphic Hymns, gold-leaf tablets buried with initiates) promising a blessed afterlife, making Orpheus a figure linking Greek myth, music, and mystery religion.
