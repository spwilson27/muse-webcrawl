Infrared astronomy

Infrared astronomy is a branch of astronomy that specialises in the observation and analysis of astronomical objects using infrared (IR) radiation, with wavelengths from about 0.75 to several hundred micrometres. Infrared astronomy reveals objects too cool or too obscured by dust to be seen in visible light, and — because the expansion of the universe redshifts light from distant galaxies into the infrared — it is essential for studying the early universe.

Observing challenges

Water vapour in Earth's atmosphere absorbs much of the infrared spectrum, so ground-based observatories are sited on high, dry mountains such as Mauna Kea and the Atacama Plateau, and observe only through atmospheric "windows" (the near-infrared J, H, and K bands). Telescopes and detectors must be cooled to cryogenic temperatures so their own heat does not swamp the signal. Space observatories avoid the atmosphere entirely but are limited by the lifetime of their coolant or the performance of mechanical cryocoolers.

Major observatories

Key missions include IRAS (1983), which performed the first all-sky infrared survey; the Infrared Space Observatory (1995); NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope (2003–2020); ESA's Herschel Space Observatory (2009–2013) for the far infrared; the airborne SOFIA observatory; and the James Webb Space Telescope (launched 2021), whose instruments cover the near- and mid-infrared and have transformed the study of exoplanet atmospheres and the first galaxies.

Discoveries

Infrared observations have revealed the dense stellar core of the Milky Way behind veiling dust, vast numbers of brown dwarfs and dusty star-forming galaxies, protoplanetary disks around young stars, and the cold outer reaches of the solar system; the Kuiper belt object surveys and much exoplanet characterisation depend on infrared techniques.
