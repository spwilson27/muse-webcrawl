Intestacy is the condition of the estate of a person who dies without having in force a valid will or other binding declaration. Alternatively this may also apply where a will or declaration has been made, but only applies to part of the estate; the remaining estate forms the "intestate estate". Intestacy law, also referred to as the law of descent and distribution, refers to the body of law (statutory and case law) that determines who is entitled to the property from the estate of a deceased person.

Terminology

A person who dies without a will is said to have died intestate. Property that is not disposed of by a valid will passes under the rules of intestate succession. Where part of the estate is covered by a valid will and part is not, the situation is one of partial intestacy. The court appoints an administrator (rather than an executor, who is named in a will) to manage and distribute the estate.

History

Rules for distributing the property of those who died intestate are ancient. Roman law provided orders of succession favouring descendants and then ascendants and collaterals. In England, the Statute of Distributions 1670 established a scheme for personal property, while land passed to the heir at law under primogeniture until reforms of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The Administration of Estates Act 1925 rationalised English law, and most common-law jurisdictions have since enacted statutory orders of succession.

Order of succession

While details vary by jurisdiction, a typical statutory order gives priority to:

  1. The surviving spouse or civil partner, who commonly receives the whole estate if there are no children, or a statutory legacy plus a share if there are children.
  2. Children and other issue of the deceased (per stirpes distribution is common).
  3. Parents.
  4. Siblings and their issue.
  5. More remote relatives, such as grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins.

If no eligible relative can be found, the estate may pass to the state as bona vacantia (ownerless property), a process comparable to escheat.

Per stirpes and per capita

Many statutes distribute a deceased child's share to that child's own descendants (per stirpes, "by branch"), while others divide property equally among members of the same generation (per capita). The choice can substantially change outcomes where a child of the intestate has predeceased them leaving children.

Community property jurisdictions

In community property jurisdictions, such as several states of the United States, a surviving spouse already owns an undivided half of the community property, and intestacy rules govern the deceased's separate property and their half of the community property.
