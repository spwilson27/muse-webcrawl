Umm Kulthum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Umm Kulthum (Arabic: أم كلثوم‎; born Fatima Ibrahim as-Sayyid al-Biltaji, c. 1904 – 3 February 1975) was an Egyptian singer, songwriter, and film actress active from the 1920s to the 1970s. Known as the "Star of the East" (Kawkab al-Sharq) and Egypt's "fourth pyramid," she is regarded as one of the greatest and most influential Arab singers of the 20th century, famed for her extraordinary vocal range, power, and ability to improvise around melodic lines for hours.

Early life

Umm Kulthum was born in the village of Tamay e-Zahayra in the Nile Delta, the daughter of an imam who taught her to recite the Quran. Dressed as a boy to avoid censure, she sang religious songs at weddings and festivals with her father's troupe, attracting attention for a voice of remarkable strength and control. In the early 1920s she moved to Cairo, where she studied with leading musicians and poets and adopted the stage name Umm Kulthum.

Career

By the 1930s she was Egypt's pre-eminent singer, recording extensively and starring in musical films. Her monthly Thursday-night radio concerts, broadcast from 1934 onward, famously emptied the streets of Cairo and cities across the Arab world as millions listened. Her performances often lasted two to three hours and featured long songs built on poems by Ahmad Shawqi and others, with music by composers such as Mohamed Abdel Wahab, Zakariya Ahmad, and Riyad al-Sunbati. Songs like "Enta Omri" (1964), "Al Atlal" (1966), and "Rubaiyat al-Khayyam" remain standards of Arabic music.

Legacy

Umm Kulthum was a symbol of Egyptian and Arab cultural pride; President Gamal Abdel Nasser held her in high esteem, and her songs rallied national sentiment after the 1967 war. Her funeral in 1975 drew an estimated four million mourners in Cairo, one of the largest gatherings in modern Egyptian history. Her recordings continue to sell, and her voice remains ubiquitous across the Arab world.
