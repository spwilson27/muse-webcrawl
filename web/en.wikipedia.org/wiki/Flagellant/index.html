Flagellants were practitioners of a form of public religious penance in which participants whipped themselves with scourges — often leather thongs fitted with metal spikes — as an act of mortification of the flesh, in imitation of the suffering of Christ and in atonement for sin. The practice, known as flagellation, appeared in various religious traditions, but the flagellant movement is most associated with late medieval Latin Christianity.

History

Organized flagellant processions first swept Italy in 1260, beginning at Perugia under the hermit Raniero Fasani, and spread across Europe amid famine, war, and plague anxiety. The movement reached its height during the Black Death of 1348–1349, when bands of flagellants — sometimes hundreds strong, dressed in white robes marked with red crosses — processed from town to town, scourging themselves in public squares in ceremonies lasting, symbolically, thirty-three and a half days. Their arrival often coincided with outbreaks of violence against Jewish communities, whom they blamed for the plague.

Condemnation and later practice

The movement's lay leadership, its claims to grant absolution, and the disorders that followed it alarmed church authorities; in October 1349 Pope Clement VI issued a bull condemning the flagellants, and many groups were suppressed or driven underground. Self-flagellation nevertheless survived within Catholic devotional life — in monastic discipline, in confraternities, and in Holy Week processions in parts of Spain, Italy, Latin America, and the Philippines, where penitents still practice ritual flagellation and crucifixion reenactments today.
