Angular resolution

Angular resolution
Also called: Spatial resolution
Rayleigh criterion: θ ≈ 1.22 λ / D
Units: Radians, arcseconds
Applies to: Telescopes, microscopes, eyes, antennas

Angular resolution describes the ability of an image-forming device — such as a telescope, microscope, camera, or the human eye — to distinguish small details of an object, i.e. the smallest angle between two point sources that can still be seen as separate.

Rayleigh criterion

Diffraction through a circular aperture spreads a point source into an Airy pattern. By the Rayleigh criterion, two sources are just resolvable when the central maximum of one pattern coincides with the first minimum of the other, giving θ = 1.22 λ / D, where λ is the wavelength and D the aperture diameter. Larger apertures and shorter wavelengths improve resolution.

Examples

The human eye resolves about one arcminute (≈ 0.02°). The Hubble Space Telescope achieves about 0.05 arcseconds in visible light. Ground-based telescopes are usually limited by atmospheric seeing to about one arcsecond unless adaptive optics or interferometry are used; radio arrays such as the Event Horizon Telescope synthesise enormous apertures and imaged a black hole's shadow in 2019.

Related concepts

The Dawes limit is an empirical variant for double stars. Resolution in imaging systems is also described by the point spread function and modulation transfer function.
