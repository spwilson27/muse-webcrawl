White Night riots

The White Night riots were a series of violent events that began on the night of May 21, 1979, in San Francisco, California, in response to the lenient verdict in the trial of Dan White, the former city supervisor who had assassinated Mayor George Moscone and Supervisor Harvey Milk — the first openly gay elected official in California — on November 27, 1978.

The trial

White, a former police officer and firefighter who had resigned his supervisor's seat and then sought reappointment, shot Moscone and Milk in City Hall. At trial his defence argued diminished capacity from depression — a strategy ridiculed in the press as the "Twinkie defense." On May 21, 1979, the jury convicted White only of voluntary manslaughter, with a sentence of seven years and eight months, of which he served about five.

The riots

Outraged, a crowd of several thousand marched from the Castro to City Hall that evening. The protest turned violent: demonstrators smashed the building's ornate doors and windows, set police cars on fire, and battled police; 61 officers and over 100 demonstrators were reported injured. Later that night, police in an unprovoked retaliatory raid attacked the Elephant Walk bar in the Castro, beating patrons — an action for which the city later paid damages.

Aftermath

The verdict led California to abolish the diminished-capacity defence. White was released in January 1984 and died by suicide in 1985. Milk's martyrdom and the riots galvanized the gay rights movement; the events were dramatized in the documentary The Times of Harvey Milk (1984) and the film Milk (2008).
