Steve Biko

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bantu Stephen Biko (18 December 1946 – 12 September 1977) was a South African anti-apartheid activist. Ideologically an African nationalist and African socialist, he was at the forefront of a grassroots campaign known as the Black Consciousness Movement during the late 1960s and 1970s. His ideas were articulated in a series of articles published under the pseudonym Frank Talk and collected in I Write What I Like (1978).

Early life and activism

Biko was born in Tarkastad and raised in Ginsberg township outside King William's Town in the Eastern Cape. Expelled from Lovedale school for his brother's political activities, he finished school at St. Francis College, Mariannhill, and enrolled to study medicine at the University of Natal's Non-European section in 1966. There he became active in the National Union of South African Students (NUSAS) but grew disillusioned with white liberal dominance of the anti-apartheid student movement.

Black Consciousness

In 1968 Biko co-founded the South African Students' Organisation (SASO), open only to "non-white" students, and was elected its first president. Around it grew the ideology of Black Consciousness: the belief that black South Africans had to liberate themselves psychologically from internalized inferiority before political liberation could follow. "The most potent weapon in the hands of the oppressor," he wrote, "is the mind of the oppressed." In 1972 he helped found the Black People's Convention and was expelled from medical school; he then worked for the Black Community Programme in Durban and later in King William's Town, establishing health, education, and literacy projects.

Ban, arrest, and death

In 1973 the apartheid government "banned" Biko, restricting him to his hometown and prohibiting him from speaking publicly or being quoted. He defied the ban to meet other activists and journalists, including Donald Woods, whose biography Biko later inspired the film Cry Freedom. On 18 August 1977, Biko was arrested at a roadblock near Grahamstown. Held naked and shackled in Port Elizabeth, he was severely beaten by security police, suffering a massive brain haemorrhage. On 11 September he was transported some 1,100 kilometres in the back of a police van to Pretoria, where he died alone in a cell on 12 September 1977, aged 30.

Legacy

News of Biko's death drew international condemnation and exposed the brutality of deaths in detention under apartheid; his funeral drew some 20,000 mourners. No one was prosecuted at the time, and a 1977 inquest controversially accepted the police account. In 1997 five former officers applied for amnesty from the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which was refused on the grounds that their account was implausible. Biko remains one of the most honoured figures of the liberation struggle: universities, hospitals, and foundations bear his name, and his writings remain central texts of Black Consciousness thought.
