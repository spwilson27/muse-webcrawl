Klein bottle

In mathematics, the Klein bottle is an example of a non-orientable surface: a two-dimensional manifold that cannot be given a consistent choice of "inside" and "outside", or equivalently a continuous choice of normal direction. Informally, it is a one-sided surface which, if travelled upon, could be followed "around" and back to the starting point while flipping the traveller upside down. It was first described in 1882 by the German mathematician Felix Klein, after whom it is named; the name is thought to derive from a mistranslation of the German Kleinsche Fläche ("Klein's surface") as Kleinsche Flasche ("Klein's bottle").

Description

The familiar glass "Klein bottle" model is made by taking a tube, drawing its neck out, bending it around, and passing it through the side of the tube so that it joins the flared base from within. In three-dimensional Euclidean space such a realisation necessarily intersects itself: the Klein bottle cannot be embedded in ℝ³ without self-intersection, though it can be immersed there (as in the glass model), and it can be embedded without self-intersection in four-dimensional space.

An ant crawling along the surface can return to its starting point mirrored, having never crossed an edge or left the surface — the same one-sidedness exhibited by the simpler Möbius strip, to which the Klein bottle is closely related: gluing two Möbius strips along their boundary circles produces a Klein bottle. Its Euler characteristic is 0, like the torus, with which it is often contrasted: the torus is orientable, the Klein bottle is not. Like the torus, the Klein bottle can be obtained from a square by identifying opposite edges — one pair directly, the other pair with a reversal of direction — and it admits a flat metric; it also carries a fibration by circles and can be decomposed into two Möbius bands.

Construction and parametrisation

Beyond the polygon identification, the Klein bottle can be described as the quotient of the torus by a fixed-point-free involution, given as a figure-8 immersion (the "figure-eight Klein bottle"), or parametrised by standard trigonometric equations. It arises naturally in algebraic topology as a test case for homology (its first homology group is Z × Z/2), and in physics and popular culture as an emblem of higher-dimensional geometry. Physical models in glass are produced commercially and famously supplied by the science gift shop trade; Alan Bennett's company Acme Klein Bottle is a well-known source.
