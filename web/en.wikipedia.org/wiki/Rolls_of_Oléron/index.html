Rolls of Oléron

The Rolls of Oléron (Rôles d'Oléron) are a code of maritime law drawn up in the late 12th or early 13th century, named after the island of Oléron off the Atlantic coast of France. They are among the earliest and most influential collections of sea law in north-western Europe and formed the basis of the maritime law of England, France, Flanders and the Hanseatic trading towns.

Origin

Tradition attributes the Rolls to Eleanor of Aquitaine, who is said to have introduced them after her travels to the Mediterranean, and to her son Richard I of England, who supposedly promulgated them on returning from the Third Crusade. The surviving texts, preserved in manuscripts known as "rolls", consist of a series of judgments and rules — commonly 24 articles in early versions, later expanded — governing the rights and duties of shipmasters, sailors and merchants.

Contents

The Rolls deal with practical matters of medieval shipping: the master's authority, wages and discipline of the crew, obligations to care for sick or injured sailors (an early form of maintenance and cure), jettison of cargo in storms, collisions, pilotage, wreck and salvage, and the division of losses. They required, for example, that a master consult the crew before jettisoning goods, and that wine casks — the staple cargo of the Bordeaux trade — be handled in particular ways.

Influence

Adopted in England and recorded in the Black Book of the Admiralty, the Rolls influenced the Laws of Wisby in the Baltic and remained authoritative in admiralty courts for centuries, shaping the later development of international maritime law.
