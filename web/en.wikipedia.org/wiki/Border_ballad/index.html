Border ballads are a group of narrative songs from the Anglo-Scottish border country, composed and transmitted orally between roughly the 15th and 17th centuries and first widely printed in the 18th and early 19th centuries. They deal with the violent world of the Border Reivers — cross-border raiding, blood feuds, rescues, battles and tragic love — and are among the most celebrated products of the British folk-ballad tradition.

Background

For three centuries the border between England and Scotland was a lawless frontier governed by powerful riding families — Armstrongs, Elliots, Nixons and Charltons on one side, and their English counterparts — who raided each other's livestock and strongholds. The ballads grew out of this society and preserve the names of its heroes, its pele towers and its battlefields, from Otterburn (1388) to Flodden (1513).

Notable ballads

  • The Ballad of Chevy Chase — the hunting expedition that turns into the Battle of Otterburn
  • Tam Lin — a young woman rescues her lover from the Queen of the Fairies
  • Thomas the Rhymer — the prophet poet carried off to Elfland
  • Kinmont Willie — a daring rescue from Carlisle Castle
  • The Dowie Dens of Yarrow and Sir Patrick Spens — tragedy at feud and at sea
  • Johnnie Armstrong — the reiver betrayed and hanged by James V

Collection and influence

Thomas Percy's Reliques of Ancient English Poetry (1765) printed many border ballads for a literary audience, and Sir Walter Scott's Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802–03) collected them from living singers in Liddesdale and Ettrick. Francis James Child included dozens in his canonical English and Scottish Popular Ballads (1882–98). Their stark dialogue, incremental repetition and tragic compression strongly influenced Romantic poets and the modern folk revival.
