Taboo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A taboo, also spelled tabu, is a social group's ban on certain behaviour, language or subjects, violations of which are considered to cause offence, shame or danger. Taboos may be grounded in religion, morality, law or etiquette, and range from near-universal prohibitions—such as incest and cannibalism—to local rules governing food, dress, mourning and speech. The word entered English in the late 18th century from the Tongan tapu, meaning "forbidden" or "sacred", and was popularised by Captain James Cook's accounts of Polynesia, where tapu structured ritual life and social hierarchy.

Origins and anthropology

In Polynesian societies, tapu applied to persons, places and things charged with sacred power (mana); contact with them by the unqualified was believed to bring supernatural harm. Anthropologists generalised the concept to cover strong prohibitions in any culture. Sigmund Freud's Totem and Taboo (1913) treated taboo as an expression of ambivalence toward forbidden desires, comparing it with obsessional neurosis. Franz Steiner and later Mary Douglas analysed taboo as a mechanism for maintaining social categories and boundaries: Douglas's Purity and Danger (1966) argued that prohibitions police the margins of a culture's classification system.

Types of taboo

  • Food taboos — prohibitions on eating certain animals or preparations, as in Jewish and Islamic dietary law, Hindu avoidance of beef, and countless local customs.
  • Incest taboo — the near-universal ban on sexual relations and marriage between close kin, whose origins (biological, social or structural) remain debated.
  • Speech taboos — profanity, blasphemy, names of the dead, and words avoided through euphemism; clinical terms often replace tabooed everyday words.
  • Death and mourning taboos — rules about handling corpses, speaking of the recently dead, and periods of seclusion for mourners.
  • Menstrual and childbirth taboos — restrictions historically imposed in many societies.

Function and change

Taboos reinforce group identity, protect resources (some food taboos plausibly preserve scarce species or prevent illness), and regulate dangerous or liminal states. They are not fixed: prohibitions once enforced by severe sanctions may fade into embarrassments, while new taboos arise around emerging harms. Because breaking a taboo carries emotional and social weight beyond mere rule-breaking, the concept remains important in sociology, psychology, linguistics and religious studies.
