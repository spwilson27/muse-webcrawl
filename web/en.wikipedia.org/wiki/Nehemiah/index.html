Nehemiah

Nehemiah (Hebrew: נְחֶמְיָה, "Yahweh has comforted") is the central figure of the Book of Nehemiah in the Hebrew Bible. A Jewish official at the court of the Persian king Artaxerxes I, he was appointed governor of Yehud (Judah) in the fifth century BC and is remembered for rebuilding the walls of Jerusalem and for social and religious reforms among the returned exiles.

Life

Nehemiah served as cupbearer — a position of trust and access — to Artaxerxes in the citadel of Susa. In 445 or 444 BC, hearing from his brother Hanani that Jerusalem's walls lay in ruins and its gates burned, he obtained the king's permission, royal letters, and timber to travel to Judah as its governor. There he led a rapid rebuilding of the city wall, traditionally completed in 52 days, despite opposition from neighbouring officials including Sanballat the Horonite and Tobiah the Ammonite.

Reforms

During his governorship, Nehemiah cancelled debts and forbade usury among the Jews, required observance of the Sabbath, opposed intermarriage with foreign peoples, and supported the priest-scribe Ezra in the public reading and enforcement of the Law. After twelve years he returned to the Persian court, then came back to Jerusalem for a second term in which he renewed his reforms.

Historicity and sources

The first part of the Book of Nehemiah is written in the first person and is often treated by scholars as derived from Nehemiah's own memoir, giving it considerable historical value. Archaeological and epigraphic evidence for Persian-period Yehud, including references to a governor of Judah and the province's small size, is broadly consistent with the biblical picture, though details such as the precise extent of the rebuilt walls remain debated.
