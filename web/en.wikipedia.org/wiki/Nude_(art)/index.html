Nude (art)

The nude, as a form of visual art that focuses on the unclothed human figure, is an enduring tradition in Western art, and can be found in nearly every culture. In English-language usage, a distinction is sometimes drawn between the naked (the unclothed body as vulnerable or ordinary) and the nude (the body as a balanced, confident subject of art), most famously formulated by Kenneth Clark in The Nude: A Study in Ideal Form (1956).

History

Nude figures appear in prehistoric art such as the Venus figurines. In ancient Greece the athletic male nude embodied civic and heroic ideals, while the female nude became prominent in the Hellenistic period. Roman art adapted Greek models. Medieval Christian art restricted nudity largely to biblical scenes such as Adam and Eve and the Last Judgment. The Renaissance revived the classical nude through artists such as Michelangelo and Titian, and it became central to academic training through life drawing. In the nineteenth century, works like Manet's Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe and Olympia provoked scandal by placing recognisably contemporary nudes in modern settings.

Modern and contemporary art

Modernism diversified the nude: Matisse, Picasso and Egon Schiele used it to explore form and psychology, while photography from its earliest decades adopted the nude as a subject. Feminist artists and critics, notably following John Berger's Ways of Seeing (1972) and Lynda Nead's work, interrogated the tradition of the female nude and the male gaze; artists such as Jenny Saville and Lucian Freud painted fleshy, unidealised bodies.

Depictions by medium

The nude is a major genre in sculpture, painting, drawing and photography; conventions governing its display vary by period and culture, and many museums and public spaces balance artistic significance against local norms.
