Typical antipsychotic

Typical antipsychotics, also known as first-generation antipsychotics (FGAs) or conventional antipsychotics, are a class of antipsychotic drugs first developed in the 1950s and used to treat psychosis — particularly the positive symptoms of schizophrenia such as hallucinations and delusions. They were the mainstay of antipsychotic treatment until the introduction of the atypical (second-generation) antipsychotics, beginning with clozapine.

History

The era of modern antipsychotic pharmacotherapy began with chlorpromazine, synthesized in 1950 and introduced into psychiatry in 1952. Its calming effect on agitated psychotic patients transformed psychiatric care and enabled large-scale deinstitutionalization. Haloperidol, a butyrophenone, followed in the late 1950s and became one of the most widely used antipsychotics in the world; it remains on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism of action

Typical antipsychotics act primarily as antagonists of dopamine D2 receptors. Blockade of dopaminergic transmission in the mesolimbic pathway is thought to account for their antipsychotic effect, while blockade in other pathways produces many of their adverse effects. Clinical potency correlates closely with D2 affinity, and drugs are often classified as high-potency (e.g., haloperidol, fluphenazine), mid-potency, or low-potency (e.g., chlorpromazine, thioridazine) agents.

Adverse effects

The class is associated with extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) — acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism — and, with chronic use, tardive dyskinesia, a potentially irreversible movement disorder. Other adverse effects include hyperprolactinaemia, sedation, orthostatic hypotension, anticholinergic effects (especially with low-potency agents), QT prolongation, and the rare but life-threatening neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Their higher risk of EPS and tardive dyskinesia relative to most atypical antipsychotics is the main reason atypicals are often preferred, although typicals remain effective, inexpensive, and widely used.

Examples

Phenothiazines (chlorpromazine, fluphenazine, perphenazine, thioridazine), butyrophenones (haloperidol, droperidol), thioxanthenes (flupentixol, zuclopenthixol), and others such as pimozide and sulpiride. Long-acting injectable ("depot") preparations of fluphenazine and haloperidol are used to support adherence.
