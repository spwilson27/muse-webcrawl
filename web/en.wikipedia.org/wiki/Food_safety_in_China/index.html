Food safety in China

Food safety in China is a significant public concern. A series of scandals from the mid-2000s onward — most notoriously the 2008 melamine-tainted milk powder scandal, which sickened some 300,000 infants and killed at least six — prompted major regulatory reforms, though enforcement challenges persist in a vast and fragmented food industry.

Major incidents

Notable incidents include the use of hair to produce artificial soy sauce (2004), "gutter oil" recycled from restaurant waste being resold as cooking oil, cadmium-contaminated rice, clenbuterol ("lean meat powder") in pork, and the 2008 milk scandal involving the Sanlu Group and other major dairies. Sanlu's chairwoman was sentenced to life imprisonment; two men judged primarily responsible were executed in 2009.

Regulatory framework

China adopted a comprehensive Food Safety Law in 2009, revised in 2015 to be considerably stricter, with heavier penalties and stronger traceability requirements. Regulatory consolidation followed: the China Food and Drug Administration was created in 2013, and in 2018 food safety oversight was folded into the new State Administration for Market Regulation. In 2019 the government announced a further action plan targeting standards, inspection, and accountability for local officials.

Challenges

Difficulties include the sheer number of small producers and vendors, local protectionism, inconsistent inspection capacity, and widespread use of agricultural chemicals. Public anxiety about food safety remains high and has driven demand for imported infant formula and premium domestic brands.
