Total synthesis

Total synthesis
FieldOrganic chemistry
ObjectiveBuild a complex molecule from simple starting materials
LandmarksUrea (1828); vitamin B12 (1973); Taxol (1994)

Total synthesis is the complete chemical synthesis of a complex molecule, typically a natural product, from simple, commercially available starting materials. Unlike partial or semisynthesis, which begins from a complex precursor isolated from nature, total synthesis constructs the target from the ground up and so serves both as a proof of the molecule's structure and as a proving ground for new reactions and strategies. The discipline is regarded as one of the highest expressions of synthetic organic chemistry.

History

The field is often traced to Friedrich Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate, an early blow to vitalism. Landmark achievements followed: Kolbe's acetic acid, the synthesis of glucose by Emil Fischer, Robert Robinson's tropinone synthesis (1917), and Robert Burns Woodward's syntheses of quinine, cholesterol, cortisone, strychnine, chlorophyll, and — with Albert Eschenmoser's group — vitamin B12 (1973). E. J. Corey formalised the planning of syntheses as retrosynthetic analysis, earning the 1990 Nobel Prize. Later celebrated targets include palytoxin (Kishi), Taxol (Holton and Nicolaou, 1994), and vancomycin.

Purpose and practice

Modern total synthesis aims not only to reach the target but to do so efficiently — in few steps, with high overall yield, and with control of stereochemistry — and to develop new methodology along the way. Synthesis can confirm or correct proposed structures, supply scarce natural products for biological study, and generate analogues for drug discovery. It remains an active research area, with contemporary emphasis on step economy, catalysis, and sustainable methods.
