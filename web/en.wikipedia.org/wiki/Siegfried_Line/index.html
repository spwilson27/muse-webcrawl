Siegfried Line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Siegfried Line, known in German as the Westwall, was a German defensive line of fortifications built between 1936 and 1940 along Germany's western frontier, opposite the French Maginot Line. It stretched more than 630 km (390 mi) from Kleve on the Dutch border to Weil am Rhein on the Swiss frontier, and comprised over 18,000 bunkers, tunnels and tank traps.

Construction

Built initially as a propaganda-heavy crash programme employing hundreds of thousands of workers of the Organisation Todt, the line included belts of concrete "dragon's teeth" anti-tank obstacles, artillery casemates, and air-defence positions. Its name in English derived from the German Siegfriedstellung of the First World War.

Role in the Second World War

The line deterred a French offensive in 1939 and was largely stripped during Germany's western offensive of 1940. After the Allied landings in Normandy in 1944, the hurriedly reactivated Westwall imposed serious delays: fighting at Aachen, in the Hürtgen Forest, and in the Saar–Palatinate consumed months in the autumn and winter of 1944–45. Allied forces finally breached the line in early 1945 (notably in Operation Grenade and at the Remagen crossing beyond it), rendering it strategically irrelevant.

Legacy

Many sections survive as ruins and nature refuges; the concrete obstacles now form a chain of protected habitats sometimes called the "green belt" of the Westwall.
