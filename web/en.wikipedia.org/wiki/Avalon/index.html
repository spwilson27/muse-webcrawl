Avalon (Latin: Insula Avalonis; Welsh: Ynys Afallon) is an island featured in the Arthurian legend. It first appeared in Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136) as the place where King Arthur's sword Excalibur was forged and later where Arthur was taken to recover from his wounds after the Battle of Camlann. Avalon is also said to be the resting place of Arthur, from which he will one day return — a belief that made him the "once and future king."

Etymology and origins

The name is generally understood to derive from the Welsh afal, "apple," making Avalon the "Isle of Apples" — a fruit associated with otherworldly plenty in Celtic tradition. Geoffrey described the island as a fortunate place where grain and grapes grow untended and inhabitants live to a great age. Scholars compare Avalon with other mythic western islands of the blessed, such as the Irish Tír na nÓg and Annwn.

In Arthurian literature

In Geoffrey's later Vita Merlini (c. 1150), Avalon is ruled by nine sisters, chief among them Morgen (Morgan le Fay), who receives the wounded Arthur. Later romances, including the Vulgate Cycle and Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, developed the motif of Arthur being ferried to Avalon by boat by queens and ladies, leaving his ultimate fate ambiguous. Avalon also appears in the Grail legend as a place of enchantment and healing.

Glastonbury

In 1191, monks of Glastonbury Abbey claimed to have discovered the grave of Arthur and Guinevere in their churchyard, reinforcing the identification of Glastonbury — once an island-like hill surrounded by marshy Somerset Levels — with Avalon. The association endures in tourism, literature, and modern pagan and New Age spirituality, for which Glastonbury remains a centre.
