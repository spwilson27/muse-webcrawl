The New Technology Telescope (NTT) is a 3.58-metre Ritchey–Chrétien telescope operated by the European Southern Observatory (ESO) at the La Silla Observatory in Chile. It saw first light in 1989 and is famous as the first large telescope to employ active optics: its thin primary mirror is continuously adjusted by computer-controlled actuators to maintain a perfect shape against gravity and temperature effects, with image quality monitored in real time.

Design

Instead of the massive, rigid mirrors of earlier telescopes, the NTT used a thin meniscus primary mirror whose figure is maintained by 75 axial supports with force actuators, plus a control system that analyses star images and corrects deformations. The compact alt-azimuth mount and a co-rotating, octagonal enclosure minimize thermal disturbances. This approach drastically reduced cost and weight relative to conventional designs of the same aperture.

Legacy

The NTT was explicitly a demonstrator: the technologies proven on it — active optics, real-time wavefront analysis, and integrated computer control — were scaled up for ESO's Very Large Telescope (VLT), whose four 8.2-metre unit telescopes each use active optics. Nearly all large optical telescopes built since, including those planned with adaptive optics, trace their design philosophy to the NTT.

Instruments

The NTT has hosted instruments including the ESO Faint Object Spectrograph and Camera (EFOSC2), the infrared spectrograph and camera SOFI, and the high-resolution spectrograph and imagers used for studies of supernovae, gamma-ray burst afterglows, exoplanets, and the Galactic centre. A major upgrade, NTT Breakthrough, has modernized its controls for continued operation.
