Sudanese architecture

Sudanese architecture is the body of architectural styles developed in the territory of present-day Sudan, from the mud-brick temples and pyramids of ancient Nubia and the kingdoms of Kerma, Kush and Meroë, through the medieval Christian Nubian churches, the Islamic-period mosques and palaces of the Funj Sultanate and Tunjur kingdom, to the colonial-era buildings of Anglo-Egyptian Sudan and the modern architecture of the independent republic.

Sudanese buildings range from the pyramids of Meroë to Khartoum's modernist towers.

Ancient Nubian architecture

The earliest monumental architecture in Sudan belongs to the Kerma culture (c. 2500–1500 BC), whose massive mud-brick deffufa temples are among the largest Bronze Age structures in Africa. The Napatan and Meroitic kingdoms built pyramids — more numerous, though smaller and steeper, than those of Egypt — at sites such as el-Kurru, Nuri and Meroë, together with temples dedicated to Amun and local deities at Jebel Barkal and Naqa.

Medieval and Islamic periods

From the 6th century, the Christian kingdoms of Nobatia, Makuria and Alodia raised mud-brick and red-brick churches, cathedrals and monasteries, often painted with frescoes, as at Faras and Old Dongola. After the gradual Islamisation of the region, the Funj Sultanate (1504–1821) developed a distinctive style of domed tombs (qubbas), mosques with conical minarets, and fortified palaces at Sinnar. Vernacular styles adapted to climate include the jalousieh — mud walls pierced with decorative vents — and courtyard houses along the Nile.

Colonial and modern eras

Under Anglo-Egyptian rule (1899–1956), Khartoum and Omdurman acquired government buildings, mosques such as the Grand Mosque of Khartoum, and garden suburbs in imported styles. After independence, architects adapted modernism to the Sudanese climate, producing landmarks such as the Friendship Hall and the Greater Nile Petroleum tower in Khartoum, while traditional Nubian vaulting techniques using mud brick attracted renewed international interest.
