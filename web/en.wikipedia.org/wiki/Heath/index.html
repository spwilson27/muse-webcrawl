Heath

Habitat typeShrubland
Characteristic plantsHeathers (Calluna, Erica), gorse, bracken, grasses
SoilsAcidic, sandy, nutrient-poor, freely draining
DistributionTemperate zones worldwide; classic lowland heaths of north-western Europe; related heathlands in Australia and South Africa (fynbos)

A heath is a shrubland habitat found mainly on free-draining, infertile, acidic soils, characterised by open, low-growing woody vegetation dominated by plants of the heather family (Ericaceae). Heaths occur in temperate regions around the world; the classic lowland heaths of Britain, the Netherlands, Denmark and northern Germany are largely cultural landscapes, created and maintained by centuries of grazing, burning and turf-cutting on land cleared of forest.

Vegetation

The signature plant is heather — ling (Calluna vulgaris) and bell heathers (Erica species) — which colours whole hillsides purple in late summer. Gorse, bracken, wavy hair-grass and scattered birch and Scots pine are common associates; without management, heath is eventually invaded by scrub and reverts to woodland.

Ecology and wildlife

Lowland heath is an internationally scarce habitat supporting specialised wildlife: Dartford warblers, nightjars, woodlarks, sand lizards and smooth snakes in Britain, and a rich invertebrate fauna including silver-studded blue butterflies. Because so much heath was lost to agriculture, forestry and development in the twentieth century, surviving fragments are heavily protected and actively managed.

Analogous habitats

Related vegetation occurs worldwide under different names: the fynbos of South Africa's Cape, the kwongan and heathlands of Australia, the maquis and garrigue of the Mediterranean, and upland moorland in Britain, which differs from lowland heath chiefly in its wetter, peaty soils.

In culture

Heaths feature prominently in British literature and folklore as wild, liminal places — from the "blasted heath" of Shakespeare's Macbeth to Hardy's Egdon Heath in The Return of the Native and the moors of the Brontës.
