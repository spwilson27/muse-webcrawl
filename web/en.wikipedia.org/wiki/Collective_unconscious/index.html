Collective unconscious

The collective unconscious (German: kollektives Unbewusstes) is a term coined by psychoanalyst Carl Jung. It refers to the unconscious mind shared by human beings, composed of what Jung called archetypes — innate, universal predispositions to experience and symbolise the world in particular ways. Unlike Sigmund Freud's personal unconscious, which is made of repressed individual experiences, the collective unconscious is inherited and identical in all people.

Archetypes

Jung proposed that the collective unconscious contains archetypes such as the Self, the Shadow, the Anima and Animus, the Persona, the Mother, the Hero, and the Trickster. Archetypes are not inherited images but inherited tendencies or "forms without content" that shape how individuals perceive and respond; they manifest in dreams, myths, fairy tales, religious symbolism, and art across otherwise unconnected cultures.

Evidence and reasoning

Jung drew evidence from his patients' dreams and fantasies, which sometimes contained mythological motifs he believed they could not have learned, and from comparative study of world mythology and religion. He first used the term in his 1916 essay "The Structure of the Unconscious," and developed the concept as part of his split from Freud, founding analytical psychology.

Criticism

Critics, including many Freudians and empirical psychologists, object that the collective unconscious is difficult or impossible to test, and that shared myths may be explained by cultural diffusion, common environments, and shared cognitive structures rather than inherited psychic content. Modern sympathetic interpretations sometimes relate the idea to evolutionary psychology and universal features of human cognition.
