Mahabodhi Temple

The Mahabodhi Temple ("Great Awakening Temple") is an ancient Buddhist temple complex at Bodh Gaya, Bihar, India, marking the location where Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, is said to have attained enlightenment (bodhi) while meditating beneath the Bodhi Tree. The site is one of the four holy places of Buddhism and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The present pyramidal temple is mainly a product of the Gupta period (5th–6th century CE), heavily restored in the 19th century.

History

Tradition holds that the emperor Ashoka visited the site around 260 BCE and built the first temple and the Vajrasana ("diamond throne") marking the exact spot of the enlightenment. The standing temple, built largely of brick in the late Gupta style and crowned by a 55-metre shikhara, is one of the earliest surviving brick temples in India. As Buddhism declined in India the site fell into neglect and came under the control of a Hindu mahant; a restoration campaign in the 1880s, led by Alexander Cunningham, J. D. Beglar and Rajendralal Mitra, rebuilt much of the structure. The Sri Lankan monk Anagarika Dharmapala founded the Maha Bodhi Society in 1891 to campaign for Buddhist control, achieved substantially through the Bodh Gaya Temple Act of 1949.

The complex

Beside the temple stands the sacred Bodhi Tree, a descendant (through saplings preserved in Sri Lanka) of the original tree, and the Vajrasana platform. The grounds include the Muchalinda Lake, where the serpent king sheltered the meditating Buddha, the Animeshlochan Chaitya where he gazed at the tree in gratitude, and monasteries and temples built by Buddhist communities from across Asia. The temple is administered by a committee with both Buddhist and Hindu members.
