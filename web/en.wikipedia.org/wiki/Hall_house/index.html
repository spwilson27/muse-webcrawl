Hall house

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Image placeholder: Hall house

The hall house is a type of vernacular house traditional in many parts of England, particularly the south-east, in which a large open hall forms the centre of the dwelling. The hall, originally heated by an open fire on a central hearth with smoke escaping through the roof, typically rose the full height of the building. Hall houses were built from the medieval period through the Tudor era, by occupants ranging from wealthy yeomen to manorial lords; surviving examples are often timber-framed, including the Wealden hall houses of Kent and Sussex.

Layout

The classic plan placed the open hall between two end bays under the same roof. At the low end, a screens passage separated the hall from service rooms such as the pantry and buttery; at the high end, beyond the dais where the household head sat, lay the parlour or solar, often with a chamber above. Cross-wings were sometimes added at one or both ends.

Evolution

From the late 16th century, owners modernised hall houses by inserting chimneys and ceilings, dividing the open hall into two storeys. Many halls were floored over during the "Great Rebuilding" of rural England. The form influenced later house types and manor houses, and numerous hall houses survive as listed buildings, some preserved at open-air museums such as the Weald and Downland Living Museum.
