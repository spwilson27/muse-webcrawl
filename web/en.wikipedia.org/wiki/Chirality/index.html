Chirality is a property of asymmetry important in several branches of science. The word derives from the Greek χείρ (kheir), "hand", a familiar chiral object. An object or system is chiral if it is distinguishable from its mirror image — that is, it cannot be superimposed on it, just as a left hand cannot be superimposed on a right hand. Conversely, an object that can be superimposed on its mirror image is achiral.

In chemistry

In chemistry, chirality usually refers to molecules. Two mirror-image forms of a chiral molecule are called enantiomers. A carbon atom bonded to four different groups is a stereocentre and typically makes the molecule chiral. Enantiomers have identical physical properties (melting point, solubility, spectra) except that they rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions and react differently with other chiral substances. A 50:50 mixture is a racemate. Chirality is described by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog R/S system and, historically, by D/L notation for sugars and amino acids.

The distinction matters enormously in biology and pharmacology because living systems are themselves chiral: proteins are built almost exclusively from L-amino acids and nucleic acids from D-sugars — a bias called homochirality whose origin remains debated. Consequently, enantiomers of a drug can differ radically in effect; the thalidomide tragedy, in which one enantiomer caused severe birth defects, drove modern requirements for enantiopure drug production. Asymmetric catalysis for making single enantiomers earned the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Knowles, Noyori, Sharpless).

In physics

In particle physics, chirality is the projection of a particle's spin onto its direction of motion ( helicity for massless particles). The weak interaction couples only to left-handed particles and right-handed antiparticles — a maximal violation of parity symmetry demonstrated by Chien-Shiung Wu's experiment in 1956 with cobalt-60. In materials physics, chiral structures such as helical magnets and cholesteric liquid crystals show distinctive optical and magnetic behaviour.

In mathematics and geometry

A geometric figure is chiral if it has no improper rotation symmetry (no mirror planes or inversion centre). Spirals, helices, screws and knots can be chiral: the trefoil knot, for example, exists in non-equivalent left- and right-handed forms. Kelvin gave the concept its name and definition in his 1894 Baltimore lectures.

Examples in nature

Most snail shells coil in one direction; climbing plants twine with species-specific handedness; the tusks of narwhals spiral to the left; and flatfish develop eyes on one side only. Human hands, gloves and shoes are everyday examples of chiral objects, reflected in the words "left" and "right" (dextro- and levo-).
