The Forfeiture Act 1870 (33 & 34 Vict. c. 23) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that abolished the automatic forfeiture of goods and land to the Crown upon conviction for treason or felony. It also abolished the consequences of corruption of blood, under which the heirs of a person attainted of treason were barred from inheriting property through the attainted person.

Background

At common law, a person convicted of treason or felony forfeited all of his lands and goods to the Crown, and in cases of treason his blood was said to be "corrupted", so that his descendants could not inherit through him. Reformers regarded these penalties as archaic and unjust, punishing innocent families for the crimes of their relatives. The Act formed part of a broader nineteenth-century programme of simplifying and humanising the criminal law.

Provisions

Section 1 of the Act provides that no confession, verdict, inquest, conviction or judgment of or for any treason or felony, or felo de se, shall cause any attainder or corruption of blood, or any forfeiture or escheat. The Act nevertheless preserved the power of the courts to order the payment of compensation and costs out of the property of a convicted felon, and provided machinery for the administration of a convict's property during his imprisonment, including the appointment of administrators.

The Act also abolished the penalty of forfeiture for felo de se (suicide), complementing the Burial of Suicide Act 1823 and anticipating the eventual decriminalisation of suicide by the Suicide Act 1961.

Later developments

Much of the Act's administrative machinery concerning convicts' property was later overtaken by modern legislation, but the central abolition of forfeiture and corruption of blood remains in force. In Scots law, forfeiture for treason had a separate history, and the Act's application north of the border was limited.
