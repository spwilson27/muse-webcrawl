Concord grape

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Concord grapes are a slip-skin variety of Vitis labrusca

The Concord grape is a cultivar derived from the grape species Vitis labrusca (also known as the fox grape) that are used as table grapes, wine grapes and juice grapes. They are often used to make grape jelly, grape juice, grape pies, grape-flavored soft drinks, and candy. The grape is sometimes used to make wine, particularly sacramental and kosher wine.

History

The Concord cultivar was developed in 1849 by Ephraim Wales Bull of Concord, Massachusetts, who planted seeds from wild Vitis labrusca and evaluated some 22,000 seedlings before settling on the ideal vine, which he planted in 1843 and exhibited in 1853. It proved hardy, productive, and well suited to the climate of the northeastern United States. Bull famously received little profit from his creation, as the variety was widely propagated by others; his epitaph reads, "He sowed — others reaped."

Description

Concord grapes are large, dark blue-black, and highly aromatic, with a loose "slip skin" that separates readily from the pulp. The fruit ripens in mid-to-late season and the vines are vigorous and disease-resistant. Thomas Bramwell Welch applied Louis Pasteur's pasteurization process to Concord juice in 1869, creating unfermented grape juice and founding Welch's.

Production

Major production areas include the Finger Lakes and Lake Erie regions of New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan, and Washington state. Concord is the most widely planted juice grape in the United States.
