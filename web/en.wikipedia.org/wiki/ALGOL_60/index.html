ALGOL 60 (short for Algorithmic Language 1960) is a member of the ALGOL family of computer programming languages. Defined by the Report on the Algorithmic Language ALGOL 60 (1960, revised 1963), it consolidated many ideas then circulating in language design and became the standard way of describing algorithms in print for more than two decades.

Features

ALGOL 60 introduced block structure with local variables, nested scopes, recursion, and both call-by-value and call-by-name parameter passing. Its syntax was specified in Backus–Naur form, a notation developed for the report that became foundational in formal language description. The language famously had no standard input/output facilities, leaving them to implementations. Distinctive constructs included the for loop with flexible step-until elements, conditional expressions, and the ability to pass procedures as parameters.

Influence

Although ALGOL 60 saw more use in Europe than in the United States — where Fortran and COBOL dominated — its influence on later language design was immense. Tony Hoare remarked that it was "a language so far ahead of its time that it was not only an improvement on its predecessors but also on nearly all its successors". Pascal, Simula, C and their descendants all borrowed its block structure and control constructs; John McCarthy's work on ALGOL fed into Lisp's design as well.

Revised Report and sample

The committee included John Backus, Friedrich Bauer, Peter Naur (editor of the report), Edsger Dijkstra, John McCarthy and Alan Perlis, among others. A characteristic feature, "Jensen's device", exploited call-by-name to write general summation procedures.
