Mass wasting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mass wasting, also known as mass movement, is the geomorphic process by which soil, sand, regolith and rock move downslope, typically as a solid, continuous or discontinuous mass, largely under the force of gravity. Unlike erosion by running water, wind or ice, mass wasting involves material moving under its own weight, although water, ice and other agents often play a role in triggering or lubricating the movement.

Types

Mass wasting events are classified by the type of material involved, the mechanism of movement and the speed of motion:

  • Falls — rockfalls and debris falls, in which material drops freely through the air from a steep slope or cliff.
  • Topples — blocks of rock or debris tilt and rotate forward away from a slope.
  • Slides — movement along a distinct surface of rupture; includes rotational slides (slumps) and translational slides, as well as rockslides and landslides.
  • Flows — material behaves like a viscous fluid; includes debris flows, mudflows, earthflows, avalanches and lahars.
  • Creep — very slow, gradual downslope movement of soil and regolith, evidenced by tilted trees, fences and retaining walls.
  • Solifluction — slow flow of water-saturated soil over frozen ground in periglacial environments.

Triggers and contributing factors

Mass wasting occurs when the shear stress acting down a slope exceeds the shear strength of the slope material. Contributing factors include steepness of slope, the nature and layering of the material, loss of vegetation, weathering, and increases in water content, which add weight and raise pore-water pressure, reducing friction. Sudden triggers include heavy rainfall, snowmelt, earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and human activities such as undercutting slopes for roads and construction, loading slopes with fill or structures, and removing vegetation.

Significance

Mass wasting is a major natural hazard, destroying homes, roads and infrastructure and causing thousands of deaths worldwide each year; notable disasters include the 1970 Huascarán avalanche in Peru and the 2014 Oso landslide in Washington state. It is also a primary mechanism by which landscapes are lowered and sediment is delivered to streams.
