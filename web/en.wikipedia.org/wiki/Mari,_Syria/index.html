Mari (modern Tell Hariri) was an ancient Semitic city-state in present-day Syria, on the western bank of the Euphrates near the Iraqi border. Founded around 2900 BC, it flourished as a trading hub between Sumer and the Levant and is famous for the royal palace of Zimri-Lim and its archive of more than 20,000 cuneiform tablets.

History

Mari passed through three urban phases. The second city was destroyed by Sargon of Akkad; the third was ruled by the shakkanakku governors and then by an Amorite dynasty. Under Zimri-Lim (c. 1775–1761 BC) Mari reached its zenith; his palace covered some 2.5 hectares with about 300 rooms and contained celebrated wall paintings. In 1761 BC Hammurabi of Babylon captured the city and destroyed it two years later.

Excavations

The site was discovered in 1933 and excavated for decades by André Parrot and his successors. The Mari tablets, written mostly in Akkadian, illuminate diplomacy, prophecy and daily life across the ancient Near East.
