Hatepe eruption

The Hatepe eruption, named for the Hatepe Plinian pumice deposits on the shores of Lake Taupo, was the most recent major eruption of the Taupo Volcano in New Zealand's North Island. It occurred around 180–232 CE and is regarded as the most violent volcanic eruption in the world in the last 5,000 years, with a Volcanic Explosivity Index of 7.

Eruption sequence

The eruption proceeded through several phases over a period of hours to days. Initial phreatomagmatic explosions were followed by a dry Plinian column that deposited the Hatepe pumice fall over a vast area of the North Island. The climactic phase produced pyroclastic flows, culminating in an enormous ignimbrite that swept over about 20,000 km² of land, filling valleys and crossing hills up to 1,500 m high; almost all vegetation within the flow area was destroyed.

Dating

The eruption was long dated to 186 CE from ice-core and tree-ring evidence and from a possible reference in Chinese and Roman records of unusual atmospheric phenomena around that time. Later radiocarbon wiggle-matching of trees killed by the ignimbrite suggested a slightly later date, in the range of about 180–232 CE.

Effects

New Zealand was not yet settled by humans at the time, so the eruption caused no known human casualties, but it devastated ecosystems across the central North Island and significantly enlarged Lake Taupo, which eventually filled the collapsed caldera. Pyroclastic material still forms the distinctive pumice deposits around the lake.
