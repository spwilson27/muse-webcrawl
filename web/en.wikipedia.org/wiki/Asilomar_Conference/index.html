Asilomar Conference

Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA
Date24–27 February 1975
LocationAsilomar Conference Grounds, Pacific Grove, California, USA
ParticipantsAbout 140 scientists, with lawyers, physicians, and journalists
Key organizersPaul Berg and colleagues (Nucleic Acids Gordon Conference group)
OutcomeGuidelines for containment of recombinant DNA research; basis for NIH guidelines (1976)

The Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA, held in February 1975 at the Asilomar Conference Grounds in Pacific Grove, California, was a landmark meeting at which scientists who had developed recombinant DNA technology debated its potential biohazards and agreed on voluntary guidelines for conducting the research safely. It is a frequently cited model of scientific self-governance and responsible innovation.

Background

In 1971, Paul Berg's planned experiment inserting DNA from the monkey virus SV40 into E. coli raised fears that a cancer-causing virus might escape into human gut bacteria. After the first successful recombinant DNA molecules were created in 1972–1973, participants at the 1973 Gordon Conference on Nucleic Acids voted to alert the scientific community and funding agencies. In July 1974, Berg and ten colleagues published letters in Science and Nature calling for a voluntary worldwide moratorium on certain classes of experiments until risks could be assessed, and an international conference was convened.

The conference

About 140 participants — mostly molecular biologists, along with some lawyers, physicians, and members of the press — met for four days. Working groups considered the risks of different types of experiments. The conference's final statement, published in 1975, recommended that work proceed under a graded scheme of physical containment (laboratory practices and facilities, later codified as P1–P4) and biological containment (using enfeebled host organisms and vectors unable to survive outside the lab), and that the most hazardous experiments — such as cloning DNA from highly pathogenic organisms or toxin genes — should not be performed at all at that time.

Legacy

The recommendations shaped the U.S. National Institutes of Health recombinant DNA guidelines issued in 1976, and similar rules abroad; most restrictions were progressively relaxed as evidence accumulated that the work was safer than feared. "Asilomar" has since become a byword for anticipatory governance of powerful technologies: a 2017 meeting at the same site produced the Asilomar AI Principles for artificial intelligence, and the 1975 conference is routinely invoked in debates over gene editing, gain-of-function research, and synthetic biology. Critics have noted that the conference largely kept the discussion within the scientific community, sidestepping broader public debate about whether the research should proceed.
