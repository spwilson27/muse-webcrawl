Koreans in Japan

Koreans in Japan comprise one of Japan's largest ethnic minority communities. The majority are Zainichi Koreans — permanent residents descended from Koreans who came to Japan during the period of Japanese colonial rule over Korea (1910–1945), whether as migrants or as conscripted labourers.

History

After Japan annexed Korea in 1910, Koreans moved to Japan in growing numbers for work in mines, factories and construction. By 1945 about two million Koreans were in Japan. Most returned to Korea after the Second World War, but roughly 600,000 remained. With the Treaty of San Francisco in 1952 they lost Japanese nationality, becoming stateless residents whose legal position was only regularised through later agreements: the 1965 treaty with South Korea created a permanent-residence track for those taking South Korean nationality, while those affiliated with North Korea remained in a separate status, unified under "special permanent resident" status in 1991.

Organisations and identity

The community split politically between Mindan, affiliated with South Korea, and Chongryon, affiliated with North Korea, which runs a network of Korean schools (Chosen gakko). Successive generations are increasingly Japan-born and Japanese-speaking; rates of naturalisation and intermarriage are high, and many ethnic Koreans live under Japanese names. Discrimination in employment, housing and public life, and periodic hate speech targeting Korean schools and neighbourhoods, remain documented concerns.

Newcomers

Since the 1980s, a distinct population of "newcomer" Koreans — students, professionals and spouses from South Korea — has grown, concentrated in areas such as Tokyo's Shin-Ōkubo district, which has become a centre of the Korean Wave (Hallyu) in Japan. Koreatowns also thrive in Osaka's Tsuruhashi and Ikuno districts.
