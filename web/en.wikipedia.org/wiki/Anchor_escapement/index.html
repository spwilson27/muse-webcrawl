The anchor escapement is a type of escapement used in pendulum clocks. Invented around 1657, it is named for its anchor-shaped rocking piece, whose two arms (pallets) alternately engage the teeth of the escape wheel, releasing one tooth at a time and giving the pendulum a small push at each swing. Its introduction — credited variously to Robert Hooke and to the clockmaker William Clement, who applied it around 1671 — was a major advance over the older verge escapement.

Operation

The escape wheel, driven by the clock's weights, presses against whichever pallet is engaged. As the pendulum swings, it rocks the anchor: one pallet lifts clear, the wheel advances briefly until the opposite pallet intercepts a tooth, and the cycle repeats. Because the geometry of the pallets pushes the wheel slightly backwards during part of each cycle, the anchor escapement is classified as a "recoil" escapement; the second hand of a clock so equipped can be seen to shudder backwards slightly at each tick.

Advantages

Compared with the verge, the anchor allowed a much narrower pendulum swing (about 3–6 degrees instead of near 100), which reduced circular error, permitted the use of long, heavy, seconds-beating pendulums, and made tall, narrow clock cases practical. Combined with the long pendulum it made possible the longcase ("grandfather") clock and improved domestic timekeeping accuracy to within a few seconds a day. The anchor remained the standard escapement for pendulum clocks for some 150 years, until refined forms such as the deadbeat escapement (c. 1715) superseded it in precision regulators.
