Syllabification

Syllabification is the separation of a word into syllables, whether spoken or written. The written separation is usually marked by a hyphen or an interpunct when breaking words across lines in typesetting, and in dictionaries syllabification shows pronunciation structure.

Principles

Phonological syllabification is governed by the structure of the syllable — an onset (consonants before the vowel), a nucleus (usually a vowel) and an optional coda. A widely used heuristic is the maximum onset principle: consonants between vowels are assigned to the onset of the following syllable wherever the language's phonotactics permit. Sonority sequencing also plays a role: segments generally rise in sonority towards the nucleus and fall after it. Languages differ in the complexity of syllables they allow; Japanese favours simple (C)V syllables, whereas English permits heavy clusters, as in strengths /strɛŋkθs/.

Orthographic division

Written word division does not always match phonological syllabification: English typesetting traditionally splits words by etymological and morphological boundaries (e.g., know-ledge in British practice versus American knowl-edge based on pronunciation). Automatic hyphenation algorithms, such as the Liang–Knuth algorithm used in TeX, apply pattern-based syllabification for justification in printing.
