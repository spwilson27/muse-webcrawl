Federal holidays in the United States

Federal holidays in the United States are public holidays established by Congress for employees of the federal government (5 U.S.C. § 6103). On these days, non-essential federal offices are closed and federal employees are paid for the day off. There are currently eleven federal holidays; most are widely observed by state governments, schools and private employers, though private businesses are not required to close.

History

The first federal holidays, in 1870, applied to federal workers in the District of Columbia and covered New Year's Day, Independence Day, Thanksgiving and Christmas; the list was expanded in 1879–1885 to all federal employees and grew with Washington's Birthday, Memorial Day and Labor Day. Martin Luther King Jr. Day was signed into law in 1983 and first observed federally in 1986. The most recent addition, Juneteenth — commemorating the emancipation of enslaved people in Texas on 19 June 1865 — became a federal holiday in 2021. The Uniform Monday Holiday Act of 1968 moved several holidays to Mondays to create three-day weekends.
