Uncontained engine failure

An uncontained engine failure is an aircraft engine failure in which rotating components of the engine break apart and are ejected through — rather than being contained within — the engine casing. Because fragments such as fan, compressor, or turbine disc pieces can be released with enormous kinetic energy, uncontained failures can puncture fuel tanks, sever hydraulic and control lines, and injure or kill passengers, making them among the most hazardous types of engine malfunction.

Causes and certification

Uncontained failures typically result from fatigue cracking of discs, manufacturing defects in titanium alloys (such as hard-alpha inclusions), overspeed, or foreign-object damage. Engine certification standards require manufacturers to demonstrate containment of a single failed fan blade, but a burst disc historically could not be contained by any practical casing, so design rules instead emphasize prevention, inspection, and minimizing the damage when fragments escape — for example by shielding critical systems and separating redundant hydraulic lines.

Notable incidents

  • United Airlines Flight 232 (1989) — a fatigue crack in the fan disc of the tail engine of a DC-10 caused an uncontained failure that destroyed all three hydraulic systems; the crash landing at Sioux City killed 112 of 296 aboard, and prompted sweeping changes in titanium inspection.
  • Qantas Flight 32 (2010) — an Airbus A380 suffered an uncontained turbine disc failure in a Rolls-Royce Trent 900; debris damaged the wing and systems, but the crew landed safely at Singapore with no injuries.
  • Southwest Airlines Flight 1380 (2018) — a fan blade separated on a Boeing 737; debris struck the fuselage and a window, causing a passenger's death, the first fatality on a US airline in nearly a decade.
  • United Airlines Flight 328 (2021) — fan blade failure in a Pratt & Whitney PW4000 shed the inlet and cowling over Colorado; the aircraft landed safely.
