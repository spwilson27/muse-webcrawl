Daimyo

Daimyo (Japanese: 大名, daimyō) were powerful Japanese feudal lords who ruled most of the country from their vast hereditary land holdings from the 10th century until the Meiji Restoration in 1868. Subordinate to the shogun, the daimyo were among the most powerful rulers and, at the height of the system, numbered around 300, each governing a domain (han) assessed at a minimum of 10,000 koku of rice.

History

The earliest daimyo emerged from the shugo (military governors) of the Muromachi period. During the Sengoku period of civil war (1467–1600), independent warrior lords — the sengoku daimyo such as Oda Nobunaga, Takeda Shingen, and Uesugi Kenshin — fought for supremacy until Japan was unified under Tokugawa Ieyasu. Under the Tokugawa shogunate, daimyo were classified as shinpan (relatives of the Tokugawa), fudai (hereditary vassals), or tozama (outside lords), and were controlled through the sankin-kōtai system requiring alternate-year residence in Edo, leaving their families there as hostages.

Abolition

The daimyo were stripped of their domains in 1871 with the abolition of the han system (haihan-chiken) by the Meiji government. Former daimyo were pensioned off and many were later incorporated into the new kazoku peerage.
