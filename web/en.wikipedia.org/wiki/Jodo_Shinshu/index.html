Jōdo Shinshū (浄土真宗, "The True Essence of the Pure Land Way"), also known as Shin Buddhism, is a school of Pure Land Buddhism founded by the Japanese monk Shinran (1173–1263). It is one of the most widely practised branches of Buddhism in Japan. Shinran taught that in the present degenerate age (mappō) no one can attain enlightenment by their own effort ("self power", jiriki), and that salvation comes entirely through the "other power" (tariki) of Amida Buddha, whose Primal Vow promises birth in the Pure Land to all who entrust themselves to him. The recitation of the Buddha's name — Namu Amida Butsu ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha") — is therefore an expression of gratitude rather than a meritorious technique.

Shinran's teaching

Shinran was a disciple of Hōnen, founder of the independent Jōdo-shū, and was exiled to Echigo in the persecution of 1207; he called himself "neither monk nor layman", married, and ate meat, rejecting the monastic framework altogether — a stance that made Shin teaching uniquely accessible to peasants, fishermen and outcasts. His masterwork, the Kyōgyōshinshō, expounds the doctrine of shinjin — true entrusting given by Amida — as the sole cause of birth in the Pure Land. A celebrated provocation attributed to him holds that "even a good person attains birth in the Pure Land; how much more so an evil person", inverting conventional merit logic.

Development and history

After Shinran's death, his daughter Kakushinni helped establish his mausoleum at Ōtani in Kyoto, which grew into the Hongan-ji temple, led by hereditary descendants. Under the eighth head, Rennyo (1415–1499), the school expanded explosively among the peasantry, and its congregations (monto) launched the Ikkō-ikki uprisings, ruling Kaga province for nearly a century until crushed by Oda Nobunaga after the ten-year Ishiyama Hongan-ji War (1570–1580). In 1602 Tokugawa Ieyasu split the Hongan-ji into eastern (Higashi) and western (Nishi) branches, which remain the school's two great denominations. Under the Tokugawa temple-registration system, Shin temples became fixtures of nearly every community.

Practice and diaspora

Shin practice centres on hearing the teaching, congregational chanting of Shinran's hymns (wasan) and texts such as the Shōshinge, and services at temples and home altars; Shin clergy (sōryo) may marry and eat meat, a model that later influenced all Japanese Buddhism. From the late 19th century, Japanese emigration carried Jōdo Shinshū to Hawaii, North and South America, where organisations such as the Buddhist Churches of America serve descendant communities today.
