The Swiss Plateau or Central Plateau (German: Schweizer Mittelland; French: Plateau suisse) is one of the three major landscapes in Switzerland, lying between the Alps to the south and the Jura Mountains to the north-west. It covers about 30 per cent of Switzerland's land area but contains the great majority of the population, the largest cities, and most of the country's industry, agriculture and transport infrastructure.

The rolling landscape of the Swiss Plateau.

Geography

The plateau stretches from Lake Geneva in the south-west to Lake Constance in the north-east, in a band roughly 300 kilometres long and up to 50 kilometres wide. It consists largely of molasse — sedimentary rock formed from debris eroded from the rising Alps — shaped by glaciers during the ice ages into rolling hills, valleys and lakes such as Lake Zurich, Lake Neuchâtel, Lake Biel and Lake Murten. Major rivers include the Aare, the Reuss, the Limmat and the Thur.

Settlement and economy

With an average population density among the highest in Europe for a largely rural region, the plateau hosts Zurich, Bern (the federal capital), Lausanne, Winterthur, St. Gallen, Lucerne and most other large Swiss cities, as well as dense networks of towns and villages. Its fertile soils support intensive agriculture, and its flat terrain carries Switzerland's principal motorways and railways, including the east–west A1 motorway. Continued population growth has produced extensive suburbanisation and debate about landscape protection and zoning.

Climate

The plateau has a temperate climate with cold winters — often characterised by persistent fog and low cloud (Hochnebel) in the cooler months — and warm summers. Fog frequently blankets the plateau while the Alps above are in sunshine, a phenomenon that shapes regional recreation and settlement patterns.
