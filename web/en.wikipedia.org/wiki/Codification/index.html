Codification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, codification is the process of collecting and restating the law of a jurisdiction in certain areas, usually into a single code. The impulse runs from the ancient codes (Hammurabi's stele, Rome's Twelve Tables) through Justinian's 6th-century Corpus Juris Civilis — the rediscovery of which seeded Europe's civil-law tradition — to the great modern systematising projects driven by Enlightenment faith that law could be rational, complete and accessible to citizens.

The modern codes

The Napoleonic Code (1804) — clear, egalitarian in private law, exportable by conquest and prestige — was followed by Austria's ABGB (1811), Germany's BGB (1900, conceptually dense and professor-built), Switzerland's ZGB (1907, praised for plain language) and codifications across Latin America, East Asia and the post-colonial world. Codification debates recur within common-law countries too (Bentham coined "codification" and campaigned for it; criminal codes and the US Uniform Commercial Code show partial success). Design tensions are permanent: codes claim systematic coherence and accessibility, yet immediately re-accrete doctrine, commentary and case law — the living law escapes the book in every system.
