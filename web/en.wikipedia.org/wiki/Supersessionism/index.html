Supersessionism

Supersessionism holds that the Christian Church has superseded Israel in God's covenant.

Supersessionism, also called replacement theology, is the Christian doctrine that the Christian Church has superseded the Jewish people, assuming their role as God's covenanted people, thus asserting that the New Covenant through Jesus Christ has superseded or replaced the Mosaic covenant. Supersessionist views are held by many historic Christian denominations, though the doctrine has been increasingly scrutinized and, in some churches, formally repudiated since the mid-twentieth century.

Types

Scholars distinguish several forms: economic supersessionism, in which Israel's role in salvation history is considered completed; punitive supersessionism, in which Jews are seen as punished by God for rejecting Christ; and structural supersessionism, in which the Old Testament is read as meaningful only as a backdrop to the New. These categories were systematized by theologians such as R. Kendall Soulen.

History

Elements of supersessionism appear in early Christian writings, including the Epistle of Barnabas and the works of Justin Martyr, and were elaborated by Church Fathers such as Augustine. The doctrine shaped Christian attitudes toward Jews through the medieval and Reformation periods and is regarded by many scholars as a contributing factor to Christian anti-Judaism.

Modern reassessment

After the Holocaust, many churches reconsidered the doctrine. The Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra aetate (1965) rejected the charge of collective Jewish guilt for the crucifixion and affirmed the enduring value of God's covenant with the Jewish people. Numerous Protestant bodies have issued similar statements, and dual-covenant theology proposes that God's covenant with Israel remains in effect alongside the Christian covenant.
