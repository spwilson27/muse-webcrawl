Occupational cancer

Occupational cancer is cancer caused by exposure to carcinogenic agents in the workplace. Estimates commonly attribute roughly 2–8% of all cancers to occupational exposures, though the fraction varies by country and era. Because such cancers typically develop decades after exposure, they are often under-recognised.

History

The earliest clear description of an occupational cancer is Percivall Pott's 1775 identification of scrotal cancer in chimney sweeps exposed to soot. In the late 19th century, bladder cancer was observed among workers in the synthetic dye industry exposed to aromatic amines such as benzidine and 2-naphthylamine. The 20th century brought recognition of lung cancer and mesothelioma from asbestos, leukaemia from benzene, and nasal cancer in woodworkers and leather workers.

Common agents and cancers

Well-established occupational carcinogens include asbestos (lung cancer, mesothelioma), crystalline silica (lung cancer), diesel engine exhaust (lung cancer), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, hexavalent chromium compounds, nickel compounds, radon in underground mines, ionising radiation, and ultraviolet radiation for outdoor workers (skin cancer). The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies agents by the strength of evidence that they cause cancer.

Prevention

Control follows the hierarchy of controls: eliminating or substituting carcinogens, engineering controls such as ventilation, administrative controls, and personal protective equipment. Regulation includes occupational exposure limits and, in some jurisdictions, presumption laws that make it easier for affected workers to obtain compensation.
