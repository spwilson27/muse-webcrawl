Gibbs' phase rule

In thermodynamics, Gibbs' phase rule describes the possible number of degrees of freedom (F) in a chemical system at equilibrium, in terms of the number of separate phases (P) and the number of chemical components (C) in the system. It is given by the relation:

F = C − P + 2

The rule was derived by the American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs in his landmark paper "On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances", published between 1875 and 1878. The two additional variables are temperature and pressure, which are assumed to be the same throughout the system at equilibrium.

Derivation

At equilibrium, the state of a system with P phases and C components in each phase can be described by the composition of each phase (requiring C − 1 mole fraction variables per phase) together with temperature and pressure, giving P(C − 1) + 2 variables in total. Equilibrium also requires that the chemical potential of each component be equal in all phases, imposing C(P − 1) independent constraints. Subtracting the constraints from the variables leaves F = C − P + 2 freely variable quantities.

Examples

For a single-component system such as pure water (C = 1), a single phase (liquid water) has F = 2 degrees of freedom: temperature and pressure can vary independently. Where two phases coexist, such as liquid water and ice along the melting curve, F = 1, so pressure is fixed once temperature is chosen. At the triple point, three phases coexist and F = 0: the state is invariant, occurring at a unique temperature (273.16 K for water) and pressure.

Applications and limitations

The phase rule is fundamental to the construction and interpretation of phase diagrams in chemistry, materials science, and geology. Variations exist for systems with additional variables (such as magnetic fields) or constraints (such as fixed pressure in condensed systems, which reduces the rule to F = C − P + 1). The rule applies only when the system is at thermodynamic equilibrium and all phases are in mutual contact.
