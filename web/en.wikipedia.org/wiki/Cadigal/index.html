The Cadigal, also spelled Gadigal, are a group of Aboriginal Australians whose traditional lands are located in what is now inner Sydney, New South Wales, Australia. The Cadigal originally inhabited the area that they called ‘Cadi’, on Sydney Cove, from South Head to the inner reaches of the harbour near modern-day Petersham. Their territory is generally described as stretching along the southern shore of Sydney Harbour between South Head and Long Cove (now Darling Harbour), and inland to the area around present-day Leichhardt and Petersham.

The Cadigal were one of some 29 clan groups that made up the wider Eora nation, whose people spoke dialects of the Dharug language. They were a coastal or ‘saltwater’ people, relying heavily on fishing and the resources of the harbour for food, including fish, shellfish and other marine life gathered from the coves and inlets of the harbour.

History

Before European colonisation, the Cadigal population is estimated to have been in the range of several hundred people. Following the arrival of the First Fleet in 1788 and the establishment of the penal colony at Sydney Cove, the Cadigal were among the first Aboriginal people to come into sustained contact with British settlers. A devastating smallpox epidemic in 1789 killed a large proportion of the Aboriginal population around Sydney, including many Cadigal.

The Cadigal man Woollarawarre Bennelong, of the neighbouring Wangal clan but closely associated with the Sydney clans, became a prominent intermediary between the Aboriginal people of Sydney and Governor Arthur Phillip. Another notable figure was Barangaroo, a Cadigal woman who resisted aspects of British colonisation and is commemorated in the name of the Barangaroo precinct on Darling Harbour.

Despite colonisation, dispossession and disease, Cadigal descendants and the broader Aboriginal community of Sydney maintain a continuing connection to the land. The name Gadigal is widely commemorated in modern Sydney, including Gadigal Avenue and the Gadigal metro station.
