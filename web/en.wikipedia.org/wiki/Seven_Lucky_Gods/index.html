The Seven Lucky Gods or Seven Gods of Fortune (七福神, Shichifukujin) are a group of seven deities of good luck in Japanese mythology and folklore. The group reflects Japan's syncretic religious culture: only one of the seven, Ebisu, is a native Japanese kami; three derive from Indian Hinduism as transmitted through Buddhism, and three from Chinese Taoism and Buddhism.

The seven

  • Ebisu — god of fishermen, luck and commerce, usually shown holding a fishing rod and a fat sea bream. The only purely Japanese member of the group.
  • Daikokuten — god of wealth and the household, from the Indian Mahākāla; shown standing on rice bales, wielding a wish-granting mallet and wearing a sack of treasure.
  • Bishamonten — armoured warrior god of warriors and defence, from the Indian Vaiśravaṇa (Kubera), holding a spear and a miniature pagoda.
  • Benzaiten — goddess of music, learning and water, the group's only goddess, derived from the Indian Saraswati and shown playing the biwa lute.
  • Fukurokuju — god of happiness, wealth and longevity, of Taoist origin, notable for his enormously elongated forehead.
  • Jurōjin — god of longevity, another Taoist-derived figure, shown with a staff, a scroll and a deer.
  • Hotei — the fat, laughing god of contentment and abundance, based on the Chinese monk Budai, shown with a huge belly and a sack of gifts.

The treasure ship and New Year customs

The gods are often depicted together aboard the Takarabune, a treasure ship loaded with gold, coral and the inexhaustible purse, which sails into port on New Year's Eve. A picture of the ship placed under the pillow on the night of 1–2 January is believed to bring a lucky first dream (hatsuyume). In January many Japanese make pilgrimages to seven shrines and temples, one for each god (Shichifukujin meguri), collecting stamps and stamps of the gods on nōsatsu or calligraphy scrolls — a custom dating to the Edo period.
