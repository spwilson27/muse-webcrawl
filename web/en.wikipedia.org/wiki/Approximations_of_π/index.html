Approximations of π

Value3.14159265358979…

TypeIrrational, transcendental

Approximations of π include the many numerical estimates of the mathematical constant π developed across history, from crude fractional values in ancient Egypt and Babylon to modern computations giving trillions of decimal digits. Because π is irrational, any finite decimal or fraction can only approximate it; practical work typically needs only a handful of digits, while record computations serve as benchmarks and tests of algorithms.

Early history

The earliest written approximations come from Babylon (about 25/8 = 3.125) and from the Egyptian Rhind Papyrus (about 256/81 ≈ 3.1605) around 1650 BCE. The Hebrew Bible's account of the molten sea in Solomon's temple implies a value of 3.

Polygon approximations

Archimedes (c. 250 BCE) gave the first rigorous bounds by inscribing and circumscribing polygons of up to 96 sides around a circle, concluding that 223/71 < π < 22/7. The Chinese mathematician Zu Chongzhi (5th century CE) found the remarkably accurate fraction 355/113 (≈ 3.1415929), which remained the best-known approximation for nearly a millennium. In India, Madhava of Sangamagrama developed infinite series for π in the 14th century.

Modern computations

The development of infinite series, such as the Leibniz formula and the rapidly converging Machin-like formulae, allowed hand computations of hundreds of digits by the 19th century. Electronic computers changed the scale entirely: by 1949 ENIAC had computed over 2,000 digits, and algorithms such as the Chudnovsky algorithm (based on work of Ramanujan) have pushed the record beyond 100 trillion digits in the 2020s.

Memorization

The practice of memorising digits of π, sometimes called piphilology, uses mnemonic sentences in which word lengths encode digits. Competitive memorisers have recited tens of thousands of digits.
