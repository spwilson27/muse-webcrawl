Neocolonialism

Neocolonialism is the continued economic and political control exerted over formerly colonized countries by former colonial powers and other developed states, despite the formal achievement of political independence. The term was popularized in the mid-twentieth century by leaders of newly independent nations, most notably Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana, whose 1965 book Neo-Colonialism, the Last Stage of Imperialism argued that apparent sovereignty could mask external control over a state's economy, resources, and policy.

Etymology and history

The French philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre used the term in Colonialism and Neocolonialism (1964). The concept framed debates at the 1955 Bandung Conference and in the Non-Aligned Movement, and was formalized in resolutions of the All-African Peoples' Conference in the early 1960s.

Mechanisms

Critics of neocolonialism point to multinational corporations' control of extractive industries, conditional foreign aid and structural adjustment lending, trade arrangements favoring raw-material exports, currency arrangements such as the CFA franc zone, military basing agreements, and support for compliant client regimes. Dependency theory and world-systems theory developed related analyses in Latin America and the Caribbean.

Criticism

Opponents argue the term oversimplifies global interdependence, discounts the agency of postcolonial governments, and that poor outcomes often stem from domestic corruption or policy failure rather than external domination. Others respond that structural constraints make this a false dichotomy.
