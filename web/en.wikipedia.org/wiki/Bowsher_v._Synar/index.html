Bowsher v. Synar, 478 U.S. 714 (1986), was a United States Supreme Court decision that struck down part of the Gramm–Rudman–Hollings Balanced Budget and Emergency Deficit Control Act of 1985 for violating the constitutional separation of powers.

Background

The Gramm–Rudman–Hollings Act set maximum federal deficit amounts for each fiscal year through 1991, with the goal of eliminating the deficit. If the projected deficit exceeded the target, automatic spending cuts ("sequestration") would be triggered. Central to the scheme, the directors of the Office of Management and Budget and the Congressional Budget Office would estimate the required cuts, but the final determination was to be made by the Comptroller General, Charles A. Bowsher, head of the General Accounting Office. Congressman Mike Synar and others challenged the arrangement.

Decision

In an opinion by Chief Justice Warren Burger, the Court held by a 7–2 margin (on the core holding) that Congress could not constitutionally vest executive functions in an officer who is subject to removal by Congress. Because the Comptroller General could be removed by Congress through a joint resolution — and was therefore, in the Court's view, subservient to the legislative branch — assigning him the executive task of determining the spending cuts violated the separation of powers. Executive authority, the Court reasoned, must be exercised by officers accountable to the President.

"The Constitution does not permit Congress to execute the laws; it follows that Congress cannot grant to an officer under its control what it does not possess itself — the power to execute the laws of the United States."

Aftermath

The Court severed the offending provision, leaving the Act's fallback procedure in effect, under which the sequestration report was submitted to Congress and implemented through a joint resolution. Congress later amended the statute in 1987 to assign the sequestration role to the director of the Office of Management and Budget, an executive officer. Bowsher remains a leading formalist precedent on the separation of powers and congressional control over executive officers.
