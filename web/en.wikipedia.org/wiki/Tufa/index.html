Tufa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. Not to be confused with tuff, a volcanic rock.

Tufa
TypeSedimentary rock (chemical)
CompositionCalcium carbonate (calcite)
FormationPrecipitation from ambient-temperature, carbonate-rich water
Notable localitiesMono Lake (California), Plitvice Lakes (Croatia), Pamukkale (Turkey)

Tufa is a variety of limestone formed when carbonate minerals precipitate out of ambient-temperature water — typically spring water, lake water, or river water rich in dissolved calcium carbonate. Geologists distinguish tufa from travertine, a related and denser deposit often (though not always) associated with hot springs, though the terms have historically been used loosely and interchangeably. Tufa is typically porous and spongy in texture because it often forms by encrusting plants, mosses, and algae, whose decay leaves cavities in the stone.

Formation

Groundwater or lake water saturated with calcium carbonate becomes supersaturated where it emerges, warms, evaporates, or loses dissolved carbon dioxide — for instance at spring mouths, waterfalls, and lakeshores — causing calcite to precipitate. Photosynthesis by algae and aquatic plants also removes CO₂ and accelerates deposition, so growing tufa commonly encrusts living organisms, fossilizing them in the process. Growth can be rapid, on the order of millimetres to centimetres per year, and tufa formations can record past climate and lake levels in their chemistry and growth layers.

Notable formations

The most famous tufa towers are at Mono Lake in California, where underwater springs deposited limestone pinnacles that were exposed when the lake level fell after water diversions began in 1941. Barrier dams of tufa create the cascading lakes of Plitvice Lakes in Croatia and the Krka river falls; the white terraces of Pamukkale in Turkey are usually classed as travertine. In England, "petrifying wells" such as Mother Shipton's Cave in Knaresborough encrust objects in tufa, and spongy tufa was used locally as a lightweight building stone in churches and grottoes.

Uses

Soft when quarried and hardening on exposure, tufa has served since Roman times as a building material; it is used in landscaping and aquaria, and porous tufa is prized in gardens because plants root readily in it. Scientific study of tufa deposits contributes to palaeoclimate reconstruction, since their isotopic composition captures the temperature and hydrology of the waters that formed them.
