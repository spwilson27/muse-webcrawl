The Tiger I (German: Panzerkampfwagen VI Tiger Ausf. E; ordnance inventory designation Sd.Kfz. 181) was a German heavy tank of the Second World War, deployed from 1942 in Africa and on both European fronts. Armed with the powerful 88 mm KwK 36 gun and protected by up to 100 mm of frontal armour, it outgunned and out-armoured nearly every Allied and Soviet tank it first met, and it built a formidable reputation — out of proportion, historians note, to the 1,347 examples built and their reliability in service.

Development

German heavy-tank studies began in the late 1930s, but the shock of meeting the Soviet T-34 and KV-1 in 1941 accelerated the programme. Rival prototypes by Henschel and Porsche were demonstrated on Hitler's birthday, 20 April 1942; Henschel's design won on maturity and mobility. Porsche's losing chassis were converted into the Ferdinand (later Elefant) tank destroyers. Production began in August 1942, and the first Tigers went into action that September near Leningrad, where bogging in swampy ground made a poor debut; they fought in Tunisia from late 1942.

Design

The Tiger's armour was thick but essentially vertical, relying on mass rather than slope. Its 88 mm gun, derived from the famous FlaK anti-aircraft gun, could defeat contemporary enemies at ranges beyond their effective reply. The tank weighed about 54 tonnes, rising to 57 in later production — heavy enough to strain its Maybach engine, transmission and interleaved road wheels, to limit the bridges it could cross, and to require special transport tracks for rail movement. Tigers were issued to independent heavy tank battalions (schwere Panzerabteilungen) and a few elite divisions, used as breakthrough and fire-brigade units.

Combat record and legacy

At the Battle of Kursk (1943), in Normandy — where Michael Wittmann's company ambushed a British column at Villers-Bocage in June 1944 — and in desperate defensive fighting in 1944–45, Tigers destroyed enemy armour at highly favourable exchange ratios when properly supported. Against them were chronic mechanical breakdowns, fuel hunger, vulnerability to air attack and the sheer numbers of Allied and Soviet production. Production ceased in August 1944 in favour of the larger Tiger II. Seven Tigers survive complete; the example at the Tank Museum, Bovington (Tiger 131), captured in Tunisia, is the only one running under its own power and is a celebrity of modern tank exhibitions.
