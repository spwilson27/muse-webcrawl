The Babylonian captivity or Babylonian exile was the period in Jewish history during which a large number of Judeans from the ancient Kingdom of Judah were forcibly deported to Babylonia by the Neo-Babylonian Empire. The deportations occurred in several waves, culminating in the destruction of Jerusalem and Solomon's Temple in 587/586 BCE, and ended after the fall of Babylon to the Achaemenid Empire, when Cyrus the Great permitted the exiles to return and rebuild the Temple.

Background

After the death of King Josiah at Megiddo in 609 BCE, Judah became a contested vassal between Egypt and the rising Neo-Babylonian Empire. Following the Babylonian victory at Carchemish (605 BCE), King Jehoiakim submitted to Nebuchadnezzar II, but his son Jehoiachin's revolt led to the first siege of Jerusalem in 597 BCE, in which the king, his court, and thousands of craftsmen and soldiers were deported.

Destruction of Jerusalem

Nebuchadnezzar installed Zedekiah as a puppet king, but Zedekiah also rebelled. After an eighteen-month siege, Babylonian forces breached Jerusalem in 587/586 BCE; the city and Solomon's Temple were burned, its walls razed, and much of the remaining population — apart from the poorest rural inhabitants — was deported to Babylonia.

Life in exile

The exiles settled in communities in Babylonia, notably along the Chebar canal near Nippur. Cuneiform documents such as the Al-Yahudu tablets attest to a community that maintained its identity, names, and legal life over generations. The exile is regarded as a formative period in which much of the Hebrew Bible was compiled and edited, and in which distinctly Jewish practices — Sabbath observance, circumcision, and communal prayer — took on renewed importance in the absence of the Temple.

Return

In 539 BCE Cyrus of Persia conquered Babylon and issued a decree, recorded in the Book of Ezra and echoed in the Cyrus Cylinder, allowing deported peoples to return and restore their sanctuaries. Waves of Judeans returned under leaders such as Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel, and the Second Temple was completed around 516/515 BCE.
