Mahayana ('Great Vehicle') is one of the two main branches of Buddhism, arising in India around the 1st century BC–1st century AD and spreading along the Silk Road to become dominant in East and Central Asia. Its hallmarks are the bodhisattva ideal — postponing one's own final nirvana to liberate all beings — the philosophy of emptiness (sunyata) in Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka, and devotion to celestial buddhas and bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin/Kannon), the embodiment of compassion.

Schools

Mahayana flowered into Pure Land devotion (rebirth in Amitabha's paradise), Chan/Zen meditation, Tiantai and Huayan systematics in China, and — interwoven with tantric practice — Tibetan Tibetan Buddhism. Its sutras present themselves as the Buddha's deeper teaching, a claim Theravada does not accept for its Pali canon; modern scholarship treats both canons as layered historical products.
