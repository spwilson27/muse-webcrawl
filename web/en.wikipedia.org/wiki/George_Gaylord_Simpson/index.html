George Gaylord Simpson

George Gaylord Simpson (June 16, 1902 – October 6, 1984) was an American paleontologist.

Born: Chicago, Illinois
Died: Tucson, Arizona
Fields: Paleontology, evolutionary biology
Known for: Modern synthesis; Tempo and Mode in Evolution

George Gaylord Simpson was an American paleontologist and one of the architects of the modern evolutionary synthesis. He showed that the fossil record of mammals was consistent with the mechanisms of Mendelian genetics and natural selection, reconciling paleontology with the new population genetics.

Life and career

Simpson was born in Chicago and raised in Colorado. He studied at the University of Colorado and Yale University, receiving his PhD from Yale in 1926 with a dissertation on Mesozoic mammals. He spent most of his career at the American Museum of Natural History in New York, where he became curator of vertebrate paleontology, and later taught at Columbia University, Harvard, and the University of Arizona.

Work

Simpson led expeditions to Patagonia in the 1930s, studying South America's extinct mammal faunas, and developed statistical methods for taxonomy in works such as Quantitative Zoogeography (with Anne Roe). His Tempo and Mode in Evolution (1944) was a landmark of the modern synthesis, introducing the concept of "quantum evolution" — rapid shifts between adaptive zones — and demonstrating that evolutionary rates in the fossil record varied widely. Later works included The Major Features of Evolution (1953) and the influential classification Principles of Animal Taxonomy (1961).

Views and legacy

Simpson was a combative defender of Darwinian selection against orthogenesis and Lamarckism, and later an outspoken sceptic of some claims of continental drift's role in mammal distribution (he favoured land bridges and sweepstakes dispersal) and of human evolution as inevitable progress. He received the Daniel Giraud Elliot Medal and the Linnean Society's Darwin–Wallace Medal, among many honours.
