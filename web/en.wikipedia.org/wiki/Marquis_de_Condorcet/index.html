Marie Jean Antoine Nicolas de Caritat, Marquis de Condorcet (17 September 1743 – 29 March 1794), known as Nicolas de Condorcet, was a French philosopher, mathematician and political scientist. A leading figure of the Enlightenment, he advocated for a liberal economy, free public instruction, constitutionalism and equal rights for women and people of all races. He died in prison during the French Revolution, likely to escape execution during the Reign of Terror.

Voting theory

Condorcet founded the mathematical study of voting. His Essai sur l'application de l'analyse à la probabilité des décisions (1785) contains the Condorcet paradox: majority preferences can cycle (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A) even when every individual voter is consistent, so no candidate beats all others head-to-head. A candidate who does beat every other in pairwise contests — the Condorcet winner — is, in his view, the rightful winner, and "Condorcet methods" are voting rules that always elect such a candidate when one exists. This contrasts with the positional count championed by his rival Jean-Charles de Borda, with whom he disputed priority and principle in the Academy of Sciences. The Condorcet jury theorem shows that, under stated conditions, larger majorities are more likely to reach the correct decision. His analysis underlies modern treatments of the spoiler effect and strategic voting.

Other ideas

Condorcet promoted public education free of religious control, drafted a "Girondin" constitution in 1793, argued for women's suffrage as early as 1790 — remarkably early — and opposed slavery as a member of the Society of the Friends of the Blacks. While in hiding from the Revolutionary authorities he wrote his most famous work, the Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind, a vision of indefinite human progress published posthumously in 1795.
