Acute fatty liver of pregnancy

Acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP) is a rare but serious complication of pregnancy in which fat accumulates in the liver cells (microvesicular steatosis), leading to liver dysfunction and potentially liver failure. It most often occurs in the third trimester and requires prompt delivery.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically develop over one to two weeks and include nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain (especially in the upper right or epigastric region), loss of appetite, fatigue, headache, jaundice, and excessive thirst and urination. Complications can include hypoglycemia, coagulopathy (including disseminated intravascular coagulation), kidney failure, pancreatitis, encephalopathy, and stillbirth.

Causes and pathophysiology

The condition is associated with disorders of fatty acid oxidation. Some cases involve a fetus homozygous for long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCHAD) deficiency: the affected fetus and placenta produce toxic fatty acid metabolites that the mother, herself a carrier with reduced enzyme capacity, cannot adequately clear, injuring her liver. Risk factors include first pregnancy, male fetus, multiple gestation, and pre-eclampsia, with which it may overlap; it must be distinguished from HELLP syndrome.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by laboratory findings of elevated transaminases and bilirubin, hypoglycemia, elevated ammonia, and coagulopathy; the Swansea criteria are commonly used. Imaging may show a bright, fatty liver, but biopsy — showing microvesicular fat — is rarely performed because of bleeding risk.

Treatment

The definitive treatment is stabilization of the mother followed by prompt delivery of the baby, regardless of gestational age. Supportive intensive care addresses hypoglycemia, coagulopathy, and organ failure. Most patients recover within days to weeks after delivery, though severe cases may require liver transplantation. AFLP occurs in roughly 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 20,000 pregnancies; maternal mortality, once very high, has fallen to under 10–20 percent with early recognition, while perinatal mortality remains significant.
