Black swan theory

Europeans assumed all swans were white until black swans were seen in Australia

The black swan theory or theory of black swan events is a metaphor that describes an event that comes as a surprise, has a major effect, and is often inappropriately rationalized after the fact with the benefit of hindsight. The theory was developed by Nassim Nicholas Taleb, starting in 2001, to explain the disproportionate role of high-profile, hard-to-predict, and rare events; the underestimation of such events in scientific and financial reasoning; and the psychological biases that blind people to uncertainty.

Background

The expression "black swan" derives from a Latin phrase by the Roman poet Juvenal — a thing compared to a "rare bird in these lands, very much like a black swan." For centuries Europeans assumed all swans were white, an unquestioned belief overturned when Dutch explorers encountered black swans in western Australia in 1697. The phrase thus became a metaphor for a perceived impossibility that is later disproven.

Taleb's criteria

Taleb identifies a black swan event as having three attributes: it is an outlier, lying outside the realm of regular expectations because nothing in the past can convincingly point to its possibility; it carries an extreme impact; and in spite of its outlier status, human nature makes us concoct explanations for its occurrence after the fact, making it seem explainable and predictable. Examples Taleb cites include the rise of the Internet, the personal computer, the First World War, the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and the September 11 attacks. The 2008 financial crisis became the theory's best-known application after Taleb's 2007 book The Black Swan appeared shortly before it.

Mediocristan and Extremistan

Taleb contrasts "Mediocristan," domains governed by averages where no single event can dominate (such as human height), with "Extremistan," domains dominated by rare extremes (such as wealth or book sales), arguing that conventional statistics fail badly in the latter. His earlier book Fooled by Randomness (2001) introduced related ideas.

Criticism

Critics argue that the theory is difficult to falsify, that labeling events as unpredictable after they happen risks triviality, and that Taleb's advice on preparing for extremes is hard to operationalize. Nevertheless, the term "black swan" has entered common usage in finance, risk management, and journalism as a synonym for a devastating surprise.
