Sociology of scientific knowledge

Sociology of scientific knowledge (SSK)

The sociology of scientific knowledge (SSK) is a field within the sociology of science that investigates the social conditions and practices that shape the production of scientific knowledge. Where the older sociology of science studied scientists as a profession and institution, SSK turned its attention to the content of science itself, asking how social factors enter into the acceptance and rejection of scientific claims.

Intellectual origins

SSK drew on Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962), which portrayed scientific change as driven by paradigm shifts rather than steady accumulation, and on the philosophy of science of Feyerabend and the later Wittgenstein. Ethnomethodology and the sociology of knowledge of Mannheim and Merton provided further resources.

The strong programme

The Edinburgh "strong programme", associated with David Bloor, Barry Barnes, and Steven Shapin, proposed four tenets for explaining belief: causality, impartiality, symmetry, and reflexivity. Symmetry — the demand that true and false beliefs be explained by the same kinds of causes — was the most radical, implying that social factors help explain even beliefs we now regard as true.

Laboratory studies

A second strand, associated with Bruno Latour, Steve Woolgar (Laboratory Life, 1979), and Karin Knorr-Cetina, used ethnographic observation inside laboratories to show how facts are constructed through instruments, negotiations, and inscription devices. From this work grew actor–network theory.

Debates and criticism

SSK was attacked by scientists and philosophers during the "science wars" of the 1990s, notably in Alan Sokal's hoax and Paul Gross and Norman Levitt's Higher Superstition (1994), which accused SSK of relativism. Defenders replied that explaining the social shaping of knowledge does not deny an independent reality, and that symmetry is a methodological principle, not a metaphysical claim.
