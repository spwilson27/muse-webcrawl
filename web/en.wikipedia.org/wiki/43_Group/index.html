The 43 Group was a British anti-fascist group founded in London in 1946 by Jewish ex-servicemen who had returned from the Second World War to find Oswald Mosley's fascist movement attempting to revive. Named for the number of people at its founding meeting, the group physically disrupted fascist meetings, broke up street-corner speeches and fought Mosley's black-uniformed stewards, and is widely credited with driving organised fascism off London's streets by 1950.

Origins

The group was founded after Morris Beckman, a former merchant seaman and RAF radar operator, attended a meeting of the British League of Ex-Servicemen addressed by a fascist speaker who blamed Jews for the war. Of the servicemen who met in April 1946 at the Maccabi sports club in the West End — 43 people in the room — many were decorated veterans of units such as the RAF and the Royal Navy. They adopted military discipline, organising into squads, funding intelligence work on fascist organisations, and infiltrating Mosley's revived Union Movement, founded in 1948.

Tactics

The 43 Group's signature tactic was the massed "wedge" that stormed fascist platforms and broke up meetings, combined with sustained street fighting at pitches such as Ridley Road market in Dalston, where the teenage Vidal Sassoon — later a famous hairdresser — was among its members. The group also published a newspaper, On Guard. Its activities were denounced by some communal Jewish bodies, which preferred legal and political pressure, but historians generally judge that its direct action, alongside the Public Order Act restrictions, made open fascist organising untenable in the late 1940s.

Dissolution and legacy

Believing its work done after Mosley's poor showing in the 1950 general election, the Group voted to disband in 1950. Its methods were revived a decade later by the 62 Group against a new generation of far-right organisations. Beckman's memoir The 43 Group (1992) is the principal insider account of the organisation.
