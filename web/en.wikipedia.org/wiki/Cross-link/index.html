Cross-link

Cross-link
TypeChemical bond joining polymer chains

EffectIncreased rigidity, elasticity, thermal and solvent resistance; insolubility

In chemistry and biology, a cross-link is a bond or a short sequence of bonds that links one polymer chain to another. These links may be covalent or ionic, and the polymers may be synthetic (such as rubber and plastics) or natural (such as proteins and DNA). Cross-linking transforms individual chains into a three-dimensional network, dramatically changing a material's properties.

Formation and effects

Cross-links can form during polymerisation, by irradiation, by heat, or by the addition of cross-linking agents. In vulcanisation, invented by Charles Goodyear in 1839, natural rubber is heated with sulfur to create disulfide bridges between polyisoprene chains, converting sticky latex into elastic, durable rubber. Lightly cross-linked polymers are elastomers that swell but do not dissolve; heavily cross-linked polymers are hard thermosets such as Bakelite and epoxy resins.

In biology

  • Proteins: disulfide bonds stabilise tertiary structure; collagen's strength in skin, bone, and tendon derives from enzymatic cross-links.
  • DNA: cross-links between strands can arise from chemotherapy drugs such as cisplatin, blocking replication in cancer cells; defective repair of cross-links causes Fanconi anaemia.
  • Ageing: accumulation of sugar-derived cross-links (advanced glycation end-products) stiffens tissues over time.

Applications

Cross-linking is used in hydrogels for contact lenses and drug delivery, in polyethylene pipes (PEX), in hair perms (breaking and reforming disulfide bonds), and in fixing tissue samples with formaldehyde for microscopy.
