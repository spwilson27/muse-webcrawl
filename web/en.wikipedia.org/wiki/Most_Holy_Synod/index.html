The Most Holy Governing Synod (Russian: Святейший Правительствующий Синод) was the highest governing body of the Russian Orthodox Church between 1721 and 1917. It was established by Peter the Great, who abolished the patriarchate and replaced it with a collegial body of clergy supervised by the state.

The Synod was created as part of Peter's church reform, set out in the Spiritual Regulation of 1721, drafted largely by Archbishop Feofan Prokopovich. Its members were appointed by the emperor, and from 1722 its work was overseen by the Ober-Procurator, a lay official who acted as "the sovereign's eye" and came to exercise enormous influence over church affairs.

History

After the death of Patriarch Adrian in 1700, Peter left the patriarchal throne vacant for two decades, administering the church through a locum tenens. In 1721 he formally abolished the office, judging an independent patriarch a potential rival to imperial authority. The Synod initially consisted of a president, two vice-presidents and a small college of bishops, archimandrites and priests, all sworn to regard the emperor as the "supreme judge" of the body.

Under Peter's successors the Ober-Procurator's power grew steadily. In the nineteenth century, procurators such as Nicholas Protasov and, above all, Konstantin Pobedonostsev (in office 1880–1905) effectively directed the church's administration, education and publishing. Meanwhile the church's landed wealth had already been secularised by Catherine the Great in 1764, leaving clergy dependent on state salaries.

Abolition

Following the February Revolution of 1917, the Provisional Government dismissed Pobedonostsev's successor and convened the All-Russian Church Council (Sobor), which in November 1917 restored the patriarchate and elected Metropolitan Tikhon as Patriarch of Moscow. The Synod was accordingly dissolved, ending nearly two centuries of direct state administration of the Russian Church.
