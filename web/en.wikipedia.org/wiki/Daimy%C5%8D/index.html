Daimyo

Daimyo (大名, daimyō) were powerful Japanese magnates and feudal lords who, from the 10th century into the Meiji period of the 19th century, ruled large hereditary domains and maintained their own armies of samurai retainers. Subordinate to the shōgun and nominally to the emperor, the daimyo were among the most powerful ruling families in premodern Japan.

History

The term first described great landholding houses (the shugo daimyo arose from the military governors of the Muromachi period). During the Sengoku period of civil war in the 15th and 16th centuries, independent sengoku daimyo such as Oda Nobunaga, Takeda Shingen, and Uesugi Kenshin fought to dominate the country.

The Edo period

Under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1868), roughly 300 daimyo were ranked by the assessed rice yield (kokudaka) of their domains and classified as shinpan (relatives of the Tokugawa), fudai (hereditary vassals), or tozama (outside lords). The shogunate controlled them through the sankin-kōtai system of alternate attendance, which required each daimyo to reside part of the time in Edo and leave his family there as hostages. The daimyo class was abolished in 1871 with the replacement of the domains by prefectures, and former daimyo were merged into the new kazoku peerage.
