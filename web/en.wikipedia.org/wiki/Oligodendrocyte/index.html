Oligodendrocytes (from Greek, "cells with a few branches") are a type of neuroglia whose main function is to provide support and insulation to axons in the central nervous system (CNS) of vertebrates. They do this by creating the myelin sheath that wraps around axons, enabling rapid saltatory conduction of action potentials.

A single oligodendrocyte can extend its processes to dozens of axons, myelinating segments of up to about 50 different axons — in contrast to peripheral Schwann cells, each of which myelinates a single axon segment.

Development

Oligodendrocytes arise from oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), which migrate throughout the CNS during development and persist in the adult brain, where they can generate new oligodendrocytes for myelin repair. Differentiation is controlled by transcription factors including Olig2 and Sox10, and myelination is regulated by neuronal activity.

Function

Beyond insulation, oligodendrocytes provide metabolic support to axons, supplying energy substrates such as lactate through monocarboxylate transporters. The myelin sheath is a multilamellar membrane rich in lipids and proteins such as myelin basic protein (MBP) and proteolipid protein (PLP).

Clinical significance

Damage to oligodendrocytes and myelin underlies demyelinating diseases, most notably multiple sclerosis, in which autoimmune attack destroys CNS myelin. Leukodystrophies are inherited disorders of myelin formation, and oligodendrocyte injury also contributes to spinal cord injury pathology and perinatal white-matter injury.
