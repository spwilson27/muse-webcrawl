Total graph

In graph theory, the total graph of a graph G, denoted T(G), is a graph whose vertex set corresponds to the union of the vertices and edges of G. Two such elements are adjacent in T(G) if they are adjacent or incident in G: vertices adjacent in G stay adjacent, edges sharing an endpoint stay adjacent, and each edge is joined to its two endpoints.

Properties

The total graph thus contains G itself and the line graph L(G) as induced subgraphs, plus the incidence edges between them. A proper colouring of T(G) is exactly a total colouring of G — an assignment of colours to vertices and edges so that no adjacent or incident elements share a colour. This gives the context for the famous total colouring conjecture of Behzad and Vizing (1960s), which states that the total chromatic number is at most Δ + 2, where Δ is the maximum degree; the conjecture remains unproven in general, though confirmed for many classes of graphs.

Examples

The total graph of a path or cycle has a regular, highly symmetric structure, and total graphs of complete graphs are strongly regular in small cases. They are used to model problems in which both stations and links require resources, such as frequency assignment.
