Bride price

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bride price, also known as bride wealth or bride token, is money, property, or other form of wealth paid by a groom or his family to the family of the woman he is to marry. It may be paid in cash, livestock, goods, or a combination of these, and in some societies payment is accompanied by a period of bride service, in which the groom works for the bride's family.

Distinctions

Bride price is distinct from dowry, which is property transferred by the bride's family to the groom or to the couple, and from dower, property settled on the bride herself by the groom or his family. Anthropologists emphasise that bride price is not a "purchase" of a wife in the commercial sense: it typically legitimises the marriage, establishes an alliance between two kin groups, secures rights over the couple's children, and may compensate the bride's family for the loss of her labour. In many systems the payment must be returned, in whole or in part, if the marriage breaks down.

Distribution and history

The custom is widespread historically and today, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Asia including China (where it is known as pìnjīn), Thailand (sin sod), Papua New Guinea and Melanesia, and among various Indigenous peoples of the Americas. In cattle-keeping societies of Africa, payments in cattle — such as the lobola of southern Africa — remain central to marriage negotiations. In ancient Near Eastern law codes, including the Code of Hammurabi, the bride price (Akkadian terhatum) was a recognised legal institution, and it appears in the Hebrew Bible as the mohar.

Social and economic analysis

Economic and anthropological studies link bride price to women's status, agricultural systems and inheritance patterns. Jack Goody and others contrasted Eurasian dowry systems, associated with plough agriculture and women's inheritance, with African bridewealth systems associated with hoe agriculture and lineage control of marriage. High bride prices have been associated both with the value placed on women's reproductive and agricultural labour and, critically, with delayed marriage, indebtedness and disputes. In regions where bride prices have inflated rapidly, governments and activists have debated caps or regulation; in Uganda, the Supreme Court in 2015 upheld the legality of the custom while ruling that demanding a refund on divorce was unconstitutional.

Criticism and reform

Critics, including some women's organisations, argue that bride price can commodify women, trap wives in abusive marriages because families cannot repay the payment, and encourage families to marry daughters early. Defenders respond that, properly understood, the custom honours the bride, cements kinship bonds and gives women security, pointing out that in many communities women themselves support its retention. Reform efforts have therefore often focused on limiting excessive payments and on guaranteeing women's rights within customary marriage rather than abolition.
