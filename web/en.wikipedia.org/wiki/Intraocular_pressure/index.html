Intraocular pressure (IOP) is the fluid pressure inside the eye. Tonometry is the method eye care professionals use to determine it. IOP is an important aspect in the evaluation of patients at risk of glaucoma. Most tonometers are calibrated to measure pressure in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).

Goldmann applanation tonometry is the reference standard for measuring IOP.

Physiology

IOP is determined by the balance between production of aqueous humour by the ciliary body and its drainage, mainly through the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm, with a secondary uveoscleral pathway. The steady-state relationship is summarized by the Goldmann equation, IOP = (F/C) + Pv, where F is aqueous formation rate, C is outflow facility, and Pv is episcleral venous pressure. IOP also varies with time of day (typically highest in the early morning), posture, and systemic factors.

Measurement

Goldmann applanation tonometry flattens a standardized area of cornea and is influenced by corneal thickness and biomechanics; central corneal pachymetry is used in interpretation. Other methods include non-contact ("air puff") tonometry, Tono-Pen, Schiøtz indentation tonometry, rebound tonometry (iCare), and continuous contact-lens sensors.

Clinical significance

  • Ocular hypertension — IOP above 21 mmHg without optic nerve damage; a risk factor for conversion to glaucoma.
  • Glaucoma — most forms are associated with elevated or damaging IOP, though normal-tension glaucoma occurs within the statistical normal range; lowering IOP (drops such as prostaglandin analogues, laser trabeculoplasty, or surgery) is the only proven treatment.
  • Hypotony — abnormally low IOP (often <6.5 mmHg), may follow surgery, trauma or uveitis and can cause maculopathy and globe deformation.
