The Outback is a vast, remote and arid area of Australia. Comprising the deserts and semi-arid rangelands of the continental interior—far from the coastal cities where most Australians live—the term has no precise boundary but generally covers most of the Northern Territory, Western Australia, South Australia, western Queensland and north-western New South Wales. The Outback covers some 80 per cent of the continent's land area yet holds well under a tenth of its population.

History

Aboriginal peoples have inhabited the Outback for tens of thousands of years, managing the land with fire-stick farming and intricate knowledge of water sources. European explorers such as Burke and Wills (1860–61) perished attempting to cross the interior; successful crossings, the Overland Telegraph Line (1872) and the pastoral and mining industries opened the region to settlers at great cost to Indigenous communities. Camel trains—worked by Afghan cameleers—were for decades the main transport across the desert.

Life in the Outback

Enormous cattle and sheep stations, some larger than small countries, mining towns and Aboriginal communities are linked by unsealed roads and long-distance services. Distinctive institutions arose to serve the scattered population: the Royal Flying Doctor Service (founded 1928) provides medical care by air, and the School of the Air delivers lessons by radio and internet. Iconic features include Uluru (Ayers Rock), the MacDonnell Ranges, opal-mining Coober Pedy, and the Birdsville Track.

Ecology

Though arid, the Outback supports diverse ecosystems of spinifex grassland, mulga woodland and salt lakes, with wildlife such as red kangaroos, emus, wedge-tailed eagles and the dingo. Introduced species—feral camels, rabbits, cane toads and cats—pose major conservation challenges.
