Public school (United Kingdom)

A public school in England, Wales and Northern Ireland is a type of fee-charging private school, traditionally a single-sex boarding secondary school for children aged 13–18. The term "public" historically distinguishes these schools from private tutors and local (church or dame) schools: they were open to any member of the public who could pay the fees, drawing pupils from across the country rather than from a locality. The usage is the opposite of the American one, where a "public school" is a state-funded school open to all without fees.

History

Many public schools have medieval or early-modern origins as grammar schools or charitable foundations — Winchester College (1382), Eton College (1440) and St Paul's School (1509) among them. In the 19th century, reforms associated with headmasters such as Thomas Arnold of Rugby emphasised character, games and imperial service. The Clarendon Commission (1861–64) investigated the nine leading schools, and the Public Schools Act 1868 reformed the governance of seven of them (Eton, Harrow, Winchester, Rugby, Shrewsbury, Westminster and Charterhouse).

Characteristics

Public schools are typically characterised by:

  • fee-charging, independent (private) status, usually with charitable registration;
  • a strong boarding tradition organised in houses, though most now also take day pupils;
  • selective admission, commonly via the Common Entrance examination at 13+;
  • historic links to the universities of Oxford and Cambridge, the armed forces and the professions;
  • extensive extracurricular provision, especially team sports, and — traditionally — their own customs, slang and sporting fixtures (e.g. the Eton v Harrow cricket match at Lord's).

Criticism and debate

Public schools educate roughly 6–7% of British schoolchildren yet supply a disproportionate share of entrants to elite universities and professions, making them a persistent focus of debate about social mobility and privilege. Their charitable status and tax treatment are periodically contested, and from January 2025 VAT was applied to private school fees in the UK. Defenders point to bursary provision, academic results and partnerships with state schools.
