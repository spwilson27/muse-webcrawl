Dynamic programming

Dynamic programming is both a mathematical optimization method and an algorithmic paradigm. The method was developed by Richard Bellman in the 1950s and has found applications in numerous fields, from aerospace engineering to economics. In both contexts it refers to simplifying a complicated problem by breaking it down into simpler sub-problems in a recursive manner.

Overview

For a problem to be suitable for dynamic programming, it must have two properties. First, it must exhibit optimal substructure: an optimal solution can be constructed from optimal solutions of its sub-problems. Second, the sub-problems must overlap, so that solving each one once and storing its answer saves repeated work. The stored answers are typically kept in a table (memoization in a top-down implementation, or iterative tabulation in a bottom-up one).

Bellman coined the term partly, by his own account, to give the work an impressive-sounding name: "programming" referred to planning and scheduling, and "dynamic" was chosen because it sounded strong and could not give the research a negative connotation.

Examples

  • Computing Fibonacci numbers efficiently by caching previous terms.
  • The Bellman–Ford algorithm for shortest paths, and the Viterbi algorithm for hidden Markov models.
  • The knapsack problem and the matrix chain multiplication problem.
  • Sequence alignment in bioinformatics (Needleman–Wunsch, Smith–Waterman).
  • The Bellman equation in control theory and reinforcement learning.

Relation to other methods

Dynamic programming contrasts with divide and conquer, whose sub-problems are disjoint, and with greedy algorithms, which make locally optimal choices without combining sub-solutions.
