Ides of March

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ides of March (Latin: Idus Martiae) is the day on the Roman calendar marked as the Idus, roughly the midpoint of a month, of Martius (March). In the Roman system, the Ides fell on the 15th day of March, May, July and October, and on the 13th of the other months. The Ides of March is best known as the date on which Julius Caesar was assassinated in 44 BC.

The assassination

On 15 March 44 BC, a group of some sixty conspirators, the self-styled Liberators led by Marcus Junius Brutus and Gaius Cassius Longinus, stabbed Caesar to death at a meeting of the Senate held in the Curia of Pompey in Rome. According to Plutarch, a seer had warned Caesar that harm would befall him no later than the Ides of March; on the day, Caesar is said to have joked "The Ides of March are come," to which the seer replied "Aye, Caesar; but not gone."

Cultural resonance

William Shakespeare's play Julius Caesar (1599) immortalised the soothsayer's warning "Beware the ides of March," and the date has since carried an omen-laden reputation in popular culture. In Rome itself, the day was a deadline for settling debts and was sacred to Jupiter; the Anna Perenna festival was also celebrated on the Ides of March.

Other events

Later events on 15 March include the 1917 abdication of Tsar Nicholas II (in the Julian calendar then used in Russia) and numerous entries in modern almanacs; the date is also marked by the Latin phrase's use in the names of works such as Thornton Wilder's 1948 epistolary novel The Ides of March.
