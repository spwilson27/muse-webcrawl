Zanamivir

Zanamivir is an antiviral medication used to treat and prevent influenza caused by influenza A and influenza B viruses. It is a neuraminidase inhibitor and was the first in its class to be commercially developed. Marketed by GlaxoSmithKline under the trade name Relenza, it is administered by inhalation through a device called a Diskhaler because it has poor oral bioavailability.

Medical uses

Zanamivir is used for the treatment of influenza in people who have been symptomatic for no more than two days, and for the prevention of influenza in adults and children five years of age and older. Treatment shortens the duration of symptoms by roughly one to one and a half days when started early. Because it is inhaled directly into the respiratory tract, it reaches high concentrations where influenza viruses replicate.

Mechanism of action

Zanamivir blocks the viral enzyme neuraminidase, which influenza viruses use to release newly formed virus particles from infected cells and to penetrate mucus. By inhibiting neuraminidase, the drug causes viral particles to aggregate at the cell surface, reducing spread of the infection within the respiratory tract.

History

Zanamivir was discovered by scientists at the Australian biotechnology company Biota in the late 1980s, working with the Victorian College of Pharmacy and Monash University, using structure-based drug design guided by the crystal structure of influenza neuraminidase. It was developed and licensed to Glaxo (now GlaxoSmithKline) and approved for medical use in 1999.

Side effects

Inhaled zanamivir is generally well tolerated. The most common adverse effects include headache, dizziness, and irritation of the nose and throat. It is not recommended in people with underlying respiratory conditions such as asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, because inhalation can provoke bronchospasm.

Resistance

Resistance to zanamivir has remained uncommon compared with resistance to the older adamantane drugs. Mutations in the neuraminidase active site can reduce binding, though such mutants often have reduced viral fitness. During the 2009 swine flu pandemic, governments stockpiled zanamivir alongside oseltamivir (Tamiflu).
