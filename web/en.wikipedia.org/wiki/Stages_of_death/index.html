Stages of death

The stages of death are the series of physical and chemical changes that occur in a body after death. Knowledge of these changes is central to forensic pathology and taphonomy, where they are used to estimate the post-mortem interval (PMI) — the time elapsed since death.

Early changes

Pallor mortis is the paleness that develops within minutes as circulation ceases. Algor mortis is the cooling of the body towards ambient temperature. Livor mortis (lividity) is the gravitational settling of blood, producing purple-red discoloration of the dependent skin, beginning within about 30 minutes to two hours. Rigor mortis is the stiffening of muscles caused by depletion of ATP; it typically begins within a few hours, is complete by about 6–12 hours, and passes off over the following day or so, though timing varies greatly with temperature and other conditions.

Decomposition

Autolysis is the self-digestion of tissues by their own enzymes. Putrefaction, driven by bacteria (especially gut flora), produces greenish discoloration of the abdomen, bloating from gas, marbling of the skin, and eventually skin slippage. Later stages include advanced decay and skeletonization. Environmental factors — temperature, humidity, burial, scavengers, and insects — strongly affect the rate; forensic entomology uses insect succession as a clock.

Other outcomes

In dry environments a body may mummify; in wet, anaerobic conditions it may form adipocere, a waxy substance that preserves tissues. Where decomposition is prevented, bodies may survive for centuries, as with bog bodies.
