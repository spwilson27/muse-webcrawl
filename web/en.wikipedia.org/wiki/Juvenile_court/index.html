Juvenile court

A juvenile court (also youth court or children's court) is a court with special authority to pass judgments for crimes that are committed by children or adolescents who have not attained the age of majority. In most modern legal systems, children who commit a crime are treated differently from adults who have committed the same crime, reflecting the doctrine that minors have diminished culpability and greater capacity for rehabilitation.

History

The first juvenile court was established in Cook County, Illinois, in 1899, under the philosophy of parens patriae — the state acting as parent of the child. The model spread rapidly through the United States and internationally. Early juvenile courts used informal procedures, avoided the language of criminal law (speaking of "delinquency" rather than guilt, and "disposition" rather than sentence), and emphasised treatment over punishment.

Jurisdiction and procedure

Juvenile courts typically handle three kinds of cases: delinquency (acts that would be crimes if committed by an adult), status offences (such as truancy or running away, unlawful only because of the child's age), and dependency or neglect cases involving abused or neglected children. Serious offences may be transferred ("waived") to adult criminal court.

In the United States, the Supreme Court decisions In re Gault (1967) and In re Winship (1970) extended due process rights — notice, counsel, confrontation of witnesses, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt — to juveniles, while McKeiver v. Pennsylvania (1971) declined to require jury trials. Later cases, including Roper v. Simmons (2005), Graham v. Florida (2010) and Miller v. Alabama (2012), restricted capital punishment and mandatory life-without-parole sentences for juveniles.

Around the world

The minimum age of criminal responsibility varies widely, from 7 or lower in some jurisdictions to 14, 16 or 18 in others. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and the Beijing Rules (1985) set international standards encouraging diversion from formal proceedings and detention only as a last resort. Some countries, such as Scotland with its children's hearings system, use lay panels instead of courts for most cases.
