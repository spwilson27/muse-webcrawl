The RBMK (Russian: реактор большой мощности канальный, "high-power channel-type reactor") is a class of graphite-moderated nuclear power reactors designed and built in the Soviet Union. The design is notorious as the type involved in the Chernobyl disaster of 1986.

Design

Unlike most Western power reactors, the RBMK has no single steel pressure vessel. Instead, hundreds of vertical pressure tubes pass through a huge graphite moderator stack; light water flows through the tubes, boils, and drives turbines directly in a single loop. Control rods are inserted into separate channels from above. The design allows refuelling while running and was economical to build with Soviet industrial capacity, but it combined several dangerous traits: a positive void coefficient (boiling increases reactivity), graphite rod tips that briefly boosted reactivity when rods were inserted from a fully withdrawn position, and no substantial containment building.

Chernobyl and aftermath

On 26 April 1986, during a safety test, Chernobyl unit 4 — an RBMK-1000 — suffered a runaway power excursion, steam explosions and a graphite fire that released large quantities of radioactive material. After the disaster, surviving RBMKs were retrofitted: higher fuel enrichment to reduce the void coefficient, redesigned control rods without the graphite-tip effect, faster scram systems and stricter operating rules. Seventeen RBMKs were completed; the Lithuanian Ignalina units (the larger RBMK-1500) were shut down as a condition of EU accession, while several Russian units continue to operate with life extensions.
