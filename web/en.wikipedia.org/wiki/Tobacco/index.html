Tobacco

Tobacco is the common name of several plants in the genus Nicotiana of the nightshade family, and the term for the agricultural product of their cured leaves, consumed chiefly by smoking in cigarettes, cigars and pipes, and also as snuff, chewing tobacco and hookah (shisha). The principal commercial species is Nicotiana tabacum, cultivated worldwide (China is the largest producer).

Indigenous peoples of the Americas used tobacco for millennia in ritual and medicine; after 1492 it spread worldwide and became a pillar of colonial economies (worked by enslaved people in the Americas) and, in the 20th century, of a global industry built on the cigarette — and then of public-health campaigns against it, as evidence mounted that smoking causes cancer, heart and lung disease. Nicotine, its main psychoactive alkaloid, is highly addictive. The WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (in force 2005) coordinates global regulation.
