Trigraph

A trigraph is a group of three letters used together to represent a single sound (phoneme), or a combination of sounds that does not correspond neatly to the written letters. It extends the idea of a digraph, which uses two letters.

Examples

English examples include sch in words borrowed from German (e.g. schadenfreude), tch in watch, igh in night, and eau (pronounced /oʊ/) in French borrowings such as beau. Other languages make heavier use of trigraphs: German sch and zsch (technically a tetragraph), Hungarian dzs for /dʒ/, Italian gli and sci, and Irish trigraphs arising from its caol/leathan vowel-harmony spelling rules, such as eai and iai.

In some contexts "trigraph" also denotes any sequence of three letters, as in cryptography or the three-letter abbreviations (trigrams/trigraphs) used in computing and in the trigram systems of languages such as Chinese romanization.
