Flight of the Wild Geese

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Flight of the Wild Geese was the departure of an Irish Jacobite army under the command of Patrick Sarsfield from Ireland to France, as agreed in the Treaty of Limerick on 3 October 1691, following the end of the Williamite War in Ireland. More broadly, the term "Wild Geese" (Na Géanna Fiáine) came to denote the Irish soldiers who left to serve in continental European armies, and by extension the generations of Irish military emigrants of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

The Treaty of Limerick and the departure

After the Williamite victory at Aughrim and the second siege of Limerick, Sarsfield negotiated terms under which Jacobite soldiers who wished could sail to France to enter the service of the exiled James II and Louis XIV. In the winter of 1691, roughly 14,000 soldiers, with several thousand women and children, embarked from Cork and Limerick. The military articles of the treaty were honored in the departure, though the civil articles guaranteeing Catholics their property and worship were soon violated by the Irish Parliament, ushering in the Penal Laws.

The Wild Geese in European service

In France the emigrants formed the Irish Brigade of the French army, which fought with distinction at Fontenoy (1745) and in France's wars for a century; its regiments — Clare's, Dillon's, Berwick's — retained Irish identity and recruited in Ireland despite legal prohibitions. Irish soldiers also served Spain (in regiments such as Hibernia, Irlanda, and Ultonia), Austria, Russia, Poland, and Sweden. Prominent Wild Geese include Peter Lacy, who became a Russian field marshal; his son Franz Moritz von Lacy, an Austrian field marshal; and numerous officers of Irish descent throughout Europe. The tradition fed into later Irish service in the armies of Britain, the United States, and elsewhere, and the term "Wild Geese" remains a resonant symbol of the Irish military diaspora.
