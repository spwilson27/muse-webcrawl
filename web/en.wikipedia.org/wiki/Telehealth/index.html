Telehealth is the distribution of health-related services and information via electronic information and telecommunication technologies. It allows long-distance patient and clinician contact, care, advice, reminders, education, intervention, monitoring, and remote admissions. Telehealth is sometimes used more narrowly to refer to remote clinical services, while the related term telemedicine is often restricted to remote clinical care specifically; in practice the two terms are used almost interchangeably.

Examples include two-way live videoconferencing between patients and providers, the transmission of still images and medical data ("store-and-forward"), remote patient monitoring using wearable sensors, and consumer mobile health (mHealth) applications. Telehealth can bridge the gap in access for patients in rural or underserved areas and reduce the need for travel.

History

Early forms of telehealth date to the early 20th century, when radio was used to give medical advice to ships at sea. In the 1960s and 1970s, organizations such as NASA developed telemetry to monitor astronauts' physiological functions, and demonstration projects linked urban hospitals with rural clinics and airports. The growth of the public internet in the 1990s and of broadband and smartphones in the 2000s made consumer-facing video visits practical.

Use of telehealth expanded dramatically during the COVID-19 pandemic beginning in 2020, as regulators temporarily relaxed licensing, privacy, and reimbursement restrictions and patients and clinicians sought to avoid in-person contact. Many of those flexibilities were later made permanent in various jurisdictions, and virtual visits became a routine part of primary and specialty care.

Modalities

Synchronous telehealth

Synchronous services occur in real time, most commonly live audio-video visits between a patient and a clinician, but also telephone consultations and real-time chat. They are used for primary care, mental health (telepsychiatry), follow-up visits, and urgent care triage.

Asynchronous telehealth

Asynchronous or store-and-forward telehealth involves collecting clinical data — photographs, imaging studies, laboratory results, structured questionnaires — and transmitting them to a specialist for later review. It is widely used in dermatology (teledermatology), radiology (teleradiology), pathology, and ophthalmology.

Remote patient monitoring

Remote patient monitoring (RPM) uses connected devices such as blood-pressure cuffs, glucometers, pulse oximeters, scales, and wearable activity trackers to collect physiological data from patients at home and transmit it to care teams, supporting chronic disease management for conditions such as diabetes, heart failure, and hypertension.

Benefits and limitations

Proponents cite improved access to care, reduced travel time and cost, better management of chronic conditions, and greater convenience. Critics note limitations including the inability to perform hands-on physical examinations, the digital divide affecting patients without broadband or devices, variable regulatory frameworks across jurisdictions, privacy and security risks, and questions about clinical quality and appropriate reimbursement.

Regulation

Telehealth is regulated through medical licensure (providers generally must be licensed where the patient is located), privacy law such as HIPAA in the United States, prescribing rules for controlled substances, and payer reimbursement policies. Rules differ substantially between countries and between public and private insurers.
