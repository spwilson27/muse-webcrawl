Germanic strong verb

In the Germanic languages, a strong verb is a verb that forms its past tense by means of a change in the vowel of its stem — a process known as ablaut or vowel gradation — rather than by adding a dental suffix (such as English -ed), which is the method of the Germanic weak verb. Examples in modern English include sing–sang–sung, drive–drove–driven, and begin–began–begun.

Origins

The strong verb system descends from the aspectual ablaut system of Proto-Indo-European, in which the root vowel alternated between e-grade, o-grade, and zero-grade. In Proto-Germanic these alternations were reorganised into a system of four principal parts: the infinitive (present stem), the past singular, the past plural, and the past participle.

The seven classes

Proto-Germanic strong verbs are conventionally divided into seven classes according to their ablaut pattern:

Development in the daughter languages

In the modern Germanic languages the system has been variously preserved, simplified, and eroded. German retains a large number of strong verbs, though many verbs have drifted from strong to weak conjugation over the centuries. English has lost many strong verbs since the Old English period — Old English had several hundred, while modern English retains roughly two hundred irregular verbs of strong origin, many of them among the most frequent words in the language. Dutch and the Scandinavian languages show similar patterns of partial preservation and levelling of the paradigm.
