Grand Coulee

The Grand Coulee is a dry canyon about 97 km (60 mi) long in central Washington.

The Grand Coulee is an ancient riverbed in the U.S. state of Washington. This National Natural Landmark stretches for about 60 miles (97 km) southwest from Grand Coulee Dam to Soap Lake, carved by Ice Age floods through the Columbia Basin. It is one of the most striking features of the Channeled Scablands.

Geology

A coulee is a type of deep ravine characteristic of the region. The Grand Coulee consists of two segments — the Upper Grand Coulee, which once carried the Columbia River, and the Lower Grand Coulee, cut by floodwaters pouring southward. During the last glacial period, the Cordilleran Ice Sheet blocked the Columbia's normal course, diverting the river down the coulee, and catastrophic outbursts from glacial Lake Missoula scoured the channel further. When the ice retreated, the river returned to its modern course, leaving the coulee dry.

Features within the coulee include Dry Falls — a 3.5-mile-wide (5.6 km), 400-foot-high (120 m) extinct waterfall, among the largest known waterfalls ever to have existed — and Steamboat Rock, a massive basalt butte.

Modern use

In the 20th century the Upper Grand Coulee was incorporated into the Columbia Basin Project: water pumped up from the reservoir behind Grand Coulee Dam fills Banks Lake, built within the upper coulee, and feeds a vast irrigation network in central Washington. The coulee also contains several lakes, including Banks Lake, Park Lake, Blue Lake and Lake Lenore, and state parks at Steamboat Rock and Sun Lakes.
