Mathematical morphology

Mathematical morphology (MM) is a theory and technique for the analysis and processing of geometrical structures, based on set theory, lattice theory, topology, and random functions. It is most commonly applied to digital images, but can also be employed on graphs, surface meshes, solids, and other spatial structures.

History

Mathematical morphology was developed in the 1960s by Georges Matheron and Jean Serra at the École des Mines de Paris, France, originally for analyzing geological and mineralogical data from binary images. Serra's 1982 book Image Analysis and Mathematical Morphology consolidated the field, which was later extended from binary images to grayscale images and complete lattices.

Basic operators

The basic morphological operators are defined in terms of a small shape known as a structuring element, which is positioned at all possible locations in the image and compared with the local neighborhood:

  • Erosion: shrinks objects; the output is 1 where the structuring element fits entirely within the object.
  • Dilation: expands objects; the output is 1 where the structuring element hits the object.
  • Opening: erosion followed by dilation — removes small objects and smooths contours.
  • Closing: dilation followed by erosion — fills small holes and gaps.

For binary images these operators are defined set-theoretically using Minkowski addition and subtraction. Derived operations include morphological gradients, skeletonization, watershed segmentation, hit-or-miss transforms, and granulometry.

Applications

Mathematical morphology is used in image processing for noise removal, shape analysis, texture analysis, object detection, document analysis, and medical imaging.
