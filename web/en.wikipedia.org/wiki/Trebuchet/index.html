Trebuchet

A trebuchet (French: trébuchet) is a type of catapult that uses a long arm and a counterweight to hurl projectiles. It was a powerful siege engine used in medieval warfare, most notably in Europe and China, until the increasing use of gunpowder artillery made it obsolete in the 15th and 16th centuries.

Design

The counterweight trebuchet consists of a long beam balanced on a fulcrum. A heavy counterweight is attached to the short end of the beam, while the long end carries a sling holding the projectile. When released, the falling counterweight swings the long arm upward; the sling whips around and releases the projectile at high speed.

An earlier form, the traction trebuchet, was powered by people pulling on ropes attached to the short end of the arm. Traction trebuchets appeared in China by the 4th century BCE and spread westward; the much more powerful counterweight type emerged in the Mediterranean region around the 12th century CE.

Performance

Large counterweight trebuchets could throw stones of 100–160 kg over distances of 150–300 metres. The famous Warwolf, built for Edward I of England during the siege of Stirling Castle in 1304, is often described as the largest trebuchet ever constructed.

Use

Trebuchets were used to batter fortifications, throw incendiary devices, and occasionally to hurl diseased carcasses or corpses into besieged cities as an early form of biological warfare. See also catapult and siege.
