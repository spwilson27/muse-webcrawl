Battle of Cynoscephalae

The Battle of Cynoscephalae was an encounter battle fought in 197 BC between the Roman Republic, led by Titus Quinctius Flamininus, and the Antigonid kingdom of Macedon under Philip V, for a pair of hills (Cynoscephalae, "Dogs' Heads") in Thessaly. It was the decisive battle of the Second Macedonian War and one of the landmark engagements of military history, demonstrating the superiority of the flexible Roman legion over the Macedonian phalanx on broken ground.

Background

Rome declared war on Philip V in 200 BC after his aggression against Rome's Greek allies. Flamininus, taking command in 198 BC, forced Philip out of his defensive position on the Aoös river and invaded Thessaly in 197 BC, seeking a decisive engagement. The two armies, marching on opposite sides of the Cynoscephalae ridge in fog, blundered into one another in June 197 BC.

The battle

A skirmish between advance guards on the ridge escalated as both commanders fed in reinforcements. Philip, urged on by early success, charged downhill with the right half of his phalanx in wedge formation and drove the Roman left back in disorder. But the Macedonian left, still deploying over the crest in column and in half-formed order, was shattered by a Roman attack spearheaded by elephants. The turning point came when an unnamed Roman military tribune, on his own initiative, took twenty maniples from the victorious Roman right and attacked Philip's advancing phalanx from behind. Caught between two forces and unable to turn its long pikes about, the phalanx collapsed; the Macedonians raised their sarissas upright as a signal of surrender, but the Romans, not understanding the gesture, continued the slaughter until Flamininus called a halt.

Aftermath

Philip sued for peace. The settlement stripped Macedon of its fleet, its Greek possessions and its influence outside its borders, and imposed an indemnity. At the Isthmian Games of 196 BC Flamininus proclaimed the "freedom of the Greeks". The battle's lesson — that the phalanx, invincible on flat ground, was helpless once its formation was broken or attacked from the flank and rear — was confirmed a generation later at Pydna (168 BC).
