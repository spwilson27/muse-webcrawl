A hymn is a type of song — usually religious and partially coincident with devotional song — specifically written for the purpose of adoration or prayer, and typically addressed to a deity or deities, or to a prominent figure or personification. The word derives from the Greek hymnos (ὕμνος), "a song of praise". A writer of hymns is known as a hymnist, and the singing or composition of hymns is called hymnody; collections of hymns are known as hymnals.

Origins

Ancient hymns include the Egyptian Great Hymn to the Aten (c. 14th century BC), the Vedic hymns of the Rigveda, the Zoroastrian Gathas, the Homeric Hymns of Greek antiquity, and the biblical Psalms. Early Christian hymnody drew on psalms and scriptural canticles; writers such as Ambrose of Milan and Ephrem the Syrian composed hymns that shaped Latin and Syriac traditions respectively.

Western Christian hymnody

Medieval Latin hymns were sung as plainsong in the Divine Office. The Reformation transformed congregational singing: Martin Luther wrote hymns such as "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God", while the Calvinist tradition long restricted singing to metrical psalms. In 18th-century England, Isaac Watts freed hymn texts from strict psalm paraphrase, and Charles Wesley wrote thousands of hymns for the Methodist movement. The 19th century added gospel songs and writers such as Fanny Crosby; notable individual hymns include "Amazing Grace" (1779), "Abide with Me" and "Rock of Ages".

Form

Hymns are typically strophic — several stanzas sung to the same melody (the hymn tune) — and written in regular metres such as common metre (8.6.8.6) or long metre, allowing texts and tunes to be interchanged. Hymn tunes often carry names of their own, such as "Dix", "Hyfrydol" or "Slane".

Other traditions

Analogous sacred song exists across religions: Hindu bhajans and kirtan, Sikh shabad, Buddhist chant, Islamic nasheed and Jewish piyyutim all serve functions comparable to the hymn.
