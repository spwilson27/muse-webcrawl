A diner is a type of restaurant found predominantly in the United States, particularly in the Northeast and Midwest, characterized by a long counter with stools, booth seating, an extensive menu of American comfort food, and often long or round-the-clock hours. Traditional diners are housed in prefabricated, elongated buildings resembling railway dining cars, frequently clad in stainless steel.

History

The diner evolved from the horse-drawn lunch wagons of the 1870s, such as Walter Scott's wagon in Providence, Rhode Island. Manufacturers including the Worcester Lunch Car Company and the Jerry O'Mahony company later produced purpose-built diner cars that were shipped to sites and set on foundations. Mid-20th-century diners adopted streamlined, porcelain-enamel and stainless-steel styling.

Cuisine and culture

Diner menus typically span breakfast served all day (eggs, pancakes, bacon), burgers, sandwiches, meatloaf, milkshakes, and pie, with coffee refills a hallmark. Many diners in the New York metropolitan area are famously operated by Greek-American families. The diner occupies a prominent place in American popular culture, from Edward Hopper's painting Nighthawks to films and television.
