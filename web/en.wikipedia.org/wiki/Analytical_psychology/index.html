Analytical psychology (German: analytische Psychologie, sometimes called Jungian analysis) is a school of psychology founded by the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung. It proposes that the human psyche comprises the ego, the personal unconscious, and a deeper collective unconscious shared by all humanity, whose contents — the archetypes — manifest in dreams, myths, and symbols. The central aim of psychological development, in Jung's view, is individuation: the integration of conscious and unconscious aspects of the personality into a unified Self.

Carl Jung, founder of analytical psychology.

History

Jung was Freud's designated successor in the psychoanalytic movement, but their theoretical differences — over the nature of libido, the unconscious, and religion — led to a break in 1912–1913. Jung renamed his approach analytical psychology to distinguish it from psychoanalysis. After a period of intense self-exploration recorded in The Red Book, he developed his mature system in works such as Psychological Types (1921), which introduced the attitudes of extraversion and introversion and the four psychological functions (thinking, feeling, sensation, intuition).

Key concepts

The archetypes — including the persona, the shadow, the anima and animus, and the Self — are innate structuring patterns of the collective unconscious. Emotionally charged clusters in the personal unconscious are called complexes. Jung also developed ideas of synchronicity ("meaningful coincidence") and studied alchemy, Gnosticism, and Eastern religion as symbolic expressions of individuation.

Practice and influence

Jungian analysis uses dream interpretation, active imagination, and word association. Analytical psychology influenced the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator, aspects of psychotherapy, literary criticism, and the study of mythology (via Joseph Campbell). Critics have charged the theory with being difficult to test empirically.
