Diabetic foot ulcer

A diabetic foot ulcer is an open sore or wound, most often on the bottom of the foot, that occurs in people with diabetes mellitus and fails to heal normally. Estimates suggest that up to a third of people with diabetes will develop a foot ulcer during their lifetime, and foot ulcers precede the great majority of diabetes-related lower-limb amputations.

Causes

The main drivers are peripheral neuropathy, which destroys protective sensation so that minor trauma goes unnoticed; peripheral arterial disease, which starves tissue of blood; and foot deformity and repetitive pressure. Infection and poor glycaemic control compound the problem. Ulcers are classified by depth and severity using systems such as the Wagner grades and the University of Texas system.

Treatment

Standard care combines sharp debridement of dead tissue, moist wound dressings, treatment of infection with antibiotics, revascularisation where blood flow is inadequate, and — crucially — offloading of pressure, using total contact casts or removable walkers. Blood sugar control and patient education reduce recurrence, which remains high: a substantial proportion of healed ulcers recur within a year. Advanced therapies include negative-pressure wound therapy, skin substitutes and growth factors. Multidisciplinary diabetic foot clinics have been shown to reduce amputation rates.
