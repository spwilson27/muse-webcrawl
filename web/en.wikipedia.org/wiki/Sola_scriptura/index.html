Sola scriptura (Latin for "by scripture alone") is a theological doctrine held by some Protestant Christian denominations that the Bible is the sole infallible source of authority for Christian faith and practice. It is one of the five solae that summarise the Reformers' basic differences with the Roman Catholic Church of their day.

Doctrine

The doctrine holds that Scripture is self-authenticating, clear (perspicuous) in its essential teachings, and self-interpreting; all traditions, creeds, and church teachings are subject to correction by it. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) states that "the whole counsel of God concerning all things necessary for his own glory, man's salvation, faith and life, is either expressly set down in Scripture, or by good and necessary consequence may be deduced from Scripture."

History and criticism

Martin Luther's stand at the Diet of Worms (1521) — that he could be convinced only by Scripture and plain reason — became the doctrine's emblematic moment. Catholic and Orthodox critics respond that Scripture itself emerged from the Church's tradition, that the canon requires an authoritative Church to define it, and that the doctrine in practice means solo scriptura — private interpretation — producing denominational fragmentation. Methodists instead affirm the Wesleyan Quadrilateral, in which Scripture is primary but interpreted through tradition, reason, and experience (prima scriptura).
