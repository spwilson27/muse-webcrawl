Social cycle theory

Social cycle theory is a type of theory in sociology and political science that holds that historical events and stages of society repeat themselves in cycles, as opposed to theories of linear, unidirectional progress. Cyclical views of history are ancient, appearing in Greek and Roman thought (notably Polybius's cycle of constitutions, anacyclosis), in Chinese dynastic-cycle historiography, and in the writings of Ibn Khaldun. Modern versions were developed by thinkers such as Giambattista Vico, Oswald Spengler, Arnold J. Toynbee, Vilfredo Pareto, Pitirim Sorokin and, in a spiritual form, Sri Aurobindo and P. R. Sarkar.

Classical theories

In Book VI of his Histories, Polybius described anacyclosis: monarchy degenerates into tyranny, which is replaced by aristocracy, which decays into oligarchy, then democracy, then mob rule (ochlocracy), from which a monarch again emerges. The 14th-century North African historian Ibn Khaldun, in the Muqaddimah, explained the rise and fall of dynasties through the waxing and waning of asabiyyah — group solidarity — over roughly three to four generations, as desert conquerors become softened by urban luxury.

Modern theories

Giambattista Vico's New Science (1725) proposed ages of gods, heroes and men recurring in nations. Oswald Spengler's The Decline of the West (1918–1922) treated civilisations as organisms passing through seasons of growth and decay, while Arnold Toynbee's A Study of History described civilisations rising through "challenge and response" and breaking down when creative minorities fail. Pitirim Sorokin classified cultures as cycling among ideational, idealistic and sensate mentalities. Vilfredo Pareto described history as a "graveyard of aristocracies" in his theory of the circulation of elites. Nikolai Kondratieff proposed long economic waves, an idea taken up in various cyclical models of politics and generations, such as Strauss and Howe's generational theory.

Criticism

Cyclical theories are criticised as difficult to falsify, prone to selective reading of evidence, and (in organic versions) to a misleading analogy between societies and organisms. Most contemporary historians and sociologists treat cycles as heuristic metaphors rather than laws, though cyclical patterns remain influential in economics (business and long-wave cycles) and in popular writing about history.
