Chisholm Trail

Route: Texas to Kansas railheads (Abilene, Newton, Wichita, Dodge City)

Active years: 1867–1884 (main period)

Named for: Jesse Chisholm, trader

The Chisholm Trail was a major route used in the late 19th century to drive cattle from ranches in Texas to railheads in Kansas, where the animals were shipped east to market. Between roughly 1867 and 1884, millions of longhorn cattle were driven north along the trail and its branches.

History

After the American Civil War, Texas had vast herds of longhorns but no direct rail access, while beef fetched high prices in the East. In 1867 cattle buyer Joseph G. McCoy established a shipping point at Abilene, Kansas, on the Kansas Pacific Railway, and promoted a route north from Texas. The trail took its name from Jesse Chisholm, a trader of Scots-Cherokee descent who had marked a wagon road through Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma) for his freight trade; drovers adopted his trace for the central portion of the drive.

The drive

A typical herd of 2,000–3,000 head moved about 10–15 miles a day, with a crew of cowboys under a trail boss, a chuck wagon and a remuda of spare horses. Hazards included river crossings, storms that could trigger stampedes, and conflicts with farmers over crop damage and Texas fever, a tick-borne disease that led Kansas towns to enact quarantine lines pushing the railheads progressively westward to Newton, Wichita, Ellsworth, Caldwell and Dodge City.

Decline and legacy

The trail era ended in the mid-1880s as railroads reached Texas, barbed wire fenced the open range, and quarantine laws closed Kansas to Texas cattle. The Chisholm Trail became an enduring symbol of the American West in song, film and folklore, and is commemorated by museums and markers along its route in Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas.
