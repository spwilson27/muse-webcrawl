Lafayette Square is a seven-acre (30,000 m2) public park located within President's Park in Washington, D.C., directly north of the White House on H Street, bounded by Jackson Place on the west, Madison Place on the east, and Pennsylvania Avenue NW on the south. The square and the surrounding structures were designated a National Historic Landmark District in 1970.

History

What is now Lafayette Square was part of the original plan for the federal city by Pierre Charles L'Enfant, forming part of the grounds of the President's House. In 1804 President Thomas Jefferson had Pennsylvania Avenue cut through the grounds, separating the square from the White House grounds. The square was used variously as a racetrack, a graveyard, a zoo, a slave market, an encampment for soldiers during the Civil War, and a place for political protests.

It was named in 1824 for the Marquis de Lafayette, the French hero of the American Revolutionary War, during his celebrated tour of the United States. The landscaping plan was drawn up by Andrew Jackson Downing in 1851 in the picturesque style.

Statues

At the centre of the square stands Clark Mills's equestrian statue of President Andrew Jackson, cast in 1852 from bronze of captured cannon — the first equestrian statue made in the United States. At the four corners stand statues of foreign Revolutionary War heroes: the Marquis de Lafayette and Comte de Rochambeau of France, Tadeusz Kościuszko of Poland, and Baron von Steuben of Prussia.

Surrounding buildings

The square is ringed by notable buildings, including the Decatur House, Blair House (the President's official guest house), the Hay–Adams Hotel, St. John's Episcopal Church — the "Church of the Presidents" — the Eisenhower Executive Office Building and the Treasury Building to the east and west, and the Renwick Gallery. The townhouses on Jackson Place and Madison Place were home to many cabinet members and diplomats in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Protests

Because of its position facing the White House, Lafayette Square has long been a site of demonstrations, vigils and protests, from suffragists in the 1910s to peace vigils maintained for decades and mass protests in the 21st century.
