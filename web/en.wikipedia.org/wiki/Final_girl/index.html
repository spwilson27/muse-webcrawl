Final girl

The final girl is a trope in horror films, particularly slasher films: she is the last girl or woman alive to confront the killer, ostensibly the one left standing at the end. The term was coined by film scholar Carol J. Clover in her 1992 book Men, Women, and Chain Saws: Gender in the Modern Horror Film, which analysed the gender dynamics of slasher cinema.

Characteristics

In Clover's formulation, the final girl typically:

  • Is more resourceful, watchful, and mechanically competent than her peers, and often the first to sense the danger.
  • Abstains from the sex, drinking, and drugs that doom her friends — a moral dimension critics link to the genre's puritanical streak.
  • Shares traits coded as masculine — a unisex name (Laurie, Sidney, Ripley), mechanical skills, and the ability to seize a weapon and fight back in the final confrontation.
  • Serves as the audience's surrogate: the camera increasingly adopts her point of view, and cross-gender identification by the predominantly young male audience was central to Clover's argument.

Examples

Famous final girls include Jess Bradford in Black Christmas (1974), Sally Hardesty in The Texas Chain Saw Massacre (1974), Laurie Strode in Halloween (1978), Ellen Ripley in Alien (1979), Nancy Thompson in A Nightmare on Elm Street (1984), and Sidney Prescott in Scream (1996).

Evolution and criticism

Later films have played with, subverted, or celebrated the trope: Scream made its heroine self-aware of the "rules", while The Cabin in the Woods (2011) deconstructed the archetype outright. Feminist readings differ: some critics see the final girl as an empowering figure of female survival and agency; others see a fantasy in which a woman survives only by adopting masculine traits and being punished for femininity. The trope has since spread to video games, literature, and true-crime commentary.
