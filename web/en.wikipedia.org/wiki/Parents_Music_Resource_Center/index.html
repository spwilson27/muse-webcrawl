The Parents Music Resource Center (PMRC) was an American committee formed in 1985 with the stated mission of raising parental awareness of music containing explicit references to sex, violence or substance abuse, and of securing industry labelling of such recordings. Its co-founders included Tipper Gore, wife of then-Senator Al Gore, and Susan Baker, wife of Treasury Secretary James Baker; because several founders were spouses of powerful Washington figures, critics dubbed them the "Washington wives". The PMRC's campaign produced the famous "Filthy Fifteen" list of objectionable songs, televised Senate hearings in September 1985, and ultimately the Recording Industry Association of America's black-and-white "Parental Advisory" sticker.

The Filthy Fifteen

The PMRC's list of the fifteen most offensive songs included Prince's "Darling Nikki", Madonna's "Dress You Up", Sheena Easton's "Sugar Walls", AC/DC's "Let Me Put My Love into You", Judas Priest's "Eat Me Alive", Mötley Crüe's "Bastard", Twisted Sister's "We're Not Gonna Take It", W.A.S.P.'s "(Animal) F**k Like a Beast", Black Sabbath's "Trashed", Cyndi Lauper's "She Bop", the Mary Jane Girls' "In My House", Vanity's "Strap On 'Robbie Baby'", Def Leppard's "High 'n' Dry", Venom's "Possessed" and Mercyful Fate's "Into the Coven" — categorised by transgression: sex, violence, drugs/alcohol, or the occult.

The 1985 hearings

On 19 September 1985 the Senate Commerce, Science and Transportation Committee heard testimony from the PMRC and from musicians Frank Zappa, Dee Snider of Twisted Sister and John Denver — all three of whom opposed the labelling proposals as censorship in disguise. Zappa memorably called the scheme "an ill-conceived piece of nonsense" that treated drowning victims by "greedy legislation", and later sampled the hearings in his music. Rather than legislation, the RIAA agreed in November 1985 to place voluntary warning labels on selected recordings; the standardised Parental Advisory label was adopted in 1990.

Legacy

The label became an enduring feature of recorded music — and, paradoxically, a marketing asset that could boost sales to young buyers. The episode remains a touchstone in debates over art, censorship and moral panic; musicians from Ice-T to Rage Against the Machine answered the PMRC in song, and Dee Snider's testimony is widely anthologised as a model of citizen advocacy.
