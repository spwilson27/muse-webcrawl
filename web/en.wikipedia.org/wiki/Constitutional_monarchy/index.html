Constitutional monarchy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A constitutional monarchy, also historically referred to as a limited monarchy, is a form of monarchy in which the sovereign's powers are defined and limited by law — a constitution or by statute and convention — and usually exercised in accordance with ministerial advice. It differs from absolute monarchy, in which the monarch's authority is not legally constrained. Most modern constitutional monarchies operate as parliamentary democracies in which the monarch is head of state and an elected government, headed by a prime minister, holds executive power.

History

England's Glorious Revolution (1688) and Bill of Rights (1689) are the classic founding moment, curtailing royal power and asserting parliamentary supremacy; through the nineteenth century, monarchies across Europe accepted constitutions (some granted octroyée, from above, as in Prussia and Meiji Japan), and twentieth-century transitions preserved thrones that adapted — Spain's restoration of 1975 being a notable case where the king helped anchor democratisation.

Present-day examples

Constitutional monarchies include the United Kingdom and the other Commonwealth realms, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Japan, Thailand and Jordan among others, ranging from near-purely ceremonial (Sweden, Japan) to systems where monarchs retain meaningful reserve or executive roles (Liechtenstein, Morocco, Jordan). Debates in several realms concern republicanism, cost and democratic symbolism; retention usually rests on stability, continuity and the monarch's position "above politics".
