Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect

Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect
Field: Optics, quantum optics, astronomy
Discovered: 1956
Named for: Robert Hanbury Brown, Richard Q. Twiss

The Hanbury Brown and Twiss (HBT) effect is the correlation observed between intensities of light detected at two separate detectors, rather than between the amplitudes of the light waves as in conventional interferometry. Demonstrated by Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Q. Twiss in 1956, it underlies intensity interferometry.

Explanation

For thermal (chaotic) light, photons exhibit bunching: they are more likely to be detected together than a Poisson distribution predicts, because of the bosonic nature of photons and interference between indistinguishable paths. The effect initially puzzled physicists and helped launch the field of quantum optics and the study of photon statistics.

Applications

Hanbury Brown and Twiss used the effect to measure the angular diameter of Sirius with an intensity interferometer at Narrabri, Australia. Generalised HBT correlations are now used in particle and nuclear physics to measure the size and lifetime of particle-emitting sources.
