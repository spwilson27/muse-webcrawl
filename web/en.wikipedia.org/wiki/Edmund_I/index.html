Edmund I (c. 920/921 – 26 May 946), sometimes given the epithet "the Magnificent" in later tradition, was King of the English from 939 until his death. The elder son of Edward the Elder by his third wife Eadgifu, he succeeded his half-brother Æthelstan and spent much of his reign recovering control of northern England from Norse kings based at York.

Reign

Edmund had fought beside Æthelstan at the Battle of Brunanburh in 937. On his accession, Olaf Guthfrithson, the Norse king of Dublin, seized York and the Five Boroughs of the Danelaw. Edmund campaigned north; in 942 he recovered the Five Boroughs — an achievement celebrated in a poem in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle — and in 944 York itself was taken and its Norse kings expelled. In 945 he ravaged Strathclyde and handed it to Malcolm I of Scotland, in return for an alliance.

Law and church

Edmund issued several law codes, including legislation on feuds and blood-vengeance, theft, and the obligations of lords, and sought to strengthen royal control through his network of thegns and reeves. He was a notable patron of the monastic reform movement, appointing Dunstan as Abbot of Glastonbury, from which the reform of the English church would grow.

Death

On 26 May 946, at Pucklechurch in Gloucestershire, Edmund was killed — according to the Chronicle and later tradition, stabbed by an outlaw named Leofa whom he tried to seize at a feast on the feast day of St Augustine. He was buried at Glastonbury Abbey. As his sons Eadwig and Edgar were still children, he was succeeded by his younger brother Eadred.
