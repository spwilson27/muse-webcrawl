Web archiving

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Browsing an archived page capture.

Web archiving is the process of collecting portions of the World Wide Web to ensure the information is preserved in an archive, such as a data storage facility, for future researchers, historians, and the public. Because web content is ephemeral — pages are changed, moved, or deleted, a phenomenon known as link rot — archivists use web crawlers to capture snapshots of pages as they existed at particular moments in time.

History

Web archiving began in 1996 with the founding of the Internet Archive by Brewster Kahle; its Wayback Machine, opened to the public in 2001, has archived hundreds of billions of web pages. National libraries soon followed under legal deposit laws: the National Library of Australia's Pandora archive (1996), the UK Web Archive, the Bibliothèque nationale de France, and the Library of Congress web archives. The International Internet Preservation Consortium (IIPC), founded in 2003, coordinates standards and tools among these institutions.

Techniques

Most web archiving uses automated crawlers such as Heritrix, which follow links from seed URLs and store the results in the WARC (Web ARChive) file format, an ISO standard since 2009. Curators may archive broadly (whole national domains), selectively (selected sites), or by event (elections, disasters, sporting events). Capturing dynamic, database-driven, and social media content remains difficult, as does respecting robots.txt exclusions and copyright.

Access and uses

Users access archives through replay tools such as the Wayback Machine, which render archived pages with their original links and assets rewired to the archive. Web archives are used in journalism, fact-checking, litigation, academic research, and digital preservation, and they also preserve otherwise-lost digital culture, from GeoCities pages to government websites removed after changes of administration.
