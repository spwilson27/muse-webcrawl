The Don Pacifico affair was a diplomatic episode that occurred in 1850, centred on the claims of David Pacifico, a Gibraltar-born Jewish merchant and former Portuguese consul living in Athens. When Pacifico's house was ransacked by an anti-Semitic mob at Easter 1847 — allegedly with police standing by — he demanded compensation from the Greek government, invoking his status as a British subject. The affair became a celebrated episode of gunboat diplomacy under Foreign Secretary Lord Palmerston.

Background

Pacifico's house had been attacked after the Greek government banned the customary burning of an effigy of Judas, in deference to a visiting member of the Rothschild family; the crowd instead turned on Pacifico's home. His compensation claims, vastly inflated and joined with other British claims (including that of the historian George Finlay), were refused by Greece. France and Russia, Greece's co-guarantors, opposed the British demands.

Blockade of Piraeus

In January 1850 Palmerston ordered the Royal Navy's Mediterranean Fleet, under Admiral William Parker, to blockade the port of Piraeus and seize Greek shipping until the claims were settled. The blockade strained relations with France, briefly causing the French ambassador in London to be withdrawn. After mediation, Greece agreed to pay compensation, and Pacifico ultimately received a settlement far smaller than his original claim.

The Civis Romanus sum speech

The affair provoked a storm in Parliament, where the government was censured in the House of Lords. In the Commons, Palmerston defended his conduct in a famous five-hour speech on 25 June 1850, declaring that a British subject, wherever he might be, should be protected by the British state — "as the Roman, in days of old, held himself free from indignity, when he could say Civis Romanus sum ('I am a Roman citizen'), so also a British subject, in whatever land he may be, shall feel confident that the watchful eye and the strong arm of England, will protect him against injustice and wrong." The government won the vote, and the phrase became a byword for the assertive defence of British interests abroad.
