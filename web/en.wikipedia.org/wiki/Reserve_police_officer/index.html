Reserve police officer

A reserve police officer is a volunteer or part-time law enforcement officer who supplements a police agency's full-time staff. Reserve officers are common in the United States, where thousands of departments maintain reserve or auxiliary units; similar roles include the Special Constabulary in the United Kingdom and volunteer constables in other Commonwealth countries.

Authority and training

The powers of reserve officers vary widely by jurisdiction. In many US states, reserves complete a state-certified academy — sometimes the same training as full-time recruits — and, while on duty and in uniform, hold full police powers of arrest and carry firearms. In other places their authority is limited to assisting regular officers, directing traffic or staffing events. California classifies reserves into levels according to training and the degree of supervision required.

Duties

Typical duties include patrol (often riding with a full-time officer), traffic control, crowd management at public events, prisoner transport, search and rescue, and disaster response. Some reserves are retired officers maintaining certification; others are community volunteers or people considering a policing career. Agencies usually require a minimum number of duty hours per month to maintain the appointment.

Criticism and oversight

Reserve programmes have occasionally drawn scrutiny, for instance when wealthy donors received reserve badges with little training, or when reserve officers were involved in fatal shootings — the 2015 killing of Eric Harris by a Tulsa County reserve deputy brought national attention to training standards. Reforms generally push for reserves to meet the same certification and in-service training requirements as full-time officers before exercising independent authority.
