Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm

The Cooley–Tukey algorithm, named after J. W. Cooley and John Tukey, is the most common fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm. It re-expresses the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of a composite size N = N1N2 as smaller DFTs of sizes N1 and N2, recursively, reducing the computation from O(N2) operations to O(N log N).

Radix-2 case

The best-known form is the radix-2 decimation-in-time algorithm, which splits a DFT of even size into two half-size DFTs of the even-indexed and odd-indexed samples, combined using complex roots of unity called twiddle factors. The recursive data flow is often drawn as a "butterfly" diagram. When N is a power of two, repeated halving yields the classic O(N log N) procedure with in-place implementations and bit-reversed input ordering.

History

Cooley and Tukey published the algorithm in 1965, motivated by a request to analyze seismic data for detecting nuclear tests. The underlying idea had been used earlier, notably by Carl Friedrich Gauss around 1805 for computing asteroid orbits, and in various 20th-century formulations, but the 1965 paper, together with the spread of digital computers, made the FFT a cornerstone of digital signal processing, spectral analysis, fast convolution, and partial differential equation solvers.

Variants

Generalizations include mixed-radix, split-radix, and prime-factor algorithms, as well as decimation-in-frequency forms. Modern libraries such as FFTW combine Cooley–Tukey decompositions with other algorithms and machine-optimized code.
