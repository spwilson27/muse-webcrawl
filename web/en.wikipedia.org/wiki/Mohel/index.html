Mohel

A mohel (Hebrew: מוהל, plural mohalim) is a Jew trained in the practice of brit milah, the covenant of circumcision, who performs the ritual circumcision of Jewish male infants. The ceremony is traditionally carried out on the eighth day of a boy's life, as commanded in the Torah (Genesis 17 and Leviticus 12:3).

Training and role

A mohel is trained both in the surgical technique of circumcision and in the relevant Jewish law (halakha). Traditionally training was passed by apprenticeship; today, certification programmes exist in Israel and the diaspora, and in many jurisdictions mohalim must also meet secular medical and licensing requirements. Orthodox practice requires a mohel to be a Sabbath-observant, observant Jew; Reform and Reconstructionist movements also certify women (mohelot) and may permit physicians to officiate.

The ceremony

At the brit milah the mohel performs the circumcision (milah) and the unfolding of the membrane (periah), recites blessings, and the child is given his Hebrew name. Traditionally a further step, metzitzah (suction of blood from the wound), was performed orally (metzitzah b'peh); this practice has become controversial because of infection risks, and many mohalim use a sterile tube or gauze instead.

History

The office is ancient: the Bible depicts Abraham circumcising his household, and Zipporah circumcising her son. Throughout Jewish history the mohel was a central communal functionary, and registers kept by mohalim (pinkas mohalim) are valuable sources for Jewish genealogy.
