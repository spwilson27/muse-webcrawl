Transmission coefficient

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The transmission coefficient is a quantity used in physics and engineering to describe how much of a wave passes through a surface or travels through a medium. It is defined differently in different fields, but in general terms it is the ratio of the amplitude (or power) of the transmitted wave to that of the incident wave. Its counterpart is the reflection coefficient, describing the part of the wave reflected at the boundary.

Waves at an interface

When a wave strikes the boundary between two media of different impedance, part is reflected and part transmitted. For a wave at normal incidence the amplitude transmission and reflection coefficients depend only on the impedances Z1 and Z2 of the two media:

t = 2Z2 / (Z2 + Z1),    r = (Z2 − Z1) / (Z2 + Z1)

The fraction of power transmitted is T = |t|2 (Z1/Z2), and energy conservation gives R + T = 1 for a lossless interface, where R = |r|2. Definitions of this kind are used in optics (where the coefficients are given by the Fresnel equations), acoustics, seismology and transmission-line theory.

Optics and electromagnetism

In optics the transmission coefficient (transmittance) of a material sample is the ratio of transmitted to incident radiant power, and depends on wavelength, angle of incidence and absorption in the material; it underlies spectrophotometry and the Beer–Lambert law. For thin films and layered media, multiple reflections produce interference, and the overall transmission coefficient is computed with transfer-matrix methods.

Quantum mechanics

In quantum mechanics the transmission coefficient of a particle encountering a potential barrier is the ratio of the probability current of the transmitted wave to that of the incident wave — the probability that the particle passes through the barrier. For a classically forbidden barrier (whose height exceeds the particle's energy) the coefficient is non-zero because of quantum tunnelling, decaying roughly exponentially with barrier height and width; this calculation underlies the theory of alpha decay and the scanning tunnelling microscope.

Other fields

In telecommunications and chemistry the term also appears in specialised senses, e.g. the fraction of light passing through a cuvette, or, in older usage, the probability that a colliding molecule's energy is transferred in reaction dynamics.
