Helena of Adiabene

Helena of Adiabene (died c. 50–56 AD) was a queen of Adiabene, a kingdom in northern Mesopotamia centred on Arbela (modern Erbil), then a client state in the Parthian sphere. She was the wife (and sister) of King Monobaz I and the mother of Kings Izates II and Monobaz II. Helena is famous for her conversion to Judaism, along with her sons, in the first century AD—an event recorded by the historian Flavius Josephus—and for her generosity and building works in Jerusalem.

Life

According to Josephus, Helena and her son Izates adopted Judaism in stages, advised by the Jewish merchant Ananias and later by a stricter teacher, Eleazar, who insisted Izates undergo circumcision. Helena spent much of her later life in Jerusalem, where she built palaces for herself and her family in the City of David area, south of the Temple Mount. During a famine under Emperor Claudius (c. 45–48 AD) she imported grain from Egypt and dried figs from Cyprus at great expense for distribution to the starving population—an act praised in both Josephus and the Talmud. She also presented notable gifts to the Temple, including a golden lamp and a golden tablet inscribed with the passage about the suspected adulteress (sotah). After her death in Adiabene, her remains and those of Izates were brought to Jerusalem and buried in the royal tombs she had built north of the city—the "Tombs of the Kings," where a sarcophagus inscribed "Sarah the Queen" (in Hebrew and Syriac), found in the 19th century and now in the Louvre, is generally attributed to her.

Legacy

Helena is remembered in rabbinic literature for her piety and charity; the Mishnah notes that she took a Nazirite vow. Streets in modern Jerusalem bear her name (Queen Helene Street), and her tomb complex remains an archaeological landmark north of the Old City.
