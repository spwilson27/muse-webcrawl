Architecture of Japan

The architecture of Japan is characterized by wooden structures, elevated slightly off the ground, with tiled or thatched roofs, sliding doors (fusuma) and screens (shōji) in place of fixed walls, and an aesthetic of lightness, modular proportion, and harmony with nature. It developed through the adaptation of Chinese and Korean models to Japanese conditions — earthquakes, typhoons, and a humid climate — and through the indigenous traditions of Shinto shrine building and the tea house.

Prehistoric and early periods

Pit dwellings of the Jōmon period gave way to raised-floor storehouses of the Yayoi period, ancestors of the shinmei-zukuri style preserved at Ise Grand Shrine, which is ritually rebuilt every twenty years. With the arrival of Buddhism in the sixth century came continental temple architecture; Hōryū-ji (early seventh century) contains some of the world's oldest surviving wooden buildings, including its five-storey pagoda.

Medieval and early modern periods

The Heian period adapted Chinese layouts to aristocratic palaces in the shinden-zukuri style. Zen Buddhism in the Kamakura and Muromachi periods inspired restrained temple halls and rock gardens, while the tea ceremony of Sen no Rikyū shaped the rustic sukiya aesthetic. Monumental castles such as Himeji (early 1600s) crowned the Azuchi–Momoyama age, and Edo-period towns filled with timber machiya merchant houses. The detached Katsura Imperial Villa is often cited as the pinnacle of the classical tradition.

Modern period

After the Meiji Restoration (1868), Western styles and brick-and-stone construction arrived with foreign advisers and Japanese architects trained abroad. In the twentieth century, figures such as Kenzō Tange fused modernism with tradition in works like the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum and the Yoyogi National Gymnasium. Post-war Metabolism proposed megastructures and capsule buildings, and contemporary architects — Tadao Ando, Toyo Ito, SANAA, and Shigeru Ban among them — have won international acclaim, including multiple Pritzker Prizes, while seismic engineering has made Japanese high-rise construction among the most earthquake-resistant in the world.
