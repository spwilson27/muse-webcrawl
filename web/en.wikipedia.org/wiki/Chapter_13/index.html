Chapter 13

Chapter 13 of the United States Bankruptcy Code, titled "Adjustment of Debts of an Individual with Regular Income", allows individuals with regular income to develop a plan to repay all or part of their debts over three to five years, under the supervision of a bankruptcy court and a trustee. Unlike Chapter 7, which liquidates non-exempt assets, Chapter 13 generally lets the debtor keep property — in particular a home — while catching up on missed payments.

How it works

The debtor files a petition and proposes a repayment plan. Filing triggers an automatic stay that halts most collection actions, foreclosures and repossessions; a special co-debtor stay also protects co-signers on consumer debts. The debtor makes a single monthly payment to the standing trustee, who distributes it to creditors according to the plan. Secured arrears (such as mortgage arrears) can be cured over the plan term, priority debts such as recent taxes and support must be paid in full, and unsecured creditors must receive at least as much as they would in a Chapter 7 liquidation (the "best interests of creditors" test). Debtors below the applicable median income generally have a three-year plan; those above it must commit five years of projected disposable income.

Discharge

On completing the plan's payments, the debtor receives a discharge of remaining eligible debts, which is somewhat broader than the Chapter 7 discharge (a legacy of the former "super discharge"). Debts that survive include certain taxes, domestic support obligations, most student loans and debts incurred by fraud.

Advantages and drawbacks

Chapter 13 can stop foreclosure and let a debtor cure a mortgage default, protect co-debtors, and pay debts that cannot be discharged. Its main drawback is duration: many plans fail before completion, in which case the case may be dismissed or converted to Chapter 7.
