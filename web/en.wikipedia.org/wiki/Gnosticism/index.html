Gnosticism

Gnosticism (from Ancient Greek gnōstikós, "having knowledge") is a collection of religious and philosophical movements that flourished in the first centuries AD around the Mediterranean world, within and alongside early Christianity, Judaism and Hellenistic philosophy. Gnostic systems characteristically teach that the material world was created by an imperfect or ignorant being — the demiurge — and that human beings carry a divine spark trapped in matter, which can be liberated through gnosis, esoteric knowledge of one's true, divine origin imparted by a redeemer figure.

Origins and main groups

Scholars debate whether Gnosticism began within Judaism, Christianity or a broader Hellenistic milieu; the term itself is a modern umbrella, and some historians (such as Michael Williams and Karen King) have questioned whether so diverse a set of groups forms a single category. Major currents included the Sethians, whose texts reinterpret Genesis (the serpent as liberator, the biblical God as demiurge), the Valentinians — followers of Valentinus (c. 100–160), whose elaborate myth of the aeons and the fall of Sophia made his school the most influential "Christian Gnostic" movement — the followers of Basilides in Alexandria, and Marcion of Sinope, who distinguished the God of the Old Testament from the God of Jesus. Mandaeism, still practised in Iraq and Iran, is the only Gnostic religion to survive from antiquity.

Beliefs

Common themes include: a transcendent, unknowable supreme God (the Monad or "the Depth"); emanations (aeons) forming the divine fullness (Pleroma); a rupture — often the fall of Sophia, Wisdom — through which the demiurge (named Ialdabaoth in Sethian texts) fashioned the material cosmos in ignorance; the imprisonment of divine light in human bodies; and salvation by knowledge of the self's heavenly origin, assisted by a saviour who descends from the Pleroma. Ethical consequences varied widely, from strict asceticism (matter being evil) to, in the polemics of opponents, libertinism.

Suppression and rediscovery

Church Fathers such as Irenaeus (in Against Heresies, c. 180) and Epiphanius catalogued and attacked Gnostic teachings, and by the 5th century most groups had disappeared from the Roman world, though related movements resurfaced in medieval dualist heresies such as the Paulicians, Bogomils and Cathars. For centuries Gnosticism was known almost entirely through its enemies. That changed in 1945 with the discovery near Nag Hammadi in Egypt of a jar containing thirteen Coptic codices — the Nag Hammadi library — including the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Philip and the Apocryphon of John. Their publication transformed scholarship and popular imagination alike, influencing modern theology, depth psychology (Carl Jung was an early enthusiast) and literature.
