Sicilia was the first province acquired by the Roman Republic, established in 241 BC after Rome's victory over Carthage in the First Punic War. It comprised most of the island of Sicily, initially excluding the kingdom of Syracuse in the southeast, which remained a Roman ally until its capture in 212 BC during the Second Punic War, after which the whole island became a single province.

Under Roman rule Sicily became one of the empire's principal sources of grain, earning a reputation as Rome's breadbasket. The island was administered by a governor based at Syracuse, assisted by quaestors at Syracuse and Lilybaeum. Sicilian cities held varied legal statuses and paid a grain tithe (the decuma) under the system known as the lex Hieronica, inherited from Hiero II of Syracuse.

History

The province's administration was notoriously abused by the praetor Gaius Verres (73–71 BC), whose prosecution by Cicero — the Verrine Orations — is a major source for provincial government. Sicily also saw two major slave revolts, the First (135–132 BC) and Second Servile Wars (104–100 BC), fueled by the vast slave-worked estates (latifundia) that produced grain and livestock.

Augustus reorganized Sicily as a senatorial province, founded colonies of veterans, and granted Latin or Roman status to many towns. In late antiquity the island remained an important grain supplier to Rome and later Constantinople, before passing successively to the Vandals (468), Odoacer, the Ostrogoths, and finally the Byzantine Empire in 535 under Justinian I.
