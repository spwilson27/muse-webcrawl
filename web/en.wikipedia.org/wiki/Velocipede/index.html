Velocipede

A velocipede (Latin for "fast foot") is a human-powered land vehicle with one or more wheels. The most common type of velocipede today is the bicycle. The term was mainly used to describe predecessors of the modern bicycle in the 19th century, including various two- and three-wheeled machines.

A 19th-century boneshaker velocipede with pedals on the front wheel.

History

The term "velocipede" was coined by Frenchman Nicéphore Niépce in 1818 to describe his improved version of the draisine, a wooden running machine invented by Karl von Drais in 1817. The draisine had no pedals; riders pushed themselves along with their feet.

In the 1860s, French mechanic Pierre Michaux and his son Ernest attached pedals directly to the front wheel of a draisine, creating the pedal-driven velocipede. The Michaux company began mass-producing the machines in 1868. Because of their heavy iron frames and wooden wheels with iron tyres, and the jolting ride they gave on cobbled streets, these machines became known in English as "boneshakers".

Development

The velocipede evolved rapidly. The "penny-farthing" or ordinary bicycle of the 1870s and 1880s enlarged the front wheel enormously to increase speed, since the pedals drove the wheel directly. The safety bicycle of the 1880s, with equal-sized wheels and chain drive to the rear wheel, superseded the earlier designs and is the ancestor of the modern bicycle.

Three-wheeled velocipedes were also popular, especially with women and older riders, because they were stable and did not require the rider to balance.

Legacy

The velocipede craze of the late 1860s spread across France, Britain, and the United States, prompting the construction of indoor riding schools and the first bicycle races. The word survives in modern French, where vélo is the everyday word for a bicycle.
