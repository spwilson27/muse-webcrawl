The Natural History Museum in London is a museum that exhibits a vast range of specimens from various segments of natural history. It is one of three major museums on Exhibition Road in South Kensington (alongside the Science Museum and the Victoria and Albert Museum) and is home to some 80 million specimens within five main collections: botany, entomology, mineralogy, palaeontology, and zoology. The museum is a world-renowned centre of research in taxonomy, identification, and conservation.

History and building

The museum's nucleus was the collection of Sir Hans Sloane, acquired by the British Museum in 1753. Under superintendent Richard Owen, the natural history collections were moved to a new purpose-built home in South Kensington designed by Alfred Waterhouse, which opened in 1881. The building, an exuberant Romanesque "cathedral to nature", is clad in terracotta decorated with reliefs of living and extinct species, expressly to resist London's soot. The museum became institutionally independent of the British Museum in 1963 and formally took its present name in 1992.

Highlights

Famous exhibits include the Hintze Hall, dominated since 2017 by the skeleton of a blue whale ("Hope"), succeeding the much-loved cast of a Diplodocus ("Dippy", installed 1905 and later toured around Britain); the Earth Galleries with their escalator through a model planet; the Darwin Centre housing research collections; and specimens of the dodo, the great auk, and Archaeopteryx. Admission to the permanent galleries is free.
