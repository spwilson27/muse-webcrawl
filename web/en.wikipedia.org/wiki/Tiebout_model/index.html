Tiebout model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Tiebout model, also known as Tiebout sorting or the Tiebout hypothesis, is a model in public economics proposed by Charles Tiebout in his 1956 article "A Pure Theory of Local Expenditures". It argues that, under certain conditions, competition among many local jurisdictions allows the efficient provision of local public goods: residents "vote with their feet", moving to the community whose package of taxes and public services best matches their preferences.

Assumptions

Tiebout's original model rests on strong assumptions, including: a large number of communities offering different tax-service packages; fully mobile residents who can move at no cost; perfect knowledge of each community's revenues and expenditures; no inter-jurisdictional spillovers; and communities whose existing residents can exclude newcomers (for example through the cost of housing) so that each jurisdiction achieves an optimal size.

Implications and criticism

The model provided a market-like answer to the free-rider problem in Samuelson's theory of public goods, and it underlies arguments for fiscal decentralisation and intergovernmental competition. Critics note that moving is costly, information is imperfect, jobs tie people to places, and spillovers are common; sorting by income in the model can also reinforce segregation and inequality between rich and poor municipalities. Empirical research finds evidence of Tiebout-style sorting, particularly around school quality, which capitalises into house prices.
