Ellis Island is a federally owned island in New York Harbor, situated within the U.S. states of New Jersey and New York. From January 1, 1892, to November 12, 1954, it was the busiest immigrant inspection and processing station in the United States. Nearly 12 million immigrants arriving in the Port of New York and New Jersey were processed there under federal law; today, the descendants of those immigrants are estimated to account for a large fraction of the American population, and the island's name has become a byword for the nation's immigrant heritage.

History

The island was a site of fishing and oyster harvesting for the Mohegan and other Lenape peoples, and passed through Dutch and English colonial hands before Samuel Ellis purchased it in the 1770s — giving it its name. The federal government acquired it in 1808 and fortified it as Fort Gibson. When the government federalised immigration control, Ellis Island replaced the state-run Castle Garden depot at the Battery. The first station, opened in 1892, burned down in 1897; the fireproof Main Building that replaced it — in French Renaissance Revival style — opened on December 17, 1900.

The processing experience

First- and second-class passengers were inspected aboard ship and usually passed quickly into the city. Steerage passengers — the great majority — were ferried to Ellis Island, where they underwent medical and legal inspections in the vast Registry Room (the "Great Hall"). Doctors scanned crowds for signs of illness, chalking letters on the coats of those needing closer examination — "E" for eye problems such as trachoma, "H" for heart, "L" for lameness. Legal inspectors verified each arrival against the ship's manifest with a list of questions; roughly two percent of arrivals were excluded and deported, mainly as likely public charges or sufferers of contagious disease. The peak year was 1907, when about 1 million immigrants were processed and a record 11,747 arrived on a single day, April 17.

Decline and afterlife

Restrictive quota laws of 1921 and 1924 ended mass processing; thereafter Ellis Island served mainly as a detention and deportation centre, including for enemy aliens during both world wars. It closed in 1954. Added to the Statue of Liberty National Monument in 1965, the island was restored through a major fundraising campaign and reopened in 1990 as the Ellis Island National Museum of Immigration. A 1998 Supreme Court decision in New Jersey v. New York held that the landfill portions of the island — most of it — lie within New Jersey, while the original natural island remains in New York.
