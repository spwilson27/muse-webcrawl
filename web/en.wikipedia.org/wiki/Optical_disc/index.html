Optical disc

An optical disc is a flat, usually circular disc that encodes binary data in the form of pits and lands on a special material layered upon its flat surface. The data are read by a laser in an optical disc drive, which detects the changes in reflected light as the disc spins. Optical discs are a form of removable, non-volatile storage.

Each generation increased capacity principally by shortening the laser wavelength, allowing smaller pits and tighter track spacing. The first widely distributed optical format was the LaserDisc (1978), an analogue video format.

Recordable and rewritable media

Write-once formats (CD-R, DVD-R, BD-R) use an organic dye or inorganic layer permanently altered by the writing laser; rewritable formats (CD-RW, DVD-RW, BD-RE) use a phase-change alloy whose reflectivity can be switched repeatedly.

Decline and legacy

Streaming media, downloads and flash storage displaced optical discs for most consumer uses in the 2010s, and many computers no longer ship with optical drives. The discs remain in use for game consoles, archival storage and regions with limited broadband, and are valued for their longevity and resistance to some forms of degradation, though dye-based recordable discs can suffer "disc rot".
