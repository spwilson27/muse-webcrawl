Chicago school of economics

[Image: the University of Chicago campus in Hyde Park]

The Chicago school of economics is a tradition associated with the University of Chicago, characterised by faith in free markets, price theory as the master tool of analysis, scepticism of government intervention, and close attention to empirical testing. It rose to prominence in the mid-20th century.

People and ideas

Its first generation (Frank Knight, Jacob Viner) gave way to a second centred on Milton Friedman — Monetarism, the permanent-income hypothesis, floating exchange rates — and George Stigler (industrial organisation, regulation theory), alongside Gary Becker's application of economics to crime, families and discrimination, Ronald Coase's work on transaction costs and property rights, and Robert Lucas's rational-expectations revolution in Macroeconomics. Chicago economists reshaped antitrust (toward consumer-welfare standards), deregulation and central banking, and influenced the market reforms applied in Chile after 1973 by the "Chicago Boys" — a lasting source of controversy. Critics, including behavioural and post-2008 writers, argue the school underplayed market failure, finance and inequality.
