Control theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A feedback loop block diagram: setpoint, controller, process, sensor]

Control theory is a field of control engineering and applied mathematics that deals with the control of dynamical systems in engineered processes and machines. The objective is to develop a model and use feedback to influence a system to achieve a desired state, despite disturbances and uncertainty.

Its devices are feedback loops: a sensor measures the output, a controller (classically a PID controller — proportional, integral, derivative) compares it with a target, and actuators correct the difference. The thermostat and Watt's centrifugal governor are everyday ancestors; the field formalised with Nyquist and Bode's frequency-domain methods (1930s–40s) for telephone amplifiers, then flourished in aerospace.

Branches

Classical control handles single-input single-output systems with transfer functions and stability criteria (Routh–Hurwitz, root locus). Modern (state-space) control, from about 1960 with Kalman, treats multi-variable systems and optimality (LQR), stochastic estimation (the Kalman filter, used in Apollo navigation), and model-predictive control.

Robust and adaptive control address systems whose dynamics are uncertain or changing; control theory underpins robotics, autopilots, process industries, power grids, and even biological and economic modelling.
