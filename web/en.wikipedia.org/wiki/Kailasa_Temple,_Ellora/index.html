Kailasa Temple, Ellora

The Kailasa temple (also Kailasanatha, Cave 16) is the largest of the rock-cut cave temples at the Ellora Caves complex in Maharashtra, India, and one of the largest rock-cut monuments in the world. Dedicated to Shiva and representing Mount Kailasa, the god's Himalayan home, it was carved from a single basalt cliff by excavating downward — a remarkable feat of 8th-century engineering attributed chiefly to the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I (c. 756–773 CE).

Construction

Unlike structural temples built upward, Kailasa was created by cutting three great trenches into the volcanic cliff and removing an estimated 200,000 tonnes of rock, working from the top down so that no scaffolding was needed. The resulting freestanding temple rises about 32.6 metres, set in a courtyard roughly 82 by 46 metres surrounded by a cloistered gallery carved with deities. Later Rashtrakuta rulers and patrons probably added to the decoration over succeeding decades.

Architecture and sculpture

The temple follows a South Indian (Dravida) vimana form with a pyramidal shikhara-like tower over the sanctuary, a flat-roofed mandapa carried on sixteen pillars, a Nandi pavilion, and two towering flagstaffs (dhvaja-stambhas) and life-size elephant sculptures flanking the courtyard. Its walls are carved with scenes from the Ramayana, Mahabharata, and Krishna legends, and the celebrated panel of Ravana shaking Mount Kailasa beneath the main shrine. Plaster and paint once covered much of the sculpture; traces remain. Kailasa is the centrepiece of Ellora's 34 caves — 17 Hindu, 12 Buddhist, and 5 Jain — designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983.
