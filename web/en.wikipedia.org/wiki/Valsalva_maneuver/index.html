Valsalva maneuver

The Valsalva maneuver is performed by a forceful attempt to exhale against a closed airway, usually by closing one's mouth and pinching one's nose shut while expelling air as if blowing up a balloon. It is named after Antonio Maria Valsalva, the 17th-century Italian physician who described it for expelling pus from the middle ear.

Physiology

The maneuver raises intrathoracic pressure, which transiently impedes venous return to the heart. The cardiovascular response proceeds through four phases, ending with a blood-pressure overshoot on release. The normal response is used as a test of autonomic and baroreflex function.

Uses

Everyday uses include equalising pressure in the ears during aircraft descent and diving. Clinically it is used to terminate some supraventricular tachycardias (a modified version, with leg raise, is more effective), to assess heart murmurs (for example in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy), and in the diagnosis of hernias and varicoceles. Weightlifters use a similar breath-holding technique to stabilise the trunk, though it carries risks of sharp blood-pressure spikes.
