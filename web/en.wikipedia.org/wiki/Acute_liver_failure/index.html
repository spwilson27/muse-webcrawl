Acute liver failure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Acute liver failure (ALF) is the rapid deterioration of liver function in a person without pre-existing liver disease, defined by coagulopathy (an INR of 1.5 or more) and hepatic encephalopathy developing within 26 weeks of the onset of illness. It is a rare but life-threatening condition that frequently progresses to multi-organ failure.

Causes

The commonest causes vary by region. In the United Kingdom and United States, paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose — intentional or inadvertent — is the leading cause. Other causes include viral hepatitis (especially hepatitis A, B and E), idiosyncratic drug reactions, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson's disease, Budd–Chiari syndrome, ischaemic hepatitis ("shock liver"), malignant infiltration and pregnancy-related conditions such as acute fatty liver of pregnancy.

Features and complications

Patients present with jaundice, coagulopathy and encephalopathy that can progress to cerebral oedema and raised intracranial pressure — historically the commonest cause of death. Other complications include infection, hypoglycaemia, renal failure, circulatory collapse and pancreatitis.

Management and prognosis

Management is supportive, in an intensive care and specialist liver unit: N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol poisoning (and benefit in some other causes), treatment of cerebral oedema and infection, and early assessment for emergency liver transplantation. Prognostic systems such as the King's College Criteria help identify patients unlikely to recover spontaneously. With modern intensive care and transplantation, survival has improved substantially; many patients with paracetamol-induced ALF recover with supportive care alone.
