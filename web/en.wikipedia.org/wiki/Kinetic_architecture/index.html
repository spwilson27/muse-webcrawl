Kinetic architecture

Kinetic architecture
FieldArchitecture, design

Kinetic architecture is a concept within architecture in which buildings or building components are designed to allow movement: parts of a structure can rotate, slide, fold, or transform in response to environmental conditions, functional needs, or aesthetic intent. Examples range from medieval drawbridges to modern retractable stadium roofs and adaptive facades that reconfigure to control sunlight.

History

Movable structures are as old as architecture: drawbridges and portcullises in fortifications, nomadic tents, and the rotating tea houses of early modern Japan. In the 20th century, architects such as William Zuk and John Clark popularized the theoretical treatment of kinetic structures in their 1970 book Kinetic Architecture. Advances in materials, sensors, and computational control have since made large-scale kinetic buildings practical.

Examples

  • The retractable roof and rotating stands of stadiums such as Wembley Stadium's arch-supported roof systems and the movable roof of the Milwaukee Art Museum's Burke Brise Soleil, whose wing-like sunscreen opens and closes.
  • The Falkirk Wheel, a rotating boat lift in Scotland.
  • Rotating apartment towers, such as the Suite Vollard in Brazil, whose floors rotate independently.
  • Adaptive shading facades, such as those of the Al Bahar Towers in Abu Dhabi, whose computer-controlled PTFE panels open and close in response to the sun.

Types of movement

Kinetic elements may be classified as mobile (relocatable), transformable (changing shape or size), or incremental systems in which motion is built into a building's skin or structure. Movement may be driven manually, by environmental forces such as wind or water, or by embedded mechanical and robotic systems.
