Russian Revolution of 1905

The Russian Revolution of 1905, also known as the First Russian Revolution, was a wave of mass political and social unrest that spread through vast areas of the Russian Empire in 1905. It included worker strikes, peasant unrest and military mutinies, and it led to constitutional reform (the Russian Constitution of 1906), including the establishment of the State Duma, multiparty politics and civil rights, though the Tsar retained great power. The revolution is often seen as a dress rehearsal for the Revolutions of 1917.

Background and Bloody Sunday

Discontent grew from industrialisation, harsh working conditions, peasant land hunger and demands for political representation, sharpened by Russia's disastrous war with Japan. On 22 January 1905 (9 January Old Style), troops fired on a peaceful procession of workers led by Father Georgy Gapon carrying a petition to Tsar Nicholas II at the Winter Palace in St Petersburg — "Bloody Sunday" — killing and wounding hundreds and igniting nationwide strikes.

Course

Unrest spread through 1905: the mutiny on the battleship Potemkin in June, peasant seizures of land, national movements in Poland, Finland and the Baltic provinces, and a general strike in October that produced the first soviet (council) of workers' deputies in St Petersburg, with Leon Trotsky among its leaders. Facing collapse, Nicholas issued the October Manifesto, promising civil liberties and an elected Duma. The concessions split the opposition; an armed uprising in Moscow in December was crushed, and punitive expeditions restored order during 1906–1907 under Prime Minister Pyotr Stolypin.

Consequences

The revolution produced Russia's first parliament and legal political parties, trade unions and a freer press, but the Fundamental Laws of 1906 preserved the Tsar's autocratic prerogatives, and the electoral law was manipulated in 1907 to favour conservative landowners. The soviets, strike tactics and parties of 1905 provided the template for 1917.
