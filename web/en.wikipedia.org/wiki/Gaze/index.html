Gaze

FieldsPsychology, philosophy, film theory, sociology

In the social sciences, psychology, and critical theory, the gaze (French: le regard) describes the act of looking and the awareness of being looked at, and the power relations embedded in that relationship. The concept has been elaborated by existentialist and psychoanalytic thinkers, including Jean-Paul Sartre, Michel Foucault, and Jacques Lacan, and was extended to cinema in Laura Mulvey's influential notion of the "male gaze".

Philosophical accounts

In Being and Nothingness (1943), Sartre analysed "the look" of the Other as the moment in which a person becomes aware of themselves as an object in another's world, an experience he associated with shame and self-consciousness. Lacan, in his seminar on the four fundamental concepts of psychoanalysis, distinguished the eye from the gaze: the gaze is situated on the side of the object, so that the subject is, in his phrase, "photo-graphed" by the world it surveys. Foucault used the idea of the "medical gaze" and the panoptic gaze of surveillance to analyse how institutions exercise power by seeing.

The male gaze

In her 1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema", film theorist Laura Mulvey argued that classical Hollywood cinema organises looking around a masculine spectator-position: the camera looks at women as erotic spectacle ("to-be-looked-at-ness"), while male characters bear the look and drive the narrative. The concept has since been widely applied, debated, and extended to other media, and has prompted discussion of alternative and oppositional gazes.

Psychology

In developmental and social psychology, gaze direction is a key channel of nonverbal communication: mutual gaze (eye contact) regulates conversation and intimacy, gaze following underpins joint attention in infancy, and sensitivity to being watched — detectable even from the sclera-rich human eye — is thought to support social coordination and theory of mind.
