United Kingdom of the Netherlands

The United Kingdom of the Netherlands was a kingdom created in 1815 that combined the former Dutch Republic in the north with the Austrian Netherlands in the south, roughly the modern Netherlands and Belgium. Luxembourg was joined to it in personal union. The state was intended by the Congress of Vienna to form a strong buffer north of France after the Napoleonic Wars.

Creation

William I of Orange became king in 1815. The new Fundamental Law provided a constitutional monarchy, but William exercised extensive personal authority. The Hague and Brussels shared governmental functions, and the States General represented both northern and southern provinces, although the more populous south considered itself under-represented. The king promoted canals, industry, trade and infrastructure and sought to integrate the economies of the two regions.

Tensions

Northern and southern inhabitants differed in language, religion, economic interests and political traditions. The north was largely Dutch-speaking and Protestant; the south included Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia and was overwhelmingly Catholic. Policies favoring Dutch in administration and education, controls on the Catholic Church and press, disputes over taxation and debt, and liberal demands for ministerial responsibility alienated many southerners. Catholics and liberals formed a “union of oppositions” against the king's government.

Belgian Revolution and settlement

Revolution broke out in Brussels in August 1830, inspired partly by the July Revolution in France. Belgian independence was proclaimed, and Dutch attempts to restore control failed amid international intervention. The great powers recognized Belgium in principle, but William refused the settlement for years. The Treaty of London of 1839 finally established Belgian independence and neutrality, divided Luxembourg and Limburg, and ended the United Kingdom of the Netherlands. The northern state continued as the Kingdom of the Netherlands.
