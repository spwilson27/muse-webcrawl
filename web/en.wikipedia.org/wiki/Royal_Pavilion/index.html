The Royal Pavilion, also known as the Brighton Pavilion, is a Grade I listed former royal residence in Brighton, England. Built in stages between 1787 and 1823 as a seaside retreat for George, Prince of Wales — later Prince Regent and King George IV — it is remarkable for its flamboyant Indo-Saracenic exterior of domes, minarets and pinnacles, and its lavish Chinese-inspired interiors.

History

The Prince first lodged in Brighton in 1783, attracted by the fashionable sea-bathing resort and the supposed health benefits of sea water. Henry Holland converted his lodging into the classical Marine Pavilion (1787–1795), and William Porden added stables in an Indian style (1803–1808). As Regent, George commissioned John Nash to transform the palace (1815–1822): Nash clothed the building in an exotic Indian exterior while Frederick Crace and Robert Jones created spectacular oriental interiors, including the dragon-hung Banqueting Room and the Music Room with its lotus-shaped chandeliers. William IV also used the Pavilion, but Queen Victoria found it cramped and sold it to the town of Brighton in 1850 for £53,000.

Later use

The Pavilion became a civic assembly rooms and, during the First World War, a hospital for Indian soldiers — commemorated by the Chattri memorial and the India Gate. Extensive restoration, including the reinstatement of original furnishings (many on permanent loan from the Royal Collection), has returned the state rooms close to their Regency splendour; it is now a museum and one of Britain's most recognisable buildings.
