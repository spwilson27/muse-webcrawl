Ranked voting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ranked voting (also ranked-choice voting) describes electoral systems in which voters rank candidates by preference (1st, 2nd, 3rd...) rather than selecting a single choice. The ranked ballots can then be counted by several rules: instant-runoff voting (eliminating the lowest candidate and transferring ballots until one holds a majority of continuing ballots), the Single transferable vote for multi-member districts, Borda counts (points by rank) and Condorcet methods (pairwise comparisons).

Ranked ballots are used for national elections in Australia (House of Representatives, since 1918/19), Ireland and Malta (STV), in a growing number of US cities and two states (Maine and Alaska) for various offices, and by many parties and organisations internally. Advocates argue ranking reduces vote-splitting and negative campaigning, lets voters support minor candidates without “wasting” votes, and produces winners with broad support; critics cite complexity, ballot exhaustion (ballots that rank no surviving candidate), delayed counts, and non-monotonicity paradoxes in IRV (ranking a candidate higher can cause them to lose). Debates over reform — as in New York City's adoption for primaries (2021) and various repeal attempts — turn on which of plurality voting's own pathologies (spoilers, unrepresentative winners) reformers most fear.
