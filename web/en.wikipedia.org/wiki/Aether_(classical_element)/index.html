Aether (Greek: αἰθήρ, "upper air"), also spelled ether and known in Latin as the quinta essentia ("fifth essence"), was in ancient and medieval natural philosophy the fifth classical element, added to earth, water, air and fire. It was believed to fill the heavens beyond the Moon and to compose the celestial bodies.

Aristotelian physics

Aristotle introduced aether in On the Heavens as the element of the celestial region. Unlike the four terrestrial elements, which move rectilinearly toward or away from the centre of the universe and are subject to generation and corruption, aether is ungenerated, inalterable and moves naturally in eternal circles — explaining the unchanging circular motions of the stars and planets. The word derives from a Greek root meaning "to burn" or "to shine," and in earlier Greek religion Aether was also personified as the god of the bright upper air.

Later history

The concept passed through medieval scholasticism and alchemy as quintessence. In the nineteenth century the name was revived for the hypothesised "luminiferous aether," the medium supposed to carry light waves through empty space; the Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887 found no evidence for it, and Einstein's special relativity (1905) removed the need for such a medium.
