Minimally invasive procedure

A minimally invasive procedure is any medical or surgical technique that limits the size of incisions needed, so that it is associated with less pain, a faster recovery, and a lower risk of infection compared with traditional open surgery. Such procedures became common with the development of optical instruments, endoscopes, real-time imaging, and refined instruments capable of operating through small ports or natural body openings.

History

Surgery was historically performed through large incisions that exposed the operative field directly. The introduction of the endoscope in the 20th century, and of video-assisted laparoscopy in the 1980s, transformed practice. Procedures such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal) became standard, demonstrating shorter hospital stays and quicker return to normal activity.

Techniques

Common approaches include laparoscopy in the abdomen, thoracoscopy in the chest, arthroscopy in joints, and endoscopy through natural openings. Catheter-based interventions in cardiology and interventional radiology use blood vessels as access routes. Robotic surgical systems provide magnified three-dimensional vision and wristed instruments.

Advantages and limitations

Benefits include reduced postoperative pain, smaller scars, less blood loss, and shortened hospitalisation. Limitations include a steep learning curve, loss of direct tactile feedback, dependence on imaging, and the possibility that a procedure must be converted to open surgery if complications arise.

Examples

Examples include endovascular aneurysm repair, percutaneous coronary intervention, arthroscopic knee repair, natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery, and image-guided biopsy and drainage procedures.
