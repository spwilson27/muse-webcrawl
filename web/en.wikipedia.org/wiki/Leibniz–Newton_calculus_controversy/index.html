Leibniz–Newton calculus controversy

The Leibniz–Newton calculus controversy was an argument between the mathematicians Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz over who had first invented calculus. The question was a major intellectual controversy that began simmering in 1699 and broke out in full force in 1711, continuing until Leibniz's death in 1716; it divided the mathematical communities of Britain and continental Europe for a century.

Background

Newton developed his "method of fluxions" in 1665–66 but published almost nothing about it for decades, circulating manuscripts privately. Leibniz developed his differential and integral calculus in Paris in the 1670s and published first, in 1684 and 1686, using the notation — dx and ∫ — that ultimately prevailed. Leibniz visited London in the 1670s and corresponded with Newton's intermediaries, and later scholars have examined whether he saw Newton's unpublished results, including the De analysi manuscript.

The dispute

In 1699 Fatio de Duillier implied Leibniz had borrowed from Newton; in 1710 John Keill accused Leibniz of plagiarism in the Philosophical Transactions. Leibniz appealed to the Royal Society, whose committee issued the Commercium Epistolicum (1712) finding in Newton's favour — a report secretly guided, and later anonymously reviewed, by Newton himself, who was the Society's president. The two sides traded anonymous pamphlets and letters, including the famous Charta Volans and the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence on the philosophy of space and God.

Modern assessment

The modern scholarly consensus is that Newton and Leibniz invented calculus independently: Newton's work began earlier, Leibniz's publication came first, and their approaches differed in conception and notation. The controversy had lasting consequences: British mathematics' loyalty to Newton's dot notation and geometric style is often blamed for its relative stagnation in analysis during the eighteenth century, while continental mathematicians advanced rapidly using Leibnizian methods.
