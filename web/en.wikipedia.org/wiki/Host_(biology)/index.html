Host (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A parasitic organism on its host

In biology and medicine, a host is a larger organism that harbours a smaller organism, whether a parasitic, mutualistic, or commensalist guest (symbiont). The guest typically receives nourishment and shelter from the host. Examples include a human or animal hosting parasitic worms, a cell hosting a virus, a legume plant hosting nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules, and a host plant supporting a parasitic plant such as mistletoe.

Types of hosts

For parasites with complex life cycles, the definitive (primary) host is the one in which the parasite reaches maturity and, if possible, reproduces sexually; an intermediate (secondary) host harbours the parasite during development or asexual reproduction and is often necessary for transmission. A paratenic host bridges an ecological gap without parasite development, and a reservoir host maintains the parasite in nature as a source of infection to other species. Organisms that transmit parasites between hosts are vectors, and are not themselves generally counted as hosts in the strict sense.

Host–symbiont relationships

Hosts may be harmed by their guests (parasitism), unaffected (commensalism), or benefited (mutualism, as in mycorrhizal fungi on plant roots or gut microbiota in animals). Hosts evolve defences — physical barriers, immune systems, and behavioural responses — while guests evolve mechanisms of attachment, immune evasion, and transmission, producing long-running evolutionary arms races. Host specificity ranges from strict specialists confined to one species to generalists infecting many hosts.
