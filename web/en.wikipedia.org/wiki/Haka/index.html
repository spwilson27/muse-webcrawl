Haka (/ˈhɑːkə/; Māori: [ˈhaka]) are a broad variety of ceremonial dances or challenges in Māori culture. A haka is a posture dance typically performed by a group, with vigorous movements, rhythmic chanting and stamping of the feet, accompanied by facial expressions such as pukana (widening of the eyes) and whetero (protruding of the tongue). Haka are performed for cultural preservation, to welcome distinguished guests, at funerals (tangihanga), weddings, and celebrations, and by New Zealand sports teams before matches, most famously the All Blacks rugby team.

Traditional haka

Although popularly described as war dances, haka historically served many functions. The peruperu was a fierce war haka involving weapons and a high leap; other types were performed without weapons for entertainment, welcome or instruction. The words of a haka carry the history, genealogy and grievances of the group performing it; compositions are credited to named ancestors and remain the cultural property (taonga) of their iwi (tribes). The most famous composition, "Ka Mate", was composed about 1820 by Te Rauparaha, chief of Ngāti Toa, celebrating his escape from enemies — "Ka mate, ka mate! Ka ora, ka ora!" ("I die, I die! I live, I live!").

Sporting haka

New Zealand sports teams have performed haka since the 1888–89 New Zealand Native football team's tour of Britain. The All Blacks adopted "Ka Mate" in the early 20th century and their pre-match performance became one of world sport's iconic sights. In 2005 the team introduced a second haka, "Kapa o Pango", written for them by Derek Lardelli of Ngāti Porou, performed for special occasions. Opponents' responses — famously the Welsh and Irish teams advancing on the haka, or Tonga's rival sipi tau and Samoa's siva tau — have sometimes led World Rugby to regulate how close teams may approach.

Other New Zealand national teams, including the Kiwis (rugby league), Black Ferns (women's rugby, who perform "Ko Uhia Mai"), Tall Blacks (basketball) and teams in many other codes, also perform haka.

Legal and cultural recognition

The Haka Ka Mate Attribution Act 2014, arising from Ngāti Toa's Treaty of Waitangi settlement, legally recognises Te Rauparaha as the composer of "Ka Mate" and provides for attribution, though it confers no royalties or veto over performance. Haka may be protected as cultural intellectual property under Māori customary principles, and unauthorised commercial use — in advertising or by foreign sports clubs — has repeatedly drawn protest; nevertheless, haka have been adopted by school and university teams abroad, notably some American football programmes.

Kapa haka

Kapa haka is the broader performing-arts discipline of which haka is one item, along with choral singing (waiata tira), action songs (waiata-ā-ringa), poi and choral pieces. The national festival Te Matatini, held since 1972, is the pinnacle of kapa haka competition and one of New Zealand's largest cultural events.
