Infantry tactics

Infantry tactics are the methods by which infantry units fight, from the individual soldier and fireteam up to battalion level. They cover how infantry moves, deploys, uses fire and terrain, and coordinates with supporting arms in attack and defence.

Fire and movement is the foundation of modern infantry tactics.

Historical development

For centuries, infantry fought in dense formations — the phalanx, the legion, the pike square and the musket line — in which cohesion and massed fire compensated for inaccurate weapons. The spread of rifled weapons and machine guns in the 19th and early 20th centuries made such formations suicidal, driving dispersion, entrenchment and, in the First World War, the combined-arms assault tactics of stormtroops. The Second World War standardised the squad built around an automatic weapon, supported by mortars and artillery.

Modern principles

Modern infantry tactics rest on fire and movement: one element suppresses the enemy with fire while another moves, alternating roles to close with and destroy the enemy or to break contact. Small units are organised into fireteams with a mix of rifles, automatic weapons and anti-armour weapons, networked by radio and increasingly by digital battle-management systems. Common procedures include patrolling, ambushes, deliberate and hasty attacks, defence of prepared positions, and fighting in built-up areas (urban warfare), each with standard battle drills so sections can react instantly to contact.

Supporting elements

Infantry rarely fights alone: artillery and mortars provide fire support, armour and combat engineers assist mobility and breaching, and air support and drones provide reconnaissance and strike. Combined arms coordination is treated as essential to infantry effectiveness.
