Etruscan language

Etruscan was the language of the Etruscans, the civilisation of ancient Italy centred in Etruria (roughly modern Tuscany, western Umbria and northern Lazio). It was spoken from about 700 BC until it was displaced by Latin by around the 1st century AD. Etruscan is not an Indo-European language and is not closely related to any other well-attested tongue; together with Raetic of the Alps and Lemnian of the Aegean it is grouped in the small Tyrsenian language family.

Corpus

Some 13,000 Etruscan inscriptions survive, but most are very short — names on tombs, ownership marks, and dedications. Only a handful of long texts exist. The longest is the Liber Linteus Zagrabiensis, a ritual calendar written on linen that was later cut up and reused as mummy wrappings in Egypt. Other significant texts include the clay Tabula Capuana, the bronze Tabula Cortonensis (a legal document), and the gold Pyrgi Tablets, which include a bilingual dedication in Etruscan and Phoenician that has been a key aid to interpretation.

Decipherment and comprehension

Etruscan is written in an alphabet adapted from the Greek alphabet, read right to left, so inscriptions can be transliterated easily. The meaning of many words is nevertheless uncertain. Through bilingual texts, formulaic funerary language, and the combinatorial method — comparing repeated phrases across many inscriptions — scholars understand the general sense of most texts and know perhaps a few hundred words, including numerals: a set of inscribed dice confirmed θu (1), zal (2), ci (3), śa (4), maχ (5), huθ (6). The language appears to have been agglutinative, with nouns inflected by suffixes, and it lacked a distinction between voiced and voiceless stops in writing.

Legacy

Etruscan left loanwords in Latin, possibly including persona (from phersu, a masked performer), and it influenced the religious and political vocabulary of early Rome. The emperor Claudius wrote a history of the Etruscans and an Etruscan dictionary, both now lost.
