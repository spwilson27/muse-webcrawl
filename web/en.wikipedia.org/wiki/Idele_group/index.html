Idele group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In algebraic number theory, the idele group of a global field K is the multiplicative group of units of the adele ring of K. It was introduced by Claude Chevalley in the 1930s (from the French idèle, short for élément idéal) as a tool for reformulating class field theory in terms of a single abelian group, replacing the classical language of ideals and ray class groups.

Definition

An idele is a family (av) of elements of the completions Kv×, indexed by the places v of K, such that av lies in the unit group of the local ring of integers for all but finitely many finite places. With the restricted product topology, the ideles form a locally compact abelian topological group IK.

Idele class group

The diagonal embedding of K× into IK is discrete and closed, and the quotient CK = IK / K× is called the idele class group. Global class field theory identifies CK (more precisely its profinite completion) with the Galois group of the maximal abelian extension of K via the global Artin reciprocity map. For a number field, CK maps onto the ideal class group, and the connected component of the identity corresponds to the archimedean places.

Relation to adeles

Just as ideals were replaced by ideles on the multiplicative side, the additive adele ring provided the setting for John Tate's thesis (1950), which used harmonic analysis on the adeles and ideles to prove analytic continuation and functional equations for Hecke L-functions in a uniform way.
