Tom Stoppard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Tom Stoppard in conversation at a literary festival, the playwright as elegant public intellectual of the stage]

Sir Tom Stoppard (born Tomáš Sträussler, 3 July 1937) is a Czech-born British playwright and screenwriter, one of the most celebrated dramatists of the post-war stage, known for plays that stage ideas — philosophy, mathematics, history, love — with verbal brilliance and theatrical daring. His breakthrough, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead (1966, at the Edinburgh Fringe and then the National Theatre), made him famous at 29: two minor courtiers from Hamlet wait in the wings of someone else's tragedy.

His plays include Jumpers (1972), Travesties (1974), The Real Thing (1982), Arcadia (1993 — regularly voted among the greatest plays of the century), The Invention of Love (1997), the trilogy The Coast of Utopia (2002), Rock 'n' Roll (2006) and Leopoldstadt (2020), his most personal work, on his own family's erased Jewish history. As a screenwriter he won an Academy Award for Shakespeare in Love (1998, with Marc Norman).

Life

Born in Zlín, Czechoslovakia, to a Bata shoe-company doctor's family, Stoppard fled the Nazis as a small child (his father was killed in Singapore; his mother remarried a British officer, Kenneth Stoppard), grew up in India and England, and left Bristol Grammar to be a journalist at 17 without university. He learned his Jewish ancestry — and that most of his family had been murdered — only in the 1990s, from a Czech relative's inquiry: Leopoldstadt, written at 82, is the reckoning.

The work

Stoppard's theatre of ideas is never a lecture: Arcadia shuttles between 1809 and the present to think about thermodynamics, landscape and love in the same breath; Utopia stages the Russian revolutionary century as a family argument. His wit is famous; his structures are the deeper signature — plays built like theorems that end like love letters.
