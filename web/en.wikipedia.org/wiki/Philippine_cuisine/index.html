Philippine cuisine is composed of the cuisines of more than a hundred distinct ethnolinguistic groups across the Philippine archipelago. It has evolved over centuries from Austronesian roots, absorbing Malay, Chinese, Spanish (via 333 years of colonization), and American influences. Filipino cooking is known for its interplay of salty, sour, and sweet flavors.

Signature dishes

Adobo—meat braised in vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, bay leaves, and peppercorns—is widely considered the unofficial national dish. Sinigang is a sour soup soured with tamarind; lechon is whole roasted pig served at celebrations. Other staples include kare-kare (oxtail in peanut sauce), pancit (noodles of Chinese origin), lumpia (spring rolls), sisig (sizzling chopped pork), and laing—taro leaves stewed in coconut milk from the Bicol region.

Meals and customs

Rice accompanies nearly every meal, including breakfast, where garlic fried rice (sinangag) is served with egg and a protein in the silog combination. Meals may be eaten kamayan-style, by hand off banana leaves. Merienda (snacks) and desserts such as halo-halo—a shaved-ice dessert comparable in spirit to a snow cone—and bibingka rice cakes are central to food culture. Sawsawan (dipping sauces) let diners adjust flavor at the table.

Regional variation

Bicol cuisine features coconut milk and chili; Ilocos is known for pinakbet and bagnet; Pampanga is famed as a culinary capital with dishes like sisig; the Visayas and Mindanao contribute kinilaw (ceviche-like cured fish) and, in Muslim regions, dishes influenced by Malay cooking such as rendang.
