Robert Falcon Scott (6 June 1868 – 29 March 1912) was a British Royal Navy officer and explorer who led two expeditions to the Antarctic regions: the Discovery Expedition of 1901–04, and the ill-fated Terra Nova Expedition of 1910–13. On the second venture, Scott led a party of five which reached the South Pole on 17 January 1912, only to find that Roald Amundsen's Norwegian expedition had preceded them by just over a month.

Discovery Expedition

Scott, a torpedo lieutenant, was appointed to command the National Antarctic Expedition aboard the specially built Discovery. In November 1902, with Ernest Shackleton and Edward Wilson, he marched to a record farthest south of 82°17′S. The expedition conducted extensive scientific work and mapped much of the Ross Sea coastline, though Discovery was icebound for two winters before relief ships freed her in 1904.

Terra Nova Expedition

Scott sailed in the Terra Nova in 1910, aiming for the Pole itself. Basing at Cape Evans on Ross Island, his polar party — Scott, Wilson, Lawrence Oates, Henry Bowers, and Edgar Evans — set out in October 1911 using a mixture of ponies, dogs, motor sledges, and finally man-hauling. They reached the Pole on 17 January 1912 to find Amundsen's tent and flag already there.

On the return journey the party was beset by exceptionally severe weather and failing supplies. Evans died on 17 February; Oates, crippled by frostbite, walked out of the tent on 16 March with the famous words, "I am just going outside and may be some time". Scott, Wilson, and Bowers died in their tent on or about 29 March 1912, just 11 miles from a supply depot. Their bodies and diaries were recovered the following November; Scott's "Message to the Public" and last letters shaped his enduring image as a heroic figure of the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration, though later historians have debated his planning and decisions.
