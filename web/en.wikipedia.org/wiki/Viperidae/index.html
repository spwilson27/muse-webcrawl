The Viperidae (vipers) are a family of snakes found in most parts of the world, except for Antarctica, Australia, Hawaii, Madagascar, New Zealand, Ireland, and various other isolated islands. They are venomous and have long (relative to non-vipers), hinged fangs that permit deep penetration and envenomation. Three subfamilies are currently recognized: Viperinae (Old World vipers), Crotalinae (pit vipers) and Azemiopinae; some classifications also include Causinae (night adders).

Description

All viperids have a pair of relatively long, solenoglyphous (hollow) fangs at the front of the upper jaw, which fold back against the roof of the mouth when not in use. Behind the fangs lies the venom gland, connected by a duct. Viperids are generally stout-bodied snakes with distinctly triangular heads covered in small, keeled scales. Most species are terrestrial and ambush predators, striking and releasing prey, then following the scent trail left by the envenomated animal.

Pit vipers (Crotalinae) additionally possess heat-sensing pit organs between the eye and the nostril, allowing them to detect infrared radiation from warm-blooded prey. This group includes the rattlesnakes of the Americas and the habus and mamushis of Asia.

Reproduction

Most viperids are viviparous, giving birth to live young, although some, such as the genus Lachesis and most Causinae, lay eggs. Litter sizes vary considerably between species.

Venom

Viper venom typically contains proteases, enzymes that disrupt blood clotting and damage tissues, producing pain, swelling and sometimes necrosis or systemic bleeding. Bites from many species are medically significant and require antivenom treatment.
