The Goidelic languages, also called the Gaelic languages, are one of the two living branches of the Insular Celtic languages, the other being the Brittonic languages (Welsh, Cornish and Breton). The Goidelic branch comprises three living languages: Irish (Gaeilge), Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig) and Manx (Gaelg).

History

The earliest attested form is Primitive Irish, known from inscriptions in the Ogham alphabet dating from roughly the 4th to 6th centuries AD. By the Old Irish period (c. 600–900), a sophisticated literary language had developed, producing some of the earliest vernacular literature in Western Europe. In the Middle Irish period, Irish settlers carried the language to Scotland and the Isle of Man (the Dál Riata connection), and the varieties gradually diverged: by the Early Modern period (1200–1650) a shared Classical Gaelic literary standard was used across Ireland and Gaelic Scotland, but spoken dialects had already begun to differentiate into the modern languages.

Characteristics

Goidelic languages are characterised by initial consonant mutations (lenition and eclipsis in Irish), verb–subject–object word order, inflected prepositions, and a distinction between independent and dependent verb forms. Together with the Brittonic languages they form the "Q-Celtic" versus "P-Celtic" contrast: Goidelic retained the Proto-Celtic *kʷ sound as /k/ (written c), where Brittonic converted it to /p/.

Status

All three languages declined sharply under pressure from English between the 17th and 20th centuries. Irish is an official language of Ireland and the EU; Scottish Gaelic has official status in Scotland under the Gaelic Language (Scotland) Act 2005; Manx, declared extinct as a native language after the death of Ned Maddrell in 1974, has been successfully revived and is now taught in schools on the Isle of Man.
