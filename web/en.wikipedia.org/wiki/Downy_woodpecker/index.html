Downy woodpecker

Downy woodpecker
Scientific nameDryobates pubescens
FamilyPicidae (woodpeckers)
Length14–18 cm; wingspan 25–31 cm
RangeNorth America, from Alaska and Canada to the Gulf Coast
ConservationLeast Concern (IUCN)

The downy woodpecker (Dryobates pubescens, formerly Picoides pubescens) is the smallest woodpecker in North America and one of the most familiar backyard birds on the continent. Adults are mainly black and white: black upperparts and wings with white spots, a broad white patch down the back, a white underside, and a black-and-white striped head. Males carry a small red patch at the back of the head, which females lack.

Identification

The downy is easily confused with the larger hairy woodpecker, which has a nearly identical plumage pattern. The downy is distinguished by its smaller size, its short bill — shorter than the length of its head, where the hairy's is about as long — and by black spots on its white outer tail feathers.

Behaviour and diet

Downy woodpeckers forage on trees, shrubs and even weed stalks, probing bark crevices for insects, larvae and eggs, and also eating seeds and berries. They readily visit garden feeders for suet and sunflower seeds and often join mixed winter feeding flocks of chickadees, nuthatches and kinglets. Their voice is a high, descending whinny and a sharp pik note; both sexes drum on resonant wood.

Breeding

Pairs excavate a cavity in dead wood, where the female lays three to eight white eggs. Both parents incubate and feed the young, which fledge after about three weeks. The species is resident across most of its range, from deciduous woodland and orchards to suburban parks, though birds in the far north move south in winter.
