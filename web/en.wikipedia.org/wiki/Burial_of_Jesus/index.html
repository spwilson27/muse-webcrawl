Burial of Jesus

The burial of Jesus refers to the entombment of the body of Jesus after his crucifixion, before the eve of the Jewish Sabbath, described in the four canonical Gospels. According to the narratives, a wealthy disciple named Joseph of Arimathea obtained permission from the Roman governor Pontius Pilate to take the body, wrapped it in linen, and laid it in a new rock-cut tomb, rolling a stone against the entrance. In John's account Nicodemus assisted, bringing myrrh and aloes. The burial is a central element of Christian belief, recited in the Apostles' Creed ("crucified, dead, and buried") and forming the prelude to the accounts of the empty tomb and the Resurrection.

Gospel accounts

Mark, the earliest Gospel, reports that Joseph of Arimathea, a respected member of the Sanhedrin, bought a linen shroud, took the body down, wrapped it, and laid it in a tomb cut out of rock, with Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses watching. Matthew adds that the tomb was Joseph's own new tomb, and that the chief priests obtained a guard from Pilate and sealed the stone, fearing the disciples would steal the body. Luke notes that the women who had followed Jesus observed the tomb and prepared spices. John gives the fullest detail: Nicodemus brought about a hundred pounds of myrrh and aloes, and the tomb was in a garden near the place of crucifixion.

Site

Tradition since the 4th century has located the tomb within the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, where a small shrine, the Aedicule, encloses the remains of a rock-cut burial chamber. An alternative site, the Garden Tomb north of the Damascus Gate, was proposed in the 19th century and is favoured by some Protestants. Archaeology confirms that first-century Jewish rock tombs with rolling stones and burial benches of the type described existed in the Jerusalem area.

Historical discussion

Scholars debate the historicity of the honourable burial: Roman practice normally left crucified victims exposed or consigned them to common graves, though evidence such as the ossuary of Yehohanan shows crucified men could receive proper burial, and Josephus records Jewish concern to bury the crucified before sunset, in line with Deuteronomy 21:22–23. The burial is attested very early in the pre-Pauline creed quoted in 1 Corinthians 15 ("he was buried, and he was raised on the third day"). The entombment has been a frequent subject in Christian art, known as the Entombment of Christ.
