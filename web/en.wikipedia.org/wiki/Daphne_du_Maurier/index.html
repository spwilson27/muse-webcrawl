Dame Daphne du Maurier (13 May 1907 – 19 April 1989) was an English novelist, biographer and playwright. Although classified by critics as a romantic novelist, her stories are noted for their moodiness, psychological depth and unsettling, often unresolved endings, and have outlasted many more "literary" contemporaries in popularity.

Life and career

Du Maurier was the middle daughter of the actor-manager Sir Gerald du Maurier and granddaughter of the artist and writer George du Maurier, creator of Svengali. Her first novel, The Loving Spirit (1931), brought her to the attention of Frederick Browning, whom she married in 1932. She fell in love with Cornwall, which became the setting of most of her fiction; from 1943 she lived at Menabilly, the house that inspired Manderley.

Major works

Her best-known novel, Rebecca (1938) — famous for its opening line "Last night I dreamt I went to Manderley again" — won the American Booksellers Association National Book Award and was filmed by Alfred Hitchcock in 1940, winning the Academy Award for Best Picture. Hitchcock also adapted Jamaica Inn (1936) and her short story "The Birds" (1952). Nicolas Roeg filmed her story "Don't Look Now" in 1973. Other novels include Frenchman's Creek (1941), My Cousin Rachel (1951) and The House on the Strand (1969). She was made a Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire in 1969.
