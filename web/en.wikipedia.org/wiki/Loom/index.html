A loom is a device used to weave cloth and tapestry. The basic purpose of any loom is to hold the warp threads under tension to facilitate the interweaving of the weft threads. The precise shape of the loom and its mechanics may vary, but the basic function is the same.

Weaving

Weaving is done by intersecting the longitudinal threads, the warp, with the transverse threads, the weft (or filling). The main components of the loom are the warp beam, the heddles, the harnesses or shafts, the shuttle, the reed, and the take-up roll. Each warp thread passes through a heddle; raising or lowering groups of threads creates an opening (the shed) through which the shuttle carries the weft. The reed then beats the weft into place.

History

Looms date to the Neolithic period; simple warp-weighted looms were used across the ancient world, and horizontal ground looms are depicted in ancient Egyptian art. In 1733 John Kay patented the flying shuttle, greatly speeding weaving and driving demand for mechanised spinning. Edmund Cartwright patented the first practical power loom in 1785, a cornerstone of the Industrial Revolution in textiles. The Jacquard loom, developed by Joseph Marie Jacquard around 1804, used punched cards to control individual warp threads, enabling complex figured fabrics — and famously influencing early computing.

Types of looms

  • Back-strap loom — tension supplied by the weaver's body.
  • Warp-weighted loom — warp threads hang from a bar, weighted by stones.
  • Frame and rigid-heddle looms — simple looms for hand weaving.
  • Floor loom — treadle-operated handloom.
  • Power loom — mechanised loom; includes shuttle looms and shuttleless (rapier, projectile, air-jet, water-jet) types.
  • Jacquard loom — individually controlled warp threads for patterned weaving.
