Saint Malachy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[hero image: Saint Malachy]
Malachy, Primate of Ireland

Saint Malachy (Irish: Máel Máedóc Ua Morgair; 1094 – 2 November 1148) was an Irish saint and Archbishop of Armagh, to whom were attributed several miracles and the celebrated — though disputed — "Prophecy of the Popes." He was the first native-born Irishman to be formally canonised by the Catholic Church, an honour granted by Pope Clement III in 1190. His feast day is 3 November.

Life

Malachy was born in Armagh and ordained a priest around 1119 by Saint Cellach. He studied liturgy and theology under Imar of Armagh and threw himself into reforming the Irish Church, which had become largely hereditary and lay-dominated. He became abbot of Bangor in 1123, Bishop of Connor in 1124, and, after much resistance, Archbishop of Armagh in 1132, receiving the pallium's promise from Rome after journeys in 1139–40 and 1148.

Malachy worked to align the Irish Church with Roman practice, introduced the Cistercian order to Ireland by founding Mellifont Abbey in 1142, and re-established diocesan structures replacing the monastic system of church government. His close friend Saint Bernard of Clairvaux wrote his biography (Vita sancti Malachiae), our principal source for his life. Malachy died at Clairvaux on 2 November 1148, while on a second journey to Rome.

Prophecy of the Popes

The "Prophecy of the Popes" is a series of 112 short Latin mottos said to describe every pope from Celestine II (1143) to the end of time. It was first published in 1595 by the Benedictine Arnold Wion, who attributed it to Malachy; modern scholars generally regard it as a forgery composed around 1590, likely for political purposes in a papal conclave. The mottos matching popes after the publication date are increasingly strained, but the prophecy remains famous in popular culture.
