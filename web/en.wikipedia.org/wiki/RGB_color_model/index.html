RGB color model

Placeholder image: additive color mixing of red, green and blue light.

The RGB color model is an additive color model in which the primary colors red, green, and blue are added together in various ways to reproduce a broad array of colors. The name of the model comes from the initials of the three additive primary colors, red, green, and blue.

The main purpose of the RGB color model is for the sensing, representation, and display of images in electronic systems, such as televisions and computers, though it has also been used in conventional photography. Before the electronic age, the RGB color model already had a solid theory behind it, based in human perception of colors.

Principles

RGB is a device-dependent color model: different devices detect or reproduce a given RGB value differently, since the color elements and their response to the individual color levels vary from manufacturer to manufacturer, or even in the same device over time. Thus an RGB value does not define the same color across devices without some kind of color management.

Typical RGB input devices are color TV and video cameras, image scanners, and digital cameras. Typical RGB output devices are TV sets of various technologies (CRT, LCD, plasma, OLED), computer and mobile phone displays, video projectors, multicolor LED displays and large screens such as Jumbotrons.

Additive mixing

To form a color with RGB, three light beams (one red, one green, and one blue) must be superimposed. Each beam is called a component of that color, and each can vary in intensity from fully off (black) through equal full intensity of all three (white) to single-component colors such as pure red. Intermediate mixtures produce the secondary colors cyan, magenta, and yellow where pairs of beams are at full intensity.

Numeric representation

A color in the RGB color model is described by indicating how much of each of the red, green, and blue is included. The color is expressed as an RGB triplet (r, g, b), each component of which can vary from zero to a defined maximum value. In computing, the components are commonly stored as unsigned integers in the range 0 to 255, giving 256 levels per channel and about 16.7 million representable colors, often described as "24-bit color". In hexadecimal notation, colors are written as #RRGGBB, for example #FF0000 for pure red.

History

The model rests on the Young–Helmholtz theory of trichromatic color vision, developed by Thomas Young and Hermann von Helmholtz in the nineteenth century, and on James Clerk Maxwell's demonstration of additive color synthesis. It was applied in early color photography processes such as the Autochrome, and later became fundamental to color television standards including NTSC and PAL.
