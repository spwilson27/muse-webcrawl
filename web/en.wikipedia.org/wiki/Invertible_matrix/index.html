Invertible matrix

In linear algebra, an n-by-n square matrix A is called invertible (also nonsingular or nondegenerate) if there exists another n-by-n matrix B such that AB = BA = I, where I is the identity matrix. The matrix B is then unique and is called the inverse of A, denoted A−1. A square matrix without an inverse is called singular.

Characterisations

The invertible matrix theorem collects many equivalent conditions: A is invertible if and only if its determinant is non-zero; its columns (and rows) are linearly independent and span the whole space; the equation Ax = b has a unique solution for every b; the linear map defined by A is a bijection; zero is not an eigenvalue of A; and A has full rank n. Over the real numbers, invertible matrices form the general linear group GL(n, ℝ), which is open and dense in the space of all matrices.

Computing the inverse

In practice inverses are rarely formed explicitly; systems are solved instead by Gaussian elimination (LU decomposition), which can also be extended to compute A−1 via Gauss–Jordan elimination, or by the adjugate formula using cofactors, which is mainly of theoretical interest. Properties include (AB)−1 = B−1A−1 and det(A−1) = 1/det(A).
