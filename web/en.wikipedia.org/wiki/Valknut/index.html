Valknut

The valknut is a symbol found on a variety of objects from the archaeological record of the ancient Germanic peoples. It consists of three interlocking triangles — either drawn as a single unicursal figure or as three separate triangles (the "Borromean" form). The modern name combines Old Norse valr, "the slain (warriors)", and knut, "knot"; the term itself is a modern coinage and no historical name for the symbol is known.

Archaeological finds

The symbol appears on the 7th-century Tängelgårda stone and the Stora Hammars stones from Gotland, Sweden, on the bedpost buried with the Oseberg ship in Norway, on a ring from the river Nene in England, and on other Viking Age objects, frequently alongside figures interpreted as Odin and animals such as wolves, ravens and horses. On the Gotland picture stones the valknut appears in scenes plausibly connected with death in battle and the afterlife.

Interpretation

Scholars commonly associate the symbol with Odin and with the slain warriors (einherjar) he gathered to Valhalla; it has been compared with the triskele and with the "heart of Hrungnir" described in the Prose Edda. Because no contemporary text explains it, all interpretations remain tentative.

Modern use

The valknut is used today by Germanic neopagan (Heathen) groups, in jewellery and tattoos, and as a logo by various companies. Like several other Norse symbols it has also been appropriated by some white-supremacist groups, though it is not inherently a hate symbol and remains in widespread non-extremist religious and cultural use.
