Ancrene Wisse (originally titled Ancrene Riwle, "the rule for anchorites") is an anonymous monastic rule, or guide, for anchoresses, written in early thirteenth-century Middle English. It is among the most important prose works in Middle English, admired both for its practical, humane spiritual counsel and for the quality of its writing, and it stands at the head of the so-called AB language tradition of the West Midlands.

Background and audience

The work was written for three sisters of gentle birth who had been enclosed as anchoresses — religious recluses walled into cells attached to a church, where they lived lives of prayer in permanent solitude. As the text spread, it was revised and adapted for larger communities of recluses, and translated into French and Latin. It belongs to a flowering of West Midlands devotional literature that includes the Katherine Group of saints' lives and the treatise Hali Meiðhad.

Contents

The rule is divided into eight parts or "distinctions". The outer sections deal with the anchoresses' daily devotions, behaviour, clothing, servants, and contact with the outside world — which was limited to speaking through a curtained window. The inner sections address the "inner rule" of the heart: guarding the senses, the mortification of the flesh, confession, penance, and the love of Christ, expressed in vividly allegorical imagery of the soul as a besieged lady and Christ as her knightly lover. The author insists that the outer rule is merely the servant of the inner one and may be adapted, while the inner rule is unchanging.

Significance

Ancrene Wisse survives in eleven English manuscripts, the most important being Corpus Christi College, Cambridge MS 402. J. R. R. Tolkien, who edited the text, praised its language as a disciplined standard preserving Old English prose traditions, and the work remains central to the study of medieval women's spirituality.
