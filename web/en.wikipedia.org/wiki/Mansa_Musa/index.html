Mansa Musa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Mansa Musa enthroned holding a gold nugget, from the Catalan Atlas of 1375]

Mansa Musa (c. 1280 – c. 1337) was the ninth mansa (emperor) of the Mali Empire, reigning from about 1312 to 1337, at the height of its wealth and extent. A devout Muslim, he is famed above all for his pilgrimage (hajj) to Mecca in 1324–1325, during which his caravan — tens of thousands of attendants and camels laden with gold — distributed so much gold in Cairo that its price there was depressed for a decade, according to contemporary accounts. His patronage brought Arab scholars and architects to Mali and raised Timbuktu into a centre of learning; he is often cited, on inherently speculative estimates, as among the richest individuals in history.

The hajj of 1324

Musa's pilgrimage crossed the Sahara with a procession the sources describe as including 60,000 people, hundreds of camels each carrying gold, and his royal household; in Cairo he spent and gave away so freely that the Egyptian gold market took years to recover, an episode reported by the historian al-Umari from eyewitnesses. The journey advertised Mali's wealth to the Mediterranean world: the Catalan Atlas of 1375 depicts Musa enthroned in West Africa, holding a gold nugget.

Rule and legacy

Musa annexed the Songhai cities of Gao and Timbuktu, endowed mosques and madrasas (the Sankore mosque-university grew under his patronage), sent students to Fez, and imported the Andalusian architect al-Sahili, credited with Mali's Sudano-Sahelian mudbrick style. His successors inherited the empire at its greatest extent; his death around 1337 began a slow decline. In modern Mali he remains a founding national figure.
