Adrenal insufficiency

Adrenal insufficiency is a condition in which the adrenal glands do not produce adequate amounts of steroid hormones, primarily cortisol, and sometimes aldosterone. It is classified as primary (disease of the adrenal glands themselves, as in Addison's disease), secondary (inadequate pituitary ACTH secretion) or tertiary (hypothalamic dysfunction, most often from suppression by long-term glucocorticoid therapy). Symptoms are often non-specific — fatigue, weakness, weight loss, nausea and dizziness — so diagnosis is frequently delayed.

Types and causes

In developed countries the commonest cause of primary adrenal insufficiency is autoimmune adrenalitis, often as part of autoimmune polyglandular syndrome; worldwide, tuberculosis remains an important cause. Primary disease destroys the whole cortex, causing both cortisol and aldosterone deficiency — the resulting loss of negative feedback raises ACTH, whose precursor also stimulates melanocytes and causes the characteristic skin hyperpigmentation. Secondary and tertiary insufficiency spare aldosterone production (which is mainly controlled by the renin–angiotensin system), so hyperpigmentation and severe salt wasting are absent. The most frequent overall cause is suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis following prolonged exogenous glucocorticoid use.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on a low morning cortisol with confirmatory ACTH stimulation testing; plasma ACTH distinguishes primary (high) from central (low or inappropriately normal) disease. Treatment is lifelong hormone replacement — typically hydrocortisone in divided doses, plus fludrocortisone for primary disease — with education on increasing doses during illness to prevent adrenal crisis, the acute, potentially fatal decompensation of the condition.
