Romanov impostors


Most famous claimant: Anna Anderson (as Anastasia)
Period: 1920s onwards
Resolved by: DNA testing, 1990s–2000s

Romanov impostors were people who claimed to be members of the Russian imperial family who had supposedly survived the execution of Nicholas II and his family at Yekaterinburg on 17 July 1918. Because the bodies were concealed and full details suppressed by the Soviet authorities, rumours of survival flourished, and dozens of claimants emerged across the world — particularly for the youngest children, Grand Duchess Anastasia, Grand Duchess Maria, and Tsarevich Alexei.

Anna Anderson

The most famous claimant was Anna Anderson, who appeared in Berlin in 1920 after an apparent suicide attempt and claimed to be Anastasia. She attracted supporters among some émigré aristocrats and scepticism from others, including the surviving Dowager Empress's circle, who refused to see her. Her claim was litigated in West German courts for decades; the case ended in 1970 without a conclusive legal determination. After her death in 1984, DNA tests in 1994 on preserved tissue matched her to the family of Franziska Schanzkowska, a Polish factory worker, and not to the Romanovs.

Other claimants

Other notable impostors included Eugenia Smith, who published an "autobiography" as Anastasia in 1963; Marga Boodts, who claimed to be Grand Duchess Olga; and several Alexeis, among them the Polish agent Michał Goleniewski. None was accepted by the surviving Romanov family.

Resolution

The discovery of the Yekaterinburg grave in 1979 (publicly excavated in 1991) and of the second grave in 2007 allowed DNA identification of all seven members of the imperial family — Nicholas, Alexandra, and their five children — conclusively demonstrating that none survived. The remains were reburied in the Peter and Paul Cathedral in St Petersburg in 1998, and the family was canonised by the Russian Orthodox Church in 2000. The impostor phenomenon remains a fertile subject for novels, films, and the enduring myth of Anastasia.
