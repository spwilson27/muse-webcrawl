Field sobriety test

Field sobriety tests (FSTs), or standardized field sobriety tests (SFSTs), are a battery of tasks police officers use to determine whether a driver is impaired by alcohol or other drugs. The tests are administered roadside during a traffic stop and are intended to establish probable cause for arrest and to provide evidence of impairment. In the United States the standardized battery is promoted and validated through research sponsored by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA).

The standardized battery

The SFST battery validated by NHTSA consists of three tests. The horizontal gaze nystagmus test observes involuntary jerking of the eyes as they follow a moving stimulus; the walk-and-turn test requires the subject to take nine heel-to-toe steps along a line, turn, and return; and the one-leg stand requires standing on one foot while counting aloud for about thirty seconds. Officers score standardized clues of impairment for each test. Studies sponsored by NHTSA report that the battery, correctly administered, accurately identifies many drivers above legal blood alcohol limits, though the underlying validation studies are debated.

Non-standardized tests

Other commonly used tasks — reciting the alphabet, counting backwards, finger-to-nose, and the Romberg balance test — are not part of the standardized battery and are given varying weight by courts. Preliminary breath tests with portable devices are chemical tests and are legally distinct from the physical FSTs; refusal consequences and admissibility vary by jurisdiction.

Criticism

Critics note that sober people can fail the tests because of age, weight, medical conditions, injury, footwear, or uneven road surfaces, and question the reliability of officer scoring. Several courts have limited or questioned the evidentiary use of the nystagmus test as scientific evidence.
