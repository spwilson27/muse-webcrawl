Digoxin, sold under the brand name Lanoxin among others, is a medication used to treat various heart conditions. It belongs to the class of drugs known as cardiac glycosides and is obtained from the leaves of the woolly foxglove plant, Digitalis lanata.

Medical uses

Digoxin is used for the treatment of mild-to-moderate heart failure, usually alongside other medications, and for controlling the ventricular rate in chronic atrial fibrillation. It increases the force of the heart's contractions (a positive inotropic effect) while slowing electrical conduction through the atrioventricular node.

Mechanism of action

Digoxin inhibits the sodium–potassium ATPase pump in cardiac muscle cells. This raises intracellular sodium, which in turn increases intracellular calcium via the sodium–calcium exchanger, strengthening contraction. Its vagomimetic action slows conduction through the AV node, which is the basis of its use in rate control.

Adverse effects

Digoxin has a narrow therapeutic index, and toxicity is a well-known clinical problem. Side effects include nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, diarrhoea, confusion and characteristic visual disturbances such as blurred or yellow-tinted vision (xanthopsia). Serious toxicity can cause dangerous arrhythmias. Low potassium levels (hypokalaemia) increase the risk. An antibody-based antidote, digoxin-specific antibody fragments (DigiFab), is available for severe poisoning.

History

The medicinal use of foxglove was described by William Withering in 1785 for the treatment of dropsy (oedema). Digoxin itself was first isolated in 1930 by Sydney Smith at Burroughs Wellcome from Digitalis lanata. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
