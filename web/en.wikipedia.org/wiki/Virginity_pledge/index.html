Virginity pledges (also called abstinence pledges or purity pledges) are commitments made by teenagers and young adults to refrain from sexual intercourse until marriage. They are most common in the United States among evangelical Christian groups. Pledgers are often given or wear purity rings as symbols of the commitment, and pledge ceremonies sometimes take the form of "purity balls" at which daughters pledge abstinence, often accompanied by their fathers.

History

The modern pledge movement began in 1993 with True Love Waits, a campaign of the Southern Baptist Convention's Sunday School Board, which collected hundreds of thousands of signed pledge cards, displaying them on the National Mall in Washington, D.C., in 1994. The Silver Ring Thing, founded by Denny Pattyn in 1995, promoted pledges together with silver rings inscribed with a Bible verse (1 Thessalonians 4:3–4). Pledge-based programs received substantial federal funding in the United States as part of abstinence-only sex education policies, especially during the 1990s and 2000s. Similar movements have appeared in other countries, sometimes connected with HIV prevention campaigns.

Research

Virginity pledges have been extensively studied. Sociologist Peter Bearman and medical sociologist Hannah Brückner, using data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health, found that pledging was associated with delaying first intercourse by an average of about 18 months—but only in social contexts where pledgers formed a distinctive minority identity. They also found that pledgers were less likely to use contraception at first intercourse and that, in the long run, pledgers' rates of sexually transmitted infections were similar to those of non-pledgers, partly because pledgers were less likely to be tested or treated.

A 2009 study by Janet Rosenbaum, comparing pledgers with matched non-pledgers five years later, found no significant differences in premarital sex, STI rates, or anal and oral sex behaviors, though pledgers were less likely to use condoms and birth control. Critics such as Rosenbaum and the Guttmacher Institute argue that pledges are ineffective and may leave young people less protected; supporters argue that pledges express values and community standards and that some delaying effects are meaningful. A congressionally mandated evaluation of federal Title V abstinence programs (2007) similarly found no measurable impact on sexual behavior.

Cultural reception

Purity culture, of which pledges are a part, has been both defended as a counter-cultural affirmation of chastity and criticized—including by some former participants—for promoting shame about sexuality and unrealistic expectations. The topic recurs in debates over comprehensive versus abstinence-only sex education.
