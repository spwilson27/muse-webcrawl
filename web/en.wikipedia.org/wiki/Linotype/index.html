Linotype machine

The Linotype machine is a "line casting" machine used in printing which is manufactured and sold by the Linotype company and related companies. As operated by a single machinist, it produces a complete line of type as a single metal slug, replacing the slow hand composition of individual letters.

History

German-American inventor Ottmar Mergenthaler developed the machine and patented it in 1884; it was first used commercially by the New-York Tribune in 1886. The name derives from "line o' type". The Linotype revolutionised newspaper and book publishing by letting one operator set type many times faster than hand compositors, and it dominated hot-metal typesetting for most of the 20th century.

Operation

The operator types on a 90-character keyboard, causing brass matrices for each character to drop from a magazine into an assembler. When a line is complete, the matrices are justified with expanding space bands and used as a mould into which molten type metal is injected, casting a slug bearing the whole line in relief. After casting, the matrices are automatically sorted back into the magazine by a distributor bar coded to each character.

Decline

Phototypesetting and then computerised desktop publishing displaced the Linotype from the 1960s through the 1980s, though machines remain in use in some print shops and museums.
