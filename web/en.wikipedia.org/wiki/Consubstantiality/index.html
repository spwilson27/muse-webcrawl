Consubstantiality

The Shield of the Trinity illustrates the consubstantial persons of the Godhead.

Consubstantiality, from the Latin consubstantialitas, is the doctrine that persons share the same substance or essence. In Christian theology it expresses the teaching that Jesus Christ is "of one substance" (Greek: homoousios) with God the Father, and by extension that the three persons of the Trinity are consubstantial.

The term homoousios was adopted by the First Council of Nicaea in 325 in its creed, to affirm the full divinity of the Son against Arianism, which taught that the Son was a created being of a different or merely similar substance. The Nicene formula was defended by Athanasius of Alexandria and reaffirmed at the First Council of Constantinople in 381. The related doctrine of consubstantiation in eucharistic theology — a distinct concept — should not be confused with consubstantiality.

In modern English translations of the Mass, the Latin consubstantialem Patri in the Nicene Creed is rendered "consubstantial with the Father," a wording introduced in the 2011 English translation of the Roman Missal in place of "one in being with the Father."
