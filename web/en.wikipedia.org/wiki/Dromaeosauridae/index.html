Dromaeosauridae is a family of feathered theropod dinosaurs, popularly known as raptors. They were small to medium-sized carnivores that flourished from the Middle Jurassic to the end of the Cretaceous period (about 167–66 million years ago). Dromaeosaurids are distinguished by their stiffened tails, relatively large brains, and an enlarged, sickle-shaped retractable claw on the second toe of each foot, which was held off the ground while walking and probably used in predation.

Description

Most dromaeosaurids were modest in size: Microraptor was under a metre long and bore long flight feathers on both arms and legs, while Velociraptor was about the size of a turkey. The largest, such as Utahraptor and Achillobator, reached 5–7 metres. Fossil evidence — including quill knobs on the forearm of Velociraptor and spectacular feather impressions from Chinese deposits — shows that dromaeosaurids were covered in feathers, and that some small forms could glide or fly.

Discovery and research

The family was named in 1922 by William Diller Matthew and Barnum Brown. John Ostrom's 1969 description of Deinonychus revolutionised dinosaur science, launching the "dinosaur renaissance" and the modern view of dinosaurs as active, warm-blooded animals. Dromaeosaurids are among the closest known relatives of birds, sharing with them a swivel wrist, three-fingered hand, hollow bones and feathers; together they form the group Paraves.

In popular culture

Dromaeosaurids achieved worldwide fame as the "Velociraptors" of the Jurassic Park films — actually modelled largely on the bigger Deinonychus, and portrayed without feathers in the original films — which cemented the image of the intelligent pack-hunting raptor in the public imagination.
