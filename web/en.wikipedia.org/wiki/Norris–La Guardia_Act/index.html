Norris–La Guardia Act

[Image: Norris–La Guardia Act]

The Norris–La Guardia Act (also known as the Anti-Injunction Bill) is a 1932 United States federal law, codified at 29 U.S.C. §§ 101–115, that relieved American unions of certain constraints imposed by federal courts. Sponsored by Senator George Norris of Nebraska and Representative Fiorello La Guardia of New York, it was signed into law by President Herbert Hoover on March 23, 1932, in the final period of his administration before the New Deal.

Provisions

The Act withdrew the jurisdiction of federal courts to issue injunctions in most labour disputes — for example against strikes, picketing, and boycotts — except under strict procedural conditions. It declared "yellow-dog contracts", in which workers agreed not to join a union as a condition of employment, unenforceable in federal court, and stated a national policy favouring workers' freedom to organise and engage in collective bargaining.

Context and legacy

The law responded to decades of court intervention against unions under doctrines such as those in Duplex Printing Press Co. v. Deering (1921) and Bedford Cut Stone Co. v. Journeyman Stone Cutters' Association (1927), which had narrowed the protections of the Clayton Act of 1914. By curbing labour injunctions, the Norris–La Guardia Act paved the way for the National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act) of 1935, and its definition of "labor dispute" was carried into later federal labour law.
