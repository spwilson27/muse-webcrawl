Walras's law

Walras's law is a principle in general equilibrium theory asserting that budget constraints imply that the values of excess demand (or, conversely, excess supply) must sum to zero regardless of whether the prices are a general equilibrium. In other words, if all markets but one clear, the remaining market must also clear; an excess supply in one market must be matched by excess demand elsewhere.

Léon Walras (1834–1910) introduced the law in his Éléments d'économie politique pure (1874).

Statement

For prices p and excess demand functions zi(p) in an economy with k markets, budget constraints imply p · z(p) = 0 for all p. It follows that if markets 1 through k−1 are in equilibrium (z = 0), then market k is also in equilibrium.

Implications

The law is an accounting identity derived from agents' budget constraints rather than a behavioural assumption. It underpins the tâtonnement process by which Walrasian auctioneer prices adjust toward equilibrium, and its modern proofs are standard in microeconomic theory, notably in the work of Arrow and Debreu on the existence of competitive equilibrium.
