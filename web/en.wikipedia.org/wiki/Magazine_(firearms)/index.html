Magazine (firearms)

A magazine is an ammunition storage and feeding device for a repeating firearm, either integral within the gun (internal or fixed magazine) or externally attached (detachable magazine). The magazine functions by holding several cartridges within a single unit and, with the aid of a spring and follower, feeding them into position for chambering by the action of the weapon. Magazines come in many shapes and capacities, from a handful of rounds in early repeating rifles to 100 rounds or more in drum magazines for automatic weapons.

A detachable magazine is often inaccurately called a "clip", a term which properly refers to a different device used to load cartridges into a magazine.

History

Early repeating arms used tubular magazines under the barrel, popularised by lever-action rifles such as the Henry and Winchester models of the 1860s. Box magazines — fixed and later detachable — spread with bolt-action military rifles of the late 19th century; the Lee–Enfield's detachable 10-round box magazine was an early landmark. In the 20th century, detachable box magazines became standard for semi-automatic pistols and for automatic rifles and submachine guns, while drum, pan, and belt systems served machine guns.

Types

Internal (fixed) magazines

Fixed magazines are built into the firearm and are loaded in place, often by means of a stripper clip or charger. Examples include the tubular magazine of pump-action shotguns and the internal box magazine of many Mauser-pattern rifles.

Detachable box magazines

The detachable box magazine is a self-contained unit inserted into the weapon's magazine well. Cartridges may be stacked in a single column (single stack) or a staggered, roughly double column for greater capacity in shorter length. Most modern pistols and rifles use this type.

Other forms

Drum magazines store rounds in a spiral or circular track for high capacity; pan magazines lie flat on top of the receiver, as on the Lewis gun; rotary magazines, as in the Savage Model 99 and some modern rimfire rifles, spool cartridges around a rotor; and helical magazines wrap rounds in a helix, as in the Calico and Bizon designs.

Function

A spring at the base of the magazine pushes a follower (and the cartridges resting on it) upward toward the action. As each round is fired and the bolt or slide cycles, the top cartridge is stripped from the feed lips and chambered. Magazine condition — spring strength, feed-lip geometry, and cleanliness — is a leading factor in the reliability of self-loading firearms.

Legal regulation

Many jurisdictions regulate magazine capacity for civilian firearms, with "high-capacity" (or "large-capacity") magazines — commonly defined by thresholds such as more than 10 rounds — subject to restrictions or bans in some countries and some US states.
