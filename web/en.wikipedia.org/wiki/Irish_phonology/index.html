Irish phonology

The phonology of the Irish language is characterised above all by the contrast between "broad" (velarised) and "slender" (palatalised) consonants, a distinction that is phonemic for nearly every consonant and is fundamental to Irish grammar and orthography.

Consonants

Most Irish consonants occur in broad/slender pairs. Broad consonants have a velar secondary articulation (roughly [ˠ]) and slender consonants a palatal one ([ʲ]). The pairs include /pˠ, pʲ, bˠ, bʲ, tˠ, tʲ, dˠ, dʲ, k, c, ɡ, ɟ, fˠ, fʲ, v, vʲ, sˠ, ʃ, x, ç, ɣ, j, mˠ, mʲ, nˠ, nʲ, ŋ, ɲ, lˠ, lʲ, ɾˠ, ɾʲ/. The contrast carries grammatical meaning, for example distinguishing nominative from genitive or singular from plural in many nouns.

The consonant systems of the three main dialect groups — Ulster, Connacht and Munster — differ in detail, notably in the realisation of the slender r and in the treatment of the fricatives.

Vowels

Irish has five short vowels /a, ɛ, ɪ, ɔ, ʊ/ and five long vowels /aː, eː, iː, oː, uː/, along with the reduced vowel schwa /ə/ in unstressed syllables. Vowel length contrasts are phonemic. Diphthongs include /əi, əu, iə, uə/. Stress normally falls on the first syllable, though Munster Irish shifts stress to heavy later syllables.

Initial mutations and sandhi

Irish phonology interacts with the language's system of initial mutations — lenition and eclipsis — which alter the first consonant of a word in defined grammatical contexts. Processes such as syncope and epenthesis also shape the surface forms of words across dialect boundaries.
