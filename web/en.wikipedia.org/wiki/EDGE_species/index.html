EDGE species

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

EDGE species are animal species that score highly under the Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered (EDGE) metric, a conservation approach developed by the Zoological Society of London (ZSL) that combines a species' unique evolutionary history with its extinction risk on the IUCN Red List.

The metric

A species' EDGE score multiplies its evolutionary distinctiveness (ED) — the amount of unique evolutionary history it represents, calculated by distributing branch lengths of a phylogenetic tree among descendant species — by its global endangerment (GE) score, derived from its Red List category. A species on a long, species-poor branch of the tree of life that is also threatened scores highly, because its extinction would erase a large amount of irreplaceable evolutionary history.

Focal species

ZSL's EDGE of Existence programme uses the rankings to prioritise conservation action and funds EDGE Fellows working on focal species in their home countries. High-ranking mammals include the Chinese pangolin, the long-beaked echidnas, the aye-aye, the pygmy hippo and the Bactrian camel. The programme has been extended beyond mammals to amphibians, birds, reptiles, corals and other groups, producing top-100 lists for each.

Reception

Supporters argue EDGE highlights neglected, unusual species that conventional campaigns overlook. Critics note that phylogenetic distinctiveness is only one value among several, that rankings are sensitive to tree quality, and that a focus on distinct species may divert resources from ecosystems or from species with keystone ecological roles.
