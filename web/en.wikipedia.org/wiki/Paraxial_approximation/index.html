Paraxial approximation

Paraxial rays travel close to, and at small angles with, the optical axis.

In geometric optics, the paraxial approximation is a small-angle approximation used in ray tracing of light through an optical system (such as a lens). A paraxial ray is a ray that makes a small angle (θ) to the optical axis of the system and lies close to the axis throughout the system.

Approximations

Under the paraxial approximation, the trigonometric functions of the small angle θ (in radians) are replaced by their first-order terms:

sin θ ≈ θ,   tan θ ≈ θ,   cos θ ≈ 1

This linearises the ray-tracing equations, so that the propagation of a ray through a system can be expressed with simple matrices (the ray transfer matrix, or ABCD matrix formalism).

Paraxial optics

Optical design based on the paraxial approximation is called Gaussian optics, after Carl Friedrich Gauss, who laid out its principles in 1841. Within the approximation, spherical surfaces refract rays without spherical aberration, focal points and cardinal points (focal, principal and nodal points) are well defined, and the lensmaker's equation takes its simple thin-lens form. Real systems deviate from the approximation through aberrations, which are treated as perturbations of the paraxial solution.

Wave optics

An analogous paraxial approximation applied to the Helmholtz equation yields the paraxial wave equation, whose best-known solution is the Gaussian beam used to describe laser propagation.
