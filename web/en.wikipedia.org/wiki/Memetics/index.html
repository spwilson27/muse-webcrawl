Memetics

Memetics models ideas as replicators passed between minds

Memetics is a theory of mental content based on an analogy between the way genes transmit biological information and the way ideas — called memes — are transmitted between minds. It describes how ideas, behaviours, and cultural practices spread, mutate, and are subject to a form of natural selection within a population of minds. The field was popularized by Richard Dawkins, who coined the term "meme" in his 1976 book The Selfish Gene.

History

Dawkins proposed the meme as a unit of cultural transmission — examples include tunes, ideas, catch-phrases, clothes fashions, and ways of making pots or building arches. Just as genes propagate in the gene pool by leaping from body to body, memes propagate in the meme pool by leaping from brain to brain through imitation. Subsequent writers, including Daniel Dennett and Susan Blackmore, developed the idea; Blackmore's The Meme Machine (1999) argued that imitation is what makes human brains distinctive. The Journal of Memetics was published from 1997 to 2005.

Concepts

Within memetics, memes are treated as replicators characterized by longevity, fecundity, and copying-fidelity. Groups of co-adapted memes are sometimes called memeplexes, an example being religious belief systems. Memeticists argue that memes evolve by natural selection analogous to biological evolution, through variation, mutation, competition, and inheritance, and that a meme's success depends on its host population and environment rather than on its truth or benefit to the host.

Criticism

Critics argue that memetics lacks a precise mechanism of transmission, that ideas do not replicate with the fidelity of genes, and that the theory frequently restates ordinary cultural description in biological vocabulary without adding testable predictions. Despite academic scepticism, the underlying concept of the meme became hugely popular in internet culture, where "memes" came to mean humorous images, videos, and phrases spread online.
