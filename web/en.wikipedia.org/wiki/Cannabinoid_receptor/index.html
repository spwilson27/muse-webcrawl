Cannabinoid receptors are seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors activated by endocannabinoids, phytocannabinoids and synthetic cannabinoids.

Cannabinoid receptors are a class of cell membrane receptors in the G protein-coupled receptor superfamily. They are activated by three major groups of ligands: endocannabinoids produced naturally by the body (such as anandamide and 2-AG), phytocannabinoids found in the cannabis plant (such as tetrahydrocannabinol, THC), and synthetic cannabinoids. Together with their ligands and metabolic enzymes, they form the endocannabinoid system.

CB1 receptor

The CB1 receptor, cloned in 1990, is among the most abundant G protein-coupled receptors in the brain, with high densities in the basal ganglia, hippocampus, cerebellum and cerebral cortex. Its activation by THC accounts for most of the psychoactive effects of cannabis. CB1 receptors are located mainly on presynaptic terminals, where their activation suppresses neurotransmitter release. The CB1 antagonist rimonabant was marketed briefly as an anti-obesity drug before being withdrawn over psychiatric side effects.

CB2 receptor

The CB2 receptor, cloned in 1993, is expressed predominantly in cells of the immune system, including B cells, macrophages and microglia, and modulates cytokine release and immune cell migration. Because CB2 activation produces little psychoactivity, it is an attractive target for anti-inflammatory and analgesic drug development.

Physiology and pharmacology

Endocannabinoid signalling acts as a retrograde messenger system in the synapse: postsynaptic neurons release endocannabinoids that travel backwards to presynaptic CB1 receptors, dampening further transmitter release. The system participates in appetite, pain sensation, mood, memory and immune function. Other receptors, including GPR55 and the vanilloid receptor TRPV1, are sometimes proposed as additional cannabinoid targets.
