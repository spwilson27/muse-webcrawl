Mounted archery is a form of archery in which the archer shoots from horseback. A military technique perfected by the nomadic peoples of the Eurasian steppe, it dominated warfare from the ancient world through the late medieval period: fast, mobile horse archers could harass, encircle, and destroy slower armies while avoiding decisive close combat. The skill survives today as a competitive sport and in preserved ceremonial traditions, most famously the Japanese Shinto ritual of yabusame.

History

Horse archery developed on the steppe once horses were large enough to ride (by around the 9th century BCE), replacing the war chariot. Assyrian reliefs show early mounted archers; the Scythians, Sarmatians, and Parthians made the arm famous — the Parthian Empire's horse archers destroyed Crassus's Roman army at the Battle of Carrhae (53 BCE), and the "Parthian shot," firing backward over the horse's tail while feigning retreat, became proverbial. In the medieval period, Huns, Avars, Magyars, Seljuk and Ottoman Turks, and above all the Mongols of the 13th century — whose mounted archers conquered the largest contiguous land empire in history — relied on composite-bow cavalry. In Japan, mounted archery was the original way of the samurai warrior (kyūba no michi) before the sword rose to prominence.

Equipment and technique

The steppe weapon was the short, powerful composite bow of wood, horn, and sinew, usable from the saddle; arrows were carried in large quivers, and the bowstring was often drawn with a thumb ring. Riders steered with their legs, dropping the reins to shoot at the gallop in any direction, timing their release to the moment all four of the horse's hooves were off the ground to obtain a stable platform.

Modern sport

Since the 1980s, mounted archery has been revived as an international competitive sport, with track-based formats (such as the Korean, Hungarian, and "raid" tracks) in which riders gallop along a set course shooting at targets under time limits. Yabusame, performed at shrines such as Tsurugaoka Hachimangū in Kamakura, and related Japanese disciplines like kasagake preserve the ceremonial form, while Ottoman-Turkish and Korean traditions are also practised.
