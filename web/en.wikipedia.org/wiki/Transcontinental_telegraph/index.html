First transcontinental telegraph

The line joined at Salt Lake City, Utah, on 24 October 1861.

The first transcontinental telegraph was a line that connected the existing telegraph network in the eastern United States to a small network in California, by means of a link between Omaha, Nebraska and Carson City, Nevada, passing through Salt Lake City. It was a milestone in electrical engineering and in the formation of the United States. It operated from October 24, 1861, until 1869, when it was replaced by the transcontinental railroad's multi-line telegraph.

Construction

The Pacific Telegraph Act of 1860 offered a federal subsidy to the company that completed the line. Western Union, under Hiram Sibley, organised the effort through two subsidiaries: the Pacific Telegraph Company building westward from Omaha, and the Overland Telegraph Company building eastward from Carson City. Crews raced across plains and mountains, sinking poles and stringing a single iron wire.

The two ends met in Salt Lake City on 24 October 1861, and the first messages were exchanged between the coasts — including a message from California Chief Justice Stephen Field to President Abraham Lincoln. The completion immediately rendered the Pony Express obsolete; it announced its closure two days later.

Significance

The telegraph cut communication time between the coasts from weeks to minutes, binding California more closely to the Union during the American Civil War and transforming news, finance and military coordination across the continent.
