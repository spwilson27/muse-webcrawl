Marine reptile

Marine reptiles are reptiles which have become secondarily adapted for an aquatic or semi-aquatic life in a marine environment. Living marine reptiles include the sea turtles, the sea snakes, the marine iguana, and saltwater crocodiles (along with some other semi-aquatic crocodilians). In the Mesozoic era, marine reptiles — ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs, mosasaurs, and others — dominated the oceans, but all of these groups became extinct by the end of the Cretaceous.

Living groups

Sea turtles (superfamily Chelonioidea) comprise seven species, all listed as vulnerable or endangered; they spend nearly their whole lives at sea, with females coming ashore only to lay eggs. Sea snakes include about 69 species in two subfamilies; the true sea snakes are fully aquatic and give birth to live young at sea, while sea kraits come ashore to lay eggs. The marine iguana (Amblyrhynchus cristatus) of the Galápagos Islands grazes on underwater algae, the only sea-going lizard. The saltwater crocodile (Crocodylus porosus), the largest living reptile, ranges across the Indo-Pacific and can travel long distances by sea.

Adaptations

Marine reptiles share adaptations to life in salt water: salt-excreting glands that expel excess sodium (located near the eyes in turtles, producing the appearance of "tears"), streamlined or flattened bodies and paddle-like limbs for swimming, the ability to hold the breath for long periods, and, in sea snakes, cutaneous respiration through the skin. Because reptiles are ectothermic, nearly all living marine reptiles are confined to warm tropical and subtropical waters; leatherback turtles, with their dark coloration and countercurrent heat exchange, range furthest into cold water.

Extinct groups

The Mesozoic seas hosted an extraordinary diversity: ichthyosaurs (dolphin-like predators), plesiosaurs and pliosaurs, mosasaurs (giant marine lizards that became apex predators of the Late Cretaceous), marine turtles of great size such as Archelon, thalattosuchian crocodyliforms, and placodonts and other sauropterygians. The end-Cretaceous extinction event eliminated the great marine reptile groups, and their niches were later filled largely by marine mammals.

Conservation

Most living marine reptiles are threatened by fisheries bycatch, coastal development, egg and meat harvesting, plastic ingestion, and climate change; the majority of sea turtle species and the marine iguana are listed on the IUCN Red List.
