Old-growth forest

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Old-growth forest

Also calledPrimary forest, virgin forest, ancient woodland (UK)
Defining traitsOld trees, multi-layered canopy, snags and coarse woody debris, minimal human disturbance
Notable examplesPacific Northwest conifer forests; Białowieża Forest; Tasmanian temperate rainforest; Amazon and Congo basin primary forests

An old-growth forest is a forest that has attained great age without significant disturbance and thereby exhibits unique ecological features. Depending on the ecosystem, this may mean tree ages of 150 to over 1,000 years. Old-growth stands are characterised by a mix of tree ages and sizes, a complex multi-storey canopy, standing dead trees (snags), large fallen logs that act as "nurse logs" for seedlings, and a rich community of specialised plants, fungi, invertebrates and vertebrates.

Ecological significance

Old-growth forests store very large amounts of carbon in biomass and soils and continue to accumulate it for centuries, making their protection relevant to climate policy. They sustain species found nowhere else — such as the northern spotted owl and marbled murrelet of the Pacific Northwest — regulate stream flows and water quality, and serve as reference ecosystems against which managed forests are measured. In the tropics, primary forests hold the bulk of terrestrial biodiversity.

Distribution and loss

Only a fraction of the world's original forest remains in old-growth condition. Most European and eastern North American old growth was cleared before the 20th century; major surviving tracts persist in boreal Canada and Russia, the Amazon and Congo basins, Southeast Asia and parts of Australasia, and are threatened by logging, agriculture and fire. Defining "old growth" precisely is contested — definitions variously stress tree age, structural features or absence of human intervention — and the term is central to conservation debates, from the Pacific Northwest timber wars of the 1990s to current disputes over logging in British Columbia, Tasmania and the Carpathians.
