Off-balance-sheet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Off-balance-sheet (OBS) financing refers to assets, liabilities, or financing arrangements that do not appear on a company's balance sheet under applicable accounting rules. Legitimate uses include operating leases (historically), joint ventures, research and development partnerships, factoring of receivables, and commitments such as letters of credit and guarantees, which are typically disclosed in the notes to the financial statements rather than recognised as liabilities.

Motives

Companies may use off-balance-sheet structures to keep reported leverage low, to preserve borrowing capacity, to improve ratios used in loan covenants and credit ratings, or to isolate risk in special-purpose entities (SPEs). Because investors and regulators cannot see OBS obligations directly in headline figures, the practice attracted intense scrutiny after a series of scandals.

Abuses and reforms

The most notorious abuse was by Enron, which used special-purpose entities such as Chewco and the Raptors to hide debt and inflate profits, contributing to its collapse in 2001. In response, accounting standard setters tightened consolidation rules (for example FIN 46 in the US), and the later standards IFRS 16 and ASC 842 brought most leases onto the balance sheet as right-of-use assets and lease liabilities. During the 2007–2008 financial crisis, banks' off-balance-sheet vehicles (conduits and structured investment vehicles holding mortgage securities) similarly transmitted losses back onto banks, leading to further reform.

Examples

Common off-balance-sheet items include operating leases (pre-IFRS 16), take-or-pay contracts, contingent liabilities, pension deficits in some presentations, loan commitments, and assets held in unconsolidated subsidiaries or joint ventures accounted for by the equity method.
