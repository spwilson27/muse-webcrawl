Ta'ameya

Ta'ameya (Egyptian Arabic: طعمية, IPA: [tˤɑʕˈmejjɑ]) is the Egyptian form of falafel: deep-fried fritters made from ground fava beans mixed with herbs such as leek, parsley and coriander. It is one of Egypt's most popular street foods and a staple breakfast dish, commonly eaten in sandwiches of baladi bread with tahini, salad and pickles.

Origins

The dish is widely believed to have originated among Egypt's Coptic Christians, who ate the bean fritters during Lent when meat was forbidden; the name is said to derive from the Arabic word ṭaʿām, meaning food. From Egypt the dish spread across the Middle East, where in the Levant it evolved into falafel made with chickpeas, or a mixture of chickpeas and fava beans.

Preparation

Dried fava beans are soaked, then ground with leeks, onion, garlic, parsley, coriander and spices such as cumin. The mixture — vividly green inside from the herbs — is shaped into patties, sometimes stuffed with onions and tomato or coated in sesame and coriander seeds, and deep-fried until crisp. Unlike the denser chickpea version, ta'ameya is characteristically fluffy and light in the interior.
