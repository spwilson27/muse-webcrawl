Tax competition

Tax competition arises when governments lower taxes to attract mobile capital, businesses or residents from other jurisdictions.

Tax competition, sometimes called fiscal competition, exists when governments are encouraged to reduce fiscal burdens in order to encourage the inflow or discourage the outflow of productive resources or inhabitants. It occurs between national governments and also between subnational jurisdictions such as states, provinces and municipalities.

Mechanisms

Because capital, high-skilled workers and corporate headquarters are mobile, a jurisdiction that cuts taxes may attract investment and jobs at the expense of its neighbours. Instruments of competition include low headline corporate tax rates, special regimes for foreign investors, generous allowances, and—in the case of tax havens—secrecy and minimal regulation. Empirical work shows average statutory corporate tax rates worldwide fell from around 40 percent in 1980 to the low 20s by the 2020s.

Debate

Defenders, drawing on Tiebout sorting and public-choice arguments, hold that competition disciplines governments, restrains wasteful spending and matches tax-service bundles to residents' preferences. Critics describe a "race to the bottom" that erodes revenue for public goods while conferring windfalls on capital owners, noting that location decisions also depend on infrastructure, skills and market access. The literature distinguishes benign competition over real investment from harmful competition over paper profits shifted between affiliates.

Coordination efforts

International responses include the OECD's work on harmful tax practices and the BEPS project, the EU Code of Conduct on business taxation, state-aid rules, and the 2021 agreement by more than 135 jurisdictions on a 15 percent global minimum corporate tax ("Pillar Two"), implemented in many countries from 2024.
