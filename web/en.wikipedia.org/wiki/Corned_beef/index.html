Corned beef

Corned beef
TypeSalt-cured beef
Main ingredientsBeef brisket, brine, curing salts, spices
VariationsCanned corned beef; corned beef hash; Reuben sandwich

Corned beef is a salt-cured beef product. The term comes from the treatment of the meat with large-grained rock salt, also called "corns" of salt. It is prepared by curing a cut of beef, typically brisket, in a spiced brine, sometimes with sodium nitrite which gives it a characteristic pink colour. Corned beef is a key ingredient in dishes such as corned beef and cabbage, corned beef hash, and the Reuben sandwich.

History

Although corned beef is strongly associated with Ireland, the Irish historically produced it largely for export; in the 17th and 18th centuries Ireland's low salt taxes and cattle herds made it a centre of the trade supplying the Royal Navy and the Americas. Irish immigrants in the United States adopted corned beef — bought cheaply from Jewish kosher butchers, who cured brisket similarly — and made corned beef and cabbage an emblematic St. Patrick's Day meal.

During the First and Second World Wars, canned corned beef ("bully beef") was a staple ration for British and Commonwealth troops. Major canning centres included Fray Bentos in Uruguay, whose product became so famous that "Fray Bentos" entered rhyming slang.

Preparation

Fresh corned beef is made by packing brisket in a brine of water, salt, curing salt (pink salt), sugar, and pickling spices such as mustard seed, coriander, bay leaf, peppercorns and allspice, for roughly a week. It is then simmered slowly until tender. The cut is typically served sliced across the grain.

Dishes

  • Corned beef and cabbage — simmered with cabbage, potatoes and carrots.
  • Corned beef hash — chopped corned beef fried with potatoes and onions, often served with eggs.
  • Reuben sandwich — corned beef, Swiss cheese, sauerkraut and Russian dressing on grilled rye.
  • Canned corned beef — popular in the Philippines (served with garlic rice), the Pacific Islands, and the United Kingdom.
