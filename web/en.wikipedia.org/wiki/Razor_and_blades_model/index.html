Razor and blades model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Razor and blades model
TypeBusiness / pricing model
Also known asRazor–razorblade model; bait and hook; tie-in sales
Named forGillette's safety razor and disposable blades
MechanismCheap (or free) durable platform + high-margin proprietary consumables

The razor and blades business model is a business model in which one item is sold at a low price (or given away for free) in order to increase sales of a complementary good, such as consumable supplies. It is different from the loss leader model, which sells a specific product below cost in order to bring customers into the retail store; here the loss-making platform is generally sold directly by the manufacturer, who recoups the subsidy many times over from the locked-in stream of consumable purchases. It is also known as the bait and hook model.

History

The model is named after King C. Gillette, whose safety razor of 1903 used cheap disposable blades. The conventional story — that Gillette gave razors away and profited from blades — is largely a myth: Gillette in fact sold its razors at substantial prices for years and cut prices only after its patents expired in 1921, using the model defensively against competitors. A cleaner early example was Standard Oil, which sold oil lamps cheaply in China to create demand for its kerosene. The model took off in the 20th century as manufacturers learned to protect the consumable stream with patents, proprietary fittings and contracts.

Examples

  • Printers and ink: inkjet printers sold near or below cost; proprietary ink cartridges carry very high margins per millilitre.
  • Video game consoles: hardware sold at a small margin or loss, recouped through game royalties and online subscriptions.
  • Coffee pod machines: inexpensive brewers tied to patented coffee pods (e.g. Keurig, Nespresso).
  • E-book readers, mobile phones and apps: subsidised devices tied to content stores and service plans.
  • Medical and scientific instruments: analysers placed cheaply in laboratories, with proprietary reagents and test kits sold at high margins.
  • Water filters, air purifiers and vacuum cleaners: cheap units with recurring proprietary filter and bag replacements.

Economics and criticism

The model works when switching costs and proprietary lock-in let the supplier keep consumable prices well above production cost, effectively charging customers over time rather than up front; it can lower the barrier to adoption and smooth revenue into an annuity-like stream. Its viability depends on defending the tie between platform and consumable — via patents, chips and authentication, contracts and design changes — against third-party refill and clone suppliers. The model is criticised for waste (throwaway cartridges and pods), for obscuring the true total cost of ownership, and for anti-competitive lock-in; manufacturers respond that competition between platforms disciplines the combined price. Legal challenges to the model have included antitrust litigation over tying and, in Illinois Tool Works Inc. v. Independent Ink, Inc. (2006), a US Supreme Court ruling that a patent on the platform does not by itself confer market power.
