Terracotta

Terracotta ("baked earth" in Italian) is a type of earthenware pottery made from fired clay, typically unglazed, with a characteristic reddish-brown colour derived from iron oxide in the clay. The term also refers to the colour itself and to objects made of the material.

Uses and history

Terracotta has been used since antiquity for sculpture, pottery, roof tiles, bricks, and architectural decoration. Famous examples include the Terracotta Army of China's first emperor Qin Shi Huang (c. 210 BC), Greek Tanagra figurines, and Etruscan sculpture. In the 19th and early 20th centuries glazed architectural terracotta clad many steel-frame buildings, and unglazed terracotta remains common for flowerpots, tiles, and garden ornaments.

Properties

Fired at relatively low temperatures (about 1,000–1,150 °C), terracotta is porous and less hard than stoneware or porcelain, but is inexpensive, workable, and weather-resistant enough for widespread use.
