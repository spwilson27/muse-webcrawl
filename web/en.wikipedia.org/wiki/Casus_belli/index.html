Casus belli

[Image: The sinking of the USS Maine in Havana harbour, 1898, a famous casus belli]

A casus belli (from Latin casus belli, "occasion for war") is an act or event that provokes or is used to justify a war. A casus belli in the direct sense involves offenses or threats directly against the nation declaring war, whereas a casus foederis involves offenses or threats against an ally — usually bound by a mutual defence treaty. Either may be considered an act of war in international law.

Historical usage

The term came into wide use in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries through the writings of Hugo Grotius, Cornelius van Bynkershoek, and Emer de Vattel, as European states sought legal and moral justifications for warfare. In the nineteenth century, states increasingly presented themselves as acting defensively in response to a wrong, and the articulation of a plausible casus belli became an important part of managing international opinion.

Notable examples

The explosion of the battleship USS Maine in Havana harbour in 1898 supplied the immediate cause of the Spanish–American War. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in June 1914 set in train the July Crisis that produced the First World War. The staged Gleiwitz incident of 31 August 1939 gave Nazi Germany a pretext for invading Poland. The Gulf of Tonkin incident of 1964 underpinned the resolution that authorised expanded American involvement in Vietnam, and the claim that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction served as the principal public justification for the 2003 invasion of Iraq — a claim that post-war investigations did not substantiate.

Proximate and underlying causes

Historians and political scientists commonly distinguish between the proximate cause of a war — the immediate casus belli — and its underlying causes, such as territorial rivalry, alliance commitments, economic competition, or domestic political pressure. In modern international law, the UN Charter prohibits the threat or use of force except in self-defence against an armed attack or under Security Council authorisation, which has narrowed the range of legally acceptable grounds for war; states nevertheless continue to frame their actions in the language of just cause, and the concept of humanitarian intervention has been advanced, controversially, as a ground for force.
