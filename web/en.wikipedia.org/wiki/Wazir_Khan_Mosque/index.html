Wazir Khan Mosque

Location: Walled City of Lahore, Punjab, Pakistan
Denomination: Sunni Islam
Completed: 1642
Minarets: 4
Materials: Brick, glazed tile

The Wazir Khan Mosque (Urdu: مسجد وزیر خان) is a 17th-century mosque located in the city of Lahore, Punjab, Pakistan. The mosque was commissioned by the then governor of Punjab, Hakim Shaikh Ilm-ud-din Ansari, known popularly as Wazir Khan, during the reign of the Mughal Emperor Shah Jahan. Construction began in 1634 and was completed in 1641.

The mosque is considered to be the most ornately decorated Mughal-era mosque, and is renowned for its intricate faience tile work known as kashi-kari, as well as its almost entirely fresco-covered interior. Its walls are adorned with Persian motifs, elaborate calligraphy and floral patterns in brilliant blues, greens and yellows.

Location and layout

The mosque lies along the Royal Trail (Shahi Guzargah) within the Walled City of Lahore, near the Delhi Gate, on the route once taken by Mughal processions to the Lahore Fort. It adjoins a bazaar and a calligrapher's bazaar, and includes a row of shops whose rents were intended to fund the mosque's upkeep. The complex also contains a hammam (bathhouse) and the tomb of the Sufi saint Syed Muhammad Ishaq Gazruni, known as Miran Badshah.

Architecture

Built largely of brick and clad in glazed tile, the mosque is organised around a large central courtyard with a central ablution pool. The prayer hall is topped by five domes, and four tall minarets mark the corners of the courtyard — among the earliest such arrangement in Lahore. The prayer hall's interior is almost entirely painted with frescoes depicting trees, fruit, chalices and floral arabesques, while the exterior uses tile mosaics of geometric and floral designs.

Restoration

Conservation work has been undertaken since the 1990s by the Aga Khan Trust for Culture and the Government of Punjab, including the restoration of the mosque's frescoes, tiles and surrounding urban fabric.
