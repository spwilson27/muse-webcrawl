Economic rent

Economic rent is any payment to an owner or factor of production in excess of the costs needed to bring that factor into production. In classical economics, economic rent is any payment made to a factor of production (such as land, labour or capital) in excess of what is required to keep that factor in its current use — its opportunity cost. In neoclassical usage the term is often generalised to include income attributable to monopoly power, exclusive rights, or superior natural endowments.

The concept was developed by the classical economists Adam Smith, David Ricardo and Karl Marx, and was central to the work of Henry George, whose advocacy of a land value tax rested on the idea that rent from land arises from the community rather than the landowner.

Definition

For a factor in inelastic supply, the entire payment can be regarded as rent, because the factor would remain available even if paid less. If a celebrated athlete is paid far more than the salary needed to keep them in the sport, the surplus is rent. Conversely, where supply is perfectly elastic, no rent arises: the payment equals the factor's transfer earnings.

Economic rent differs from ordinary profit because profit, in competitive theory, is eroded by entry, whereas rent persists because the scarce factor cannot be replicated. It also differs from quasi-rent, the short-run return to a temporarily fixed factor such as a specialised machine, which disappears in the long run as supply adjusts.

Classical theory

Ricardo's theory of differential rent explained rent on land by differences in fertility and location: as cultivation extends to less fertile plots, the price of produce is set by the cost on the marginal (no-rent) land, and more fertile plots earn a surplus — rent — equal to their advantage. Marx treated rent as a portion of surplus value appropriated by landowners, distinguishing differential rent I and II and absolute rent.

Rent-seeking

The negative side of rent is rent-seeking: the expenditure of resources to capture rents created by government action — licences, tariffs, quotas or monopolies — rather than to create new wealth. Economists including Gordon Tullock and Anne Krueger argued that the social cost of monopoly and protection includes the resources wasted in competing for the rent itself (the "Tullock rectangle"), beyond the standard deadweight-loss triangle.
