Pacific salmon are the anadromous species of the genus Oncorhynchus native to the North Pacific Ocean and the rivers draining into it, in North America and Asia. The five principal North American species are Chinook (king, O. tshawytscha), coho (silver, O. kisutch), sockeye (red, O. nerka), pink (humpback, O. gorbuscha) and chum (dog, O. keta); the masu and amago salmon occur in Asia. Pacific salmon are a keystone species and a major commercial, recreational and subsistence resource, of profound cultural importance to Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Rim.

Life cycle

Pacific salmon hatch in freshwater gravel nests (redds) in streams and lakes, spend a species-dependent period in freshwater (almost none for pink and chum fry, one or more years for sockeye and coho), then migrate to sea as smolts and grow for one to seven years in the North Pacific. Maturing adults return — with remarkable fidelity — to their natal streams, often changing color and form dramatically (hooked jaws, humps, red or green spawning dress), spawn once, and, uniquely among salmonids together with some other Oncorhynchus, die shortly afterward. Their carcasses import marine-derived nutrients into nutrient-poor rivers and forests, feeding bears, eagles, invertebrates and even riparian trees.

Fisheries and conservation

Fisheries for Pacific salmon in Alaska, British Columbia, the U.S. Pacific Northwest, Russia and Japan land hundreds of thousands of tonnes annually, and massive hatchery programs — especially for pink and chum salmon — supplement wild production. Many populations in the southern part of the range (California, Oregon, Washington, Idaho) declined sharply in the twentieth century due to dams, habitat loss, overfishing and hatchery effects, and numerous distinct population segments are listed under the U.S. Endangered Species Act. Warming oceans and rivers, changing marine survival and competition with hatchery-reared fish are central concerns for twenty-first-century management, and salmon remain flags of river and ocean health across the North Pacific.
