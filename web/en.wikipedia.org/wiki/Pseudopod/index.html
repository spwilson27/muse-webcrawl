Pseudopod

A pseudopod or pseudopodium (plural: pseudopods or pseudopodia) is a temporary arm-like projection of a eukaryotic cell membrane that is developed in the direction of movement. Filled with cytoplasm, pseudopodia primarily consist of actin filaments and may also contain microtubules and intermediate filaments. Pseudopods are used for motility and ingestion.

Occurrence

Pseudopodia are characteristic of amoeboid cells: free-living amoebas such as Amoeba proteus, slime moulds, many foraminifera and radiolaria, and the white blood cells of vertebrates, which use them to crawl and to engulf particles by phagocytosis. Some flagellates and cancer cells invading tissue also move by pseudopod extension.

Types

  • Lobopodia — blunt, finger-like extensions containing both ectoplasm and endoplasm, typical of Amoeba.
  • Filopodia — slender, pointed projections filled with actin bundles.
  • Reticulopodia — branching, net-like networks, as in foraminifera.
  • Axopodia — stiff projections supported by microtubule arrays, as in radiolaria.
  • Lamellipodia — broad, flat sheets at the leading edge of crawling cells.

Mechanism

Extension is driven by actin polymerisation pushing the membrane forward, assisted by changes in cytoplasmic consistency between a gel-like ectoplasm and a flowing sol-like endoplasm; myosin contraction at the rear squeezes the cell body forward. Direction is controlled by chemical signals (chemotaxis), for instance towards food particles or sites of infection.
