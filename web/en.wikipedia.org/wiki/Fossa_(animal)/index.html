The fossa (Cryptoprocta ferox) is a slender, long-tailed mammal endemic to Madagascar. It is the island's largest carnivore and a member of the family Eupleridae, the Malagasy carnivorans, which genetic evidence shows descend from a single colonising ancestor related to mongooses. Superficially cat-like, with a tawny coat, rounded ears and semi-retractable claws, the fossa is an agile climber that hunts both on the ground and in trees.

Behaviour and diet

Fossas are generally solitary and cathemeral, active by day and night. Their prey consists largely of lemurs — which may make up more than half of the diet — along with rodents, birds, reptiles and other small animals. They are unusual among carnivores in their mating system: females mate with multiple males in a traditional tree site, and a transient masculinisation of young females, unique among mammals, has been documented. Litters of two to four pups are raised by the mother alone.

Conservation

The fossa is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. Its forest habitat is being destroyed for agriculture and timber, and fossas are persecuted for preying on poultry. It features prominently in Malagasy folklore and taboo (fady) traditions, which in some areas afford it protection.
