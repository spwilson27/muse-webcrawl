Hugh Dowding

Air Chief Marshal Hugh Caswall Tremenheere Dowding, 1st Baron Dowding
Born: 24 April 1882, Moffat, Scotland
Died: 15 February 1970, Tunbridge Wells, England
Service: British Army, Royal Flying Corps, Royal Air Force
Commands: RAF Fighter Command (1936–1940)
Battles: First World War, Battle of Britain

Hugh Dowding, 1st Baron Dowding (24 April 1882 – 15 February 1970) was an officer in the Royal Air Force. As Air Officer Commanding-in-Chief of RAF Fighter Command during the Battle of Britain in 1940, he is generally credited with playing a crucial role in Britain's defence against Nazi Germany's air attacks.

Early career

Educated at Winchester and the Royal Military Academy, Woolwich, Dowding was commissioned into the Royal Garrison Artillery, served overseas, and learned to fly before the First World War. He held staff and command posts in the Royal Flying Corps and the new RAF, and became Air Member for Supply and Research, overseeing the development of modern monoplane fighters such as the Hurricane and Spitfire.

Fighter Command and the Dowding system

Appointed to lead Fighter Command in 1936, Dowding built the world's first integrated air-defence network — the Dowding system — combining Chain Home radar, the Observer Corps, centralised plotting at Bentley Priory, and sector airfields linked by dedicated telephone lines. He resisted sending more fighter squadrons to France in May 1940, preserving the force that, that summer and autumn, denied the Luftwaffe air superiority and forced Hitler to postpone the planned invasion of Britain.

Later life

Dowding was replaced in November 1940 amid disputes over night defence and "big wing" tactics, was raised to the peerage as Baron Dowding in 1943, and in retirement became a prominent spiritualist. A statue of Dowding stands outside St Clement Danes church in London, and his role is commemorated at the Battle of Britain Memorial.
