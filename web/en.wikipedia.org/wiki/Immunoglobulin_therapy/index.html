Immunoglobulin therapy

Immunoglobulin therapy is the use of a mixture of antibodies (immunoglobulin G, or IgG) to treat a number of health conditions. These conditions include primary immunodeficiency, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, Kawasaki disease, certain cases of HIV/AIDS and measles, Guillain–Barré syndrome, and certain other infections when a more specific immunoglobulin is not available.

Different preparations are administered by injection into a muscle (IMIG), into a vein (IVIG), or under the skin (SCIG). Effects last a few weeks. The therapy is manufactured from the blood plasma of thousands of donors, pooled and purified so that the final product consists overwhelmingly of IgG.

Medical uses

Immunoglobulin therapy is used in two broad ways. In replacement therapy, patients whose bodies cannot produce sufficient antibodies — such as those with X-linked agammaglobulinemia or common variable immunodeficiency — receive regular infusions to maintain protective IgG levels. In immunomodulatory therapy, much larger doses are used to alter an overactive or misdirected immune response in autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, such as immune thrombocytopenia, Kawasaki disease, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy and multifocal motor neuropathy.

Side effects

Common side effects include pain at the site of injection, muscle pain, fever, headache and feeling tired. More serious reactions can include allergic reactions such as anaphylaxis, kidney problems, blood clots, and aseptic meningitis. Caution is advised in people with IgA deficiency, who may carry anti-IgA antibodies. Because the product is made from human plasma, it carries a theoretical risk of transmitting infections, mitigated by donor screening and viral inactivation steps during manufacture.

Mechanism

The exact mechanisms by which immunoglobulin therapy suppresses harmful immune responses are not completely understood. Proposed mechanisms include blockade of Fc receptors on macrophages, modulation of cytokine production, neutralisation of autoantibodies by anti-idiotypic antibodies, saturation of the neonatal Fc receptor (increasing the catabolism of pathogenic IgG), and effects on complement, T cells and B cells. In replacement therapy, the mechanism is straightforward: passively transferred antibodies provide the immunity the patient cannot make.

Supply

Because the product depends on human plasma donations, supply is limited and the therapy is expensive; global demand has repeatedly outstripped supply, leading to prioritisation schemes in several countries. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
