Process calculus

In computer science, the process calculi (or process algebras) are a diverse family of related approaches for formally modelling concurrent systems. Process calculi provide a tool for the high-level description of interactions, communications, and synchronizations between a collection of independent agents or processes. They also provide algebraic laws that allow process descriptions to be manipulated and analyzed, and permit formal reasoning about process equivalences.


Process calculi model agents that interact by passing messages.

Principal calculi

Leading examples of process calculi include the pioneering work on CSP by Tony Hoare, CCS by Robin Milner, and ACP by Jan Bergstra and Jan Willem Klop. Milner's pi-calculus extended these ideas with mobility: the topology of connections between processes can itself change as channels are passed in messages. Other calculi include the ambient calculus, join calculus, and various stochastic and timed variants.

Concepts

Process calculi treat communication as the fundamental primitive: agents perform actions such as sending and receiving on named channels, combine via parallel composition, choice, and recursion, and may hide internal channels through restriction. Behavioural equivalences such as (weak) bisimulation allow different implementations of a system to be compared rigorously. These formalisms underlie tools for verifying communication protocols and concurrent software.
