Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) is the world's third-most widely produced synthetic polymer of plastic, after polyethylene and polypropylene. It is produced by polymerisation of the monomer vinyl chloride. PVC comes in rigid form (used for pipes, window and door profiles, and bottles) and flexible form (made by adding plasticisers such as phthalates, used in cable insulation, flooring, inflatables and medical tubing).

Rigid PVC is widely used for water and drainage pipes in construction.

History

PVC was accidentally synthesised as early as 1838 by Henri Victor Regnault and again in 1872 by Eugen Baumann, appearing as a white solid inside flasks of vinyl chloride exposed to sunlight. It was not commercialised until the 1920s, when Waldo Semon at B.F. Goodrich developed plasticised, flexible PVC in 1926, opening applications from shower curtains to wire insulation. Large-scale production grew rapidly after the Second World War.

Production

Vinyl chloride monomer is made industrially by combining ethylene (from petroleum or natural gas) with chlorine, followed by cracking of the resulting ethylene dichloride. The monomer is polymerised by free-radical polymerisation, most commonly in suspension, to give a white powder that is compounded with heat stabilisers, lubricants, fillers and, for flexible grades, plasticisers.

Health and environment

Vinyl chloride is a known human carcinogen, associated with angiosarcoma of the liver in exposed workers, a link established in the 1970s that led to strict exposure limits. Concerns have also been raised over plasticiser leaching, dioxin formation during uncontrolled burning, and the difficulty of recycling PVC, though industry programmes have increased collection and recycling of PVC building products.
