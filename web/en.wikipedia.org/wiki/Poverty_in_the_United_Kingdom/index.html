Poverty in the United Kingdom

Poverty in the United Kingdom
Usual measureRelative low income: below 60% of median household income
Official statisticsHouseholds Below Average Income (HBAI), DWP
Groups at higher riskChildren, disabled people, some ethnic minority groups, lone parents

Poverty in the United Kingdom is conventionally measured in relative terms: a household is in relative low income if its income, adjusted for household size, is below 60 per cent of the contemporary median, either before or after housing costs. By this measure, around a fifth of the UK population — some 14 million people in recent years — have been recorded as in poverty, with child poverty around 30 per cent. Other approaches use absolute low income (a fixed real-terms threshold), material deprivation, and destitution.

Food banks run by charities such as the Trussell Trust expanded rapidly in the 2010s.

History

Systematic study of British poverty began with Charles Booth's survey of London (1889–1903) and Seebohm Rowntree's study of York (1901), which found roughly a quarter to a third of the urban population in poverty. The post-war welfare state, built on the Beveridge Report of 1942, sharply reduced absolute want, but researchers such as Peter Townsend argued that relative deprivation persisted. Poverty rates fell during the 1950s and 1960s, rose steeply in the 1980s with deindustrialisation and unemployment, and fell somewhat in the late 1990s and 2000s when the Blair government adopted a target to halve and then eradicate child poverty. Rates rose again relative to median incomes for some groups during the 2010s, a period of welfare reform and austerity.

Contemporary profile

  • Most people in poverty now live in working households; in-work poverty reflects low pay, insecure hours and high housing costs.
  • Pensioner poverty, once very high, fell markedly from the 1990s with pension credit and the state pension triple lock, though it remains significant.
  • Food insecurity and food-bank use grew sharply after 2010; the Trussell Trust network distributed millions of emergency food parcels annually by the early 2020s.
  • Higher poverty rates are recorded in London (after housing costs), Wales, the North of England and the West Midlands, and among Bangladeshi, Pakistani and Black households.

Policy

Key instruments include the National Minimum Wage and National Living Wage, Universal Credit (which replaced six legacy benefits), housing benefit and its successors, and child benefit. The Child Poverty Act 2010 set statutory targets that were later removed. Debates continue over benefit levels, the two-child limit, sanctions, and the five-week wait for Universal Credit.
