Yield (engineering)

In materials science and engineering, the yield point is the point on a stress–strain curve that indicates the limit of elastic behaviour and the beginning of plastic behaviour. The stress at which yielding occurs is the yield strength (or yield stress), denoted σy. Below the yield point, a material deforms elastically and returns to its original shape when the applied stress is removed; above it, some deformation is permanent.

Definitions

For materials with a sharply defined transition, such as mild steel, the yield point appears as a distinct drop in load (upper and lower yield points) on the stress–strain curve. Most materials — including aluminium alloys and high-strength steels — yield gradually, so yield strength is conventionally defined by the 0.2% offset method (proof stress): a line is drawn parallel to the elastic portion of the curve, offset by a strain of 0.002, and its intersection with the curve is taken as the yield strength.

Mechanism

Yielding corresponds to the onset of widespread dislocation motion through a crystal lattice. In body-centred cubic metals, interstitial solute atoms (such as carbon in iron) form Cottrell atmospheres that pin dislocations, producing the sharp yield-point phenomenon and Lüders bands. Yielding is pressure-insensitive in most metals and is predicted by criteria such as the von Mises and Tresca yield criteria.

Typical values and design

Yield strengths range from about 35 MPa for annealed aluminium to over 1,400 MPa for maraging and other high-strength steels; structural steel is commonly specified at 250–355 MPa. Because a structure that has yielded has permanently changed shape, engineers design most load-bearing components so that working stresses remain below yield with an appropriate factor of safety. Strengthening mechanisms that raise yield strength include work hardening, grain refinement (Hall–Petch effect), solid-solution strengthening, and precipitation hardening.
