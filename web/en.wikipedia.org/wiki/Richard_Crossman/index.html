Richard Crossman

Richard Howard Stafford Crossman (15 December 1907 – 5 April 1974) was a British Labour Party politician, writer, and editor. He served as a cabinet minister in Harold Wilson's governments and is best remembered posthumously for his candid Diaries of a Cabinet Minister.

Career

Educated at Winchester and New College, Oxford, Crossman became a philosophy don before entering journalism and politics. He was elected MP for Coventry East in 1945 and held the seat until his death. During the war he worked in psychological warfare. A leading Bevanite on the left of the Labour Party, he edited the influential essay collection The Future of Socialism (1956) and served as editor of the New Statesman from 1970 to 1972.

Cabinet and diaries

Under Wilson he was Minister of Housing and Local Government (1964–66), Lord President of the Council and Leader of the House of Commons (1966–68), and Secretary of State for Social Services (1968–70). His diaries, published from 1975 after a celebrated court case in which the government tried to block them, permanently changed conventions about the secrecy of cabinet deliberations and inspired the television satire Yes Minister.
