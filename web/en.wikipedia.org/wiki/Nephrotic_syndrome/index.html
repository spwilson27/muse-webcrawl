Nephrotic syndrome

Nephrotic syndrome is a collection of symptoms due to kidney damage. This includes protein in the urine, low blood albumin levels, high blood lipids, and significant swelling. Other symptoms may include weight gain, feeling tired, and foamy urine. Complications may include blood clots, infections, and high blood pressure.

Signs and symptoms

Nephrotic syndrome is characterized by large amounts of proteinuria (greater than 3.5 grams per 1.73 m² body surface area per day), hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin below 3 g/dL), hyperlipidemia, and edema that begins in the face. Lipiduria (lipids in urine) may also occur, but is not essential for the diagnosis of nephrotic syndrome. Hyponatremia also occurs with a low fractional sodium excretion.

Causes

Nephrotic syndrome has many causes and may either be the result of a glomerular disease that can be either limited to the kidney, called primary nephrotic syndrome (including minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and membranous nephropathy), or a condition that affects the kidney and other parts of the body, called secondary nephrotic syndrome (including diabetic kidney disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, and amyloidosis).

Pathophysiology

The glomerulus filters blood, and normally very little protein passes through its filtration barrier, which consists of fenestrated endothelium, the glomerular basement membrane, and podocytes. Damage to this barrier increases permeability to plasma proteins such as albumin, which are then lost in the urine. The resulting fall in plasma oncotic pressure allows fluid to move into the interstitial tissues, producing edema, and stimulates the liver to increase lipoprotein synthesis, producing hyperlipidemia.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on urine testing showing heavy proteinuria, blood tests showing low serum albumin and elevated lipids, and in many cases a kidney biopsy to identify the underlying glomerular lesion.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. Minimal change disease in children usually responds to corticosteroids. Additional measures include sodium restriction, diuretics for edema, ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers to reduce proteinuria, statins for hyperlipidemia, and anticoagulation when the risk of thrombosis is high.

Epidemiology

Nephrotic syndrome can affect people of any age. In children it most commonly occurs between the ages of 2 and 7 and is usually due to minimal change disease; in adults, membranous nephropathy and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis are leading causes.
