Earth shelter

TypeBuilding design
Related conceptsPassive house, green roof, underground living

An earth shelter, also referred to as an earth house, earth-bermed house or earth-sheltered house, is a structure (usually a house) in which earth (soil) is banked against the walls, covers the roof, or the building is set wholly or partly below ground. Earth sheltering takes advantage of the thermal mass and insulating properties of soil to moderate indoor temperatures, reduce heat loss and protect the building from wind and weather.

Types

Three broad arrangements are recognised. In an earth-bermed design, earth is piled against exterior walls but the roof remains exposed. In an elevational design, the building is set into a slope with one facade — usually facing the equator — left open for light and access. In a fully below-grade or subterranean design, the building is constructed in an excavated pit, often around a sunken courtyard or atrium that provides light and ventilation.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include large energy savings for heating and cooling because soil a few metres down stays at a nearly constant temperature year-round; protection from storms, fire and noise; low external maintenance; and the return of the building's footprint to vegetation. Disadvantages include high initial construction cost, the need for heavy waterproofing and structural strength to resist earth pressure, risks of damp and poor air quality if ventilation is inadequate, limited natural light, difficulty obtaining mortgages and insurance, and problems with planning permission and resale.

History

Earth-sheltered dwellings are ancient — examples include the pit houses of many cultures, the cave dwellings of Cappadocia and northern China, sod houses on the North American prairies, and Icelandic turf houses. Modern earth sheltering became popular during the 1970s energy crisis, with architects such as Malcolm Wells promoting underground designs, and it remains a niche strand of green building.
