Halakha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Halakha (Hebrew: הלכה, “the way”) is the collective body of Jewish religious laws derived from the Torah (the Written Law) and the Oral Torah — the Mishnah (redacted c. 200 CE), the Talmud (Jerusalem, c. 400; Babylonian, c. 500–600 CE), medieval codes (Maimonides' Mishneh Torah, 12th century; Joseph Karo's Shulchan Aruch, 1565) and the ongoing responsa literature in which rabbis answer questions of practice. It covers ritual (Sabbath, dietary law — kashrut, prayer), civil and criminal law, family law and ethics, traditionally classified into commandments between persons and God and between persons.

In most of Jewish history halakha functioned as the actual law of self-governing communities; emancipation made its observance voluntary in the diaspora, where Orthodox Judaism treats it as binding divine law, Conservative Judaism as binding but evolving, and Reform Judaism as advisory. In Israel it has public force in defined areas — rabbinical courts hold jurisdiction over Jewish marriage and divorce, and state institutions observe aspects of kashrut and Sabbath — generating enduring debates over religion and state, personal status (the “who is a Jew” question) and conversion.
