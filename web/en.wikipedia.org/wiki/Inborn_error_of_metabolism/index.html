Inborn error of metabolism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Inborn errors of metabolism are a class of genetic disorders in which a defect in a single gene impairs the function of an enzyme or transport protein in a metabolic pathway. The resulting block causes substances to accumulate to toxic levels, or prevents the synthesis of compounds the body needs. The term was introduced by the British physician Archibald Garrod in 1908, in his study of alkaptonuria, albinism, cystinuria, and pentosuria.

Examples

Hundreds of inborn errors are known. Among the most significant are phenylketonuria (inability to metabolize the amino acid phenylalanine), galactosemia, maple syrup urine disease, urea cycle disorders, lysosomal storage diseases such as Tay–Sachs disease and Gaucher's disease, and glycogen storage diseases. Most are inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern.

Diagnosis and treatment

Many countries operate newborn screening programmes — typically using a heel-prick blood sample analysed by tandem mass spectrometry — that can detect dozens of these disorders before symptoms appear. Early diagnosis matters: for conditions such as phenylketonuria, prompt dietary restriction prevents severe intellectual disability. Treatments include special diets, enzyme replacement therapy, vitamin cofactors, and, for some conditions, organ transplantation or gene therapy.
