Automatic drawing

Automatic drawing is a method of art-making in which the artist suppresses conscious control over the hand, allowing the drawing to develop spontaneously. Developed most prominently by the surrealists, it was intended — like automatic writing — to let the unconscious mind express itself directly, free of rational planning, aesthetic judgement or moral censoring.

Origins

Automatic techniques had precedents in spiritualist mediumship of the nineteenth century, in which mediums produced drawings and writings claimed to come from spirits, and in the experiments of psychologists exploring the subconscious. The surrealists, influenced by Sigmund Freud's method of free association and by André Breton's 1924 Manifesto of Surrealism, adopted automatism as a founding method of their movement.

Surrealist practice

André Masson is the artist most closely associated with automatic drawing: from 1923–24 he let his pen move rapidly over paper without a preconceived image, later finding and reinforcing emergent forms in the tangle of lines. Joan Miró's work of the mid-1920s grew out of automatic sketches converted into biomorphic paintings. Related chance procedures included Max Ernst's frottage (pencil rubbings of textured surfaces) and grattage, and Óscar Domínguez's decalcomania.

Influence

Automatism strongly influenced later movements. The gestural painting of abstract expressionism — particularly Jackson Pollock's poured paintings — inherited its emphasis on the unmediated act, and surrealist automatism fed into art informel and the work of artists such as Arshile Gorky and Roberto Matta. The term has also been applied retrospectively to the drawings of outsider artists and mediums such as Madge Gill and Hilma af Klint.
