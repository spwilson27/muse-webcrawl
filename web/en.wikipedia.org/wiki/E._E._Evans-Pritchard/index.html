E. E. Evans-Pritchard

Born: 21 September 1902, Crowborough, Sussex, England

Died: 11 September 1973, Oxford, England

Known for: Ethnographies of the Azande and Nuer; theories of witchcraft and religion

Sir Edward Evan Evans-Pritchard (21 September 1902 – 11 September 1973), known as E. E. Evans-Pritchard, was an English anthropologist who was instrumental in the development of social anthropology. He was Professor of Social Anthropology at the University of Oxford from 1946 to 1970.

Life and career

Evans-Pritchard was educated at Winchester College and Exeter College, Oxford, and studied at the London School of Economics under Bronisław Malinowski and Charles Seligman. Between 1926 and 1940 he carried out fieldwork in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, first among the Azande and later among the Nuer. During the Second World War he served with the Sudan Defence Force and raised irregular forces among the Anuak. In 1946 he succeeded A. R. Radcliffe-Brown in the Oxford chair. He was knighted in 1971 and converted to Roman Catholicism in 1944.

Work

His first classic monograph, Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande (1937), showed that Azande beliefs about witchcraft formed a coherent, rational system of thought for explaining misfortune, a landmark argument against the idea of a "primitive mentality". The Nuer (1940) described the political organisation of a society without rulers, based on segmentary lineage systems — a model that profoundly influenced political anthropology. Later works, including Nuer Religion (1956) and Theories of Primitive Religion (1965), turned towards history and the humanities, arguing that anthropology was closer to historiography than to natural science.

Influence

Evans-Pritchard's emphasis on translation of cultures, meaning and history shaped generations of anthropologists, and his ethnographies remain standard texts. Critics have noted the colonial context of his Sudanese fieldwork and his wartime role in colonial administration.
