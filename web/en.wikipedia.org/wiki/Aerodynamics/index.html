Aerodynamics (Ancient Greek: ἀήρ aero (air) + δυναμική (dynamics)) is the study of the motion of air, particularly when affected by a solid object, such as an aeroplane wing. It is a sub-field of fluid dynamics and gas dynamics, sharing much theory with both. Aerodynamics is used in the design of aircraft, spacecraft, automobiles, engines, wind turbines and buildings, and in the prediction of forces and moments acting on them.

History

Sir George Cayley founded the modern science in 1799–1810: he identified weight, lift, drag and thrust as the four forces of flight, showed that a cambered wing produces lift, and proposed the fixed-wing configuration with separate propulsion and control. Otto Lilienthal's gliding experiments (1891–96) and published lift data carried the science into practice. In the early twentieth century Ludwig Prandtl introduced boundary-layer theory, and researchers such as Frederick Lanchester and Nikolai Zhukovsky explained lift through circulation; high-speed and supersonic aerodynamics were developed by, among others, Theodore von Kármán.

Fundamental concepts

An airfoil moving through air experiences lift, produced by the pressure distribution around it, and drag, which is divided into skin-friction drag, pressure (form) drag and induced drag — the by-product of lift. Flow can be laminar or turbulent, attached or separated; separation causes a stall and a rapid loss of lift. Flows are classified by speed relative to the speed of sound: subsonic (below Mach 1), transonic, supersonic and hypersonic. Compressibility effects dominate at high Mach numbers, while at low speeds air is treated as incompressible and lift is related to circulation by the Kutta–Joukowski theorem.

Methods

Aerodynamicists combine theory (the Navier–Stokes and Euler equations), wind-tunnel testing of scale models, flight testing, and computational fluid dynamics (CFD). Dimensionless numbers — the Reynolds number and Mach number — allow results from models to be scaled to full-size vehicles. The professional community is represented by bodies such as the American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics.
