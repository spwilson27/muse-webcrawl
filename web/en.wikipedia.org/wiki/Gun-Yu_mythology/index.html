Gun-Yu mythology

The Gun-Yu myth (Chinese: 鯀禹神話) is a major flood myth of ancient China concerning Gun (鯀, Gǔn), his son Yu the Great (禹, Yǔ), and their attempts to control the catastrophic flooding of the Yellow River and other waterways. It is one of the central flood narratives in Chinese mythology and is closely tied to the legendary founding of the Xia dynasty.

Yu the Great controlling the flood waters

The myth

According to the myth, during the reign of the legendary Emperor Yao, China was devastated by a great flood that inundated farmland and homes. Yao appointed Gun, a descendant of the Yellow Emperor, to control the waters. Gun stole Xirang (息壤), a magical self-expanding soil, from the gods and used it to build dams and dikes to block the flood. However, his method of damming failed, and after nine years the waters continued to rise. For his failure—and for stealing Xirang—Gun was executed at Feather Mountain (Yushan) by Shun, Yao's successor, in some versions by the fire god Zhurong.

From Gun's body (in some accounts, from a dragon or after three years of non-decay of the corpse), his son Yu emerged. Yu took up his father's task but used the opposite method: instead of blocking the water, he dredged channels and guided rivers to the sea, assisted by the dragon Yinglong, who dragged its tail to carve the waterways. After thirteen years of labour—during which, famously, he passed his own home three times without entering—Yu succeeded in controlling the flood. In gratitude, Shun abdicated the throne to Yu, who founded the Xia dynasty.

Interpretation

The myth contrasts Gun's failed strategy of obstruction with Yu's successful strategy of channeling, and it has long been read as an allegory of good governance: working with natural forces rather than against them. Some scholars see in the story a memory of early hydraulic engineering on the North China Plain. Sources for the myth include the Shanhaijing (Classic of Mountains and Seas), the Shangshu (Book of Documents), and Sima Qian's Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian).
