Pyridoxine

Pyridoxine
IUPAC name4,5-Bis(hydroxymethyl)-2-methylpyridin-3-ol
FormulaC8H11NO3
Molar mass169.18 g/mol
CAS number65-23-9
GroupVitamin B6 (one of three vitamers)
Common formPyridoxine hydrochloride (supplements)
Active formPyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP)

Pyridoxine is one of the three natural vitamers of vitamin B6, together with pyridoxal and pyridoxamine. It is a pyridine derivative found abundantly in plant foods, whereas animal tissues contain mainly the phosphorylated forms of pyridoxal and pyridoxamine. In the body all B6 vitamers are converted to pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP), the coenzyme required by more than 140 enzyme activities — especially transaminases and decarboxylases of amino-acid metabolism, glycogen phosphorylase, and enzymes of neurotransmitter and haem synthesis. Pyridoxine was isolated in 1938 and first synthesised in 1939; the name derives from its pyridine ring.

Nutrition and deficiency

Good sources include poultry, fish, potatoes, chickpeas, bananas and fortified cereals. Isolated deficiency is rare, usually accompanying alcoholism, malabsorption or treatment with the tuberculosis drug isoniazid, which inactivates B6 (prophylactic pyridoxine is co-prescribed). Deficiency causes seborrhoeic dermatitis, glossitis, confusion and, particularly in infants, convulsions. Adults require roughly 1.3–1.7 mg of vitamin B6 per day.

Uses and toxicity

Pyridoxine (often combined with doxylamine) is a first-line treatment for nausea and vomiting in pregnancy, and is used for certain B6-responsive seizure disorders and sideroblastic anaemias. Paradoxically, chronic high-dose supplementation (typically hundreds of milligrams daily, and possibly lower) can itself cause a sensory peripheral neuropathy — numbness and tingling — which led regulators in several countries to set upper limits for supplemental B6.
