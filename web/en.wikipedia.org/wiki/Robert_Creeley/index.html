Robert Creeley

Born: May 21, 1926, Arlington, Massachusetts
Died: March 30, 2005, Odessa, Texas
Occupation: Poet, essayist, teacher
Movement: Black Mountain poets

Robert White Creeley (May 21, 1926 – March 30, 2005) was an American poet and author of more than sixty books. He is usually associated with the Black Mountain poets, though his spare, halting lyric style diverged from that group's other members. His most famous maxim—"form is never more than an extension of content"—became a cornerstone of postwar American poetics.

Life and career

Creeley grew up in Massachusetts, lost his father and one eye in early childhood, and entered Harvard in 1943, leaving before graduation to drive an ambulance for the American Field Service in India and Burma. In the early 1950s he corresponded intensively with Charles Olson, taught at Black Mountain College, and edited the Black Mountain Review. His first widely noticed collection was For Love: Poems 1950–1960 (1962), followed by Words (1967) and Pieces (1969), in which his lines grew even more minimal. He taught for many years at the State University of New York at Buffalo, and later at Brown University.

Style and influence

Creeley's poems are typically short, built of brief lines whose breaks register hesitations of speech and thought. Love, domestic life, aging, and the difficulty of saying what one means are his recurring subjects. He championed jazz-based improvisation, collaborated with visual artists, and influenced generations of poets from the New York School to the Language poets. He served as New York State Poet from 1989 to 1991 and received the Bollingen Prize in 1999.
