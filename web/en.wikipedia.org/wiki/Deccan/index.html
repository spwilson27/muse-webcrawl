Deccan

The Deccan is a large plateau and region in South and Central India, making up most of the southern part of the country. It is bounded on the west by the Western Ghats, on the east by the Eastern Ghats, and on the north by the Satpura and Vindhya ranges. The name derives from the Sanskrit word dakṣiṇa (दक्षिण), meaning "south", via the Prakrit dakkhiṇa and the Marathi dakhkhana.

Geography

The Deccan Plateau rises to an average elevation of about 600 metres (2,000 ft) and slopes gently eastward, so that its major rivers — the Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri — flow from the Western Ghats eastward to the Bay of Bengal. Much of the plateau is composed of basalt from the enormous Deccan Traps lava flows, erupted around 66 million years ago near the end of the Cretaceous period — eruptions that have been linked to the mass extinction that killed the non-avian dinosaurs. The region covers substantial parts of the modern states of Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu.

History

The Deccan has a rich political history distinct from that of northern India. It was ruled in antiquity and the early medieval period by dynasties such as the Satavahanas, Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas, and later the Marathas, and was home to the wealthy sultanates of the Bahmani kingdom and its successors at Bijapur, Golconda, and Ahmadnagar. The city of Hyderabad, founded in 1591, became the seat of the Nizams, among the richest rulers in the world in the 18th–20th centuries. The plateau's black cotton soil, dry climate, and mineral wealth shaped its agriculture and industry, and it hosts major technology centers including Hyderabad and Bangalore (Bengaluru).
