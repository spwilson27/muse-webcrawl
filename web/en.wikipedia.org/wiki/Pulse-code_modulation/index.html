Pulse-code modulation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pulse-code modulation (PCM) is a method used to digitally represent analog signals. It is the standard form of digital audio in computers, compact discs, digital telephony and other digital audio applications. In a PCM stream, the amplitude of the analog signal is sampled at uniform intervals, and each sample is quantized to the nearest value within a range of digital steps.

History

PCM was invented by British engineer Alec Reeves in 1937 while working for International Telephone and Telegraph in France. His patent, filed in 1938, described a system for transmitting speech by sampling the waveform and sending coded pulses. The first practical implementations came during World War II, in systems such as the SIGSALY secure voice system used by the Allies.

In the 1960s, PCM became the basis of digital telephone networks, with the T1 carrier system in North America carrying 24 digitized voice channels. In 1982 PCM was adopted for the Compact Disc standard, using 16-bit samples at a 44.1 kHz rate.

How it works

PCM conversion involves three steps. First, sampling measures the analog signal at discrete time intervals; the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states the sampling rate must exceed twice the highest signal frequency to allow perfect reconstruction. Second, quantization maps each continuous sample value to the nearest of a finite set of levels. Third, encoding represents each level as a binary number, producing the digital bit stream.

Applications

PCM underlies WAV and AIFF audio files, the audio tracks of DVDs and Blu-ray discs, and digital telephony standards such as G.711. Variants include differential PCM (DPCM) and adaptive differential PCM (ADPCM), which reduce bit rate by encoding differences between successive samples. Linear PCM (LPCM) refers to uncompressed, uniformly quantized PCM.
