Bull market

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: The Charging Bull statue near Wall Street, New York]
The Charging Bull statue, a symbol of bull markets

A bull market is a period in which investment prices rise faster than their historical average, encouraging buying. The term is most often applied to stock markets but can describe anything that is traded, such as bonds, real estate, currencies, and commodities. A common, though retrospective, working definition is a rise of 20 percent or more in a broad market index from its recent low; the opposite condition is a bear market, conventionally a fall of 20 percent or more from a recent high.

Characteristics

Bull markets are generally accompanied by strong economic indicators such as rising gross domestic product, falling unemployment, and growing corporate profits. Investor confidence is high, initial public offerings increase, and market volatility is often low. The terms "bull" and "bear" are popularly explained by the animals' methods of attack: a bull thrusts its horns upward, while a bear swipes its paws downward.

Causes

Sustained price rises may be driven by economic expansion, low interest rates, technological change, or shifts in government policy. Expectations themselves are self-reinforcing to a degree: rising prices attract buyers, whose demand pushes prices higher. Economists debate how much of any given bull market reflects fundamentals versus speculative excess, and bull markets can end in sharp corrections or crashes when sentiment turns.

Investor psychology

Bull markets are associated with optimism, greed, and risk-seeking behaviour; late stages may feature widespread speculation by inexperienced investors and the belief that prices can only rise — conditions John Kenneth Galbraith and others identified as features of financial manias. Conversely, bull markets often begin amid pessimism, when valuations are low, a pattern captured by the maxim attributed to John Templeton that bull markets are "born on pessimism, grow on scepticism, mature on optimism, and die on euphoria."

Notable examples

The United States experienced a celebrated bull market in the 1920s that ended in the Wall Street Crash of 1929. The post-1982 bull market, punctuated by the 1987 crash and the dot-com boom, ran to 2000. The bull market that began at the depths of the 2008 financial crisis in March 2009 became one of the longest on record, ending with the COVID-19 crash of February-March 2020, after which markets again rose to new highs. Japan's late-1980s asset price bubble is a classic example of a bull market in both equities and real estate.
