The Horse in Motion is a series of photographs by English-American photographer Eadweard Muybridge, produced in 1878 at Palo Alto, California, and published as cabinet cards under titles including Sallie Gardner at a Gallop. The series is celebrated as a landmark in the study of animal locomotion and a foundational precursor of motion pictures.

Background

The project was commissioned by Leland Stanford, former governor of California and founder of Stanford University, a racehorse owner eager to settle a popular question of the day: whether all four hooves of a galloping horse leave the ground at the same moment. Traditional painting depicted the gallop with legs extended fore and aft in the "rocking-horse" pose, but this had never been verified.

Muybridge had made an earlier, less conclusive attempt in Sacramento in 1873. At Stanford's Palo Alto stock farm, he arranged a battery of cameras—ultimately twelve, and later twenty-four—along a track, each triggered by threads stretched across the horse's path. On 15 June 1878, the mare Sallie Gardner galloped past the cameras, breaking the threads in sequence and producing a series of images at intervals of fractions of a second.

Findings

The photographs demonstrated that a galloping horse does indeed become fully airborne, but with its legs gathered beneath its body rather than extended as artists had conventionally shown. The result contradicted centuries of artistic convention and was initially received with astonishment; reproductions rapidly circulated in scientific and artistic journals.

Legacy

Muybridge went on to refine his techniques with the zoopraxiscope, a device that projected the sequential images to recreate the illusion of movement, an important step toward cinema. His later work at the University of Pennsylvania produced thousands of locomotion studies of humans and animals. The June 1878 experiments at Palo Alto are regarded as a pivotal moment in both photography and the prehistory of film.
