Many-valued logic

Many-valued logic
FieldMathematical and philosophical logic
Key figuresJan Łukasiewicz, Emil Post, Stephen Cole Kleene, Lotfi Zadeh
ExamplesThree-valued logic, fuzzy logic, intuitionistic logic (Kripke semantics), relevance logic

Many-valued logic is a propositional calculus in which there are more than two truth values. Classical logic is two-valued (true and false), and a long tradition — going back to Aristotle's discussion of future contingents and the sea battle tomorrow — has questioned that bivalence. In the 1920s Jan Łukasiewicz, and independently Emil Post, constructed the first systematic many-valued logics, Łukasiewicz's three-valued system taking "possible" as a third value between true and false.

Examples

Three-valued logics include Łukasiewicz's Ł3, Kleene's strong logic K3 (where the third value means "unknown"), and Bochvar's system; they differ in their truth tables for implication and conjunction. Fuzzy logic, introduced by Lotfi Zadeh in 1965, extends to infinitely many degrees of truth in the interval [0, 1] and underlies fuzzy control systems. Other systems include Post's m-valued logics, Gödel logics, and product logic. Intuitionistic logic admits a many-valued (Heyting-algebra) semantics.

Applications

Many-valued logics are applied to vagueness (the sorites paradox), database query languages handling NULL values (SQL effectively uses three-valued logic), circuit design with an "undefined" state, and linguistics. Philosophically, they raise the question of whether truth comes in degrees or whether extra values merely encode ignorance — a debate between "epistemic" and "ontic" readings that remains unresolved.

Semantics

Algebraically, many-valued logics are studied through structures such as MV-algebras (for Łukasiewicz logic) and residuated lattices, and they connect to category-theoretic and topos approaches in modern logic.
