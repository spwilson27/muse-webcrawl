Liquidity risk

Liquidity risk
CategoryFinancial risk
Main typesFunding liquidity risk; market liquidity risk
Notable episodesNorthern Rock (2007); global financial crisis (2008)
RegulationBasel III liquidity coverage ratio and net stable funding ratio

Liquidity risk is the risk that an entity will be unable to meet its financial obligations as they fall due, or will be able to do so only at excessive cost. It is often described as the risk of being solvent on paper but unable to pay: assets may exceed liabilities in value while being impossible to sell or borrow against quickly enough.

Types

Funding liquidity risk is the risk that a firm cannot obtain cash to meet withdrawals, maturing debts or margin calls. Banks are structurally exposed because they borrow short (deposits and wholesale funding) and lend long (mortgages and loans); a loss of confidence can produce a bank run or a wholesale funding freeze. Market liquidity risk is the risk that an asset cannot be sold quickly without a large price concession; it increases bid–offer spreads and can transform price falls into fire sales that depress the value of similar assets held by others. The two types reinforce one another in crises, a dynamic sometimes called a liquidity spiral.

Management and regulation

Firms manage liquidity risk by holding buffers of high-quality liquid assets, diversifying funding sources, lengthening funding maturities, stress-testing cash flows, and maintaining contingency funding plans. Central banks act as lender of last resort against good collateral. The 2007–08 financial crisis — in which the British bank Northern Rock suffered a depositor run in September 2007 after its wholesale funding dried up, and institutions worldwide faced a sudden seizure of money markets — led the Basel Committee to add the liquidity coverage ratio (requiring enough liquid assets to cover 30 days of stressed outflows) and the net stable funding ratio to the Basel III framework.

Measurement

Common metrics include liquidity gaps by maturity bucket, ratios of liquid assets to short-term liabilities, loan-to-deposit ratios, and market measures such as bid–ask spreads, trading volumes and the price impact of trades.
