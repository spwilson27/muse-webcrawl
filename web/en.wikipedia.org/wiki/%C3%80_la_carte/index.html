À la carte

À la carte (pronounced [a la kaʁt]) is an English language loan phrase from French meaning "according to the menu". It is used in restaurant terminology in one of two ways:

  • It may refer to a menu of items priced and ordered separately, in contrast to a table d'hôte or prix fixe meal, in which a complete multi-course meal is offered at a fixed price.
  • It may also refer to ordering a main course item on its own, without the accompaniments that would normally be included in a set meal.

Usage

The phrase is drawn from the French expression à la carte, "on/according to the card (i.e. the menu)". The first attested use of the phrase in English dates to 1816, in the diary of the art collector and travel writer Philip Yorke, 2nd Earl of Hardwicke. A related early usage appears in a letter by Lord Dudley (1821).

In an à la carte menu, each dish listed is priced separately, so the diner combines dishes to make a meal of their choosing and pays the sum of the individual prices. This contrasts with a set menu, in which a fixed combination of courses is offered for a single price. A counterpoint in the English language, the term is also used metaphorically outside of dining to mean obtaining services on a separate, itemised basis—for example, "à la carte" television services, in which customers pay only for the channels they choose rather than for a pre-packaged bundle.
