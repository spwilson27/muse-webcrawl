Tar

Tar is a dark brown or black viscous liquid of hydrocarbons and free carbon, obtained by the destructive distillation of organic materials such as wood, coal, peat, and petroleum. The word is often loosely applied to bitumen (asphalt), as in "tar and gravel" roofing and the La Brea Tar Pits, though natural asphalt is geologically distinct from distilled tar.

Wood tar

Pine tar has been produced in Scandinavia since the Iron Age by burning pine wood slowly in earth-covered pits or kilns. It was an essential naval store for waterproofing wooden ships, rigging, and sails — giving rise to "Jack Tar" as slang for a sailor — and Sweden and Finland exported it on a large scale. Pine tar is still used in traditional boat maintenance, veterinary care, soaps and shampoos for skin conditions such as dandruff and psoriasis, and as a flavouring (for example in Finnish tar sweets and tar-flavoured alcohol).

Coal tar

Coal tar is a by-product of coke production and coal gasification. In the 19th century it became the founding feedstock of the organic chemical industry: from it came aniline dyes (beginning with Perkin's mauveine in 1856), phenol, naphthalene, creosote for timber preservation, and pitch for electrodes and roofing. Coal tar is rich in polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, many of them carcinogenic, and it remains on pharmacopoeias as a treatment for eczema and psoriasis despite its odour and staining.

Uses and health

"Tar" in cigarette smoke is the total particulate matter minus nicotine and water, and is the principal vehicle of smoking-related carcinogens. Industrially, tars are used for road surfacing (historically "tarmac"), waterproofing, and preserving timber and ropes, though environmental and health concerns have curtailed many applications.
