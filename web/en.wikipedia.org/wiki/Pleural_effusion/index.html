Pleural effusion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pleural effusion is the accumulation of an abnormal amount of fluid in the pleural cavity, the potential space between the two layers of pleura surrounding each lung. A small amount of pleural fluid is normally present to lubricate the pleural surfaces, but effusions of hundreds of millilitres or more can compress the underlying lung and cause breathlessness.

Causes

Pleural effusions are classified by their fluid chemistry into transudates, caused by systemic factors altering fluid formation or absorption — most commonly heart failure, cirrhosis and hypoalbuminaemia — and exudates, caused by local disease of the pleura or lung such as pneumonia, malignancy, tuberculosis and pulmonary embolism. Light's criteria, comparing protein and lactate dehydrogenase levels in pleural fluid and serum, are widely used to distinguish the two.

Signs and diagnosis

Examination findings include reduced chest expansion, dullness to percussion and diminished breath sounds over the effusion. Upright chest radiographs show blunting of the costophrenic angle and, in larger effusions, a meniscus. Thoracic ultrasound guides safe diagnostic aspiration (thoracentesis); fluid is sent for chemistry, microbiology and cytology. Blood in the fluid is a haemothorax, pus an empyema, and milky lymphatic fluid a chylothorax.

Treatment

Management is directed at the underlying cause, such as diuretics for heart failure or antibiotics for pneumonia. Large symptomatic effusions are drained therapeutically; recurrent malignant effusions may be treated with pleurodesis or a long-term indwelling pleural catheter.
