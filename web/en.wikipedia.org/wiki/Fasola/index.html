Fasola

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fasola is the name given to the tradition of shape-note singing associated with The Sacred Harp and related tunebooks, taking its name from the syllables — fa, sol, la — of the four-shape solmization system used in early American singing schools. The term is also used more broadly by singers to distinguish traditional Sacred Harp practice from other forms of choral or hymn singing.

The singing tradition

In a fasola singing, participants sit in a hollow square, with one voice part on each side, facing inward toward a leader who stands in the center and beats time. There is no audience and no rehearsal: each song is led by a different singer, and the class first "sings the shapes," pronouncing the solmization syllables (fa, sol, la, mi) printed as shaped noteheads, before singing the words. Singings are typically all-day events with a potluck midday meal known as "dinner on the grounds."

History

The practice descends from the singing schools of colonial New England and the tunebooks of the early republic, which printed melodies in patent notes with distinct shapes for each syllable to teach sight-singing. The four-shape system derives from the older Guidonian hexachord. The Sacred Harp, first published in Georgia in 1844 by Benjamin Franklin White and Elisha J. King, preserved the tradition in the rural South long after it faded in the North. The late twentieth century saw a revival, with singings spreading across the United States and internationally, drawing many participants from outside the tradition's original church communities.

Musical characteristics

Fasola music features open harmonies, frequent parallel fifths and octaves avoided in classical counterpoint, modal melodies, and vigorous, unaccompanied full-voiced singing. Tunes are classified as plain tunes, fuging tunes (with imitative entries), anthems, and odes.
