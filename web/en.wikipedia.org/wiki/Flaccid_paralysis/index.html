Flaccid paralysis

Flaccid paralysis is a neurological condition characterised by weakness or paralysis and reduced muscle tone (hypotonia) without other obvious cause. It results from damage to the lower motor neurons — the motor neurons in the spinal cord and brainstem, their axons in the peripheral nerves, the neuromuscular junction, or the muscle itself. Affected muscles are floppy, reflexes are diminished or absent (hyporeflexia), and over time the muscle may atrophy and show fasciculations.

Causes

Causes include poliomyelitis (in which poliovirus destroys anterior horn cells), Guillain–Barré syndrome, peripheral nerve injury, botulism, hypokalaemic periodic paralysis, and certain toxins and medications. Acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) is a clinical syndrome of rapid-onset limb weakness; because polio is one cause, the World Health Organization uses AFP surveillance in children under 15 as a key tool in the global polio eradication programme — any case of acute flaccid paralysis must be reported and investigated with stool specimens. A related condition, acute flaccid myelitis, associated in many cases with enterovirus D68, has caused clusters of polio-like illness in children.

Comparison with spastic paralysis

Flaccid paralysis is contrasted with spastic paralysis, which arises from upper motor neuron lesions and presents with increased tone, brisk reflexes and weakness without significant atrophy. This distinction helps localise the lesion in the nervous system. In the period immediately after a spinal cord injury, "spinal shock" can transiently produce a flaccid picture below the lesion before spasticity develops.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause where possible (for example, immunotherapy for Guillain–Barré syndrome), together with supportive care, respiratory monitoring when respiratory muscles are involved, and physiotherapy to maintain joint mobility and prevent contractures.
