Sea denial

Sea denial is a naval strategy of preventing an adversary from using a sea area for military or commercial purposes, without necessarily seeking to control that area oneself. It is the weaker power's counterpart to sea control: a navy that cannot defeat an enemy fleet in battle may still make the seas too dangerous for that fleet to operate in.

Means

Sea denial is typically pursued with submarines, naval mines, land-based anti-ship missiles and aircraft, fast attack craft, and torpedoes — forces that can threaten major warships from dispersed, survivable platforms. Historical examples include Germany's U-boat campaigns against Allied shipping in both world wars, which sought to deny Britain the use of its sea lanes rather than command them.

Anti-access/area denial

In modern strategic writing, sea denial is closely associated with anti-access/area denial (A2/AD) concepts, in which integrated missile, sensor and submarine networks aim to keep carrier groups and other forces at a distance from a defended region. The sinking of the Russian cruiser Moskva in 2022 by Ukrainian shore-based missiles — used by a state whose navy was far weaker than its opponent's — is often cited as a contemporary sea denial success.

Sea control contrast

Unlike sea control, sea denial does not allow the denying power to use the sea freely itself; it is a negative, spoiling strategy, often adopted by the inferior fleet.
