Civil defense

Civil defense (civil defence in Commonwealth English) or civil protection is an effort to protect the citizens of a state (generally non-combatants) from human-made and natural disasters. It uses the principles of emergency operations: prevention, mitigation, preparation, response, or emergency evacuation and recovery. Programs of this sort were initially discussed at least as early as the 1920s and were implemented in many countries during the 1930s as the threat of war and aerial bombardment grew. Civil-defense programs became widespread after the advent of nuclear weapons, and most countries shifted to an "all-hazards" approach covering natural disasters after the Cold War.

History

Early civil-defense measures grew out of the aerial bombing campaigns of the First World War, when German Zeppelin and Gotha bomber raids on British cities led to the organization of air raid precautions. In the 1930s, Britain built up the Air Raid Precautions (ARP) organization, recruited wardens, distributed gas masks, and planned mass evacuations of children from cities — preparations that were activated during the Blitz of 1940–41, when wardens, fire watchers, and rescue services worked alongside the Auxiliary Fire Service and Women's Voluntary Service.

In the United States, a federal Office of Civilian Defense was created in May 1941 under Fiorello La Guardia and Eleanor Roosevelt. After the Second World War the organization lapsed, but the onset of the Cold War revived it: the Federal Civil Defense Act of 1950 created the Federal Civil Defense Administration, and civil defense became a prominent feature of life during the nuclear arms race.

Cold War civil defense

During the 1950s and 1960s, governments in the United States, the Soviet Union, Britain, and elsewhere promoted fallout shelters, air-raid drills, and public-information campaigns. The animated film Duck and Cover (1951), featuring Bert the Turtle, taught American schoolchildren to take cover in the event of a nuclear flash. The Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 spurred a major American shelter program, under which public buildings were stocked with water, food rations, and radiation meters and marked with distinctive yellow-and-black fallout shelter signs.

Switzerland pursued an especially comprehensive program, legally requiring shelter space for the entire population, while Sweden and Finland maintained large civil-defense organizations and stockpiles. Critics argued that civil defense could not meaningfully protect populations against thermonuclear attack, and programs were gradually scaled back from the 1970s onward.

Modern civil protection

After the Cold War, most countries merged civil defense into broader emergency management agencies concerned with floods, earthquakes, industrial accidents, and terrorism. In the United States, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), created in 1979, absorbed civil-defense functions, and the Department of Homeland Security took over FEMA in 2003. Many European states operate through civil protection directorates coordinated by the EU Civil Protection Mechanism. Air-raid sirens, emergency alert broadcasts, and volunteer organizations such as Germany's Technisches Hilfswerk (THW) and the British Civil Defence Corps' successor bodies remain legacies of the civil-defense era.
