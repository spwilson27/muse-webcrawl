The Levellers were a political movement during the English Civil Wars (1642–1651) who emphasized popular sovereignty, extended suffrage, legal equality, and religious tolerance. Their ideas were set out in a series of manifestos titled An Agreement of the People. The name "Leveller" was originally a term of abuse suggesting they wanted to abolish property distinctions—a charge their leaders, including John Lilburne and William Walwyn, denied; they called themselves "Agitators."

Ideas

The Levellers argued that government rests on the consent of free-born citizens, proposing near-universal manhood suffrage (excluding servants and beggars), annual or biennial parliaments, equality before the law, freedom of conscience, an end to tithes and monopolies, and reform of a legal system they saw as serving lawyers and landlords. Their pamphleteering was enabled by the collapse of censorship during the war.

The Putney Debates

The movement's high point came in the autumn of 1647, when elected Agitators of the New Model Army debated the Agreement with Oliver Cromwell and Henry Ireton at St Mary's Church, Putney—the Putney Debates. Colonel Thomas Rainsborough declared "that the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live as the greatest he." Ireton insisted the franchise belong only to those with a "permanent fixed interest" in the kingdom—property.

Suppression and legacy

After failed army mutinies at Corkbush Field (1647) and Bishopsgate and Burford (1649) were crushed, and Lilburne was tried (and acquitted) for treason, the movement faded. Historians regard the Levellers as pioneers of liberal and democratic thought, anticipating ideas later embodied in written constitutions and bills of rights.
