The General Certificate of Secondary Education (GCSE) is an academic qualification in a range of subjects taken in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland, having replaced the GCE O-Level and CSE qualifications. It was introduced in September 1986, with the first examinations held in 1988, and is generally taken by students at the end of compulsory secondary education, aged 15 to 16.

Structure

Students typically study eight to ten subjects over two years (Years 10 and 11), including compulsory English language, English literature, mathematics, and science, alongside options such as history, geography, modern languages, and the arts. Assessment is mainly by terminal written examinations, with coursework or controlled assessment in some practical subjects. Awarding bodies include AQA, Edexcel, OCR, WJEC/Eduqas, and CCEA.

Grading

In England, reformed GCSEs phased in from 2017 use a 9-to-1 scale (9 highest), replacing the old A*–G grades; grade 4 is a "standard pass" and grade 5 a "strong pass". Wales and Northern Ireland retain letter grades, though Northern Ireland introduced a C* grade to align with the 9–1 scale. Scotland uses the separate National 5 qualification instead.

Progression and criticism

GCSE results determine entry to sixth-form and college courses such as A-Levels and are required by many employers. Debates continue over exam stress, the narrow academic focus of the EBacc measure, grade inflation, and reform proposals following the disrupted pandemic-era grading of 2020–21, when teacher-assessed grades replaced exams.
