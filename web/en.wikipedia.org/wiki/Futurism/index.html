Futurism

Futurism (Italian: Futurismo) was an artistic and social movement that originated in Italy in the early 20th century. It emphasised speed, technology, youth, industry, and violence, together with modern objects such as the automobile, the aeroplane, and the industrial city. Futurism influenced art, architecture, literature, theatre, music, and even cuisine, and spread beyond Italy — most notably to Russia — contributing to the development of Art Deco, Constructivism, Surrealism, and Dada.

The Manifesto of 1909

Filippo Tommaso Marinetti launched Futurism with his Manifesto of Futurism, published on the front page of the French newspaper Le Figaro on 20 February 1909. The manifesto glorified the racing car as more beautiful than the Victory of Samothrace, declared war "the world's only hygiene", and called for the destruction of museums and libraries as symbols of a suffocating past. Marinetti and his followers issued dozens of further manifestos covering painting, sculpture, music, theatre, architecture, photography, and food.

Futurist art

Painters such as Umberto Boccioni, Giacomo Balla, Carlo Carrà, and Gino Severini sought to depict movement and dynamism, drawing on Divisionism and Cubism to show objects in successive states of motion. Boccioni's bronze Unique Forms of Continuity in Space (1913) became the movement's sculptural emblem. Luigi Russolo's 1913 manifesto The Art of Noises pioneered the idea of noise music, and his intonarumori instruments anticipated electronic music.

Politics and decline

Futurism's celebration of violence and nationalism made it an ally of Italian Fascism: Marinetti co-wrote the Fascist Manifesto of 1919, though the movement's avant-garde aesthetics later fell out of favour with the regime. Many Futurists died in the First World War, which they had enthusiastically supported, and the movement never fully recovered its early momentum; it faded with Marinetti's death in 1944. A distinct Russian Futurism produced figures such as Vladimir Mayakovsky and Kazimir Malevich.
