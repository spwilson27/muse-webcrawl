Georgian architecture is the name given to the architectural styles current between about 1714 and 1830, during the reigns of the first four British monarchs named George — George I, George II, George III and George IV — of the House of Hanover. It covers the Palladian revival of the early eighteenth century and the Neoclassicism that followed, and it produced the harmonious terraces, squares and crescents that still define cities such as London, Edinburgh, Dublin and Bath.

Characteristics

Georgian design prizes symmetry, proportion and restraint. Typical features include:

  • Symmetrical facades, often of five bays, with a central doorcase
  • Construction in brick (often red or, later, stuccoed and painted) with stone dressings
  • Sash windows of diminishing height on successive floors, with fine glazing bars
  • Classical orders used sparingly — a pilastered door surround, a cornice, a dentilled eaves course
  • Hipped roofs set behind parapets, and panelled front doors with fanlights
  • Interiors with plasterwork, marble chimneypieces and, in grander houses, rooms in the manner of Robert Adam

Development

The early Georgian style revived the classicism of Andrea Palladio and Inigo Jones as a deliberate reaction against the English Baroque. Lord Burlington and Colen Campbell, whose pattern book Vitruvius Britannicus (1715) promoted the Palladian manner, shaped the taste of the age; William Kent applied it to interiors and landscape gardens alike. From the 1760s the excavations of Herculaneum and Pompeii and the work of Robert and James Adam popularised a lighter, more decorative Neoclassicism, sometimes called the Adam style.

Speculative builders used builders' pattern books to lay out whole estates of terraced houses: the Bedford and Grosvenor estates in London, the New Town of Edinburgh (planned by James Craig from 1767) and John Wood the Elder and Younger's Queen Square, Circus and Royal Crescent at Bath (1767–1775) are famous examples. In Ireland, Georgian Dublin produced Merrion Square and the great public buildings of James Gandon. In colonial and early-republican America the style shaped plantation houses and townhouses from Boston to Charleston, and it evolved into the Federal style.

Revival

The twentieth century saw several Georgian revivals — Neo-Georgian in Britain and Colonial Revival in the United States — and the term "Georgian" became standard estate-agent shorthand for an elegant early-nineteenth-century house.
