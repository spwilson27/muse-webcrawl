Bakken Formation

The Bakken Formation is a rock unit from the Late Devonian to Early Mississippian age occupying about 200,000 square miles (520,000 km²) of the subsurface of the Williston Basin, underlying parts of Montana, North Dakota, Saskatchewan and Manitoba. The formation is a major source of oil, exploited at scale since the 2000s through horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing.

Geology

The formation consists of three members: a lower organic-rich black shale, a middle silty dolomite and sandstone member, and an upper dark shale. The shales are the source rock, containing large amounts of organic matter matured into oil; the middle member serves as the reservoir targeted by horizontal wells. The formation is named after Henry O. Bakken, a North Dakota farmer on whose land it was first described from well logs in 1953.

Oil production

Oil was first produced from the Bakken in 1951, but the tight rock yielded poorly until horizontal drilling combined with multi-stage hydraulic fracturing unlocked it in the 2000s, notably at Elm Coulee field in Montana and the Parshall Field discovery in North Dakota in 2006. The resulting North Dakota oil boom made the state the second-largest oil producer in the United States and drove rapid growth in towns such as Williston and Watford City.

Estimates and issues

USGS assessments have estimated billions of barrels of technically recoverable oil in the Bakken and underlying Three Forks Formation. Production is accompanied by natural gas flaring, water use for fracturing, induced seismicity concerns, and boom-and-bust economic cycles in producing communities.
