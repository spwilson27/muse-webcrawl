Pantomime (informally panto) is a type of musical comedy stage production designed for family entertainment. It was developed in England and remains especially popular in the United Kingdom, Ireland and other English-speaking countries, traditionally performed during the Christmas and New Year season. Modern pantomime follows a well-loved fairy tale or folk story — such as Cinderella, Aladdin, Jack and the Beanstalk or Dick Whittington — with songs, slapstick, topical jokes and heavy audience participation.

History

Pantomime descends in part from the Italian commedia dell'arte, whose masked characters — Harlequin, Columbine, Pantaloon and Clown — reached London in the 17th and 18th centuries. Theatre manager John Rich made the "harlequinade" the hit afterpiece of the London stage, and in the 19th century performers such as Joseph Grimaldi transformed the Clown into the star of the show. As the harlequinade faded, the fairy-tale opening expanded into the full-length family show recognisable today, with music-hall comedians and, later, television celebrities in leading roles.

Conventions

Pantomime is governed by cherished conventions. The pantomime dame — a comic matron played by a man in outrageous costumes — is a central figure, while the principal boy, the young male hero, is traditionally played by a woman. Animals such as the pantomime horse or cow are played by two performers in a skin costume. Audiences are expected to shout responses: "He's behind you!", "Oh no he isn't!" / "Oh yes he is!", and to hiss the villain. Shows include a singalong, sweet-throwing into the stalls, topical gags about local news and politicians, and a closing "walkdown" in full costume.

Cultural importance

For many British theatres, the pantomime season generates the income that subsidises the rest of the year's programme, and for many children panto is their first experience of live theatre. Large productions at venues such as the London Palladium compete with small village-hall shows in fidelity to the same basic formula.
