Mull of Galloway

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Mull of Galloway Lighthouse (1830), at Scotland's southern tip.

The Mull of Galloway (Scottish Gaelic: Maol nan Gall) is the southernmost point of Scotland. It lies at the end of the Rhins of Galloway peninsula in the parish of Kirkmaiden, Dumfries and Galloway, about 10 miles (16 km) south of the village of Drummore. On a clear day the Isle of Man, the Mountains of Mourne in Ireland, and the English Lake District are all visible from the headland.

Lighthouse

The Mull of Galloway Lighthouse, built in 1828–30 by the engineer Robert Stevenson for the Northern Lighthouse Board, is a whitewashed tower 26 m (85 ft) high standing on cliffs about 84 m above the sea; its light has a range of some 28 nautical miles. The station was automated in 1988, and the former keepers' cottages and engine house now form a visitor centre and exhibition, with the lighthouse tower open to visitors seasonally. A foghorn at the site, no longer in use, is preserved.

Nature and history

The headland and its cliffs are protected as a nature reserve managed by the RSPB, designated a Site of Special Scientific Interest and Special Protection Area. The cliffs host breeding seabirds including guillemots, razorbills, kittiwakes, and shags, with gannets from nearby Scares Rocks; the surrounding heathland and the Galloway Hills backdrop support peregrine falcons and a rich maritime flora. Offshore, the meeting of tides in the North Channel produces a visible race. The Mull is also the start (or finish) point of long-distance walkers' routes up the Rhins peninsula.

The name Mull derives from the Gaelic maol, a bare, rounded headland. Nearby stand the earthworks of an Iron Age promontory fort.
