Inverse condemnation

Inverse condemnation is a legal concept in United States property law in which a property owner brings a claim against a government entity alleging that the government has taken private property without paying just compensation, as required by the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment and by state constitutions. The claim is "inverse" because it is the owner, rather than the government, who initiates the proceedings — the reverse of a direct condemnation (eminent domain) action.

Background

The Fifth Amendment provides: "nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation." Where government formally expropriates land, it files a condemnation action and deposits compensation. Inverse condemnation covers situations where government action — flooding caused by a public project, denial of all economically beneficial use by regulation, or physical occupation — amounts to a taking without formal proceedings.

Regulatory takings

Much inverse condemnation litigation concerns regulatory takings. In Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon (1922), Justice Holmes held that a regulation that "goes too far" may be recognised as a taking. The Supreme Court's later framework in Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City (1978) weighs the economic impact of the regulation, its interference with distinct investment-backed expectations, and the character of the government action. In Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992), the Court held that a regulation depriving land of all economically beneficial use is a categorical taking unless the restriction inheres in background principles of state property law. Physical appropriations, such as the permanent cable installation in Loretto v. Teleprompter (1982) and the access easement in Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid (2021), are treated as per se takings.

Remedy

The usual remedy is just compensation — typically the fair market value of the property interest taken — rather than invalidation of the government action. In Knick v. Township of Scott (2019), the Court held that owners need not exhaust state administrative remedies before suing in federal court.
