The first hundred days of Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency began on his inauguration on 4 March 1933 and encompassed the special session of Congress from 9 March to 16 June 1933, during which an unprecedented volume of New Deal legislation was enacted to combat the Great Depression. The term "hundred days" has since become a benchmark against which the early effectiveness of subsequent United States presidents is measured.

Background

Roosevelt took office amid a banking panic, with unemployment around 25 percent. In his inaugural address he declared that "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself" and promised "action, and action now." He immediately declared a national bank holiday to halt runs on banks.

Legislation

During the special session Congress passed fifteen major laws, including:

  • Emergency Banking Act (9 March) — stabilised the banking system
  • Economy Act — cut federal spending and salaries
  • Beer-Wine Revenue Act — legalised and taxed low-alcohol beer ahead of the repeal of Prohibition
  • Agricultural Adjustment Act — sought to raise farm prices
  • Federal Emergency Relief Act
  • Tennessee Valley Authority Act
  • Securities Act of 1933 and the Glass–Steagall Act (Banking Act of 1933), separating commercial and investment banking
  • National Industrial Recovery Act (16 June), the last major bill of the session

New agencies such as the Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), the Civil Works Administration's predecessors and the Home Owners' Loan Corporation were also created in or around this period.

Legacy

The Hundred Days established the expectation that new presidents act quickly on their agendas. Journalists and historians routinely assess modern presidents — from Lyndon B. Johnson to Barack Obama and Donald Trump — by what they achieve in their first hundred days, even though modern legislating typically moves more slowly.
