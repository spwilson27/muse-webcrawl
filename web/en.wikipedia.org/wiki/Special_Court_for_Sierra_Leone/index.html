The Special Court for Sierra Leone (SCSL) was a judicial body set up jointly by the Government of Sierra Leone and the United Nations to prosecute "persons who bear the greatest responsibility" for serious violations of international humanitarian law committed during the Sierra Leone Civil War after 30 November 1996. It was the first international criminal court to be based in the country where the crimes occurred, the first funded by voluntary contributions, and the first to convict defendants for the use of child soldiers and for forced marriage as a crime against humanity.

Structure and cases

The Court indicted 13 persons, organized into four cases: the Revolutionary United Front (RUF) leaders (Foday Sankoh, Sam Bockarie, Issa Sesay, Morris Kallon, Augustine Gbao); the Armed Forces Revolutionary Council (AFRC); the pro-government Civil Defence Forces (CDF), including Sam Hinga Norman; and Charles Taylor, the former president of Liberia.

Sankoh and Bockarie died before trial, and Norman died in custody in 2007. The trials of the AFRC (Brima, Kamara, Kanu), CDF (Fofana, Kondewa), and RUF (Sesay, Kallon, Gbao) accused ended in convictions, most upheld on appeal.

Trial of Charles Taylor

Charles Taylor was arrested in 2006 and, for security reasons, tried in The Hague using facilities of the ICC. On 26 April 2012 he was convicted of aiding and abetting war crimes and crimes against humanity — including terror, murder, rape, enslavement, and the use of child soldiers — and sentenced to 50 years in prison. He was the first former head of state convicted by an international court since the Nuremberg trials. His conviction and sentence were upheld on appeal in September 2013, and he is serving his sentence in the United Kingdom.

Legacy

The Court completed its mandate and closed on 2 December 2013, handing residual functions (witness protection, archives, supervision of sentences) to the Residual Special Court for Sierra Leone. It is regarded as a model of the "hybrid tribunal," combining international and domestic law and judges, though it was criticized for its cost, limited number of accused, and dependence on voluntary funding.
