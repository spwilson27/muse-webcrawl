Gestational trophoblastic disease

SpecialtyGynaecology; oncology
TypesHydatidiform mole; invasive mole; choriocarcinoma; placental site trophoblastic tumour
Key markerHuman chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)

Gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) is a group of rare conditions in which abnormal trophoblast cells — the cells that normally develop into the placenta — grow inside the uterus after conception. The group ranges from the usually benign hydatidiform mole (molar pregnancy) to malignant forms collectively called gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, including invasive mole and choriocarcinoma.

Types

A hydatidiform mole results from abnormal fertilisation. A complete mole contains no fetal tissue and arises when an egg without maternal chromosomes is fertilised; a partial mole contains some fetal tissue and usually has a triploid karyotype. In a minority of cases molar tissue persists or becomes malignant: an invasive mole grows into the uterine muscle, while choriocarcinoma is an aggressive cancer that can spread to the lungs, liver and brain.

Signs and diagnosis

GTD may present with vaginal bleeding in early pregnancy, a uterus larger than expected for gestational age, severe nausea, or passage of grape-like vesicles. Diagnosis rests on ultrasound — a complete mole classically shows a "snowstorm" pattern — and on markedly elevated levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which serves as a highly sensitive tumour marker for monitoring treatment.

Treatment

Molar pregnancies are usually removed by suction dilation and curettage, followed by serial hCG monitoring. Persistent or malignant disease is treated with chemotherapy, to which it is usually highly sensitive; methotrexate and actinomycin D are commonly used, and cure rates exceed 90 percent even for metastatic disease, making GTD one of the most curable of cancers.
