Mucous membrane

A mucous membrane, or mucosa, is a membrane that lines the internal cavities and canals of the body that communicate with the exterior, including the respiratory, digestive and urogenital tracts. Mucosae are composed of one or more layers of epithelial cells overlying a layer of loose connective tissue called the lamina propria, and in most of the digestive tract a thin layer of smooth muscle, the muscularis mucosae. They secrete mucus, a protective fluid that lubricates surfaces, traps particles and pathogens, and keeps the underlying tissues moist.

Structure

The epithelium of a mucous membrane may be simple or stratified, squamous, columnar or cuboidal, depending on location, and often contains goblet cells and multicellular glands that produce mucus. Beneath the epithelium, the lamina propria contains blood vessels, nerves and lymphoid tissue — the mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), an important component of the immune system.

Functions and locations

Mucous membranes line the mouth, nose, eyelids, trachea and lungs, stomach and intestines, and the urinary and reproductive tracts. Depending on the site they perform absorption (as in the gut), secretion (of mucus, enzymes and acid), and barrier and immune defence. The oral mucosa, gastric mucosa, intestinal mucosa, nasal mucosa and endometrium (the mucosa of the uterus) are frequently studied examples. Because they are moist and vascular, mucosae are also common sites of drug absorption and of infection, and their inflammation is termed mucositis.
