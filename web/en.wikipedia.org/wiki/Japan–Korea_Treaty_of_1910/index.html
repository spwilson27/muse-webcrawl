The Japan–Korea Treaty of 1910, also known as the Japan–Korea Annexation Treaty, was made between the Empire of Japan and the Korean Empire on August 22, 1910. By its terms the Emperor of Korea made "complete and permanent cession" of sovereignty over Korea to the Emperor of Japan. It took effect on August 29, 1910, beginning thirty-five years of Japanese colonial rule that ended only with Japan's defeat in the Second World War in 1945.

Background

The treaty was the culmination of a sequence of unequal instruments imposed after Japan's victory in the First Sino-Japanese War (1895) and the Russo-Japanese War (1905). The Eulsa Treaty of 1905 made Korea a Japanese protectorate, stripping it of diplomatic sovereignty; Korean Emperor Gojong's attempt to appeal to the Hague Peace Conference in 1907 led Japan to force his abdication in favour of his son Sunjong. Prime Minister Yi Wan-yong, remembered in Korea as the chief of the "Five Traitors of Eulsa," signed the annexation treaty with Resident-General Terauchi Masatake.

Legality and dispute

The treaty's legality has been disputed ever since. Korean historians and the governments of both North and South Korea maintain it was coerced and invalid — Sunjong's seal and the formalities of assent have been questioned, and Sunjong himself left statements of protest. The 1965 Treaty on Basic Relations between Japan and South Korea declared the earlier treaties and agreements "already null and void," a formulation each side interprets differently: Japan reads it as confirming the treaties were valid until 1948, while Korea reads it as a confirmation they were void from the outset.

Consequences

Annexation inaugurated a period of colonial administration under a Governor-General, with phases of harsh military rule (budan seiji), the "cultural rule" of the 1920s, and wartime assimilation and mobilization in the 1930s–40s. Resistance included the March First Movement of 1919. The treaty's legacy continues to shape Japan–Korea relations.
