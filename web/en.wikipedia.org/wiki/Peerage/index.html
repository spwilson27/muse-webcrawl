Peerage

Members of the House of Lords in ceremonial dress.

A peerage is a legal system of noble titles in various European countries, and in the British case a rank of hereditary or life nobility comprising five degrees: duke, marquess, earl, viscount, and baron. Holders of peerages are collectively called peers, and their dignity historically carried the right to sit in the upper house of the legislature — in the United Kingdom, the House of Lords.

The British peerage

The peerages of England, Scotland, Great Britain, Ireland, and the United Kingdom were created over centuries by the Crown. Wives of peers hold feminine forms of the titles (duchess, marchioness, countess, viscountess, baroness). The Life Peerages Act 1958 introduced peerages for life only, whose holders — addressed as Lord or Lady — sit in the House of Lords alongside a reduced number of hereditary peers: the House of Lords Act 1999 removed the automatic right of most hereditaries to sit, leaving 92 elected hereditary peers. Peerages are recorded in works such as Burke's Peerage and Debrett's, and new creations are now almost exclusively life peerages.

Other systems

Comparable systems existed across Europe — the French pairie, the Spanish grandees, and the nobilities of the Holy Roman Empire — most of which lost legal force with the abolition of monarchy or privilege, though titles often survive socially. Japan's kazoku peerage (1869–1947) was modelled partly on the British system.
