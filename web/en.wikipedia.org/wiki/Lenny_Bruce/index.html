Lenny Bruce

Leonard Alfred Schneider (October 13, 1925 – August 3, 1966), better known by his stage name Lenny Bruce, was an American stand-up comedian, social critic, and satirist. Renowned for his open, free-style, and critical form of comedy that integrated satire, politics, religion, sex, and vulgarity, Bruce paved the way for a generation of counterculture-era comedians and became a landmark figure in the history of free speech in the United States.

Career

Bruce began performing in clubs after serving in the U.S. Navy during the Second World War. By the late 1950s he had become a celebrated figure in jazz clubs and beatnik circles, releasing influential comedy albums such as The Sick Humor of Lenny Bruce (1959) and I Am Not a Nut, Elect Me! (1960). His routines — improvisational, stream-of-consciousness, and delivered in a jazz-inflected cadence — attacked organized religion, racial hypocrisy, sexual taboos, and political corruption.

Legal battles

Bruce was arrested repeatedly on obscenity charges, beginning with a 1961 arrest at the Jazz Workshop in San Francisco (where he was acquitted) and culminating in his November 1964 conviction, alongside club owner Howard Solomon, following a performance at the Café Au Go Go in Greenwich Village, New York. The trials made him a cause célèbre; supporters included Woody Allen, Allen Ginsberg, Norman Mailer, and Bob Dylan. Increasingly blacklisted and consumed by his legal defense, Bruce was found dead at home in Hollywood in 1966 of an acute morphine overdose, aged forty.

Legacy

In 2003 Bruce received a posthumous pardon from New York Governor George Pataki — the first in the state's history. His influence on performers from George Carlin and Richard Pryor to Bill Hicks is widely acknowledged, and his 1974 portrayal by Dustin Hoffman in Bob Fosse's film Lenny renewed interest in his work. Bruce is routinely ranked among the greatest stand-up comedians of all time.
