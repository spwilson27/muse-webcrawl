Racemase

A racemase is an enzyme that catalyzes the stereochemical inversion of a chiral centre in a substrate, converting a compound between its two enantiomeric forms. Racemases belong to the class of isomerases (EC 5.1) and are widely distributed in bacteria, plants and animals, where they help supply cells with the less common enantiomer of amino acids and other metabolites.

Mechanism

Most racemases act by abstracting a proton from the alpha carbon of the substrate to form a planar carbanion (or enolate) intermediate, which is then reprotonated from the opposite face. Many amino acid racemases use two cysteine residues positioned on either side of the substrate as acid/base catalysts, a so-called two-base mechanism. Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)-dependent racemases, such as alanine racemase, instead stabilize the intermediate through the cofactor.

Examples

Well-studied racemases include alanine racemase, which provides D-alanine for bacterial peptidoglycan synthesis and is a target of antibiotics such as cycloserine; glutamate racemase, which supplies D-glutamate for the bacterial cell wall; proline racemase, important in the parasite Trypanosoma cruzi; and mandelate racemase, a classic model enzyme for mechanistic studies of proton transfer.

Biological roles

In bacteria, racemases produce D-amino acids needed for cell wall construction and for signalling molecules in biofilms. In mammals, serine racemase generates D-serine, a co-agonist of NMDA receptors in the brain, linking racemase activity to synaptic transmission and to disorders such as schizophrenia.
