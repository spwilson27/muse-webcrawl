Partially ordered set

In mathematics, especially order theory, a partial order on a set is an arrangement such that, for certain pairs of elements, one precedes the other. A partially ordered set (or poset) formalises and generalises the intuitive concept of an ordering, sequencing or arrangement of the elements of a set. It consists of a set together with a binary relation (written ≤) that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive.

Examples

Standard examples include the real numbers ordered by the usual ≤; the subsets of a given set ordered by inclusion; the natural numbers ordered by divisibility; and the vertices of a directed acyclic graph ordered by reachability. Unlike a total order, a partial order need not compare every pair of elements — in the divisibility order, 2 and 3 are incomparable.

Structure and applications

Posets are drawn compactly as Hasse diagrams, in which an edge upward from x to y indicates that y covers x. Important derived notions include maximal and minimal elements, chains, antichains, ideals and filters; Dilworth's theorem and Mirsky's theorem relate the structure of a poset to partitions into chains and antichains. Partial orders arise throughout mathematics and computer science: in lattice theory, domain theory for programming-language semantics, scheduling with precedence constraints, and causal sets in physics.
