Naram-Sin of Akkad

Naram-Sin, also transcribed Narām-Sîn or Naram-Suen (Akkadian: "beloved of Sin"), was the fourth king of the Akkadian Empire, reigning in the 23rd century BC (c. 2254–2218 BC by the middle chronology). The grandson of Sargon of Akkad, the empire's founder, he ruled at the height of Akkad's power and was the first Mesopotamian king to claim divinity during his own lifetime, styling himself "god of Akkad" and "King of the Four Quarters of the World".

Reign and conquests

Naram-Sin succeeded his father Manishtushu and faced major revolts at the start of his reign, which he crushed in a series of campaigns he commemorated as the "Great Revolt". He campaigned widely: westward to the cedar forests of the Amanus and Lebanon, against Ebla and Armanum in Syria, north into the Zagros foothills against the Lullubi, and eastward into Elam, where he concluded a treaty with Khita of Awan — the earliest known written treaty in the Akkadian language. His victory over the Lullubi king Satuni is commemorated by the famous Victory Stele, now in the Louvre, which shows the king, wearing a horned helmet as a sign of divinity, ascending a mountain over his fallen enemies.

Deification and administration

After his victories Naram-Sin was deified by the citizens of Akkad, who built him a temple, and his name was thereafter written with the divine determinative. His reign saw a programme of administrative standardisation: a uniform system of weights and measures (the "gur of Akkad"), the use of Akkadian rather than Sumerian in official documents across the empire, and the appointment of royal relatives, including his daughter Enmenanna as high priestess of Nanna at Ur, to key positions. Royal building works included a great temple at Nippur and a fortified residence at Tell Brak in Syria.

The Curse of Akkad

Later Mesopotamian tradition remembered Naram-Sin less kindly. The Sumerian literary composition known as The Curse of Akkad blamed the empire's collapse on his sacrilege in plundering and destroying the Ekur temple of Enlil at Nippur, for which the gods sent the Gutian invaders to destroy Akkad. In fact the empire survived Naram-Sin for some decades under his son Shar-Kali-Sharri before fragmenting amid Gutian pressure, drought and internal revolt. The "Curse" reflects later theological interpretation rather than contemporary fact, but it shaped his reputation for two thousand years.
