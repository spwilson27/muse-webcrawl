Fifth disease, also known as erythema infectiosum and slapped cheek syndrome, is a common and contagious disease caused by infection with parvovirus B19. It is called "fifth disease" because it was historically the fifth of six classical childhood exanthems (after measles, scarlet fever, rubella and Dukes' disease, followed by roseola).

Signs and symptoms

After a mild prodrome of low-grade fever, headache and cold-like symptoms, the hallmark bright red rash appears on the cheeks — the "slapped cheek" appearance — followed by a lacy, net-like (reticulated) rash on the trunk and limbs. By the time the rash appears, the child is usually no longer infectious. Adults are more likely to develop joint pain (arthropathy). Infection during pregnancy can cause fetal anemia and hydrops fetalis; in people with hemolytic anemias it can trigger an aplastic crisis.

Transmission and diagnosis

Parvovirus B19 spreads mainly through respiratory secretions. Diagnosis is usually clinical, confirmed where needed by B19-specific IgM antibodies or PCR. There is no specific antiviral treatment and no vaccine; management is supportive.
