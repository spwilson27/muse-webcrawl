Cervus

Cervus is a genus of deer that primarily inhabits Eurasia, although one species is found in North America and another in North Africa. The best-known species is the red deer (Cervus elaphus), widespread across Europe and western Asia. Members of the genus are large deer whose males typically carry elaborate branched antlers that are shed and regrown annually.

Species

The taxonomy of the genus has been repeatedly revised, with molecular studies reshuffling species formerly grouped together. Commonly recognised species include:

  • Cervus elaphus — red deer
  • Cervus canadensis — elk or wapiti
  • Cervus nippon — sika deer
  • Cervus albirostris — Thorold's deer
  • Cervus wallichii — Tibetan red deer

Until the early twenty-first century the elk of North America and East Asia was usually treated as a subspecies of red deer, but genetic evidence supports its recognition as the separate species Cervus canadensis.

Biology

Species of Cervus are grazing and browsing herbivores of woodland, moorland, steppe and mountainous country. Males (stags) grow antlers that increase in size and complexity with age up to maturity; during the autumn rut they compete by roaring, parallel walking and, where rivals are closely matched, direct clashes of antlers. Females (hinds) lack antlers and typically give birth to a single calf in late spring or early summer.

Relationship with humans

Red deer have been hunted since prehistory and are managed across much of Europe for venison, sport and habitat conservation. They are also farmed and have been introduced to New Zealand, Australia, South America and elsewhere outside their native range, in some places becoming invasive.
