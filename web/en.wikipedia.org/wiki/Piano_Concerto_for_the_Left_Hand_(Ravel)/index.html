The Piano Concerto for the Left Hand in D major was composed by Maurice Ravel between 1929 and 1930. It was commissioned by the Austrian pianist Paul Wittgenstein, who had lost his right arm during the First World War. The concerto is in a single movement and is famous for creating the illusion of a two-handed texture from one hand alone, exploiting the piano's full range through wide leaps, arpeggiation and a registrally deep, sonorous solo part.


Autograph score of the concerto's opening, beginning with contrabassoon and double basses.

Background

Wittgenstein, the brother of the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein, commissioned left-hand works from many leading composers, including Richard Strauss, Prokofiev, Britten and Hindemith. Ravel composed the concerto at the same time as his Piano Concerto in G major. Before writing it he studied other single-hand repertoire, including Saint-Saëns's studies for the left hand, and Liszt's transcriptions.

The premiere took place in Vienna on 27 November 1931, with Wittgenstein as soloist and Robert Heger conducting the Vienna Symphony. Ravel and Wittgenstein later quarrelled over the pianist's alterations to the solo part; Wittgenstein held exclusive performing rights for a period, but the work soon entered the general repertoire.

Music

The concerto opens with a slow orchestral introduction (Lento) rising from the depths of the orchestra — contrabassoon, basses and horns — before the piano enters with a cadenza built on the main theme. The single continuous movement falls into contrasting sections resembling a slow movement and a scherzo-like fast movement, with jazz-inflected episodes, a massive central development, and a final solo cadenza before the brief close. Ravel remarked that the work is "in only one movement" and contains "a good deal of jazz — but only in the ingredients, not in the language."

Instrumentation

The orchestra is large: piccolo, two flutes, two oboes, English horn, clarinets (including E♭ and bass clarinet), bassoons and contrabassoon, four horns, three trumpets, three trombones, tuba, percussion (including whip and wood block), harp, and strings.
