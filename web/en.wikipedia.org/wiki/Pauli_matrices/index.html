In mathematical physics and mathematics, the Pauli matrices are a set of three 2 × 2 complex matrices which are Hermitian, unitary, and traceless. Usually indicated by the Greek letter sigma (σ), they are occasionally denoted by tau (τ) when used in connection with isospin symmetries. They are named after the physicist Wolfgang Pauli, who introduced them in 1927 in his theory of electron spin.

Definition

The Pauli matrices are:

σ1 = σx = [[0, 1], [1, 0]];   σ2 = σy = [[0, −i], [i, 0]];   σ3 = σz = [[1, 0], [0, −1]].

Together with the 2 × 2 identity matrix I (sometimes denoted σ0), they form an orthogonal basis for the real vector space of 2 × 2 Hermitian matrices, and for the complex vector space of all 2 × 2 matrices.

Algebraic properties

Each Pauli matrix is its own inverse: σi2 = I. They satisfy the commutation relations [σa, σb] = 2iεabcσc and the anticommutation relations {σa, σb} = 2δabI, which combine into the fundamental identity σaσb = δabI + iεabcσc. It follows that (a·σ)(b·σ) = (a·b)I + i(a×b)·σ for vectors a and b.

Physics

In quantum mechanics, the Pauli matrices represent the spin angular momentum operators for a spin-1/2 particle: Si = (ħ/2)σi. They appear in the Pauli equation, which extends the Schrödinger equation to account for the interaction of a particle's spin with an external electromagnetic field. In quantum information science, they describe single-qubit gates and errors, and in the algebra of the Dirac equation they underpin the Clifford algebra structure of spacetime.
