Intercalation (timekeeping)

Intercalation, in timekeeping, is the insertion of an additional day, week or month into a calendar to align it with astronomical or seasonal cycles. Because the astronomical periods that calendars track — the day, the synodic month (about 29.53 days) and the tropical year (about 365.2422 days) — are not whole multiples of one another, any practical calendar must periodically add (or, in principle, omit) units to prevent its dates from drifting through the seasons or out of step with the Moon.

Leap days

Solar calendars intercalate days. The Julian calendar added a leap day every four years; the Gregorian calendar refined this by omitting leap days in century years not divisible by 400, giving an average year of 365.2425 days, close to the tropical year. In the Roman calendar the intercalary day was created by doubling 24 February, the sixth day before the Kalends of March, which is why leap years are called bissextile. Modern timekeeping also intercalates leap seconds into Coordinated Universal Time to keep it aligned with Earth's irregular rotation, though plans have been made to discontinue them.

Intercalary months

Lunisolar calendars intercalate whole months to reconcile twelve lunar months (about 354 days) with the solar year. The Hebrew calendar adds a thirteenth month, Adar I, in 7 years out of every 19-year Metonic cycle. The traditional Chinese calendar likewise inserts a leap month, determined by the rule that a month lacking a major solar term becomes the leap month, keeping festivals in their proper seasons; the Hindu calendars use the Adhik Maas system. The ancient Greek calendars and the Roman calendar before Julius Caesar's reform also relied on intercalary months, inserted by officials — in Rome by the pontifices — whose manipulation for political ends was one motive for reform.

Other systems

Purely lunar calendars, such as the Islamic calendar, do not intercalate against the solar year and therefore cycle through the seasons. Some calendars intercalate weeks: the ISO week date system has years of 53 weeks, and historical proposals for perpetual calendars, such as the World Calendar, use intercalary "blank days" outside the week.
