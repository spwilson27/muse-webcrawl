Storm drain

A storm drain, storm sewer or surface water drain is infrastructure designed to drain excess rain and groundwater from impervious surfaces such as paved streets, car parks, footpaths and roofs. Storm drains vary hugely in design, from small residential dry wells to enormous municipal systems, but most discharge untreated water into rivers, streams or the sea.

Design

Water enters through kerb inlets or grates and is conveyed by gravity through pipes, culverts and open channels to an outfall. Catch basins trap grit and debris and prevent sewer gases escaping. Modern sustainable drainage systems (SuDS) add swales, permeable paving, retention ponds and rain gardens that slow runoff, improve water quality and reduce flooding. In older cities, combined sewers carry storm water and sanitary sewage in the same pipes; heavy rain can then cause combined sewer overflows, discharging diluted sewage into waterways.

Environmental issues

Runoff from roads carries oil, heavy metals, fertilisers, litter and road salt into watercourses, making urban storm water a significant diffuse source of pollution. Many jurisdictions stencil drains with warnings such as "Dump no waste — drains to river". Explorers of large city storm systems ("urban exploration" of drains) face serious hazards from flash flooding and toxic gases, and entry is usually illegal.

History

Ancient drainage works are known from the Indus Valley and Mesopotamia, and Roman cities had elaborate cloacae. Systematic municipal storm sewers arrived with nineteenth-century urban growth, when engineers such as Joseph Bazalgette rebuilt London's drainage after the Great Stink of 1858.
