Arithmetic-geometric mean

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Plot of the arithmetic–geometric mean agm(1, x) against x.

In mathematics, the arithmetic-geometric mean (AGM or agM) of two positive real numbers x and y is the mutual limit of a pair of sequences: the arithmetic means and geometric means of successive iterates. Formally, define

a0 = x,   g0 = y;
an+1 = (an + gn)/2,   gn+1 = √(angn).

These sequences converge to the same limit, the arithmetic-geometric mean of x and y, denoted M(x, y) or agm(x, y). Convergence is quadratic—very rapid—which makes the AGM valuable in numerical computation.

History

The arithmetic-geometric mean was first studied by Lagrange and by Gauss, who found its remarkable connection with elliptic integrals. Gauss showed that the complete elliptic integral of the first kind can be expressed in terms of the AGM: K(k) = π / (2 M(1, √(1−k²))). This relationship underlies fast algorithms for computing π, including the Brent–Salamin algorithm, which uses the AGM to compute π to billions of digits.

Properties

The AGM is a homogeneous, symmetric function lying between the geometric and arithmetic means: √(xy) ≤ M(x, y) ≤ (x+y)/2. It is smooth and analytic, and satisfies various identities connected to elliptic functions and theta functions. A related iteration, the arithmetic-harmonic mean, converges to the geometric mean.

Applications

Besides computing π and elliptic integrals, the AGM appears in efficient algorithms for elementary functions, in the theory of modular equations, and in the numerical evaluation of special functions.
