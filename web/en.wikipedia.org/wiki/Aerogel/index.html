Aerogel

Aerogel
InventorSamuel Stephens Kistler (1931)
CompositionSolids (silica, carbon, metal oxides, polymers) with up to ~99.8% air by volume
Nickname"Frozen smoke", "solid smoke"
Key propertyExtremely low density and thermal conductivity

Aerogels are a class of synthetic porous ultralight materials derived from a gel in which the liquid component has been replaced with gas, without significant collapse of the solid structure. The result is a solid with extremely low density and extremely low thermal conductivity — silica aerogel feels like fragile polystyrene foam and looks like frozen smoke.

History

The first aerogels were made by the American chemist Samuel Stephens Kistler in 1931, reportedly as the result of a bet that he could replace the liquid in a gel with gas without shrinking it. He succeeded using supercritical drying, which avoids the surface-tension forces that would otherwise crush the delicate pore network. Interest revived in the 1980s–1990s with improved carbon-dioxide supercritical drying and new applications.

Properties

Aerogels are among the least dense solids known (silica aerogels can be as light as about 1 mg/cm³ above the density of air itself) and are excellent thermal insulators because their nanoscale pores suppress convection and conduction — the Knudsen effect. They are, however, brittle and hygroscopic unless specially treated. Carbon aerogels and newer polymer and graphene aerogels extend the family to electrically conducting and more flexible materials.

Applications

NASA has used silica aerogel to insulate Mars rovers and, in the Stardust mission, to capture comet dust particles intact at high speed. Commercial aerogel blankets insulate pipelines, buildings and apparel, and aerogels serve in Cherenkov detectors, catalysts supports and oil-spill cleanup research.
