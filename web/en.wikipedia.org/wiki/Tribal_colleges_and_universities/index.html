Tribal colleges and universities

Tribal colleges and universities (TCUs) are a category of higher education institutions in the United States, defined in federal law as institutions chartered by federally recognised Native American tribal governments or controlled by them. They are located on or near reservations, serve predominantly Indigenous students, and combine mainstream degree programmes with curricula in tribal language, culture and history.

History

The first tribally controlled college was Navajo Community College (now Diné College), founded in 1968 at Tsaile, Arizona, during the era of Native American self-determination. The American Indian Higher Education Consortium (AIHEC) was formed in 1972 to link the growing number of institutions. The Tribally Controlled Colleges and Universities Assistance Act of 1978 provided federal funding, and by the 1990s many TCUs had also been granted land-grant status under the Equity in Educational Land-Grant Status Act of 1994.

Role

TCUs typically offer open admissions, remedial education, and associate degrees alongside a growing number of bachelor's and a few master's programmes. Because many are in remote rural communities, they often serve as cultural centres, libraries, small-business incubators and technology hubs for the surrounding population, not just their students. There are around three dozen TCUs, mostly in the Great Plains and Southwest.
