Ichthys

The ichthys (from Greek ἰχθύς, ikhthûs, "fish"), also known as the Jesus fish, is a symbol consisting of two intersecting arcs resembling the profile of a fish, used by early Christians as a secret sign of recognition and later adopted as one of the most common Christian symbols.

The acrostic

The Greek word for fish was read as an acrostic: Ἰησοῦς Χριστός, Θεοῦ Υἱός, Σωτήρ — "Jesus Christ, Son of God, Saviour." The symbol appears in early Christian art in the Roman catacombs from the 2nd century, alongside other fish imagery drawn from the Gospels: the feeding of the multitude with loaves and fishes, the calling of the apostles as "fishers of men," and the risen Christ eating fish.

History and use

A popular tradition holds that persecuted Christians used the fish to identify one another discreetly — one person drawing the first arc in the dust and the other completing it — though the extent of this practical use is uncertain. The symbol faded after Christianity became established, but it was revived in the 1970s and became ubiquitous in evangelical culture, appearing on car bumpers, jewelry, and business signs as a profession of faith.

Variants and parodies

The emblem has spawned variations, including versions with "Jesus" or a cross inside the outline, and parodies such as the "Darwin fish," which adds evolutionary legs and is used by atheists and supporters of evolution. The symbol is also used by some messianic and early-church-oriented groups seeking to recover pre-Constantinian Christian imagery.
