Direct-to-video

Direct-to-video (DTV), also called straight-to-video (STV), describes a film released directly to home video formats (originally VHS, later DVD and Blu-ray, and now streaming) without a prior theatrical release. The model arose with the home-video boom of the 1980s, when video stores created an enormous market for content that could be produced cheaply and marketed by cover art and title alone, bypassing cinemas entirely.

Categories and economics

Direct-to-video production covers several kinds of material: low-budget genre films (horror, action, erotic thrillers and "mockbusters" timed to theatrical blockbusters); sequels to theatrical hits made without the original stars — Disney's string of animated sequels, beginning with The Return of Jafar (1994), was famously profitable; children's and faith-based content; and "vanity" or instructional productions. Budgets were typically a fraction of theatrical features, recovered through high-margin rental sales (stores paid premium prices for rental copies). The term acquired a stigma of low quality, though some theatrical-quality works were released DTV for commercial reasons, and critics later reassessed parts of the era's output.

Successors

As physical video declined, the category blurred into direct-to-streaming releases: Netflix, Amazon and other platforms commission or acquire films that premiere online, including prestige productions, while the older DTV industry continues on DVD, digital rental (TVOD) and advertising-supported streaming channels, notably for action and horror titles fronted by ageing stars.
