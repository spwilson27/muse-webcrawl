Citric acid cycle

Diagram of the citric acid (Krebs) cycle.

The citric acid cycle—also known as the Krebs cycle or the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle—is a series of biochemical reactions that release energy from acetyl-CoA derived from carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. It is the second stage of cellular respiration, following glycolysis and preceding the electron transport chain. In eukaryotes the cycle takes place in the mitochondrial matrix; in most prokaryotes it occurs in the cytosol.

Overview

Acetyl-CoA (two carbons) combines with oxaloacetate (four carbons) to form citrate (six carbons). Through a series of oxidations, two carbons are released as carbon dioxide, regenerating oxaloacetate. Each turn of the cycle produces one GTP (or ATP), three NADH, and one FADH2, whose electrons feed the electron transport chain to generate most of the cell's ATP.

Steps

  1. Citrate synthase condenses acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate into citrate.
  2. Aconitase isomerizes citrate to isocitrate.
  3. Isocitrate dehydrogenase produces α-ketoglutarate, CO2, and NADH.
  4. The α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex yields succinyl-CoA, CO2, and NADH.
  5. Succinyl-CoA synthetase forms succinate and GTP.
  6. Succinate dehydrogenase oxidizes succinate to fumarate, producing FADH2.
  7. Fumarase hydrates fumarate to malate.
  8. Malate dehydrogenase regenerates oxaloacetate and a third NADH.

Discovery

The cycle was elucidated in 1937 by Hans Adolf Krebs, who worked out the cyclic pathway in pigeon breast muscle; he shared the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery. Key precursor work by Albert Szent-Györgyi and by Carl Martius and Franz Knoop established several of the intermediate steps.

Regulation and roles

The cycle is regulated at the pyruvate dehydrogenase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase steps by energy charge and metabolite levels. Beyond energy production, its intermediates serve as precursors for amino acids, nucleotides, porphyrins, and fatty acids, making it an amphibolic (both catabolic and anabolic) pathway.
