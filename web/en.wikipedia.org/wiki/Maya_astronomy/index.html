Maya astronomy

Maya astronomy was the naked-eye astronomical tradition of the pre-Columbian Maya civilisation of Mesoamerica. Without telescopes or clocks, Maya astronomer-priests achieved remarkably accurate knowledge of the cycles of the Moon, Venus, Mars and the Sun, recorded eclipse-prediction tables, and integrated these observations into their calendar system and ritual life.

Venus

Venus was of supreme importance. The Maya tracked its synodic period as 584 days (the modern value is 583.92 days) and harmonised it with the calendar: five Venus cycles equal eight 365-day years almost exactly (5 × 584 = 2,920 = 8 × 365). The Dresden Codex contains a Venus table spanning centuries, used to time rituals and warfare — Venus as morning star was considered dangerous. The Maya Codex of Mexico is likewise a Venus almanac.

Eclipses and the Moon

The Dresden Codex also holds an eclipse table covering 405 lunations (about 11,960 days), designed to warn of possible solar and lunar eclipses. Maya astronomers computed the lunar month as 29.53086 days, extremely close to the modern value of 29.53059 days. The Long Count calendar allowed cycles to be projected over thousands of years; inscriptions record dates millions of years in the past and future.

Architecture and observation

Buildings were aligned to astronomical events: the E-Group complexes at sites such as Uaxactún mark solstice and equinox sunrises, and the Caracol tower at Chichen Itza has sight lines toward Venus's extreme northern and southern settings. Naked-eye observations were made from rooftops and specially oriented structures, with events recorded in hieroglyphic books and carved monuments using Maya numerals.
