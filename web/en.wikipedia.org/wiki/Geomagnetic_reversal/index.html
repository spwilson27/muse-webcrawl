Geomagnetic reversal

A geomagnetic reversal is a change in a planet's dipole magnetic field such that magnetic north and magnetic south are interchanged: after a reversal, a compass needle that pointed north would point south. Such events occur irregularly, on average a few times per million years, and the transition itself is estimated to take between roughly 1,000 and 10,000 years.

History of research

Evidence for reversals was found in the magnetisation of ancient lavas by Bernard Brunhes (1906) and Motonori Matuyama, and was spectacularly confirmed in the 1960s by the symmetric stripes of normal and reversed magnetic polarity recorded in oceanic crust on either side of mid-ocean ridges — a key line of evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.

Chronology

Intervals of constant polarity are called chrons. The last complete reversal, the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal, occurred about 780,000 years ago; the present normal-polarity interval is the Brunhes Chron. Shorter-lived reversals or large deviations within a chron are called excursions, such as the Laschamp event about 41,000 years ago. Reversal frequency varies greatly: during the Cretaceous Long Normal Superchron (roughly 121–83 million years ago) no reversals occurred at all for nearly 40 million years.

Causes

Earth's field is generated by the geodynamo in the liquid outer core. Computer simulations of the dynamo show spontaneous reversals arising from the chaotic dynamics of the convecting molten iron, and most scientists consider reversals an intrinsic feature of the system rather than the result of external events.

Effects

During a transition the field weakens substantially and becomes more complex, potentially increasing cosmic ray flux at the surface. No clear correlation has been established between reversals and mass extinctions or other catastrophes in the fossil record.
