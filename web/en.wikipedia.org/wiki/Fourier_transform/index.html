Fourier transform

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A waveform decomposed into its component sine waves]

The Fourier transform is a mathematical transform that decomposes a function into its constituent frequencies, much as a musical chord can be described by the notes it contains. A function of time or space is converted into a function of frequency; the inverse transform reconstructs the original.

It generalises the Fourier series, by which Joseph Fourier (1822) had shown that periodic functions can be represented as sums of sines and cosines — originally in the study of heat conduction.

Definition and properties

For an integrable function f(x), the transform F(ξ) integrates f(x) multiplied by a complex exponential e^(−2πixξ). The transform is linear, converts convolution into multiplication, and differentiates into multiplication by frequency — properties that make it a master tool for differential equations and signal analysis.

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) applies to sampled data, and the fast Fourier transform (FFT) — popularised by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, with roots in Gauss's work — computes it in O(n log n) time, one of the most important algorithms ever devised.

Applications

Fourier methods are used in signal processing, image and audio compression (JPEG, MP3), solving partial differential equations, X-ray crystallography, spectroscopy, quantum mechanics, and probability (characteristic functions).
