Born–Oppenheimer approximation

Proposed1927
ProponentsMax Born and J. Robert Oppenheimer
FieldQuantum chemistry, molecular physics
BasisNuclei are far more massive than electrons

In quantum chemistry and molecular physics, the Born–Oppenheimer approximation is the assumption that the motion of atomic nuclei and the motion of electrons in a molecule can be treated separately. Because nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, they move much more slowly, so the electron distribution can be computed for effectively fixed nuclear positions.

Origins

The approximation was introduced by Max Born and J. Robert Oppenheimer in a 1927 paper on the quantum theory of molecules. It underlies the concept of the potential energy surface, on which molecular geometry, vibrations, and reactions are analyzed.

Description

The total molecular wavefunction is written as a product of an electronic wavefunction, which depends parametrically on the nuclear coordinates, and a nuclear wavefunction. Solving the electronic Schrödinger equation at many fixed nuclear geometries yields potential energy surfaces on which the nuclear motion is then treated. This separation makes the calculation of molecular structure and spectra computationally tractable.

Breakdown

The approximation fails when electronic states come close in energy, so that nuclear motion can induce transitions between them. Such non-adiabatic effects — including conical intersections and the Jahn–Teller effect — are important in photochemistry and fast reaction dynamics.
