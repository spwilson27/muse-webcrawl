Treaty of Fort Jackson

The Treaty of Fort Jackson, also known as the Treaty with the Creeks, 1814, was signed on 9 August 1814 at Fort Jackson, Alabama, between the United States government and the Creek Nation. It ended the Creek War of 1813–1814 and was imposed by Major General Andrew Jackson, who had defeated the Red Stick faction of the Creeks at the Battle of Horseshoe Bend in March 1814.

The treaty forced the Creek Nation to cede approximately 23 million acres (about 93,000 square kilometres) of land — more than half of their ancestral territory — in what is now Alabama and southern Georgia. The cessions applied to the entire Creek Nation, including the Lower Creeks who had fought alongside the United States, a fact that caused lasting resentment.

Terms

In addition to the land cession, the treaty required the Creeks to sever contact with British and Spanish agents in Florida, to permit the United States to build roads, forts and trading posts within the remaining Creek territory, and to surrender the prophets and leaders who had instigated the war. The United States in return guaranteed the integrity of the Creeks' remaining lands, though this guarantee would be progressively undermined by later treaties and by the Indian Removal Act of 1830.

Significance

The treaty opened vast tracts of fertile land in the Old Southwest to white settlement and cotton cultivation, fuelling the boom known as "Alabama fever." It also marked the emergence of Andrew Jackson as a national political figure and set a precedent for the punitive treaties imposed on Native American nations in the decades that followed.
