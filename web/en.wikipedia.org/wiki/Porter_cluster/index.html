Porter cluster

A Porter cluster, in the usage of business strategy scholar Michael Porter, is a geographic concentration of interconnected businesses, suppliers, service providers, and associated institutions in a particular field, linked by commonalities and complementarities. Porter's theory of clusters and competitiveness, developed in his 1990 book The Competitive Advantage of Nations and subsequent work, holds that such clusters raise productivity, drive innovation, and stimulate the formation of new businesses.

Characteristics

Clusters typically include final-product and service companies, suppliers of specialised inputs and machinery, financial institutions, firms in related industries, manufacturers of complementary products, infrastructure providers, and institutions supplying specialised training, research, and standards. Examples commonly cited include Silicon Valley in information technology, Hollywood in film production, and the Italian industrial districts for leather goods and textiles.

Relation to Porter's diamond

The cluster forms part of Porter's "diamond" model of national advantage, which links factor conditions, demand conditions, related and supporting industries, and firm strategy, structure and rivalry.

Criticism

Critics have questioned the vagueness of cluster boundaries, the difficulty of translating the concept into regional policy, and whether observed concentrations are causes or effects of economic success.
