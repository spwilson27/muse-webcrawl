Cornett

The cornett, or cornetto, is an early wind instrument popular from the medieval period through the Baroque, particularly in the 16th and 17th centuries. It should not be confused with the modern cornet, a brass instrument. The cornett was made of wood, ivory or horn, curved or straight, with finger holes like a recorder but sounded with a small cup-shaped mouthpiece like a trumpet, classifying it among the lip-vibrated aerophones.

Description

Cornetts were typically built in a gently curved shape, made from two pieces of wood joined and covered in leather, with an octagonal cross-section. Treble cornetts were about 60 cm long. Muted cornetts were turned from a single piece of wood with an integral mouthpiece and had a softer sound.

History

The cornett was one of the most important instruments of the Renaissance and early Baroque, valued for its ability to imitate the human voice. It was used in civic wind bands (the Venetian piffari), in church music alongside sackbuts, and in the works of composers such as Giovanni Gabrieli, Heinrich Schütz and Claudio Monteverdi, whose Vespers (1610) and L'Orfeo call for cornetts. The instrument declined in the later 17th century as the violin family rose, though it survived into the 18th century in German town bands. A revival in the early music movement has restored it to performance.
