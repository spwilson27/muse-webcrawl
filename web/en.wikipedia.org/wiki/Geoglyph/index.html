Geoglyph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A geoglyph is a large design or motif — generally longer than four metres — produced on the ground, typically formed of durable elements of the landscape such as stones, gravel or earth. Geoglyphs may be "positive", built up from arranged material, or "negative", made by removing surface material to expose differently coloured ground beneath. Many are so large that their design can only be fully appreciated from the air.

Famous examples

The best-known geoglyphs are the Nazca Lines of coastal Peru, created by the Nazca culture between about 500 BCE and 500 CE, depicting animals, plants and geometric shapes. In Britain, hill figures such as the Uffington White Horse and the Cerne Abbas Giant were cut into chalk hillsides. The Americas also hold the Blythe Intaglios of California and numerous Amazonian earthworks revealed by deforestation. The Atacama Giant in Chile is among the largest anthropomorphic geoglyphs in the world.

Purposes

Proposed functions include religious or ritual use, astronomical calendars, markers of water sources or routes, and territorial symbols. Because most geoglyphs predate written records in their regions, their meanings remain debated.

Modern geoglyphs

Contemporary artists have created geoglyphs as land art, and large modern examples include memorial and advertising earthworks visible from aircraft and satellites.
