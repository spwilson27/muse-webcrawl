Battle of Colachel

The Battle of Colachel was fought on 10 August 1741 (New Style) between the forces of the Indian kingdom of Travancore, led by King Marthanda Varma, and the Dutch East India Company (VOC), commanded by Captain Eustachius De Lannoy. Fought at Colachel, now in Kanyakumari district of Tamil Nadu, it ended in a decisive Travancore victory and is widely regarded as one of the earliest examples of an Asian power defeating a European naval and military force. The defeat marked the beginning of the decline of Dutch power in India.

Background

The Dutch East India Company held a near-monopoly on the pepper trade of the Malabar Coast, secured by treaties with local rulers. Marthanda Varma, who became king of Travancore in 1729, set out to break that monopoly and expand his kingdom, alarming the Dutch at Cochin. In 1739–41 tensions escalated into open war; the Dutch, under Governor Julius Valentyn Stein van Gollenesse, supported Travancore's rivals and dispatched an expedition from Ceylon under Captain Eustachius De Lannoy to seize the southern coast. The Dutch occupied Colachel and advanced inland, defeating Travancore forces at several points before Marthanda Varma blockaded them, cutting their supplies by sea and land.

The battle

On 10 August 1741 Marthanda Varma's army, supported by artillery firing from the shore, attacked the Dutch positions at Colachel. Travancore guns struck the Dutch warships anchored offshore; an artillery store explosion and the loss of powder forced the Dutch fleet to withdraw. Ashore, De Lannoy's troops, short of ammunition and surrounded, were overwhelmed. De Lannoy and 24 of his men surrendered and were taken prisoner. A popular account holds that the Dutch defeat was hastened when their gunpowder magazines caught fire during the bombardment.

Aftermath

The captured De Lannoy entered Travancore service and, as "Valiya Kappithan" (Great Captain), spent more than three decades modernising the Travancore army — introducing firearms, discipline and European drill — and designing fortifications such as the Travancore Lines. The Dutch never recovered their position: the Treaty of Mavelikkara (1753) ended hostilities on terms favourable to Travancore and effectively ended Dutch political ambitions in India, leaving the field to the British and French. A victory pillar erected by the Travancore government stands at Colachel commemorating the battle.
