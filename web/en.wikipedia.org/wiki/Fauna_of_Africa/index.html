Fauna of Africa

The fauna of Africa is among the richest and most distinctive on Earth. Because Africa straddles the equator and was largely spared the severe glaciations that extinguished much of the megafauna elsewhere, the continent retains an exceptional assemblage of large mammals — the last great surviving community of its kind — alongside immense diversity of birds, reptiles, fish and invertebrates.

Mammals

Africa is famous for its savanna megafauna: the African bush elephant, the largest land animal; both species of rhinoceros; the hippopotamus; the giraffe, the tallest land animal; and great herds of antelope such as wildebeest, whose annual migration in the Serengeti–Masai Mara ecosystem involves over a million animals. Large carnivores include the lion, leopard, cheetah, spotted hyena and African wild dog. Primates are diverse, from gorillas and chimpanzees in the central forests to baboons and vervet monkeys on the savannas, and Madagascar — separated from the mainland for some 160 million years — evolved unique fauna including all the world's lemurs.

Birds, reptiles and fish

More than 2,600 bird species occur in Africa, including the ostrich, the largest living bird, and huge Rift Valley lake flocks of flamingos. Reptiles include the Nile crocodile and numerous snakes such as the black mamba. The African Great Lakes hold hundreds of endemic cichlid fish species, a celebrated case of rapid evolution.

Conservation

African wildlife faces pressure from habitat loss, poaching — particularly of elephants and rhinos for ivory and horn — and human–wildlife conflict. Protected areas such as the Serengeti, Kruger, Okavango and Virunga national parks, together with community conservancies, play a central role in conservation, and wildlife tourism is a major economic driver in many countries.
