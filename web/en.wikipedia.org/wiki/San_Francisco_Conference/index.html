San Francisco Conference

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United Nations Conference on International Organization, commonly known as the San Francisco Conference, was a convention of delegates from 50 Allied nations that took place from 25 April to 26 June 1945 in San Francisco, California, which concluded with the signing of the United Nations Charter on 26 June 1945. Delegations (some 850 delegates, with thousands of advisers, staff and press) worked from the Dumbarton Oaks proposals and the Yalta voting formula, in commissions steered by the chief delegates of the sponsoring powers.

The conference decided the UN's enduring features: the Security Council with five permanent members holding the veto (the price, the small states were told, of great-power membership); a General Assembly of all members; the International Court of Justice at The Hague; and — pressed by smaller states and by consultants from civic organisations — a stronger human-rights and social mandate than Dumbarton Oaks had envisaged (the Charter's human-rights references and ECOSOC's prominence). Australia (Evatt), New Zealand, Mexico and others led the small-power amendments; the phrase “We the peoples” opened a charter adopted in the war's final months and in force from 24 October 1945, after ratification by the five permanent members and a majority of signatories. Poland, absent, signed later and counts as an original member (51).
