Pyrotechnics is the science and craft of using materials capable of undergoing self-contained, self-sustained exothermic chemical reactions to produce heat, light, gas, smoke and sound. Its best-known products are fireworks, but the field also covers theatrical special effects, safety flares, gas generators (including automotive airbag inflators), matches, and military devices such as tracers and thermal decoys. The word derives from the Greek pyr (fire) and tekhnē (art/craft).

Principles

A pyrotechnic composition mixes an oxidiser — classically potassium nitrate (saltpetre), today often potassium perchlorate — with fuels such as charcoal, sulfur, aluminium or magnesium, plus agents that colour the flame (strontium salts for red, barium for green, sodium for yellow, copper compounds for blue) or create effects (iron filings for gold sparks, titanium for silver crackle). Reaction rates are far slower than detonation: pyrotechnics burn or deflagrate rather than explode, though confinement can make them violent. Black powder (about 75% saltpetre, 15% charcoal, 10% sulfur), invented in China by the 9th century, remains foundational.

Applications

Display fireworks (shells, roman candles, cakes, fountains) and consumer fireworks dominate the industry, with major traditions in China — the historic centre of manufacture at Liuyang — Japan, Malta and Italy. "Close proximity" pyrotechnics serve concerts, sport and theatre, using low-smoke, low-debris compositions and electronic firing systems for split-second choreography. Safety and signalling devices include marine flares, highway fuses, and the solid-propellant gas generators that inflate airbags (originally sodium azide based). In film and live events, pyrotechnicians produce bullet hits, explosions and fire effects under licence.

Safety and regulation

Pyrotechnics manufacture and display are tightly regulated because accidents — from factory explosions in China and the Netherlands (Enschede, 2000) to warehouse fires — have caused mass casualties. Practitioners emphasise separation distances, static control, and the incompatibility of certain mixtures (notably chlorate–sulfur combinations). Modern practice increasingly replaces perchlorates and heavy-metal colourants for environmental reasons.
