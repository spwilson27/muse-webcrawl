EMV

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An EMV chip on a payment card

EMV is a payment method based on a technical standard for smart payment cards and for payment terminals and automated teller machines that can accept them. The name derives from Europay, Mastercard and Visa, the three companies that originally created the standard; it is now managed by EMVCo, whose members also include Discover, JCB, American Express and UnionPay.

How it works

Unlike magnetic-stripe cards, which store static data that can be skimmed and cloned, an EMV chip generates a unique cryptographic transaction code for each purchase, making counterfeit-card fraud far harder. Cardholder verification is commonly by PIN ("chip and PIN") or signature ("chip and signature"). EMV specifications also underpin contactless payments: tapping a card or phone uses the same chip technology via near-field communication, with tokenisation in mobile wallets such as Apple Pay and Google Pay.

Adoption

Chip cards were adopted across Europe in the early 2000s — the United Kingdom completed its chip-and-PIN rollout in 2006 — and most of the world followed. The United States migrated later, with a liability shift in October 2015 that moved counterfeit-fraud costs to the party (issuer or merchant) that had not enabled chip acceptance. As in-person fraud fell, criminal activity shifted toward card-not-present (online) fraud, prompting additional measures such as 3-D Secure.
