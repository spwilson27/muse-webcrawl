Tenancy by the entirety

A tenancy by the entirety (sometimes called a tenancy by the entireties) is a type of concurrent estate in real property that occurs when the property is owned by a single married couple as a single legal entity, with both spouses having an equal undivided interest in the whole property. It is recognized in roughly half of the states of the United States, and derives from the common-law doctrine that husband and wife were one person in law.

Characteristics

Tenancy by the entirety requires the traditional "four unities" of a joint tenancy — time, title, interest and possession — plus a fifth, the unity of marriage. Its defining features include:

  • Right of survivorship: when one spouse dies, the entire property automatically passes to the survivor.
  • No severance by one spouse alone: neither spouse can convey or encumber their interest without the consent of the other.
  • Creditor protection: in most states that recognize it, the property cannot be reached by the creditors of only one spouse, because the debt is not a debt of the marital entity.

Termination

The estate is terminated by the death of one spouse (vesting full ownership in the survivor), by divorce — which usually converts it to a tenancy in common or joint tenancy — or by the joint conveyance or mutual agreement of both spouses.

Availability

Tenancy by the entirety is available only to married couples, and in some jurisdictions has been extended to same-sex marriages and registered domestic partners. It exists in states such as Florida, New York, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts and Maryland, among others; some states restrict it to real property, while others allow it for bank accounts and other personal property as well.
