Congrès Internationaux d'Architecture Moderne

The Congrès internationaux d'architecture moderne (CIAM), the International Congresses of Modern Architecture, was an organization founded in 1928 and disbanded in 1959, responsible for a series of events and congresses arranged across Europe by leading architects of the modernist movement. CIAM was one of the most influential bodies in shaping modern architecture and urban planning in the twentieth century.

Formation

CIAM was founded in June 1928 at the Château de la Sarraz in Switzerland by a group of 28 European architects organized by Le Corbusier, Hélène de Mandrot (owner of the castle), and Sigfried Giedion, who served as the organization's secretary. The founding declaration, the La Sarraz Declaration, asserted architecture's social and economic responsibilities and its independence from historicist styles.

Congresses and the Athens Charter

Eleven congresses were held, including Frankfurt (1929, on the minimum dwelling), Brussels (1930), Athens/Marseilles (1933, on the "Functional City"), Paris (1937), Bridgwater (1947), Bergamo (1949), Hoddesdon (1951), Aix-en-Provence (1953), Dubrovnik (1956), and Otterlo (1959).

The fourth congress (1933) produced the principles later published by Le Corbusier as the Athens Charter (1943), which proposed the functional city organized into four functions: dwelling, work, recreation, and transportation, with high-rise housing set in green space and strict separation of uses. The Charter profoundly influenced postwar urban planning worldwide, from Brasília to European mass housing estates.

Dissolution and legacy

By the 1950s a younger generation — including Aldo van Eyck, Alison and Peter Smithson, and Jacob Bakema — challenged CIAM's rigid functionalism, forming Team 10. CIAM dissolved after the Otterlo congress in 1959. Its ideas remain central to debates about modernist planning, both as inspiration and as a cautionary example in critiques of zoning and tower-block urbanism.
