Mining (military)

Mining, in military terms, is the technique of digging tunnels beneath enemy fortifications or positions and filling them with explosives (or, in earlier eras, combustible material) to collapse the defences above. It was a principal method of siege warfare from antiquity until the early 20th century, and it also refers to the laying of land mines and naval mines, though those are usually treated separately.

Siege mining

Attackers dug a tunnel — a mine — toward the walls of a besieged castle or fortress, supporting the roof with timber props. Once beneath the wall, the chamber was filled with flammable material and the props burned away, causing the tunnel, and the wall above, to collapse. Defenders countered with counter-mines, listening for digging and fighting underground battles. The word "undermine" preserves this origin.

Modern use

Gunpowder greatly increased the effect. Mining reached its largest scale in the First World War, where specialist tunnelling companies drove mines beneath enemy trenches on the Western Front. At the Battle of Messines in 1917, nineteen enormous mines were detonated simultaneously beneath German positions on Messines Ridge, killing thousands and creating some of the largest deliberate non-nuclear explosions of the era. Underground warfare reappeared in later conflicts, including tunnel networks in Vietnam and Korea.
