Pulmonary edema

Pulmonary edema (American English) or pulmonary oedema (British English) is the abnormal accumulation of fluid in the tissue and air spaces of the lungs. It impairs gas exchange and can cause respiratory failure. It is a common medical emergency, most often caused by heart failure.

Causes

Cardiogenic pulmonary edema results from raised pressure in the pulmonary capillaries when the failing left heart cannot keep up: causes include systolic or diastolic heart failure, myocardial infarction, valvular disease (especially acute mitral regurgitation), arrhythmias and fluid overload. Non-cardiogenic edema arises from injury to the alveolar–capillary membrane, as in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), sepsis, pneumonia, inhaled toxins, and transfusion-related acute lung injury. Special forms include high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE), neurogenic edema after brain injury, and negative-pressure edema after upper-airway obstruction.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms are breathlessness, orthopnea (needing to sit upright), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, cough sometimes with pink frothy sputum, and anxiety; signs include tachypnoea, hypoxaemia, crackles, a third heart sound and raised jugular venous pressure in the cardiogenic form. Chest radiographs classically show cardiomegaly, upper-lobe blood diversion, Kerley B lines and perihilar "bat's wing" shadowing.

Treatment

Immediate management is to sit the patient upright, give high-flow oxygen and, in cardiogenic edema, vasodilators such as nitrates and loop diuretics such as intravenous furosemide; non-invasive ventilation (CPAP) reduces the need for intubation. Morphine was traditionally used but is now reserved cautiously. The underlying cause must be treated — revascularisation for infarction, rate control, dialysis for renal failure — and HAPE is treated by descent and oxygen.
