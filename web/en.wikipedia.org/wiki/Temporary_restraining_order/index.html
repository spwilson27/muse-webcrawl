Temporary restraining order

A temporary restraining order (TRO) is a short-term, pre-trial, emergency court order in civil procedure that compels a party to do or refrain from doing specified acts. It is intended to maintain the status quo until a hearing on a preliminary injunction can be held, usually within days.

United States federal law

Under Rule 65(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a TRO may issue without notice only if specific facts in an affidavit or verified complaint clearly show immediate and irreparable injury, and the movant's attorney certifies efforts made to give notice. A TRO expires at the time set by the court, not to exceed 14 days (extendable once for 14 more days for good cause). It must state its terms specifically and describe the restrained act in reasonable detail. Security (a bond) is usually required under Rule 65(c).

Standards and uses

Courts consider likelihood of success on the merits, likelihood of irreparable harm, balance of equities and the public interest — the factors also used for preliminary injunctions. TROs are common in cases involving threatened disclosure, asset dissipation, eviction, elections, and domestic protection orders (where the term is also used in family law for protective orders).

Appealability

TROs are generally not appealable, unlike preliminary injunctions, unless they effectively decide the case or are extended beyond the Rule 65 limits.
