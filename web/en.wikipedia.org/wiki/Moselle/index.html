The Moselle (French: Moselle; German: Mosel) is a river in north-eastern France, Luxembourg, and western Germany, and a left-bank tributary of the Rhine, which it joins at Koblenz. It rises in the Vosges mountains and flows north past Épinal, Toul, Metz, and Thionville, forms part of the border between Luxembourg and Germany, and then winds through a famous wine-growing valley past Trier to the Rhine.

Course

From its source near the Col de Bussang, the Moselle flows about 545 kilometres. Its major tributaries include the Meurthe, the Seille, the Saar, and the Ruwer. Between France and Germany the river valley is noted for tight meanders cut into slate highlands — the Eifel and Hunsrück — and for steep vineyard slopes that produce the Mosel, Luxembourg Crémant, and French Moselle AOC wines.

Navigation and history

The Moselle was canalised for large barges between 1958 and 1964 under a treaty between France, Germany, and Luxembourg, with a series of locks linking the French industrial region of Lorraine to the Rhine; the canalised river carries iron ore, steel, coal, and agricultural goods. Roman Trier (Augusta Treverorum) on its banks became a major imperial city, and the river was celebrated in late antiquity by Ausonius's poem Mosella. The Schengen Agreement of 1985 — which created Europe's border-free area — was signed aboard a river cruiser on the Moselle near the Luxembourg village of Schengen.
