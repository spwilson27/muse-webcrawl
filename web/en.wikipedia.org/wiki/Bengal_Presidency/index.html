Bengal Presidency

The Bengal Presidency, officially the Presidency of Fort William in Bengal, was the largest administrative subdivision of British India, established by the English East India Company and centred on Fort William in Calcutta. At its greatest extent it covered not only modern West Bengal and Bangladesh but also, at various times, Bihar, Odisha, Assam and territories stretching up the Ganges as far as Delhi and into Burma.

History

The East India Company established factories in Bengal in the 17th century, and the settlement at Calcutta was founded in 1690. After the Battle of Plassey (1757) and the Battle of Buxar (1764), the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II granted the Company the Diwani — the right to collect revenue — of Bengal, Bihar and Odisha in 1765, making the Company the effective ruler of the region.

Under the Regulating Act of 1773 the Governor of Bengal became Governor-General of India, with Warren Hastings the first holder. The presidency was administered by a Governor (later Lieutenant-Governor for the reduced province) and was the base of the Bengal Army, one of the three presidency armies.

Partitions

Because the presidency had grown unwieldy, the North-Western Provinces were separated in 1836 and Punjab annexed in 1849 was administered separately. Assam was separated in 1874. In 1905 Lord Curzon carried out the first Partition of Bengal, creating Eastern Bengal and Assam; the move provoked the Swadeshi movement and was annulled in 1911, when Bihar and Orissa were instead made a separate province. The presidency in its reduced form lasted until the partition of 1947 divided Bengal between India and Pakistan.

Administration and economy

The presidency operated the Permanent Settlement of 1793, which fixed land revenue with zamindars. It was the economic heart of British India, exporting opium, indigo, jute, tea and cotton, and its port of Calcutta was second only to London in the Empire's trade at its peak. The Bengal famine of 1770 and the famine of 1943 both occurred under its administration and remain major subjects of historical debate.
