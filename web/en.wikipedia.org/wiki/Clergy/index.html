Clergy

[Image: A group of Christian clergy in vestments processing into a cathedral, showing bishops, priests and deacons in rank order]

Clergy are religious leaders who are ordained or otherwise formally authorised to perform sacred functions within a religion — leading worship, administering rites and sacraments, teaching doctrine and providing pastoral care. The term is most commonly used of Christianity, but analogous offices exist in most organised religions.

Christian clergy

In Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican traditions, ordained ministry is structured in the threefold order of bishops, priests (presbyters) and deacons, forming a hierarchy headed in Roman Catholicism by the Pope. Many Protestant churches recognise pastors, ministers or elders, with varying degrees of ordination and congregational independence. Clergy typically undergo theological education and a process of discernment and ordination.

Other religions

In Islam, religious functions are led by imams, scholars (ulama) and muftis, though Islam has no ordained priesthood in the sacramental sense. Judaism is led by rabbis; Hinduism by priests (pujaris) and gurus; Buddhism by monks and nuns (the sangha) and, in Tibetan traditions, lamas; Sikhism by granthis.

Historical role

Clergy formed a distinct estate in many pre-modern societies — in medieval Europe, the First Estate — with privileges such as benefit of clergy. Their influence on education, law and politics has declined in many countries with secularisation, though clergy remain significant public figures in much of the world.
