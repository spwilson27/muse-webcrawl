Sociocracy

Sociocracy is a theory and method of governance and organisational decision-making that aims to create inclusive, effective organisations in which all members' voices count. The term was coined by Auguste Comte in the 19th century (from Latin socius, companion, and Greek krátos, power) and was given its modern form by the Dutch engineer and educator Gerard Endenburg, who developed the Sociocratic Circle Organising Method in his electronics company in the 1970s–80s, building on the educational work of Kees Boeke.

Principles

Endenburg's method rests on four rules. Consent: policy decisions are made by consent — not unanimity or majority vote, but the absence of a reasoned, paramount objection. Circles: the organisation is structured as linked circles, semi-autonomous teams that govern their own domain. Double linking: each circle is connected to the next higher circle by two people — its operational leader and an elected representative — ensuring information flows both ways. Sociocratic elections: people are chosen for roles by consent after an open discussion of nominations and arguments. Day-to-day operational decisions may remain hierarchical; sociocracy governs how policy and roles are decided, and emphasises feedback loops ("measure, analyse, adjust") borrowed from cybernetics.

Use

Sociocracy is used by companies, schools, cohousing communities, cooperatives and NGOs, particularly in the Netherlands and increasingly internationally; in the United States it is also promoted as "dynamic governance". Its circle-and-consent pattern influenced the later management system Holacracy, though the two differ in significant details.
