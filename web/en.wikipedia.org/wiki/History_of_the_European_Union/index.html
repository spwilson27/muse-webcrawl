History of the European Union

The history of the European Union spans the period from the end of the Second World War to the present, during which European states created a series of supranational institutions that evolved into the modern European Union (EU).

Origins (1945–1957)

After the devastation of two world wars, European leaders sought to prevent further conflict by binding national economies together. The Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950 proposed placing French and West German coal and steel production under a common authority. The Treaty of Paris (1951) created the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) with six members: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg.

The Treaties of Rome and the EEC

In 1957 the six signed the Treaties of Rome, establishing the European Economic Community (EEC) and the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom). The EEC created a common market and customs union, gradually removing internal trade barriers and adopting common policies, notably the Common Agricultural Policy.

Enlargement and integration (1973–1992)

The United Kingdom, Ireland, and Denmark joined in 1973, followed by Greece (1981), Spain and Portugal (1986). The Single European Act (1986) set the goal of completing the single market by 1992 and expanded qualified majority voting. Direct elections to the European Parliament began in 1979.

Maastricht and the euro

The Maastricht Treaty (1992) formally created the European Union and laid the path to economic and monetary union. The euro was introduced as an accounting currency in 1999 and as notes and coins in 2002. Austria, Finland, and Sweden joined in 1995.

Twenty-first century

Ten countries, mostly in Central and Eastern Europe, joined in 2004, followed by Romania and Bulgaria (2007) and Croatia (2013). The Treaty of Lisbon (2009) reformed the EU's institutions. The euro area debt crisis after 2009 strained cohesion, and in 2016 the United Kingdom voted to leave the EU, formally withdrawing in 2020 (Brexit), the first member state to do so.
