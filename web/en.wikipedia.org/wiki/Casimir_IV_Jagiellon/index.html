Casimir IV Andrew Jagiellon (Polish: Kazimierz Andrzej Jagiellończyk; Lithuanian: Kazimieras Jogailaitis; 30 November 1427 – 7 June 1492) was Grand Duke of Lithuania from 1440 and King of Poland from 1447 until his death in 1492. One of the longest-reigning Polish monarchs, he was a shrewd and patient ruler who recovered the Baltic coast for Poland in the Thirteen Years' War against the Teutonic Order and greatly extended Jagiellonian influence in central Europe. He was the father of four kings and of Saint Casimir.

Early life and accession

The younger son of Władysław II Jagiełło, founder of the Jagiellonian dynasty, Casimir was elected Grand Duke of Lithuania at the age of twelve after the assassination of Sigismund Kęstutaitis. When his elder brother, Władysław III, was killed fighting the Ottomans at the Battle of Varna in 1444, Casimir — after long negotiations to preserve Lithuanian autonomy — accepted the Polish crown in 1447, reuniting the two states in his person.

Thirteen Years' War

In 1454 the Prussian Confederation of towns and nobles rebelled against the Teutonic Order and offered allegiance to Casimir, who incorporated Prussia into Poland by the Act of Incorporation. The resulting Thirteen Years' War (1454–1466) see-sawed until Polish forces and their allies prevailed; by the Second Peace of Thorn (1466) Poland regained Pomerelia (including Gdańsk/Danzig), Chełmno Land, Malbork and Warmia — the province of Royal Prussia — while the Order retained eastern Prussia as a Polish fief. Poland thereby regained access to the Baltic Sea, transforming its economy and strategic position.

Dynastic politics and family

Casimir's marriage in 1454 to Elizabeth of Austria, daughter of the Habsburg king Albert II, produced thirteen children, earning her the nickname "mother of the Jagiellonians". Four sons became kings: Vladislaus II (Bohemia and Hungary), John I Albert and Alexander (Poland and Lithuania), and Sigismund I the Old (Poland and Lithuania). His son Casimir was canonised as Saint Casimir, patron saint of Lithuania and Poland. Through careful diplomacy Casimir made the Jagiellonians the leading dynasty of east-central Europe, while at home he balanced the magnates and the growing power of the gentry (szlachta), granting privileges such as those of Nieszawa (1454) in exchange for war taxes.

Death and legacy

Casimir died at Grodno in 1492 and was buried in Wawel Cathedral, Kraków, beneath a celebrated red-marble tomb carved by Veit Stoss. His reign is generally judged one of the most successful in Polish history, establishing Poland–Lithuania as a great power.
