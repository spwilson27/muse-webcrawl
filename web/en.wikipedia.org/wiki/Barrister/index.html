Barrister

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A barrister is a type of lawyer in common law jurisdictions, specialising in courtroom advocacy, litigation and expert legal opinions. In England and Wales, barristers are regulated by the Bar Standards Board, are called to the Bar by one of the four Inns of Court, and practise (mostly as self-employed advocates in chambers) having traditionally received work through solicitors rather than directly from clients — a split profession contrasting with fused systems (such as the United States) and with solicitor-advocates' expanded rights of audience.

Role and organisation

Barristers draft pleadings and opinions, appear in higher courts, and include the senior rank of King's Counsel ("silks"). The cab-rank rule obliges barristers to accept briefs in their field regardless of the client's identity or cause — a principle invoked to explain representation of unpopular defendants. Pupillage (a year's supervised training) gates entry. Other split jurisdictions include Ireland, most Australian states, Hong Kong and parts of the Commonwealth; Scotland's advocates and South Africa's advocates are close analogues.
