Syllogism

A syllogism (Greek: συλλογισμός, syllogismos, 'conclusion, inference') is a kind of logical argument that applies deductive reasoning to arrive at a conclusion based on two propositions that are asserted or assumed to be true. In its earliest form (defined by Aristotle in his 4th-century BC works Prior Analytics and On Interpretation), a syllogism arises when two true premises (propositions or statements) validly imply a conclusion, or the main point that the syllogism aims to establish. For example, knowing that all men are mortal (major premise) and that Socrates is a man (minor premise), we may validly conclude that Socrates is mortal. Syllogistic arguments are usually represented in a three-line form:

All men are mortal. (major premise)
Socrates is a man. (minor premise)
Therefore, Socrates is mortal. (conclusion)

Syllogisms were the dominant form of formal logic until the 19th century, when Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) introduced modern predicate logic, which subsumes syllogistic reasoning as a special case.

Structure

A categorical syllogism consists of three categorical propositions, each containing exactly three terms appearing twice across the propositions:

  • Major premise: a general statement, e.g. "All men are mortal."
  • Minor premise: a specific statement, e.g. "Socrates is a man."
  • Conclusion: which follows from the two premises, e.g. "Socrates is mortal."

The three terms are the major term (the predicate of the conclusion, "mortal"), the minor term (the subject of the conclusion, "Socrates"), and the middle term ("man"), which appears in both premises but not in the conclusion and links the other two.

Proposition types and figures

Categorical propositions are classified by quantity and quality into four types: universal affirmative (A, "All S are P"), universal negative (E, "No S are P"), particular affirmative (I, "Some S are P"), and particular negative (O, "Some S are not P"). Depending on the position of the middle term in the premises, a syllogism falls into one of four figures. Of the 256 possible combinations of forms, only a minority — traditionally counted as 19 (or 24 including weakened forms) — are valid. Medieval students memorised the valid moods using mnemonic names such as Barbara, Celarent, Darii, and Ferio, in which the vowels indicate the proposition types of the major premise, minor premise, and conclusion.

Aristotle's syllogistic

Aristotle developed the first formal system of logic in the Organon, especially the Prior Analytics. He treated syllogisms with premises of differing modal status (necessary, assertoric, problematic) and attempted to reduce all valid syllogisms to those of the first figure. Aristotelian syllogistic was elaborated by Greek, Arabic, and Latin medieval commentators, including Avicenna and the scholastic logicians, and remained central to the university curriculum for centuries.

Other kinds of syllogism

  • Hypothetical syllogism: reasons with conditional premises, as in modus ponens and modus tollens, prominent in Stoic logic.
  • Disjunctive syllogism: reasons from a disjunction and the denial of one disjunct.
  • Polysyllogism (or sorites): a chain of syllogisms in which the conclusion of one serves as a premise of the next.
  • Enthymeme: an informal syllogism with one premise left unstated, common in rhetoric.
