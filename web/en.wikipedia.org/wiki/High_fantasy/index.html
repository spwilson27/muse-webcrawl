High fantasy

High fantasy, or epic fantasy, is a subgenre of fantasy defined by the epic nature of its setting or by the epic stature of its characters, themes, or plot. The term was coined by Lloyd Alexander in 1971. High fantasy is typically set in a fictional secondary world distinct from the real world, with magic, mythical creatures, and a struggle between good and evil on a grand scale.

High fantasy
Coined: Lloyd Alexander, 1971
Key works: The Lord of the Rings, The Chronicles of Narnia, A Wizard of Earthsea, The Wheel of Time

Characteristics

Protagonists are often humble figures — a hobbit, farm boy, or apprentice — who grow through a quest, mentored by a wizard figure. Works feature detailed worldbuilding, invented languages, maps, prophecies, magical artefacts and dark lords. Tone is serious and themes include maturity, sacrifice, and the corrupting nature of power.

History

Precursors include William Morris's The Well at the World's End (1896), E.R. Eddison, and Lord Dunsany. J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) and The Lord of the Rings (1954–55) codified the genre, followed by C.S. Lewis, Ursula K. Le Guin, Terry Brooks, David Eddings, Robert Jordan, Brandon Sanderson and George R.R. Martin, whose work complicates its moral conventions.

High vs low fantasy

Low fantasy is set in the real world with intrusions of magic; high fantasy's secondary world is fully separate, though portal fantasies such as Narnia blur the distinction.
