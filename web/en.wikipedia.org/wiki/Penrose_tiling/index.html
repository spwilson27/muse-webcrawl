Penrose tiling

TypeAperiodic tiling
Proposed byRoger Penrose (1970s)
Tile setsKite and dart; thin and thick rhombs

A Penrose tiling is an example of an aperiodic tiling — a non-periodic covering of the plane generated by an aperiodic set of prototiles. It is named after the mathematician and physicist Roger Penrose, who investigated these sets in the 1970s. Penrose tilings are self-similar (they display inflation symmetry related to the golden ratio) and proved to be a mathematical forerunner of the physical discovery of quasicrystals.

Tile sets

Penrose produced several sets of tiles that force aperiodicity when matching rules are respected. The best known are the kite and dart pair, two quadrilaterals derived from a rhombus with angles based on 72°, and the rhombus tiling (P3), using a "thick" rhombus (angles 72° and 108°) and a "thin" rhombus (36° and 144°). Matching rules, often shown by colouring the tiles or marking their edges, prevent the tiles from being assembled periodically.

Properties

Any finite patch of a Penrose tiling occurs infinitely often in every other such tiling (the local isomorphism property), yet the whole tiling has no translational symmetry. The tilings can be generated by substitution (inflation and deflation), in which tiles are subdivided into smaller copies; the scaling factor is the golden ratio φ, and the ratio of the numbers of the two tile types approaches φ. John Conway's work popularised the patterns, and Martin Gardner's January 1977 Scientific American column brought them to a wide audience.

Quasicrystals

In 1982 Dan Shechtman observed electron diffraction patterns with five-fold symmetry in an aluminium–manganese alloy — impossible for a conventional periodic crystal. Penrose tilings provided the conceptual model: an ordered but non-periodic structure can produce sharp diffraction patterns. Such materials became known as quasicrystals, and Shechtman received the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Penrose tiling patterns have also been used decoratively, for example in the floor of the Andrew Wiles Building at Oxford's Mathematical Institute.
