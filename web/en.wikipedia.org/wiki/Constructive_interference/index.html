Constructive interference

Constructive interference is a phenomenon in wave physics in which two or more waves combine to produce a resultant wave of greater amplitude than any of the individual waves. It occurs when the waves meet in phase — that is, when their peaks (crests) and troughs align — so that their displacements add together.

Principle

By the principle of superposition, the displacement of the combined wave at any point is the sum of the displacements of the individual waves. If two waves of equal amplitude meet exactly in phase, the resulting amplitude is doubled. Constructive interference for waves from two sources occurs when the path difference between them is a whole number of wavelengths; a path difference of an odd number of half-wavelengths produces destructive interference instead.

Examples

Constructive interference creates the bright fringes in Young's double-slit experiment and in thin-film interference such as soap bubbles and oil slicks. It is exploited in interferometers, in phased-array antennas, and in noise-cancelling technologies in reverse (via deliberately produced destructive interference).
