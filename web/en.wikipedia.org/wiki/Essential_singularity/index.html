Essential singularity

The function exp(1/z) oscillates wildly near its essential singularity at z = 0.

In complex analysis, an essential singularity of a holomorphic function is a "severe" singularity near which the function exhibits extreme behaviour. Formally, an isolated singularity that is neither removable nor a pole is called essential. Near such a point the function does not tend to any limit, finite or infinite.

Formal definitions

Let U be an open subset of the complex plane, a a point of U, and f holomorphic on U \ {a}. The point a is an essential singularity if it is neither a removable singularity nor a pole. In terms of the Laurent series of f about a, the singularity is essential exactly when the principal part contains infinitely many non-zero terms.

Characterisation

The Casorati–Weierstrass theorem states that near an essential singularity the image of any punctured neighbourhood is dense in the complex plane. Picard's great theorem strengthens this dramatically: in any punctured neighbourhood, the function takes every complex value infinitely often, with the possible exception of one value. By contrast, near a pole the function tends to infinity uniformly, and near a removable singularity it is bounded.

Examples

The prototypical example is f(z) = exp(1/z) at z = 0; along the positive real axis it explodes, along the negative real axis it tends to zero, and along the imaginary axis it oscillates on the unit circle. Likewise sin(1/z) and cos(1/z) have essential singularities at the origin, while 1/z has a simple pole and sin(z)/z has a removable singularity.
