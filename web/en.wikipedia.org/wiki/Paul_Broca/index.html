Paul Pierre Broca (28 June 1824 – 9 July 1880) was a French physician, anatomist and anthropologist. He is best known for his research on the speech production centre of the brain — the region of the frontal lobe now called Broca's area — a discovery that founded the modern study of the localisation of brain function and the clinical science of aphasia. Broca also founded the Société d'Anthropologie de Paris (1859) and was a central, controversial figure in nineteenth-century physical anthropology and craniometry.

Early life and medical career

Broca was born at Sainte-Foy-la-Grande in the Gironde, the son of a Protestant army surgeon. A prodigy, he entered medical school in Paris at seventeen and graduated in 1844. He rose through Paris hospital appointments — the Bicêtre, the Salpêtrière, the Pitié and the Hôpital Necker — and became professor of surgery and a member of the Academy of Medicine. His early work included studies of cancer histology, rickets, aneurysms and the trepanation practised by prehistoric peoples.

Broca's area

In 1861 Broca examined a patient, Louis Victor Leborgne, known as "Tan" because that syllable was nearly all he could say. After Leborgne's death, autopsy showed a lesion in the posterior inferior frontal gyrus of the left hemisphere. Broca presented the case to the Anthropological Society, arguing that articulated speech was localised there. Further cases confirmed the finding; in 1865 he established that speech is usually served by the left hemisphere. The deficit he described — loss of speech production with relatively preserved comprehension — is called Broca's aphasia (expressive aphasia). Leborgne's brain is preserved in the Musée Dupuytren in Paris; modern imaging of it has refined understanding of the lesion.

Anthropology

Broca founded French physical anthropology, developing dozens of craniometric instruments and measurements and studying brain size, skull form and human variation. His conclusions about race and sex — including claims about the supposedly smaller brains of women and colonised peoples — reflected the prejudices of his era and are now regarded as classic examples of biased science, criticised notably by Stephen Jay Gould in The Mismeasure of Man. Broca was nonetheless an empiricist who opposed slavery and supported Darwinian evolution at a time when it was contested in France. In 1880 he was elected a life senator shortly before his death from a brain haemorrhage. His name is one of the 72 inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.
