Vocal range

Vocal range is the range of pitches that a human voice can phonate. A common application is within the context of singing, where it is used as a defining characteristic for classifying singing voices into voice types. It is also a topic of study within linguistics, phonetics, and speech-language pathology, particularly in relation to the study of tonal languages and certain types of vocal disorders.

Singing and voice classification

The typical human vocal range is about three octaves, though trained singers may exceed this. In choral and operatic practice, voices are grouped into standard ranges:

Voice typeTypical range (scientific pitch notation)
SopranoC4 – C6
Mezzo-sopranoA3 – A5
ContraltoF3 – F5
CountertenorE3 – E5
TenorC3 – C5
BaritoneA2 – A4
Bass-baritoneF2 – F4
BassE2 – E4

These classifications also consider vocal weight, timbre, tessitura (the range in which the voice is most comfortable), and the location of register transitions (passaggi), so two singers with the same total range may be classified differently.

Measurement

Vocal range is measured from the lowest to the highest pitch a singer can produce with musical quality; extreme registers such as whistle register and vocal fry may extend the measured span considerably. World-record holders for range include Tim Storms, credited with a range of about ten octaves at the extreme low end, and Georgia Brown, noted for an eight-octave span reaching into the whistle register.

Speech

In ordinary speech, a speaker uses only a small portion of their total range, typically around an octave, varying pitch for intonation and emphasis. Fundamental frequency averages roughly 85–155 Hz for adult men and 165–255 Hz for adult women.
