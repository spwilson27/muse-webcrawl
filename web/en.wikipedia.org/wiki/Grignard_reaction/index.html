Grignard reaction

The Grignard reaction is an organometallic chemical reaction in which a Grignard reagent — an organomagnesium halide, R−Mg−X — acts as a nucleophile and adds to a carbonyl group in an aldehyde or ketone, forming a new carbon–carbon bond and, after work-up, an alcohol. It is one of the most important methods for constructing carbon skeletons in organic synthesis.

Overview

Grignard reagents are prepared by treating an alkyl or aryl halide with magnesium metal in dry ether, a method developed by the French chemist Victor Grignard, who reported it in 1900 and shared the 1912 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery. The carbon–magnesium bond is highly polarised, making the carbon effectively carbanionic and strongly basic and nucleophilic. Grignard reagents react with formaldehyde to give primary alcohols, other aldehydes to give secondary alcohols, ketones to give tertiary alcohols, and carbon dioxide to give carboxylic acids. Because they are destroyed by water and react with acidic protons, the reactions must be run under rigorously dry, inert conditions. Industry uses Grignard chemistry on a large scale, for example in the manufacture of pharmaceuticals and silicones.
