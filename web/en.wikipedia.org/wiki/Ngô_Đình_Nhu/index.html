Ngô Đình Nhu (7 October 1910 – 2 November 1963) was a Vietnamese politician and the younger brother and chief political adviser of Ngô Đình Diệm, the first President of South Vietnam. Although he held no formal post of comparable rank, Nhu effectively controlled much of the regime: he founded and ran the secretive Cần Lao party, directed the Special Forces under Colonel Lê Quang Tung, and oversaw the Strategic Hamlet Program.

Early life

Nhu was born into a prominent Catholic mandarin family in Huế. He studied in Paris at the École des Chartes, qualifying as an archivist and palaeographer, and worked at the National Library in Hanoi before entering politics. In 1943 he married Trần Lệ Xuân, who as "Madame Nhu" became the regime's de facto first lady and a powerful figure in her own right.

Power in South Vietnam

Nhu shaped the regime's official ideology of Personalism (Chủ nghĩa nhân vị), adapted from the French philosopher Emmanuel Mounier. Through the Cần Lao party's cells inside the army, civil service and administration, he built a parallel structure of loyalty to the Ngô family. He was widely accused of manipulating elections, running the opium trade to fund covert operations, and directing repression of communists, rival sects and, in 1963, Buddhist dissidents. The pagoda raids of August 1963, carried out by his Special Forces, provoked international outrage and deepened the Buddhist crisis that destabilised the regime.

Death

On 1 November 1963 the Army of the Republic of Vietnam launched a coup, encouraged by the United States. Nhu and Diệm escaped the besieged Gia Long Palace through a tunnel to a safe house in Cholon, but surrendered the next morning at a church after being promised safe exile. On 2 November 1963 both brothers were murdered in the back of an armoured personnel carrier while being transported to Joint General Staff headquarters.
