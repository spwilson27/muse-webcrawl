Jean-Baptiste Donatien de Vimeur, comte de Rochambeau (1 July 1725 – 10 May 1807) was a French nobleman and general who commanded the French expeditionary force sent to aid the United States during the American Revolutionary War. His cooperation with George Washington culminated in the victory at the Siege of Yorktown in 1781, which effectively decided the war.

Early career

Born at Vendôme, Rochambeau was educated by Jesuits and originally intended for the Church, but entered the cavalry instead. He distinguished himself in the War of the Austrian Succession (wounded at Lauffeld, 1747) and the Seven Years' War, including the capture of Minorca (1756) and service in Germany, rising to lieutenant general. Between the wars he served as governor of Picardy and Artois.

American campaign

In 1780 Louis XVI appointed Rochambeau to command the Expédition Particulière, some 5,500 troops sent to Rhode Island to support the American cause — with instructions to place himself under Washington's orders. Blockaded at Newport for a year, he marched his army to join Washington on the Hudson in 1781, then joined the audacious march south to Virginia, where the combined Franco-American army, supported by de Grasse's fleet, trapped Cornwallis at Yorktown. Cornwallis's surrender on 19 October 1781 broke British political will to continue the war. Rochambeau is remembered in the United States with statues in Washington, D.C., Newport, and Yorktown, and as an original member of the Society of the Cincinnati.

Later life

Returning to France, Rochambeau governed provinces, was made a Marshal of France in 1791, and commanded the Army of the North briefly during the Revolutionary Wars before resigning in 1792. Arrested during the Terror and narrowly escaping the guillotine, he retired to his estate and died in 1807. His memoirs were published posthumously.
