Total loss

In insurance, a total loss occurs when insured property is destroyed beyond repair, or when the cost of repairing it exceeds a defined proportion of its value, so that the insurer settles the claim by paying the property's insured or actual cash value rather than funding repairs.

Types

An actual total loss means the property is wholly destroyed, sunk, or so damaged it has ceased to be the thing insured — a concept codified in marine insurance law. A constructive total loss arises when the property still exists but repair or recovery would cost more than its value, or it is abandoned to the insurer (for example a ship whose salvage costs exceed her value). In motor insurance, insurers apply a total-loss threshold — often 60–100% of pre-accident value depending on jurisdiction — above which a vehicle is "written off" and typically receives a salvage title.

Settlement

On payment of a total loss the insurer usually takes title to the salvage. Disputes commonly concern valuation; many property policies offer replacement-cost rather than depreciated-value cover. In marine insurance, notice of abandonment may be required to claim a constructive total loss.
