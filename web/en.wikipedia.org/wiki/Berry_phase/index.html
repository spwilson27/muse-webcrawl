Berry phase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of a vector being parallel-transported around a closed loop on a sphere, returning rotated by an angle]
Parallel transport around a loop: the geometric origin of Berry phase.

In quantum mechanics, a Berry phase (geometric phase) is a phase difference acquired by a quantum state over the course of a cycle, when the system is subjected to a cyclic adiabatic process — a slow tour of its parameter space that returns the Hamiltonian to its starting point. Discovered by Michael Berry in 1984 (with anticipations by Pancharatnam, 1956, in optics, and by Longuet-Higgins in molecular physics), the phase depends only on the geometry of the path, not on how slowly it is traversed.

The prototype is a spin in a magnetic field whose direction is carried around a cone: the spin acquires a phase equal to minus half the solid angle enclosed. Berry phase unifies seemingly separate phenomena — the Aharonov–Bohm effect is a Berry phase of the electromagnetic connection; molecular spectra carry it where potential surfaces intersect; polarisation optics, neutron interferometry and Foucault's pendulum (its classical Hannay-angle cousin) all display it. In condensed matter, integrating Berry curvature over the Brillouin zone gives the Chern numbers that classify the Quantum Hall effect and topological phases — making Berry's phase one of the load-bearing ideas of modern physics. Berry himself called it an example of how much generality hides inside the Schrödinger equation.

Observation

Berry phases are measured interferometrically — split a system, cycle the parameters on one arm, and read the fringe shift. They are also engineered: geometric (holonomic) quantum gates propose using Berry phases for computation intrinsically robust to certain errors.
