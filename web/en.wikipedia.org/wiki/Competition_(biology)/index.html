Competition (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biology, competition is an interaction between organisms or species that are vying for the same limited resources — food, water, light, space, mates or nesting sites — such that the success of one reduces the success of the other. It can occur within a species (intraspecific) or between species (interspecific), and is a central mechanism of natural selection and community structure.

Forms

Exploitation competition works indirectly, by consuming a shared resource first; interference competition is direct, as when animals fight over territory or plants release growth-inhibiting chemicals (allelopathy). Gause's competitive exclusion principle (1934) holds that two species competing for identical niches cannot coexist indefinitely — one will exclude the other — though in practice species persist by dividing resources (niche partitioning), as Darwin's finches did with beak sizes.

Consequences

Competition drives character displacement, restricts species' realised niches below their fundamental niches, and structures everything from forest canopies to gut microbiomes. Ecologists model it with Lotka–Volterra equations; removing a dominant competitor can release subordinates, a fact used in conservation and biological control.
