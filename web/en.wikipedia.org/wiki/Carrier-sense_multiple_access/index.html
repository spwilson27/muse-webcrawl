Carrier-sense multiple access

Carrier-sense multiple access coordinates stations sharing one medium.

Carrier-sense multiple access (CSMA) is a media access control method for shared transmission media in which a station listens for a carrier signal before attempting to transmit. If the channel appears idle, the station may send; if it is busy, the station defers according to a protocol rule. The idea is a central feature of early Ethernet and remains important in wireless networking, where collisions and hidden stations complicate simple listening.

Operation

A transmitter first senses the medium. In wired media, sensing can detect electrical activity directly. In radio systems, sensing is less reliable because a receiver may be interfered with by a station that the sender cannot hear. Protocols therefore combine carrier sensing with collision avoidance, acknowledgments, random backoff, or collision detection.

Variants

Common variants include 1-persistent CSMA, in which a station transmits immediately when the channel becomes idle; non-persistent CSMA, in which it waits a random period before sensing again; and p-persistent CSMA, in which a station transmits with probability p in an idle slot. CSMA/CD adds collision detection and was used by classic Ethernet. CSMA/CA attempts to avoid collisions and is associated with IEEE 802.11 wireless LANs.

Efficiency and limitations

Carrier sensing improves on uncoordinated ALOHA-style access by reducing avoidable collisions, but propagation delay means two stations can both sense an idle channel and begin nearly simultaneously. Performance depends on frame length, propagation delay, traffic load, and the backoff algorithm. In wireless networks, the hidden node problem and exposed node problem limit what sensing alone can achieve.
