The Nebra sky disk, with gold inlays depicting the full moon, a crescent moon and the Pleiades.

The Nebra sky disk (German: Himmelsscheibe von Nebra) is a bronze disk of around 30 centimetres (12 in) diameter and a weight of 2.2 kilograms (4.9 lb), having a blue-green patina and inlaid with gold symbols. These symbols are interpreted generally as the Sun or full moon, a lunar crescent, and stars (including a cluster interpreted as the Pleiades). Two golden arcs along the sides, marking the angle between the solstices, were added later; a final addition was a third arc at the bottom, interpreted as a "solar boat".

Discovery and recovery

The disk was found in 1999 by looters using a metal detector at a prehistoric enclosure on the Mittelberg hill near Nebra in Saxony-Anhalt, Germany, together with bronze swords, axes and other objects. Because the find was illegally excavated, the artifacts passed through the black market; in 2002 the disk was recovered by police in a sting operation in Basel, Switzerland, and the finders were arrested. The looters eventually led authorities to the find site, enabling archaeological investigation.

Dating and interpretation

The associated objects date the burial to around 1600 BC, in the early Bronze Age Únětice culture, although the disk itself may have been made somewhat earlier. It is considered one of the oldest known concrete depictions of the cosmos, and in 2013 it was added to UNESCO's Memory of the World register.

The arcs on the disk span the angle between the points where the sun rises and sets at the summer and winter solstices at the latitude of the find site, suggesting a calendrical or astronomical function. The cluster of seven stars is commonly identified with the Pleiades, whose heliacal rising and setting were used in many early cultures to regulate the agricultural calendar.

Authenticity

Although its illicit provenance initially raised doubts, analyses of the corrosion, the trace elements of the metals, and soil traces matching the find site led the scholarly consensus to accept the disk as a genuine Bronze Age artifact. The disk is now held by the State Museum of Prehistory in Halle.
