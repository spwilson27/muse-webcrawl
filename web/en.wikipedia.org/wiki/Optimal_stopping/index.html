Optimal stopping

In mathematics, the theory of optimal stopping or early stopping is concerned with the problem of choosing a time to take a particular action, based on sequentially observed random variables, in order to maximize an expected reward or minimize an expected cost. Optimal stopping problems can be found in statistics, economics, and mathematical finance (related to the pricing of American options). A key example is the secretary problem.

General formulation

Stopping rule problems are formulated in terms of a sequence of random variables observed over time and a reward function obtained if the process is stopped at a given stage. The goal is to find a stopping time — a rule that depends only on information observed so far — that maximizes the expected reward. Such problems are typically solved by backward induction or through the theory of martingales and the Snell envelope.

The secretary problem

The best-known example is the secretary problem: candidates arrive one at a time in random order, each must be accepted or rejected immediately, and the aim is to hire the single best candidate. The optimal strategy is to observe and reject the first ~1/e (about 37%) of candidates, then accept the next candidate who is better than all previous ones; this succeeds with probability about 1/e.

Applications

  • Pricing American options, where the holder may exercise at any time.
  • Sequential hypothesis testing in statistics, such as Wald's sequential probability ratio test.
  • Selling an asset or accepting a job offer under time constraints.
  • Clinical trials with early-stopping rules for efficacy or safety.
