Aharon David Gordon (Hebrew: אהרן דוד גורדון‎; 9 June 1856 – 22 February 1922), commonly known as A. D. Gordon, was a Zionist thinker and spiritual mentor of the Second Aliyah. He is best known for developing the philosophy sometimes called the "religion of labour", which held that physical work on the land was the foundation of both personal renewal and the national revival of the Jewish people.

Biography

Gordon was born into a religious family in Podolia, then part of the Russian Empire. He received a traditional Jewish education and later taught himself secular subjects, including Russian and European philosophy. For many years he managed an estate belonging to Baron Joseph Günzburg.

In 1904, at the age of 47, Gordon immigrated to Ottoman Palestine. Rather than pursue a white-collar career, he insisted on earning his living as an agricultural labourer, working as a hired hand in the orange groves and vineyards of Petah Tikva, Rishon LeZion and later in the Galilee. Despite his age and poor health, he refused lighter work. In his last years he lived at Kibbutz Degania Alef, where he died of cancer in 1922.

Philosophy

Gordon's writings, collected in works such as Man and Nature (Ha'adam vehaTeva), argue that a people becomes rooted in its land through direct creative labour. He distinguished between the life of the individual, shaped by society and "civilization", and a cosmic life experienced through unmediated contact with nature. Labour — especially farming — was for him not merely an economic activity but a spiritual discipline that heals the alienation of the modern Jew from nature and from his own body.

Gordon opposed both Marxist class struggle and armchair nationalism; he influenced the labour Zionist youth movements, particularly Hapoel Hatzair, and the emerging kibbutz movement, whose first settlement, Degania, adopted many of his ideas.

Legacy

The Gordonia youth movement, founded in 1923, was named after him, and numerous schools, streets and the A. D. Gordon educational institutions in Israel commemorate him. His essays remain central texts of Labour Zionism.
