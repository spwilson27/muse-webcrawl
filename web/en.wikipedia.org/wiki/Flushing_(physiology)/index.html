Flushing (physiology)

Flushing
ICD-10R23.2
ICD-9782.62
SpecialtyDermatology, endocrinology

Flushing is to become markedly red in the face and often other areas of the skin, from various physiological conditions. Flushing is generally distinguished from blushing, which is milder, generally restricted to the face, cheeks or ears, and generally assumed to reflect emotional stress such as embarrassment, anger, or romantic stimulation. Flushing is also a cardinal symptom of carcinoid syndrome — the syndrome that results from hormones (often serotonin, or histamine) being secreted into systemic circulation.

Causes

  • Sex flush, intensification of skin colour during sexual arousal
  • Hot flush, associated with menopause
  • Emotional stress or strong emotion
  • Fever
  • Alcohol and alcohol withdrawal; an alcohol flush reaction occurs in many people of East Asian descent owing to aldehyde dehydrogenase deficiency (ALDH2)
  • Eating spicy foods
  • Drugs, including niacin, calcium channel blockers, and tamoxifen
  • Rosacea
  • Carcinoid syndrome
  • Pheochromocytoma
  • Mastocytosis
  • Anaphylaxis
  • Dumping syndrome after gastric surgery
  • Hyperthyroidism
  • Exercise and increased body temperature

Mechanism

A flush results from vasodilation of the cutaneous blood vessels, mediated by prostaglandins, histamine, serotonin, and other vasoactive substances, or by altered autonomic nervous system control of vasomotor tone. The face, neck, and upper chest are especially prone because their vasculature is superficial and highly reactive. A "wet" flush is accompanied by sweating; a "dry" flush is not — a distinction that helps clinically, since the dry flush of carcinoid syndrome contrasts with the sweating hot flush of menopause and the flush of pheochromocytoma, which is typically accompanied by pallor and palpitations.

Sex flush

The sex flush is a flushing that occurs during the sexual response cycle. It begins in the excitement phase as a light reddening over the epigastrium and chest, spreading over the breasts, face, and much of the body during the plateau and orgasmic phases, and subsiding in resolution. It was documented in the laboratory studies of Masters and Johnson and is more commonly reported in women than in men.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. Hot flushes of menopause may be treated with hormone replacement therapy or non-hormonal agents. Flushing from rosacea may respond to topical agents and trigger avoidance. The flush of carcinoid syndrome may be controlled with somatostatin analogues such as octreotide, and niacin-induced flushing can be reduced by aspirin taken beforehand, or by the drug's extended-release formulations.
