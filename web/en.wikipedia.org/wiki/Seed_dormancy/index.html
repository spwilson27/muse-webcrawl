Seed dormancy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Seed dormancy is an evolutionary adaptation that prevents seeds from germinating during unsuitable ecological conditions that would typically lead to a low probability of seedling survival. In contrast, a seed that is not dormant but simply lacks suitable conditions is described as quiescent. Dormancy allows seeds to persist in soil seed banks, in some cases for decades or centuries, until conditions favour establishment.

Types

  • Physiological dormancy — caused by chemical inhibitors or hormonal balance within the embryo; the plant hormone abscisic acid maintains dormancy, while gibberellins promote germination. Cold stratification (a period of moist chilling) breaks it in many temperate species.
  • Morphological dormancy — the embryo is underdeveloped at dispersal and must grow before germinating.
  • Physical dormancy — a water-impermeable seed coat prevents imbibition; it is broken by scarification, fire, or passage through an animal gut.
  • Combinational and secondary dormancy — combinations of the above, or dormancy induced after dispersal by unfavourable conditions.

Ecological significance

Dormancy spreads germination over time ("bet-hedging"), so that a single bad season does not eliminate a population. Fire-following species time germination to post-fire conditions using smoke chemicals called karrikins. In agriculture, domestication has largely bred dormancy out of cereals—too much dormancy causes uneven stands, while too little risks pre-harvest sprouting in wet weather.
