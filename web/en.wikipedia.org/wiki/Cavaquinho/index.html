The cavaquinho is a small four-stringed Portuguese guitar-family instrument originating in the Minho province of northern Portugal. Played with the fingers in rapid rhythmic strumming, it is a staple of Portuguese folk music and was carried by emigrants around the world, becoming central to Brazilian samba and choro and giving rise to the Hawaiian ukulele.

Description and tuning

The Portuguese cavaquinho has a small figure-eight body, a short neck with 12 or more frets and four steel strings; the characteristic Minho tuning is C–G–A–D, though many regional tunings exist. The Braga-style instrument, associated with the luthiers of Braga, is one of the most prized forms. The Brazilian cavaquinho is similar in appearance but usually tuned D–G–B–D, like the top four strings of a guitar, suiting its role in samba harmony.

History and spread

Related small guitars such as the machete were taken by Portuguese sailors and settlers to Madeira, Hawaii, Indonesia and Brazil. In Hawaii the machete and rajão evolved into the ukulele in the late 19th century. In Brazil, the cavaquinho became the harmonic and rhythmic engine of choro ensembles and samba schools; virtuosos such as Waldir Azevedo, composer of the choro standard "Brasileirinho", made it a solo instrument.

Use

In Portugal the cavaquinho accompanies folk dances and the rusgas of Minho festivals; in Brazil it provides the characteristic syncopated chordal pattern of samba and pagode, and in Cape Verde it features in morna and coladeira bands.
