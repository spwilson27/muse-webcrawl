Chondrules are round grains of silicate material found in abundance in chondrite meteorites, the most common type of meteorite to fall to Earth. Typically about a millimetre across, chondrules formed as molten or partially molten droplets of dust in the early solar nebula, some 4.56 billion years ago, before being incorporated into the asteroids from which chondrites derive. The name comes from the Greek chondros, meaning a grain or lump.

Formation

The precise heating mechanism that melted chondrule precursors remains one of the open questions of planetary science. Proposed mechanisms include shock waves in the solar nebula, lightning, collisions between planetesimals, and radiation flares from the young Sun. Whatever the heat source, chondrules cooled quickly — at rates estimated from crystal textures at roughly 10 to 1,000 kelvin per hour — preserving distinctive internal textures such as porphyritic (large crystals in glassy groundmass), barred olivine, and radial pyroxene types.

Composition and types

Chondrules are composed mainly of the silicate minerals olivine and pyroxene, often with feldspathic glass, and may contain grains of iron–nickel metal and sulfides. They are classified by texture and chemistry; iron-rich (type II) and iron-poor (type I) varieties reflect differing oxidation conditions in the nebula. Most chondrules are of ferromagnesian composition, while rarer aluminium-rich chondrules are related to calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs).

Significance

Because chondrites are largely unaltered since the birth of the Solar System, chondrules record the conditions of the solar nebula and the earliest stages of planet formation. Radiometric dating places most chondrules a few million years after the CAIs, the oldest known Solar System solids. Studying their ages, chemistry, and isotopic compositions helps scientists reconstruct how dust in the protoplanetary disk was processed and gathered into planetesimals.
