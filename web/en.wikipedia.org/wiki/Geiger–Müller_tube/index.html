Geiger–Müller tube

A Geiger–Müller tube (or G-M tube) is the gas-filled sensing element of a Geiger counter, an instrument used to detect and measure ionising radiation. It was developed from Hans Geiger's 1908 counter by Geiger and Walther Müller in 1928.

Construction and principle

The tube consists of a sealed cylinder, usually metal or glass coated internally with a conductive layer forming the cathode, with a fine central wire anode along its axis. It is filled with a low-pressure inert gas such as helium, neon or argon, together with a small amount of a quenching vapour (a halogen or an organic compound).

A high voltage, typically 400–900 volts, is applied between anode and cathode. When an ionising particle or photon enters and ionises gas molecules, the freed electrons accelerate toward the wire, producing further ionisation in a Townsend avalanche. In the Geiger region of operation, a single ionising event triggers a full discharge pulse of fixed size regardless of the energy of the original radiation, so the device counts events but cannot measure their energy. The quench gas absorbs charge and terminates each discharge, allowing the tube to reset after a short "dead time" of roughly 100 microseconds.

Types and applications

Variants include cylindrical tubes for gamma and high-energy beta detection, thin mica "pancake" and end-window tubes sensitive to alpha particles, and halogen-quenched tubes with long working life. G-M tubes are used in portable survey meters, personal dosimetry, contamination monitors, education and nuclear industry instrumentation.
