Buddenbrooks

AuthorThomas Mann
Original titleBuddenbrooks: Verfall einer Familie
LanguageGerman
Published1901
SettingLübeck, 1835–1877

Buddenbrooks, subtitled The Decline of a Family (Verfall einer Familie), is the first novel by German writer Thomas Mann, published in 1901 when he was twenty-six years old. It chronicles the decline of a wealthy north German merchant family over the course of four generations, incidentally portraying the manner of life and ethos of the Hanseatic bourgeoisie.

Plot summary

The novel follows the Buddenbrook family of Lübeck from 1835 to 1877: the patriarch Johann Buddenbrook and his consul son Johann Jr.; the energetic but doomed Thomas, who builds the grand family house and becomes a senator; his frivolous brother Christian; their sister Tony, whose two unhappy marriages shadow the family's fortunes; and Thomas's frail, music-obsessed son Hanno, the last of the line. As the family's commercial fortune wanes, its members become ever more artistic, nervous, and unfit for business — Mann's central theme of the tension between bourgeois life and art.

Reception and legacy

Initially published in two volumes at a high price, the novel's sales took off with a cheaper one-volume edition in 1903. It made Mann's reputation, and the Swedish Academy cited Buddenbrooks principally in awarding him the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929. The Buddenbrookhaus in Lübeck, modelled on the Mann family home, now houses a museum.
