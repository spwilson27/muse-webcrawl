First Anglo-Afghan War

The First Anglo-Afghan War (Pashto: د برتانیه افغانستان لومړی جګړه) was fought between the British Empire and the Emirate of Afghanistan from 1838 to 1842. The British initially successfully invaded and occupied Kabul, restoring the exiled Shah Shujah Durrani to the throne, but the occupation unravelled in an uprising that destroyed the Kabul garrison; the retreat from Kabul in January 1842 became one of the worst disasters in British military history, with some 16,000 soldiers and camp followers killed. The war was part of the "Great Game" rivalry with Russia over Central Asia.

Causes

Fearing Russian influence in Kabul after a Russian envoy arrived at the court of Dost Mohammad Khan, Governor-General Lord Auckland resolved to replace Dost Mohammad with the former king Shah Shujah. The Simla Manifesto of October 1838 justified intervention, and the Army of the Indus invaded through the Bolan Pass in the spring of 1839, taking Kandahar and storming Ghazni before occupying Kabul in August.

Occupation and uprising

The occupation depended on subsidies to tribal chiefs; when the political agent William Macnaghten cut the subsidies, discontent erupted. In November 1841 Kabul rose in revolt: the envoy Alexander Burnes was murdered, and in December Macnaghten himself was killed by Akbar Khan, Dost Mohammad's son, during negotiations.

The retreat and aftermath

In January 1842 Major-General Elphinstone's force of about 4,500 troops and 12,000 camp followers evacuated Kabul under a promised safe passage, but was destroyed in the snowbound passes; Dr Brydon's arrival at Jalalabad became legendary. A British "Army of Retribution" retook Kabul later in 1842, rescued prisoners and burned the great bazaar, then withdrew through the Khyber Pass. Dost Mohammad returned to the throne, and British policy shifted toward non-interference for a generation.
