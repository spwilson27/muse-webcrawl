Effective field theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of nested scales: quarks inside nucleons inside nuclei inside atoms, each with its own theory]
Each scale of nature has its effective theory.

An effective field theory (EFT) is a type of approximation, or effective theory, for an underlying physical theory — such as a Quantum field theory — that captures the physics at a given length or energy scale while parametrising ignorance of shorter distances by a tower of interaction terms ordered by their importance at that scale.

The philosophy, crystallised by Kenneth G. Wilson's renormalisation group and preached by Steven Weinberg ('the quantum field theory of anything'), is that physics at one scale decouples from the details of higher scales: nuclear physics proceeds without solving Quantum chromodynamics, chemistry without nuclear physics. Fermi's theory of beta decay is the prototype — the low-energy effective theory of the Electroweak interaction, with its dimensionful coupling encoding the W boson's mass. The Standard Model itself is widely regarded as an effective theory of something deeper; 'Standard Model Effective Field Theory' (SMEFT) systematises searches for that deeper physics in precision data.

Power counting

Interactions in an EFT are classified by their mass dimension: relevant and marginal terms dominate at low energies; irrelevant (non-renormalisable) terms are suppressed by powers of E/Λ, where Λ is the cutoff scale — the hallmark that makes even 'non-renormalisable' theories predictive order by order. Gravity itself can be treated as an EFT below the Planck scale, yielding definite (if tiny) quantum corrections to Newton's potential.
