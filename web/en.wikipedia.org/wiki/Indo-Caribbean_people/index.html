Indo-Caribbean people, or Indian-Caribbean people, are people of the Caribbean whose ancestors originated in the Indian subcontinent. They are the descendants of indentured laborers brought from India by European colonial powers — principally the British, Dutch, and French — to work on sugar plantations after the abolition of slavery in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Indo-Caribbeans form the largest ethnic group in Guyana and Suriname and one of the largest in Trinidad and Tobago, with smaller communities across the region and a substantial secondary diaspora in North America and Europe.

The indenture system

Following emancipation of enslaved Africans in the British West Indies in the 1830s, planters sought a new source of plantation labor. The first ships carrying Indian indentured workers arrived in British Guiana in 1838; the system expanded to Trinidad (1845), Jamaica, and other colonies, and the Dutch and French recruited for Suriname and the French Antilles. Workers — recruited largely from the Bhojpuri-speaking regions of present-day Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, and later from South India — signed contracts (the girmit, or "agreement") binding them to several years of labor in exchange for wages and, in principle, a return passage. Conditions on estates were frequently harsh, and the system was criticized by reformers as a new form of slavery; it was abolished during the First World War era (1917–1920). In total, well over a million Indians were transported to colonies worldwide under indenture, and several hundred thousand settled permanently in the Caribbean.

Culture

Indo-Caribbeans have preserved and transformed Indian traditions within Caribbean society. Hinduism — especially Sanatana Dharma and Arya Samaj traditions — and Islam remain central institutions, with temples, mosques, and festivals such as Diwali, Phagwah (Holi), Eid, and Hosay observed publicly; many Indo-Caribbeans are also Christian, largely through conversion under colonial missions. The ancestral languages largely shifted to the colonial languages of each territory, but Caribbean Hindustani survives in speech and song, especially in Guyana, Trinidad, and Suriname (where it is called Sarnami). Cuisine — roti, curries, doubles, and sweets such as jalebi and gulab jamun — is now integral to Caribbean national food cultures. Musical forms such as chutney and chutney soca blend Indian and African-Caribbean elements.

Politics and diaspora

Indo-Caribbean communities have played major roles in the politics of Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, and Suriname, where party politics has often organized partly along ethnic lines; figures such as Cheddi Jagan of Guyana and Basdeo Panday of Trinidad and Tobago rose to national leadership. From the 1960s onward, large numbers emigrated to the United States (especially New York City), Canada (Toronto), the United Kingdom, and the Netherlands, creating a "twice-migrant" diaspora that maintains strong cultural and family ties to the Caribbean.
