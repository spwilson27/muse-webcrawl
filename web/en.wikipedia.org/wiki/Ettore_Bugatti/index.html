Ettore Arco Isidoro Bugatti (15 September 1881 – 21 August 1947) was an Italian-born French automobile designer and manufacturer, founder of the Bugatti marque at Molsheim in Alsace. Renowned for treating engineering as art, he produced some of the most beautiful and successful racing and luxury cars of the interwar period; his customers reverently called him "Le Patron".

Early life

Born in Milan into an artistic family—his father Carlo was a noted Art Nouveau furniture designer, his brother Rembrandt a sculptor—Ettore apprenticed with the bicycle and tricycle maker Prinetti & Stucchi and built his first car at seventeen. He designed vehicles for De Dietrich and others before founding his own company in 1909.

Molsheim and racing success

At Molsheim, Bugatti built lightweight, finely finished cars. The Type 35 Grand Prix car of 1924 became the most successful racing car of its era, winning around two thousand races including five consecutive Targa Florio victories. Luxury models culminated in the enormous Type 41 "Royale" and the Type 57, whose Atlantic coupé variants—styled by his son Jean—are design icons.

Later years

Jean Bugatti's death in a 1939 testing accident and the Second World War devastated the firm and its founder. The Molsheim factory was ruined, and Ettore died in Paris in 1947. The original company ceased car production in the 1950s; the brand was revived decades later by Volkswagen Group.

Legacy

Bugatti's maxim—"If it is comparable, it is no longer Bugatti"—and his dictum attributed via Bentley's W. O. Bentley rivalry that he built "the fastest lorries in the world" frame his legend. Original Bugattis are among the most valuable collector cars in existence.
