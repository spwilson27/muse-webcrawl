Salvage law

Salvage law is a principle of maritime law under which a person who voluntarily helps to save a ship, its cargo, or other maritime property from peril at sea is entitled to a reward commensurate with the value of the property saved. The doctrine encourages mariners to assist vessels in distress and to rescue property that would otherwise be lost.

Principles

Traditional pure salvage requires a marine peril, voluntary service (the salvor must not already be under a duty, for example as a crew member), and success in whole or in part—the rule of "no cure, no pay". The award is a matter of judicial discretion, guided by criteria listed in the International Convention on Salvage 1989, including the salved value, the skill and efforts of the salvors, the degree of danger, and the risk of environmental damage. Salvage of life alone earns no award from property, though life salvors may share in a property award.

Forms

Contract salvage is undertaken under an agreement made before or during the rescue—commonly Lloyd's Open Form, a "no cure, no pay" standard contract used by professional salvors. Pure (merit) salvage arises without any contract and is claimed afterwards in an admiralty court. Environmental salvage developed from provisions allowing special compensation to salvors who prevent or minimise pollution damage even when little property value is saved.

Finds and treasure

Salvage is distinct from the law of finds: salvage assumes the property still has an owner, whereas finds applies to abandoned property (derelict). Salvage claims over historic shipwrecks and treasure have produced long litigation, such as the disputes over the Spanish frigate Nuestra Señora de las Mercedes. Military vessels enjoy sovereign immunity, and the Sunken Military Craft Act and similar statutes restrict salvage of warships and military wrecks.
