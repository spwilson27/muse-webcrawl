Total internal reflection

Total internal reflection (TIR) is the phenomenon in optics in which waves arriving at the interface between two media from a medium with higher refractive index are completely reflected back into that medium, provided the angle of incidence exceeds a certain limiting value, the critical angle. It occurs when light travels from a denser medium (such as glass or water) toward a less dense medium (such as air) at a sufficiently shallow angle. Because no refraction into the second medium is possible beyond the critical angle, all of the energy is reflected.

Critical angle

By Snell's law, the critical angle θc for light passing from medium 1 (refractive index n1) to medium 2 (n2 < n1) is given by sin θc = n2/n1. For water (n ≈ 1.33) to air, the critical angle is about 48.8°; for crown glass (n ≈ 1.52) to air, about 41°. Although the wave is fully reflected, an evanescent wave penetrates a short distance into the second medium, decaying exponentially with distance — an effect exploited in fingerprint sensors and attenuated total reflectance spectroscopy. If another dense medium is brought within a fraction of a wavelength, light can "tunnel" across, a phenomenon called frustrated total internal reflection.

Applications

Total internal reflection is the operating principle of optical fibres, which guide light over long distances in telecommunications and endoscopy. It is also used in binocular and periscope prisms, retroreflectors, the brilliant sparkle of cut diamonds (which have a small critical angle), and the appearance of mirages and the underwater "Snell window" seen by swimmers and fish.
