Roots rock

Roots rock is a genre of rock music that looks back to the genre's origins in early American music, particularly blues, country music, early rock and roll and folk music. It was most popular in the late 1960s and 1970s, though the term continues to be applied to artists who draw on those traditions.

History

The term is most often used to describe the change away from the more experimental, psychedelic sounds of the late 1960s toward a more basic form of rock and roll that incorporated its early influences. The movement is closely associated with Bob Dylan and the Band, whose 1968 albums John Wesley Harding and Music from Big Pink are frequently cited as foundational. Other key acts included Creedence Clearwater Revival, whose stripped-down "swamp rock" sound dominated American airwaves around 1969–1970, and the Rolling Stones, whose albums Beggars Banquet (1968) and Exile on Main St. (1972) returned to blues and country roots.

By the mid-1970s the sensibility fed into heartland rock, Southern rock and the work of singer-songwriters, and it later informed the Americana and alternative country movements of the 1990s. Producers and labels used "roots" as a marker of authenticity, contrasting the music with arena rock and disco.

Characteristics

Roots rock typically features conventional instrumentation (electric and acoustic guitars, bass, drums, piano and sometimes harmonica, fiddle or pedal steel), verse–chorus song structures, and lyrics grounded in American places, work and vernacular speech. Production tends to be dry and direct compared with psychedelic or progressive rock.
