Ahaziah of Judah (Hebrew: אֲחַזְיָהוּ, ʾĂḥazyāhū, "Yah has seized") was the sixth king of the Kingdom of Judah and the son of Jehoram and Athaliah, the daughter (or granddaughter) of Ahab of Israel. According to the biblical account, he reigned for only a single year, conventionally dated to about 842–841 BC, before being killed during Jehu's revolt in the northern kingdom.

Reign

Ahaziah became king at the age of twenty-two (or forty-two, according to a divergent reading in 2 Chronicles) following the death of his father Jehoram. The biblical writers portray his brief reign negatively, attributing his policies to the influence of his mother Athaliah and the house of Ahab, into which the Davidic line had married. Because he was the only surviving son of Jehoram — his elder brothers having been carried off or killed during a Philistine and Arab raid — his death placed the succession in crisis.

Alliance with Israel and death

Continuing the alliance between Judah and Israel, Ahaziah joined his uncle Joram (Jehoram of Israel) in a campaign against Hazael of Aram-Damascus at Ramoth-Gilead. Joram was wounded in the fighting and withdrew to Jezreel to recover; Ahaziah went down to visit him there. While both kings were at Jezreel, Jehu, an army commander who had been anointed king of Israel, staged a revolt. Jehu killed Joram, and Ahaziah fled toward Beth-Haggan. He was overtaken and wounded at the ascent of Gur near Ibleam, escaped to Megiddo, and died there of his wounds, according to 2 Kings. The account in 2 Chronicles differs in detail, stating that he hid in Samaria, was captured, brought before Jehu, and killed.

Aftermath

Ahaziah's body was carried to Jerusalem and buried with his ancestors. On news of his death, his mother Athaliah seized the throne of Judah and ordered the destruction of the royal family, though the infant Joash was hidden and survived. Ahaziah is also named in the Mesha Stele's wider historical context and is generally identified with the "Ahaziah, son of Jehoram, king of the House of David" mentioned in the Tel Dan Stele, an inscription widely read as the earliest extra-biblical reference to the Davidic dynasty.
