Recessive gene

A recessive gene is an allele whose effect on the phenotype is masked when a dominant allele is present at the same locus. An organism must inherit two copies of a recessive allele — one from each parent — for the associated trait to be expressed; an individual with only one copy is a carrier, typically showing the dominant phenotype while being able to pass the recessive allele to offspring.

Two carrier parents (Aa × Aa) have, on average, a 25% chance of an affected (aa) child.

Mendelian inheritance

The concept originates with Gregor Mendel's pea experiments: traits such as wrinkled seeds disappeared in the first hybrid generation and reappeared in a 3:1 ratio in the second, leading Mendel to distinguish dominant from recessive factors. In a Punnett square for two heterozygous carriers (Aa × Aa), the expected offspring genotypes are 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa, so recessive traits appear in roughly one quarter of offspring.

Recessive disorders

Many genetic disorders are autosomal recessive, including cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, Tay–Sachs disease, phenylketonuria, and spinal muscular atrophy. Because carriers are usually healthy, recessive disease alleles can persist in populations; carrier frequencies may be high where the carrier state confers an advantage, as sickle cell trait does against malaria. X-linked recessive disorders — such as red–green colour blindness, haemophilia, and Duchenne muscular dystrophy — are expressed far more often in males, who have only one X chromosome. Consanguinity increases the chance that both parents carry the same recessive allele, raising the incidence of recessive disorders.

Molecular basis

At the molecular level, recessive alleles are often loss-of-function variants: one working copy of the gene usually produces enough functional protein for a normal phenotype (haplosufficiency), whereas two defective copies do not. Not all inheritance is purely dominant or recessive — intermediate patterns include incomplete dominance and codominance.
