Woodhead Commission

The Woodhead Commission, officially the Palestine Partition Commission, was a British technical commission appointed in 1938 to examine the practicality of partitioning Mandatory Palestine into Jewish and Arab states, as recommended by the Peel Commission of 1937. Chaired by Sir John Woodhead, a retired Indian civil servant, it reported in November 1938 and effectively killed the partition plan, concluding that no workable scheme could be devised; the British government then abandoned partition in favour of the policy that became the White Paper of 1939.

Background

The Peel Commission (1937) had recommended ending the Mandate by partitioning Palestine into a small Jewish state in the Galilee and coastal plain, an Arab state merged with Transjordan, and a continuing British enclave around Jerusalem. The Zionist Congress accepted partition as a basis for negotiation; Arab leaders rejected it outright. Amid the continuing Arab revolt, the government sent the Woodhead Commission to settle boundaries, economic and financial arrangements, and the question of population transfer.

The alternative plans

The commission examined three schemes. Plan A was Peel's own map. Plan B reduced the Jewish state by excluding the Galilee (which would remain under temporary mandate) and the Negev. Plan C shrank the Jewish state to a narrow coastal strip around Tel Aviv — barely some 1,000 square miles — with the Galilee and the south under continued mandate. Financial analysis showed each scheme left the prospective Arab state in deficit and dependent on subsidy, and all but Plan A left the Jewish state too small to absorb significant immigration.

Conclusion and aftermath

The commission concluded that it could not recommend any modification of Peel that would produce two viable, self-supporting states. Malcolm MacDonald, the Colonial Secretary, announced in November 1938 that partition was impracticable. Policy pivoted to the St James's Palace Conference of February 1939 and then the MacDonald White Paper of May 1939, which restricted Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years and envisaged an independent Palestinian state with an Arab majority — a reversal that Zionist leaders denounced as a betrayal of the Balfour Declaration on the eve of the Second World War.
