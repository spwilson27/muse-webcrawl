Nome (Egypt)

A nome (from Ancient Greek νομός, nomós, "district") was a territorial division of ancient Egypt. The Egyptian term was sepat (spꜢt). Egypt was divided into nomes throughout most of its pharaonic history, and the system persisted, with modifications, into the Ptolemaic and Roman periods.

Organisation

Traditionally, Upper Egypt was divided into 22 nomes and Lower Egypt into 20, giving the canonical total of 42 — a number with religious significance. Each nome had a capital city, its own patron deity and standard (emblem), canals and agricultural land, and was administered by a governor called a nomarch. Nomarchs were originally royal appointees, but in periods of weak central authority — notably the First Intermediate Period — the office became effectively hereditary and nomarchs ruled as local princes.

History

The nome system grew out of the prehistoric chiefdoms of the Nile Valley. Lists of nomes survive in temple inscriptions, most famously the nome list in the White Chapel of Senusret I at Karnak. Boundaries shifted over the millennia as nomes were merged, split or renamed. Under Greek and Roman rule the nomes remained the basic administrative units, each managed by a strategos, until the reforms of Diocletian and the Arab conquest reorganised Egyptian administration.
