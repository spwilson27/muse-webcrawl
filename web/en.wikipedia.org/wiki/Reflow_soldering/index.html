Reflow soldering

Reflow soldering is a process in which a solder paste (a sticky mixture of powdered solder and flux) is used to temporarily attach one or several electrical components to their contact pads on a printed circuit board (PCB), after which the whole assembly is subjected to controlled heat. The heat melts the solder, permanently connecting the joint. It is the most common method of attaching surface mount components to circuit boards.

Process

  1. Printing: solder paste is deposited on the pads through a stencil.
  2. Placement: pick-and-place machines set components onto the tacky paste.
  3. Reflow: the board passes through a multi-zone reflow oven following a thermal profile: preheat, soak (to activate flux and equalise temperatures), reflow (peak above the solder's liquidus — about 217 °C for common lead-free SAC alloys, peak typically 235–250 °C), and controlled cooling.
  4. Inspection: automated optical inspection and sometimes X-ray check the joints.

Thermal profiling

Getting the profile right prevents defects such as tombstoning (a component standing on one end), insufficient wetting, solder balling, voiding, and heat damage to components. Convection ovens dominate production; vapour phase (condensation) soldering and infrared systems are also used, and hobbyists employ modified toaster ovens or hot plates for small boards.
