Hemostasis

Hemostasis (from Ancient Greek haima, "blood", and stasis, "standing") is a process to prevent and stop bleeding, meaning to keep blood within a damaged blood vessel. It is the first step of wound healing and the opposite of hemorrhage. In hemostasis, blood changes from a fluid liquid to a gelatinous state through the formation of a clot.

Steps

Hemostasis occurs in several overlapping steps. First, the injured vessel constricts (vasoconstriction) to reduce blood flow. In primary hemostasis, platelets adhere to exposed collagen at the injury site, become activated, and aggregate to form a temporary platelet plug, assisted by von Willebrand factor. In secondary hemostasis, the coagulation cascade — a series of enzymatic activations of clotting factors through the extrinsic (tissue factor) and intrinsic pathways — generates thrombin, which converts soluble fibrinogen into fibrin strands that stabilise the plug into a durable clot. Finally, as the vessel heals, the clot is broken down by fibrinolysis.

Disorders

Failure of hemostasis causes bleeding disorders such as hemophilia and von Willebrand disease, while excessive or inappropriate clotting produces thrombosis, including deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, and myocardial infarction. Anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs manipulate these pathways for prevention and treatment.
