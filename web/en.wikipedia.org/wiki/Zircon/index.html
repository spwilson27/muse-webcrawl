Zircon is a mineral belonging to the group of nesosilicates and is a source of the metal zirconium. Its chemical name is zirconium(IV) silicate, and its corresponding chemical formula is ZrSiO4. The name derives from the Persian zargun, meaning "gold-hued". Zircon is ubiquitous in the crust of the Earth and occurs as a common accessory mineral in igneous rocks, metamorphic rocks and detrital grains in sedimentary rocks.

A zircon crystal. Large, well-formed crystals are prized as gemstones.

Properties

Zircon crystallises in the tetragonal crystal system. It is relatively hard (7.5 on the Mohs scale) and remarkably resistant to chemical weathering and heat, so zircon grains survive erosion, transport and even metamorphism, preserving a record of the conditions under which they formed. Natural zircon is usually yellow, reddish brown, green or colourless; heat treatment can produce blue and colourless stones popular in jewellery. Because of a high refractive index and strong dispersion, brilliant-cut zircon approximates the look of diamond, though it is not to be confused with cubic zirconia, a synthetic material.

Geology and dating

Zircon commonly incorporates trace amounts of uranium and thorium, which decay to lead at known rates. This makes zircon the premier mineral for radiometric dating: U–Pb dating of zircons can determine crystallisation ages with high precision. Tiny zircon grains from Jack Hills in Western Australia have been dated to about 4.4 billion years ago, making them the oldest dated material formed on Earth and evidence that liquid water may have existed on the surface very early in the planet's history. Oxygen-isotope ratios and hafnium isotopes in zircons are also used to trace crustal evolution.

Uses

Zircon sand is a principal ore of zirconium and hafnium and is used in refractories, foundry casting and ceramics. Milling produces zircon flour and zirconia (ZrO2), used in abrasives, insulation and dental ceramics. Gem-quality zircon, especially blue material from Cambodia, is cut for jewellery. The mineral is mined principally in Australia, South Africa and China, commonly as a by-product of heavy-mineral sand operations for ilmenite and rutile.
