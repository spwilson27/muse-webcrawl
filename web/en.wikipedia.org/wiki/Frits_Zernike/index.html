Frits Zernike

Born: 16 July 1888, Amsterdam, Netherlands
Died: 10 March 1966 (aged 77), Amersfoort, Netherlands
Fields: Physics
Known for: Phase-contrast microscopy; Zernike polynomials
Awards: Nobel Prize in Physics (1953)

Frits Zernike (16 July 1888 – 10 March 1966) was a Dutch physicist and winner of the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1953 for his invention of the phase-contrast microscope, an instrument that allows the study of internal cell structure without the need to stain — and thus kill — the cells.

Life and career

Zernike was born in Amsterdam into a family of teachers; his brother-in-law was the statistician... he studied chemistry, mathematics and physics at the University of Amsterdam, receiving his doctorate in 1915 for work on critical opalescence. In 1920 he became professor of theoretical physics at the University of Groningen, where he spent the rest of his career. With Leonard Ornstein he developed the Ornstein–Zernike equation in the theory of critical phenomena.

Phase contrast

In the early 1930s, applying his work on diffraction gratings and optical phase, Zernike realised that phase differences in light passing through a transparent specimen could be converted into visible amplitude differences. By inserting a phase plate in the microscope's focal plane, transparent structures such as living cells become clearly visible. The principle, patented in 1932 and developed commercially by Zeiss, transformed biological microscopy. Zernike polynomials, an orthogonal set of functions on the unit disk introduced in his optical work, remain fundamental in optics and ophthalmology for describing wavefront aberrations.

Recognition

Zernike received the Rumford Medal in 1952 and the Nobel Prize the following year. The campus of the University of Groningen's science faculty is named the Zernike Campus in his honour, as is a lunar crater.
