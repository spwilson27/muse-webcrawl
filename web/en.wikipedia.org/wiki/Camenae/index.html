Camenae

The grove and spring of the Camenae outside the Porta Capena.

The Camenae were goddesses of Roman religion, originally nymphs of springs and water associated with prophecy and childbirth, who were later identified with the Greek Muses. Four are named: Carmenta, Egeria, Antevorta and Postvorta. Their sanctuary was a grove and spring outside the Porta Capena in Rome, where the Vestal Virgins drew water for their rites.

The individual Camenae

  • Carmenta, the most important, was a prophetic goddess of childbirth and mother of Evander; the Carmentalia festival in January honoured her.
  • Egeria was the divine consort and adviser of King Numa Pompilius, Rome's second king, whom she was said to counsel in his religious reforms in their sacred grove.
  • Antevorta and Postvorta were aspects associated with Carmenta, looking forward and backward — to the future and the past — and invoked in childbirth according to the presentation of the infant.

Identification with the Muses

Early Roman poets introduced Greek literary forms by appealing to the Camenae in place of the Muses: Livius Andronicus began his translation of the Odyssey by invoking the "Camena". As Roman literature became more thoroughly Hellenised, the Greek Muses displaced the Camenae, though the name survived in poetic usage.
