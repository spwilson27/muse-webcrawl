Witch bottle

A witch bottle is a apotropaic magical device used in folk magic in early modern Europe and colonial America, intended to protect against or reverse witchcraft and harmful sorcery. Witch bottles were typically made of stoneware or glass vessels filled with items such as urine, hair, nail clippings, pins, needles, thorns, and sometimes a small cloth heart or other objects, then sealed and buried, often under a hearth, doorway, or threshold of a house.

History

Witch bottles are first documented in England in the 17th century, in a climate of widespread fear of malevolent witchcraft. Joseph Glanvill's Saducismus Triumphatus (1681) described a case from the 1670s in Suffolk in which a cunning man advised a man troubled by witchcraft to make a "bottle spell" against the witch. The practice continued into the 18th and 19th centuries, and examples have been found in archaeological contexts in Britain and the United States.

Construction and use

The classic form was a Bartmann or "Bellarmine" jug — a stoneware bottle decorated with a bearded man's face — filled with the urine of the victim, along with bent pins, needles, and nail parings. According to the sympathetic magic logic of the practice, the bottle was meant to cause the witch intense pain, forcing her to withdraw the curse or reveal herself. Some bottles were boiled, buried upright with the neck pointing up, or placed in a fire until they exploded.

Archaeological finds

More than 200 witch bottles have been recorded in England, many found during building work and river dredging. A well-preserved example discovered in Watford in 2019 contained fish hooks, teeth, and liquid residue. Analysis of such finds has confirmed the presence of urine and other organic material, corroborating historical accounts of their contents.
