Allele frequency

Allele frequency, or gene frequency, is the relative frequency of an allele (a variant of a gene) at a particular locus in a population, expressed as a fraction or percentage. Specifically, it is the fraction of all chromosomes in the population that carry that allele among the total population or sample size. Together with genotype frequencies, allele frequencies are a central quantity in population genetics.

Definition

For a locus with two alleles, A and a, in a population of diploid individuals, if NAA, NAa and Naa are the numbers of the three genotypes among a total of N individuals, then the frequency of allele A is:

freq(A) = (2·NAA + NAa) / (2N)

Allele frequencies at all alleles at a locus sum to 1 (or 100%).

Evolution

Microevolution is defined as a change in allele frequencies in a population over time. Four basic processes can change them: mutation, natural selection, genetic drift and gene flow. In an idealised population with no selection, mutation or migration, and with random mating and infinite size, allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation — the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, which serves as a null model for detecting evolutionary change. The minor allele frequency (MAF) is widely used in genome-wide association studies to describe the frequency of the less common allele at a variant site.

Example

If in a sample population 10% of alleles at a given locus are the mutant allele, then the allele frequency is 0.10, and under Hardy–Weinberg assumptions about 18% of individuals would be expected to be heterozygous carriers and about 1% homozygous for the mutation.
