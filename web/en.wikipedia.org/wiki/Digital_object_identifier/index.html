A digital object identifier (DOI) is a persistent identifier used to uniquely identify digital objects such as journal articles, research reports, datasets, books and official publications. Unlike a URL, a DOI does not change when the object moves to a new location: the identifier resolves through a central directory to the object's current address, so citations using DOIs remain functional even when publishers reorganise their websites.

Structure

A DOI consists of a prefix and a suffix separated by a forward slash. The prefix begins with "10." followed by a registrant code assigned to the publisher or organisation (for example, 10.1038 for Nature Portfolio). The suffix is chosen by the registrant and often encodes journal, volume or article information. DOIs are case-insensitive and are normally presented as a full URL, e.g. https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys1170.

Administration

The system is administered by the International DOI Foundation through registration agencies, the largest of which is Crossref, founded in 2000 by scholarly publishers; others include DataCite for research data and agencies for books, audiovisual works and government publications. Registrants supply bibliographic metadata for each DOI, enabling services such as citation linking, reference matching and usage statistics. The system is standardised as ISO 26324 and is built on the Handle System technology.

Use

DOIs are now near-universal in scholarly publishing: most journal articles, many conference papers, books, chapters, preprints and datasets carry them, and major citation styles (APA, MLA, Chicago) recommend including the DOI in references. By the mid-2020s well over 150 million DOIs had been registered.
