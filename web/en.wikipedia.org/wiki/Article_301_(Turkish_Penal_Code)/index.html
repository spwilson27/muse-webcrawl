Article 301 (Turkish Penal Code)

Article 301
Enacted1 June 2005 (new Turkish Penal Code); amended 2008
SubjectInsulting the Turkish nation, the state and its institutions
PenaltySix months to two years' imprisonment (after 2008 amendment)

Article 301 is a provision of the Turkish Penal Code that makes it a criminal offence to insult the Turkish nation, the Republic of Turkey, the Grand National Assembly, the government, the judiciary, the military or the security forces. Introduced with the new penal code on 1 June 2005, it became internationally notorious through a series of prosecutions of writers, journalists and academics, and has been a persistent point of friction in Turkey's relations with the European Union.

Text and amendment

As originally enacted, Article 301 punished publicly denigrating "Turkishness, the Republic or the Grand National Assembly of Turkey" with six months to three years' imprisonment, increased if committed abroad. The vagueness of "Turkishness" drew wide criticism. An amendment passed on 30 April 2008 replaced "Turkishness" with "the Turkish nation," reduced the maximum sentence to two years, and required the personal permission of the Minister of Justice before any prosecution could proceed.

Notable cases

The novelist Orhan Pamuk was charged in 2005 after remarks to a Swiss newspaper about the deaths of Armenians and Kurds; the case was dropped on a technicality after international outcry, shortly before he won the Nobel Prize in Literature. The Turkish-Armenian journalist Hrant Dink was convicted under the article in 2005 for writing about Armenian identity and received a suspended sentence; he was assassinated in January 2007 by a nationalist gunman. The novelist Elif Shafak was tried (and acquitted) in 2006 for statements made by fictional characters in her novel The Bastard of Istanbul.

Criticism

Human rights organisations including Amnesty International and PEN International have campaigned for the article's repeal, arguing that it is used to suppress freedom of expression and debate about sensitive history, including the Armenian genocide. Turkish governments have defended it by pointing to similar laws protecting state institutions in other countries, while nonetheless tightening its terms in 2008.
