Riders of the Purple Sage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Riders of the Purple Sage is a Western novel by Zane Grey first published by Harper & Brothers in 1912. It is Grey's best-known and most popular novel, and is generally regarded as the book that established the formula of the modern Western genre. Set in Utah Territory in 1871, the novel tells the story of Jane Withersteen, a Mormon ranch owner besieged by hostile forces, and the gunfighter Jim Lassiter who comes to her aid.

Plot summary

Jane Withersteen, a devout Mormon woman who has inherited a large ranch and valuable water rights at Cottonwoods, Utah, defies her church elders when she refuses to marry Elder Tull and befriends Bern Venters, a Gentile (non-Mormon) rider. When Tull threatens Venters, the mysterious gunman Lassiter arrives and intervenes. Lassiter has come to Utah searching for the grave of his long-lost sister, Milly Erne, and gradually uncovers the religious persecution that drove her to her death.

Meanwhile Venters, fleeing into the canyons, discovers Surprise Valley, a hidden valley where the outlaw Bess lives disguised as a masked rider. The interwoven plots converge in a dramatic climax in which Lassiter rolls a balancing rock down onto their pursuers to seal the valley and protect Jane and the child Fay Larkin.

Themes

The novel is notable for its critique of religious authoritarianism and polygamy within the early Mormon church, a stance that made it controversial on publication. It also features vivid descriptions of the Utah-Arizona canyon country, the purple sage of the title giving the landscape an almost spiritual character.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted to film numerous times, including silent versions in 1918 and 1925 and sound versions in 1931, 1941 and a television film in 1996 starring Ed Harris and Amy Madigan. A sequel, The Rainbow Trail (1915), continues the story.

Legacy

Riders of the Purple Sage remains one of the best-selling Western novels of all time and is credited with codifying many conventions of the Western: the lone gunfighter, the besieged ranch, the climactic gunfight and the redemptive power of the landscape.
