Synovial membrane

The synovial membrane (also known as the synovium or stratum synoviale) is a specialized connective tissue that lines the inner surface of capsules of synovial joints and tendon sheaths. It makes direct contact with the synovial fluid in the joint cavity, which it produces, and it is in contact with the fibrous membrane of the capsule on its outer surface.

Structure

The membrane consists of an inner lining layer (the intima), one to three cells thick, resting on a vascular subintimal connective tissue. The intima contains two main cell types: macrophage-like synoviocytes (type A), which clear debris from the joint space, and fibroblast-like synoviocytes (type B), which synthesize hyaluronic acid and lubricin — the molecules that give synovial fluid its lubricating properties. Unlike an epithelium, the intima lacks a basement membrane, allowing rapid exchange between blood vessels and joint fluid.

Synovial membranes are classified by their subintimal tissue as areolar, adipose, or fibrous. Where the membrane lines tendon sheaths and bursae it performs the same lubricating function.

Function

  • Production and turnover of synovial fluid.
  • Nutrition of avascular structures within the joint, such as articular cartilage.
  • Removal of debris and microorganisms by phagocytic cells.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the synovial membrane, synovitis, is a hallmark of rheumatoid arthritis, in which the membrane proliferates into an invasive tissue called pannus that erodes cartilage and bone. Surgical removal of diseased synovium (synovectomy) was historically used to treat such joints. Tumours arising from synovial-like tissue include synovial sarcoma, despite typically not arising within joints.
