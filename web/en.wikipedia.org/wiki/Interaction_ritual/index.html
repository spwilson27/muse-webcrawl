Interaction ritual

An interaction ritual, in sociology, is a mechanism by which social solidarity and emotional energy are generated in face-to-face encounters. The concept derives from Émile Durkheim's analysis of religious ritual and Erving Goffman's studies of the interaction order, and was formalised by Randall Collins in his theory of interaction ritual chains (2004).

Ingredients and outcomes

According to Collins, a successful interaction ritual requires bodily co-presence of participants, barriers excluding outsiders, mutual focus of attention, and a shared mood. When these ingredients feed back on one another — through rhythmic synchronisation and shared entrainment — the ritual produces group solidarity, emotional energy in individuals, symbols of the social relationship (sacred objects), and moral standards. Failed rituals drain emotional energy.

Applications

The theory has been applied to religious gatherings, political rallies, concerts, sports crowds, workplaces, and — with debate about whether bodily co-presence can be substituted — to online interaction.

References

Collins, Interaction Ritual Chains (2004); Durkheim, The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912).
