Rocket artillery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rocket artillery is artillery that uses unguided or guided rockets as its primary projectile, delivered from static emplacements or mobile launch vehicles. Because rockets carry their own propulsion, launchers need not withstand chamber pressures like a cannon barrel, allowing multi-tube multiple launch rocket systems (MLRS) to deliver huge salvoes of high explosive over a wide area in seconds — at the cost of lower accuracy and greater logistical burden than tube artillery.

History

Military rockets began with Chinese fire arrows and were developed in India by Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan, whose iron-cased Mysorean rockets inspired the British Congreve rockets used in the Napoleonic Wars and the War of 1812 (the "rockets' red glare" of the US national anthem). Early rockets were inaccurate; the spin-stabilised Hale rocket improved matters, but tube artillery displaced rockets by the late 19th century.

Modern era

Rocket artillery returned decisively in the Second World War with the Soviet Katyusha truck-mounted launchers, the German Nebelwerfer, and the American T34 Calliope. Postwar systems include the Soviet BM-21 Grad (122 mm, 40 tubes), the US M270 MLRS and the lighter HIMARS, whose GMLRS guided rockets (and longer-range ATACMS missiles) give near-precision effects. Other widely used families include China's PHL series, Russia's BM-27 Uragan and BM-30 Smerch, and India's Pinaka.

Characteristics

  • Massed fire: saturation of area targets with submunitions or unitary warheads.
  • Shoot-and-scoot: mobile launchers fire and relocate before counter-battery fire arrives.
  • Guidance: modern GPS/inertial-guided rockets blur the line between MLRS and short-range missiles.
