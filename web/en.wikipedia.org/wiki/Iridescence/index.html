Iridescence

Iridescence (also known as goniochromism) is the phenomenon of certain surfaces that appear gradually to change colour as the angle of view or the angle of illumination changes. Iridescence is caused by multiple reflections from two or more translucent or semi-transparent surfaces in which phase shift and interference of the reflections modulate the incident light by amplifying or attenuating some frequencies more than others. The word derives from the Greek goddess Iris, personification of the rainbow.

Mechanism

Iridescence is a form of structural colouration. Thin films, multi-layer reflectors, diffraction gratings, and photonic crystals all produce it: light reflecting from successive interfaces interferes, so that the wavelengths reinforced depend on the optical path difference and hence on viewing angle. Unlike pigmentary colours, iridescent colours shift, brighten, and extinguish as the geometry changes.

Examples in nature

Iridescence is widespread in the animal kingdom: the neck feathers of pigeons and hummingbirds, the wings of Morpho butterflies, the shells of abalone and beetles, the scales of many fish, and the skin of some cephalopods. In animals it serves in display, camouflage, and species recognition. Soap bubbles, oil films on water, nacre (mother of pearl), and the play of colour in opal are familiar non-biological examples.

Applications

Engineers exploit iridescent and other structural colour effects in security printing (banknotes and passports), cosmetics, automotive paints, and optical sensors, where colour change can signal strain, temperature, or chemical exposure.
