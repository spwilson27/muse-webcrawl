Joule

The joule (symbol: J) is the unit of energy in the International System of Units (SI). It is equal to the amount of work done when a force of one newton displaces a mass through a distance of one metre in the direction of that force. It is also the energy dissipated as heat when an electric current of one ampere passes through a resistance of one ohm for one second. It is named after the English physicist James Prescott Joule (1818–1889).

Definition

In terms of SI base units, one joule equals one kilogram metre squared per second squared (1 J = 1 kg·m²·s⁻²). Equivalently, it is one newton-metre (N·m), one watt-second (W·s), and one coulomb-volt (C·V) — reflecting the unification of mechanical, thermal, and electrical energy that James Prescott Joule's experiments established through the mechanical equivalent of heat.

Examples

One joule represents approximately:

  • The energy required to lift a small apple (≈102 g) one metre against Earth's gravity.
  • The energy released when that apple falls one metre.
  • The heat required to raise the temperature of 1 g of dry air by about 1 K... more precisely, the energy needed to heat 1 kg of water by about 0.00024 °C.
  • The kinetic energy of a 2 kg mass moving at 1 m/s.

Everyday quantities are commonly expressed in kilojoules (kJ), megajoules (MJ), and gigajoules (GJ); food energy, traditionally measured in calories, is labelled in kilojoules in many countries (1 food Calorie = 4.184 kJ).

History

The unit was adopted in honour of Joule's pioneering measurements of the mechanical equivalent of heat in the 1840s, which established the conservation of energy. It was first proposed as a practical electrical unit in the 1880s and became the SI unit of energy with the adoption of the International System of Units in 1960.
