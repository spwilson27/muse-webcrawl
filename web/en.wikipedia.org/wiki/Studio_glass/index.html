Studio glass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Studio glass is the use of glass as an artistic medium by artists working in individual studios rather than factories, and the name of the art movement that began in the United States in the 1960s. Before the studio glass movement, artistic glass was designed by artists but executed by skilled craftspeople in industrial settings; the movement made it possible for a single artist to design and produce work in a small workshop.

Origins

The movement is generally dated to 1962, when Harvey Littleton, a ceramics professor at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, held two workshops at the Toledo Museum of Art. Working with scientist Dominick Labino, who developed a low-melting glass composition and a small furnace suitable for a studio, Littleton demonstrated that artists could melt and blow glass outside a factory. Littleton went on to establish the first university glass programme, training students including Marvin Lipofsky and Dale Chihuly, who spread the practice internationally.

Development

Early studio glass emphasised free-blown vessels and sculptural forms. As furnaces, annealers, and cold-working equipment became widely available, and as university programmes and workshops such as the Pilchuck Glass School (founded 1971 in Washington state) trained new generations, techniques expanded to include kiln-forming, fusing, slumping, casting, flameworking, and pâte de verre. The movement spread strongly in Europe, Australia, and Japan.

Notable artists

Dale Chihuly became the movement's best-known figure, noted for large-scale installations and architectural works produced with a team of glassblowers. Other prominent studio glass artists include Lino Tagliapietra, William Morris, Toots Zynsky, Stanislav Libenský and Jaroslava Brychtová, and Bertil Vallien.
