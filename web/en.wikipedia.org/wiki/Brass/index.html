Brass

Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc, golden in colour, known since antiquity and still made in huge quantities for plumbing fittings, musical instruments, ammunition cases, locks, gears and decorative ware. Common brasses run from about 5% to 45% zinc.

Zinc hardens and strengthens copper while keeping it corrosion-resistant and easy to cast and machine ('free-cutting' brass adds a little lead). Brass does not spark when struck, suiting it to tools for flammable environments, and its acoustic properties make it the material of trumpets, trombones and horns — the 'brass family'.

History

Early brass was made by heating copper with calamine (zinc ore) — cementation — because metallic zinc was unknown in the West; true spelter brass followed once zinc metal was produced in the 16th–18th centuries.

Dezincification

In some waters, zinc can leach selectively from brass, leaving spongy weak copper; modern plumbing brasses are formulated (DZR) to resist this.
