Logical form (linguistics)

In generative linguistics, the term logical form (LF) has two related but distinct meanings:

  • Logical form in its broad sense, roughly equivalent to what was called semantic representation in certain grammatical models of the 1970s.
  • Logical Form as a specific level of representation in the grammar — a syntactic level which mediates between surface structure (the phonological form, PF) and semantic representation, derived by covert syntactic movement.

It is this second sense, developed within government and binding theory and its successors, that is most influential. Most modern theories of the syntax–semantics interface, including minimalism, use LF in the sense of a distinct level of syntactic representation which is input to semantic interpretation.

Overview

In the Y-model of grammar associated with Noam Chomsky's work, deep structure (or D-structure) is transformed into surface structure (S-structure), which then branches into two interpretive levels: phonetic form (PF), the level pronounced, and logical form (LF), the level interpreted by the semantic component. Operations that occur after S-structure on the branch to LF — so-called "covert" operations — do not affect pronunciation.

The most important motivation for LF comes from the treatment of quantifier scope. In a sentence such as "Every student read a book," the scope relations between the quantifiers may not be transparently reflected in the surface word order. The operation of quantifier raising (QR), proposed by Robert May in 1977, covertly moves quantifier phrases at LF, producing representations in which scope relations can be read directly off the structure. LF representations are thus intended to encode the aspects of structure relevant to meaning, such as scope, binding relationships, and the interpretation of wh-movement traces and empty categories.

Not to be confused with

LF in linguistics is related to, but not identical with, the notion of logical form in logic and philosophy of language, where it refers to the abstract form of a statement stripped of content in the vocabulary of formal logic. The linguistic LF is a level of natural-language syntactic structure, though it shares with the logician's notion the goal of making semantic relations structurally explicit.
