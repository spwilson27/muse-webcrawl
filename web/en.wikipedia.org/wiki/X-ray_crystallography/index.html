X-ray crystallography

[Image: An X-ray diffraction pattern from a crystal — the raw data of crystallography]

Overview

X-ray crystallography is the experimental science of determining the atomic and molecular structure of a crystal, in which the crystalline material causes a beam of incident X-rays to diffract in specific directions. By measuring the angles and intensities of the diffracted beams, a crystallographer produces a three-dimensional picture of the density of electrons within the crystal, from which the positions of atoms and their chemical bonds are deduced.

History and achievements

Founded by Max von Laue's 1912 diffraction discovery and the Braggs' law and first structures (1912–13), crystallography solved the structures of salts, minerals, metals and then molecules: penicillin (Dorothy Hodgkin), vitamin B12, myoglobin and haemoglobin (Kendrew and Perutz), DNA's supporting diffraction evidence (Franklin and Gosling's Photo 51; Wilkins), insulin, and the ribosome. It remains a Nobel-rich technique; from 2013 micro-electron diffraction and serial femtosecond crystallography at X-ray free-electron lasers extended it to tiny crystals and molecular movies. The Cambridge Structural Database archives over a million small-molecule structures.
