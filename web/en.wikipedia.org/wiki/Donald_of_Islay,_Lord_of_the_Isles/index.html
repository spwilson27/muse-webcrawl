Donald of Islay (Scottish Gaelic: Dòmhnall Ìle), known as Donald MacDonald, was the second Lord of the Isles (1386–1423), the head of Clan Donald, the great Norse-Gaelic lordship of the Hebrides and western seaboard of Scotland. The most powerful magnate of his generation in the west, he is best remembered for staking his wife's claim to the Earldom of Ross by force of arms, culminating in the Battle of Harlaw in 1411, one of the bloodiest battles fought on Scottish soil.

Inheritance and lordship

Donald succeeded his father John of Islay in 1386, ruling from his strongholds at Finlaggan on Islay — the administrative and ceremonial centre of the lordship — and Ardtornish in Morvern. His lordship commanded a fleet of birlinns (West Highland galleys) and a Gaelic court that patronised bards, harpists and the hereditary medical and legal families of the Isles. His marriage to Mariota Leslie brought the lordship a claim, through her descent, to the Earldom of Ross in the northern mainland.

The Ross claim and Harlaw

When the Earl of Ross's heiress Euphemia entered a nunnery, the earldom was manoeuvred toward the family of Robert Stewart, Duke of Albany, regent of Scotland. Donald asserted his wife's right and in 1411 led a great host — later tradition says 10,000 men from the Isles and Ross — eastward through Ross and into Aberdeenshire, defeating a force at Dingwall and advancing toward Aberdeen. On 24 July 1411, at Harlaw near Inverurie, his army met the levies of the Earl of Mar and the northern burghs in a day-long slaughter remembered in ballad as "Red Harlaw." Neither side could claim a clear victory: Donald withdrew to the west, but the earldom of Ross effectively passed to his son Alexander, and Albany's design on Ross was checked.

Later years and succession

Donald spent his final years consolidating the lordship and its church patronage, including ties to Iona. He died in 1423, traditionally while visiting Islay, and was succeeded by his son Alexander of Islay, Earl of Ross, under whom the lordship reached its greatest territorial extent. The Lordship of the Isles endured until its forfeiture to the Scottish Crown in 1493.
