Whole life insurance

Whole life insurance, or whole of life assurance, sometimes called "straight life" or "ordinary life", is a life insurance policy which is guaranteed to remain in force for the insured's entire lifetime, provided required premiums are paid, or to the maturity date. Because whole life policies are guaranteed to pay a death benefit, they typically carry higher premiums than term life insurance.

Characteristics

A whole life policy combines a death benefit with a savings component called the cash value. Premiums are generally level and fixed for the life of the policy. Part of each premium pays for the insurance coverage, part covers expenses, and part accumulates in the cash value, which grows on a tax-deferred basis at a guaranteed rate specified in the contract. The policyholder may borrow against the cash value or surrender the policy for its cash surrender value.

Policies issued by mutual insurance companies may pay dividends to policyholders ("participating" policies), which can be used to reduce premiums, purchase additional paid-up insurance, or be taken in cash. Whole life policies typically mature at a specified age, commonly 100 or 121, at which point the cash value equals the face amount and is paid out.

Comparison with term insurance

Term life insurance provides coverage for a fixed period at lower initial premiums but builds no cash value and expires at the end of the term. Whole life costs substantially more for the same face amount; advocates point to the lifelong guarantee and forced-saving aspect, while critics often argue that buying term insurance and investing the premium difference yields greater value. Whole life is also compared with universal life insurance, which offers flexible premiums and an interest-sensitive cash value.

Variants

  • Limited-pay whole life — premiums are paid over a shorter period (e.g., 20 years), after which the policy is paid up.
  • Single-premium whole life — funded by one lump-sum payment.
  • Modified whole life — lower premiums in the early years, increasing later.
