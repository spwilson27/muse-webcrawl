Witold Lutosławski

Witold Lutosławski
BornJanuary 25, 1913, Warsaw, Poland
DiedFebruary 7, 1994 (aged 81), Warsaw
OccupationComposer, conductor

Witold Roman Lutosławski (January 25, 1913 – February 7, 1994) was a Polish composer and conductor, widely regarded as one of the major European composers of the twentieth century and Poland's most significant composer since Chopin. His mature works combine folk-derived material, rigorous formal design, and controlled aleatory techniques.

Life and career

Lutosławski studied piano and composition in Warsaw, and during the Second World War played piano duos in cafés with Andrzej Panufnik. His First Symphony (1947) was condemned as "formalist" by the communist authorities, but the Concerto for Orchestra (1954) established his international reputation. Jeux vénitiens (1961) introduced his hallmark "limited aleatorism", in which performers repeat material semi-independently within conductor-cued sections, creating shimmering textures.

Later works and legacy

His major late works include the Second (1967), Third (1983), and Fourth (1992) Symphonies, the Cello Concerto written for Mstislav Rostropovich, and Les espaces du sommeil for Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau. The Third Symphony won the first Grawemeyer Award for Music Composition in 1985. The Warsaw Autumn festival, which he co-founded in 1956, remains a leading showcase for new music.
