Statistical inference

Statistical inference is the process of using data analysis to infer properties of an underlying distribution of probability. Inferential statistics generalizes from a sample to a population with a calculated degree of uncertainty, in contrast to descriptive statistics, which merely summarizes the data at hand.

Main tasks

The core tasks of inference include estimation — producing point estimates and interval estimates (confidence intervals) of unknown parameters; hypothesis testing — assessing evidence against a null hypothesis, conventionally summarized with significance tests and p-values; and prediction of future or unobserved values. Inference depends on assumptions about how the sample was generated, often embodied in a statistical model, and its validity rests on the sampling design — random sampling in surveys, randomization in experiments.

Schools of inference

The two dominant frameworks are frequentist inference, developed by Fisher, Neyman, and Pearson, which treats parameters as fixed and procedures as good if they have correct long-run frequency properties; and Bayesian inference, which treats parameters as random variables, combines prior beliefs with the likelihood to form a posterior distribution, and reports credible intervals. Likelihood-based and computational approaches (bootstrap, permutation tests, simulation) cut across these schools. The contrast between design-based and model-based inference, and model misspecification, are continuing themes in statistical theory.

Applications

Statistical inference underlies clinical trials, opinion polling, quality control, econometrics, and experimental science generally. Misuse — selective reporting, p-hacking, confusion of statistical with practical significance — has been implicated in the replication crisis in several scientific fields, prompting reforms such as preregistration and greater emphasis on effect sizes and intervals.
