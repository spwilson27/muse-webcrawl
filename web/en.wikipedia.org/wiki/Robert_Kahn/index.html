Robert Kahn

Robert Elliot Kahn
Born: 23 December 1938, New York City, United States
Fields: Computer science, electrical engineering
Known for: Co-inventing TCP/IP; work on ARPANET
Awards: Turing Award (2004), Presidential Medal of Freedom (2005)

Robert Elliot Kahn (born 23 December 1938) is an American electrical engineer and computer scientist who, together with Vint Cerf, invented the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) and Internet Protocol (IP) — the core communication protocols of the Internet. He is regarded as one of the "fathers of the Internet".

Career

Kahn studied at the City College of New York and Princeton University, where he earned his doctorate in 1964. After teaching at MIT and working at Bolt Beranek and Newman (BBN), where he helped design the ARPANET's Interface Message Processors, he joined DARPA in 1972 and directed its Information Processing Techniques Office. At DARPA he conceived the idea of open-architecture networking, in which independent networks could interconnect seamlessly.

TCP/IP

Working with Vint Cerf in 1973–74, Kahn developed the architecture and protocols published in their 1974 paper "A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication", from which TCP/IP evolved. Kahn formulated key principles of internetworking, including the idea that networks retain autonomy, communication is on a best-effort basis, and gateways (routers) connect networks without retaining packet state.

Later work

In 1986 Kahn founded the Corporation for National Research Initiatives (CNRI), which he continues to lead, working on digital information infrastructure, including the Digital Object Architecture and the Handle System for persistent identifiers. He received the Turing Award in 2004 jointly with Cerf, and the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2005.
