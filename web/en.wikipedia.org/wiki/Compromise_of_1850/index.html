Compromise of 1850

The Compromise of 1850 was a package of five separate bills passed by the United States Congress in September 1850 that temporarily defused tensions between slave and free states in the years between the Mexican–American War and the Civil War.

Compromise of 1850
DateSeptember 1850
Key figuresHenry Clay, Stephen A. Douglas, Daniel Webster, John C. Calhoun
PresidentMillard Fillmore

Background

The Mexican Cession (1848) reopened the question of slavery's expansion. California applied for admission as a free state, upsetting the Senate balance; Texas claimed parts of New Mexico; and the slave trade in Washington, D.C., and rendition of fugitive slaves were contentious.

Terms

  • California admitted as a free state.
  • Utah and New Mexico territories organised with popular sovereignty on slavery.
  • Texas surrendered claims to New Mexico in exchange for federal assumption of its debt.
  • Slave trade (but not slavery) abolished in the District of Columbia.
  • A much stricter Fugitive Slave Act required citizens to assist in returning escaped slaves.

Passage and consequences

Henry Clay introduced the package; after his omnibus failed, Stephen A. Douglas passed the bills separately with shifting coalitions, helped by President Zachary Taylor's death and Millard Fillmore's support. The compromise averted secession for a decade, but the Fugitive Slave Act inflamed Northern opinion, spurring Uncle Tom's Cabin and personal liberty laws, while the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 effectively repealed the earlier Missouri Compromise balance.
