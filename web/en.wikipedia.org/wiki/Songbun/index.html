Songbun

Songbun (Korean: 성분, sŏngbun) is the system of ascribed social classification used in North Korea. Based on the political loyalty of a person and their family background, it ranks citizens into three broad classes — the "core" class, the "wavering" class, and the "hostile" class — subdivided (according to studies by the scholar Robert Collins and defector accounts) into some 51 categories. Songbun profoundly affects access to education, employment, housing, food distribution, residence in Pyongyang, and marriage prospects, though its strictness has eroded somewhat with the growth of markets since the 1990s famine.

The three songbun classes, from loyal "core" to "hostile".

Origins

The system developed from the late 1950s as Kim Il-sung's regime investigated and classified the population according to family history: whether ancestors had been workers, peasants, or officials of the Workers' Party and anti-Japanese guerrillas (favoured), or landlords, capitalists, Christians, pro-Japanese collaborators, or families of those who fled south (disfavoured). Major classification drives occurred in 1957–60, 1966–67, and the "Reinvestigation Project" of 1980–81. Files are maintained by security agencies and reviewed across three generations.

Effects and evolution

Songbun has historically determined rations under the Public Distribution System, admission to Kim Il-sung University and other elite institutions, military assignments, and residence permits. Since the famine of the 1990s and the rise of private markets (jangmadang), wealth and bribery have increasingly allowed people of low songbun to buy opportunities, while some high-songbun families have fallen economically. Defectors and outside researchers describe songbun as a hereditary caste-like system; the North Korean government does not publicly acknowledge its operation in these terms, and a 2014 UN Commission of Inquiry cited it as a mechanism of systematic discrimination.
