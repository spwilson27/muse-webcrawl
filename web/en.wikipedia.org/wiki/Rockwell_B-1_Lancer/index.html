The Rockwell B-1 Lancer is a supersonic variable-sweep wing, heavy strategic bomber used by the United States Air Force. It is commonly called the "Bone" (from "B-One"). Along with the B-2 Spirit and the B-52 Stratofortress, it forms the backbone of the United States long-range bomber fleet. The B-1 was conceived in the 1960s as a supersonic bomber to replace the B-52 and complement the later cancelled XB-70 Valkyrie concept of operations.

Development

The B-1A, developed in the 1970s, could reach Mach 2.2 at high altitude but was cancelled by President Jimmy Carter in 1977 in favour of cruise missiles and the stealth bomber program. President Ronald Reagan revived the program in 1981 as the B-1B, revised for low-level penetration at Mach 0.85 with a greatly reduced radar cross-section, extra fuel and weapons capacity, and a maximum speed of Mach 1.25. One hundred B-1Bs were delivered between 1985 and 1988.

Design

The B-1B has a blended wing-body configuration with variable geometry wings swept between 15° and 67.5°, four afterburning F101 engines in two pods, and three internal weapons bays capable of carrying up to 75,000 lb (34,000 kg) of ordnance — the largest payload of any U.S. bomber. Originally nuclear-armed, the type was converted to a purely conventional role under START and New START treaty provisions in the 1990s.

Operational history

The B-1B first saw combat in Operation Desert Fox (Iraq, 1998) and flew extensively in Kosovo, Afghanistan, and Iraq, where its large payload, loiter time, and precision-guided munitions made it a close-air-support workhorse; in Afghanistan it delivered a large share of the JDAM tonnage. In 2021 the Air Force retired 17 of its 62 aircraft to sustain the remaining fleet of 45. The B-1 is planned to be replaced by the B-21 Raider beginning in the 2030s.
