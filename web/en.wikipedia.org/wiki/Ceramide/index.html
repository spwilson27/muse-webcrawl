Ceramide

[Image: Chemical structure of a ceramide — sphingosine linked to a fatty acid]

Ceramides are a family of waxy lipid molecules. A ceramide consists of sphingosine (or a related sphingoid base) joined to a fatty acid by an amide bond. Ceramides are found in high concentrations within the cell membrane of eukaryotic cells, where they are one of the component lipids that make up sphingomyelin, one of the major lipids in the lipid bilayer. They are also the structural backbone of all sphingolipids.

Biological roles

Beyond their structural role, ceramides act as bioactive signalling molecules. They participate in regulating cellular differentiation, proliferation and programmed cell death (apoptosis), and accumulate in response to stresses such as radiation, heat and chemotherapeutic agents. Ceramide is generated either by hydrolysis of sphingomyelin by sphingomyelinases, or by de novo synthesis starting with the condensation of serine and palmitoyl-CoA by serine palmitoyltransferase, a pathway that proceeds through dihydroceramide.

In skin

Ceramides are the predominant lipids of the stratum corneum, the outermost layer of skin, where together with cholesterol and free fatty acids they form the barrier that limits water loss and excludes irritants. Deficiency or abnormal ceramide composition is associated with conditions such as atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, and synthetic ceramides are widely used in moisturising skincare products.

Health associations

Elevated levels of certain ceramide species in blood and tissues are associated with insulin resistance, cardiovascular disease, and neurodegeneration; plasma ceramide ratios are used in some clinical cardiovascular risk scores.
