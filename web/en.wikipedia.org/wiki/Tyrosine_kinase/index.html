Tyrosine kinase

A tyrosine kinase is an enzyme that catalyzes the transfer of a phosphate group from ATP to the hydroxyl group of a tyrosine residue in a protein. This phosphorylation acts as a molecular switch in signal transduction, regulating processes such as cell growth, division, differentiation, metabolism and apoptosis. Tyrosine kinases are a subset of the larger protein kinase family.

Classification

Tyrosine kinases are divided into two main groups. Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) are cell-surface receptors — including the insulin receptor, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), platelet-derived growth factor receptors (PDGFR) and fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFR) — whose extracellular domains bind ligands and whose intracellular domains possess kinase activity. Ligand binding typically induces receptor dimerization and autophosphorylation, creating docking sites for downstream signalling proteins. Non-receptor (cytoplasmic) tyrosine kinases, such as the Src family, JAK family and Abl, act inside the cell as components of signalling cascades.

Function and signalling

Phosphorylated tyrosines recruit proteins containing SH2 or PTB domains, assembling signalling complexes that activate cascades such as the Ras–MAPK and PI3K–Akt pathways. The human genome encodes about 90 tyrosine kinases, roughly 58 of them receptor-type, underscoring their centrality to multicellular signalling.

Clinical significance

Deregulated tyrosine kinase activity is a major driver of cancer: examples include the BCR-ABL fusion protein of chronic myeloid leukaemia, HER2 amplification in breast cancer, and activating EGFR mutations in lung cancer. This has made tyrosine kinases prime drug targets; small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib, gefitinib and erlotinib, and monoclonal antibodies against RTKs, are mainstays of targeted cancer therapy. Mutations in tyrosine kinases also underlie developmental and immune disorders, including insulin-receptor defects causing severe insulin resistance.
