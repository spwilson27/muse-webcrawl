Earnest payment

Also calledEarnest money, good-faith deposit
FieldContract law; real estate
Typical size1–3% of purchase price (US real estate)

An earnest payment or earnest money deposit is a sum of money paid by a buyer to a seller at the time a contract is made, as evidence of the buyer's good faith and serious intention to complete the transaction. The practice is most familiar in real estate, where the deposit is usually held in escrow by a broker, title company or attorney until closing, at which point it is applied to the purchase price.

Purpose and treatment

The deposit gives the seller assurance that the buyer is committed, compensating the seller for taking the property off the market during the closing period. If the buyer defaults without a contractual justification, the seller may be entitled to keep the earnest money, sometimes as liquidated damages; if the buyer withdraws under a contract contingency — such as a failed inspection or inability to obtain financing — or if the seller breaches, the deposit is normally refunded. The contract typically spells out the conditions for forfeiture and refund, and disputes are a common source of litigation.

Amount

There is no universal required amount. In United States residential real estate, deposits of around 1% to 3% of the price are customary, with larger deposits expected in competitive markets or for new construction. In some civil-law traditions, related institutions include the French arrhes and the German Angeld.

History

The word "earnest" in this sense derives from Old English eornost via the notion of a pledge; historical forms included the handing over of a coin, ring or other token ("earnest penny") to bind a bargain, a custom reflected in Roman law's arrha.
