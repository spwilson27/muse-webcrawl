Lashed-lug boat

A lashed-lug boat is a type of traditional watercraft built in Island Southeast Asia and parts of the western Pacific, distinguished by hull construction in which planks are fastened together using carved lugs and vegetable-fibre lashings rather than metal nails. The technique is one of the oldest documented boatbuilding traditions in the Austronesian world.

Construction

Lashed-lug construction begins with a keel plank, to which strakes are added edge to edge. On the inner face of each plank, a series of protruding lugs is carved, pierced with holes. Corresponding lugs on adjacent planks are aligned and lashed tightly together with rattan or other fibre cordage, while the plank edges themselves are joined with wooden dowels. A waterproof wadding of palm fibre is placed between the seams. The result is a flexible hull that can be partially dismantled and repaired with locally available materials.

Historical significance

Archaeological remains, including the balangay boats of Butuan in the Philippines and the Pontian boat of Peninsular Malaysia, show that lashed-lug construction was in use well over a thousand years ago. The method is associated with the great Austronesian expansion, in which seafarers settled islands from Madagascar to Easter Island and Hawaiʻi.
