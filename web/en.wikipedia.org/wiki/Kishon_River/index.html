The Kishon River (Hebrew: נחל קישון, Naḥal Qishon; Arabic: نهر المقطع, Nahr al-Muqatta) is a river in northern Israel, flowing about 70 kilometres (43 mi) from the hills near Jenin and Mount Gilboa northwestward through the Jezreel Valley to the Mediterranean Sea at Haifa Bay. It is named in the Hebrew Bible as the site of the defeat of Sisera's army and of Elijah's confrontation with the prophets of Baal, and in modern times became notorious as Israel's most polluted river before major cleanup efforts in the twenty-first century.

Course

The Kishon drains a basin of about 1,100 square kilometres, gathering tributaries from the Samarian hills, Mount Gilboa, the Nazareth hills, and Mount Carmel. It crosses the fertile Jezreel Valley past Tel Megiddo and Tel Yokneam, then passes between Mount Carmel and the Tivon hills to its mouth beside the Kishon fishing harbour and Haifa port.

In the Bible

In the Book of Judges, the river is where the Canaanite general Sisera's chariots were swept away and his army destroyed after the prophetess Deborah and Barak's victory (Judges 4–5) — the Song of Deborah calls it "the river Kishon, that ancient river". In 1 Kings 18, after the contest on Mount Carmel, Elijah had the prophets of Baal seized and slaughtered at the brook Kishon.

Pollution and restoration

For much of the twentieth century the lower Kishon received untreated industrial effluent from the petrochemical, fertiliser, and refinery complex of Haifa Bay, along with sewage from surrounding towns. Heavy metals and organic pollutants accumulated in its sediments; fishing was banned, and studies linking polluted water to illness among naval commandos who trained in the river became a national controversy in Israel in 2000, prompting official inquiries. The Kishon River Authority and the Ministry of Environmental Protection undertook a major remediation project (2012–2015) that dredged and treated the most contaminated downstream stretch, diverted it into a new park channel, and constructed the Kishon River Park along its banks. Industrial discharges have been greatly reduced, and fish and bird life have partially returned, though the river's lower reaches remain subject to ongoing monitoring.
