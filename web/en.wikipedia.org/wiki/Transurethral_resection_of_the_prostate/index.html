Transurethral resection of the prostate

Specialty: Urology

Indication: Benign prostatic hyperplasia

Approach: Transurethral, via resectoscope

Transurethral resection of the prostate (commonly known as a TURP) is a urological operation. It is used to treat benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). As the name indicates, it is performed by visualising the prostate through the urethra and removing tissue by electrocautery or sharp dissection. It has long been considered the gold standard surgical treatment for bladder outlet obstruction due to BPH, against which other treatments are compared.

Procedure

The operation is performed under spinal or general anaesthesia. A resectoscope is passed through the urethra to the prostate, and an electrically heated wire loop is used to shave away the enlarged inner portion of the gland in strips, which are then flushed out and sent for histological examination. A catheter is left in place for a day or two while the urine clears of blood.

Risks and complications

Possible complications include bleeding, infection, urethral stricture, retrograde ejaculation (which is common), and erectile dysfunction. A recognised complication of the monopolar technique is TURP syndrome, caused by absorption of the hypotonic irrigation fluid into the circulation, leading to dilutional hyponatraemia; bipolar resection in saline largely avoids this. Long-term outcomes are generally durable, though a minority of men require repeat surgery over the following decade.

Alternatives

Alternatives include laser prostatectomy (such as holmium laser enucleation, HoLEP), transurethral incision of the prostate, prostatic urethral lift, water vapour thermal therapy and simple prostatectomy for very large glands.
