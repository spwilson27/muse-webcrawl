Sashimi

Sashimi (Japanese: 刺身) is a Japanese delicacy consisting of fresh raw fish or meat sliced into thin pieces and eaten with dipping sauce, typically soy sauce. The word sashimi means "pierced body" — possibly because the fish's fin was traditionally pierced and stuck into the slices to identify what was being eaten.

Preparation and serving

Sashimi is usually served with shredded daikon radish, shiso leaves, wasabi, and pickled ginger, and is eaten near the start of a meal before other flavours affect the palate. Common varieties include salmon (sake), tuna (maguro), yellowtail (hamachi), sea bream (tai), mackerel (saba), and squid (ika). Chefs train for years in the knife techniques — such as hira-zukuri (rectangular slices) and usu-zukuri (paper-thin slices) — required to cut different fish correctly, and in recognising freshness and quality.

Sashimi and sushi

Sashimi is often confused with sushi, but the two are distinct: sushi always includes vinegared rice, whereas sashimi is served without rice. A related preparation is tataki, in which fish or meat is briefly seared on the outside and left raw within.

Safety

Raw fish carries risks of parasites such as anisakis and of bacterial contamination. Regulations in many countries require fish intended for raw consumption to be frozen first to kill parasites; in Japan, freshness and skilled preparation are the traditional safeguards. Freshwater fish are generally avoided for sashimi because of parasite risk.
