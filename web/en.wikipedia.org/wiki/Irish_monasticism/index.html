Irish monasticism

Irish monasticism refers to the distinctive form of Christian monastic life that developed in early medieval Ireland from the 5th and 6th centuries onwards. Irish monasteries became renowned centres of learning, manuscript production and missionary activity, and Irish monks played a major role in preserving classical and Christian learning in Western Europe during the early Middle Ages, as well as re-evangelising parts of Britain and the Continent after the fall of the Roman Empire.

Origins

Monasticism reached Ireland in the wake of its Christianisation, traditionally associated with Saint Patrick in the 5th century. Early Irish monasteries were often founded by charismatic saints such as Enda of Aran, Finnian of Clonard, Brendan the Navigator, Brigid of Kildare, Columba (Colum Cille) and Columbanus. Great monasteries such as Clonard, Bangor, Clonmacnoise, Glendalough and Iona grew into monastic cities containing churches, round towers, scriptoria, schools and workshops.

Character

Irish monasticism was noted for its emphasis on scholarship, producing illuminated manuscripts such as the Book of Kells, and for a distinctive penitential discipline set out in penitential handbooks. Irish monks developed their own method of computing Easter and their own tonsure, which brought them into conflict with Roman practice, resolved at the Synod of Whitby (664) in Northumbria. A distinctive feature was peregrinatio — voluntary exile for Christ — which sent missionaries such as Columbanus to found monasteries at Luxeuil and Bobbio, and Gall to Switzerland.

Decline

From the late 8th century Viking raids repeatedly plundered the monasteries. In the 12th century, church reforms under figures such as Malachy of Armagh brought continental orders — Cistercians, Augustinians and others — to Ireland, and the Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169 further integrated the Irish church into the wider Latin Church, ending the distinctively Irish monastic system.
