Hilda of Whitby (c. 614–680) was a Christian saint and the founding abbess of the monastery at Whitby (Streoneshalh), a double monastery of monks and nuns over which she presided. A great-niece of King Edwin of Northumbria, she was baptised in 627 and, after time at the abbey of Hartlepool under Abbess Hieu, founded Whitby Abbey in 657.

Life and influence

According to Bede, Hilda's wisdom was so esteemed that kings and princes sought her advice, and her monastery became a major centre of learning: five of its monks became bishops, and it was there that the herdsman Cædmon, the first named English poet, was encouraged to compose his hymns. In 664 her abbey hosted the Synod of Whitby, at which King Oswiu ruled in favour of the Roman method of calculating Easter and the Roman tonsure over Celtic practice — a landmark in the history of the English Church. Hilda herself favoured the Celtic tradition but accepted the decision.

She died in 680 after a long illness. Ammonite fossils found near Whitby were long associated with her as petrified snakes said to have been beheaded by her prayers, and the ammonite genus Hildoceras is named after her.
