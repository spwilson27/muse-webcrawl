Propellant

A propellant (or propellent) is a mass that is expelled or expanded in such a way as to create a thrust or other motive force in accordance with Newton's third law of motion, and "propel" a vehicle, projectile, or payload. In vehicles, the engine that expels the propellant is called a reaction engine.

Rocket propellants

Chemical rocket propellants are energetic materials that undergo exothermic chemical reactions producing hot gas. They are classified by physical state:

  • Liquid propellants — stored in tanks and pumped into a combustion chamber. Examples include liquid oxygen (LOX) with kerosene (RP-1) or liquid hydrogen, and hypergolic combinations such as nitrogen tetroxide with hydrazine derivatives.
  • Solid propellants — fuel and oxidizer are mixed and cast into a solid grain inside the motor casing, as in ammonium perchlorate composite propellant (APCP) used in space shuttle boosters.
  • Hybrid propellants — typically a solid fuel with a liquid or gaseous oxidizer.

The performance of a rocket propellant is measured principally by its specific impulse, the thrust produced per unit weight of propellant consumed per second.

Other uses

In firearms, propellants such as gunpowder and smokeless powder (nitrocellulose-based) burn rapidly to produce gas that drives a projectile from the barrel. Aerosol cans use compressed or liquefied gases — historically chlorofluorocarbons, now commonly hydrocarbons or hydrofluoroalkanes — as propellants to expel the product. Air guns, nail guns, and some torpedoes also use stored-gas propellants.
