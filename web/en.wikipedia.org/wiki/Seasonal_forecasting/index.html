Seasonal forecasting

Seasonal forecasting is the prediction of weather and climate conditions averaged over a coming season — typically one to six months ahead — expressed in probabilistic terms, such as the likelihood that temperature or rainfall over the next three months will be above, near or below normal. Unlike day-to-day weather forecasting, which becomes unreliable beyond about two weeks because of the atmosphere's chaotic sensitivity to initial conditions, seasonal prediction exploits slowly varying components of the climate system that impart some predictability at longer range.

Sources of predictability

The chief source of seasonal predictability is the tropical oceans, above all the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO): warm (El Niño) or cold (La Niña) phases of the equatorial Pacific shift rainfall and temperature patterns worldwide through atmospheric teleconnections, in ways that persist for seasons. Other boundary forcings include sea-surface temperature anomalies in the Indian and Atlantic Oceans, soil moisture, snow cover, sea ice extent, stratospheric conditions such as the quasi-biennial oscillation, and large volcanic eruptions.

Methods

Modern seasonal forecasts are produced from ensembles of coupled ocean–atmosphere global climate models, run many times from slightly different initial conditions by centres such as the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (whose SEAS5 system contributes to the Copernicus Climate Change Service multi-model forecasts), the UK Met Office (GloSea), NOAA's Climate Prediction Center in the United States, and the Bureau of Meteorology in Australia. Statistical methods based on historical relationships and analogue years supplement dynamical models. Because individual outcomes remain uncertain, products are presented as probabilities across categories (terciles) and are verified statistically over many seasons.

Skill and applications

Seasonal forecast skill is highest in the tropics and for ENSO-related rainfall in regions such as Indonesia, northern Australia, parts of South America and southern Africa; it is modest in the mid-latitude storm-track regions, though useful skill exists for North Atlantic Oscillation–influenced European winters. Users include agriculture (planting and irrigation decisions), water-resource management, energy utilities planning heating and hydroelectric supply, disaster preparedness for droughts and floods, public health (malaria and dengue outlooks), and commodity and insurance markets. Effective use requires presenting uncertainty honestly and tailoring outlooks to decisions, an approach promoted under the name "climate services".
