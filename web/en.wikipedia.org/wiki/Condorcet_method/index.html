Condorcet method

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Condorcet method is an election method that always elects the Condorcet winner — the candidate who would defeat each other candidate in a head-to-head contest — if one exists. Named for the 18th-century mathematician and philosopher Marie Jean Antoine Nicolas de Caritat, Marquis de Condorcet, who also identified the Condorcet paradox: majority preferences can cycle (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A), so a Condorcet winner may not exist, in which case specific rules (Copeland, Schulze, ranked pairs, minimax, Kemeny–Young) resolve the contest within the "Smith set" of mutually undominated candidates.

Significance

Condorcet methods are the gold standard of much social-choice theory — they satisfy majority and mutual-majority criteria that plurality and IRV fail, handle the spoiler effect cleanly, and anchor criteria debates against Arrow's theorem and the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result on strategy. Adoption in public elections is rare (a handful of small jurisdictions and organisations, including historically the town of Marquette, Michigan's early-20th-century experiments and some academic and open-source communities) — the head-to-head machinery being less transparent to voters than counting marks — but Condorcet reasoning shapes how all systems are evaluated, and pairwise matrices are the diagnostic tool of election scientists.
