Henri Chrétien


Henri Jacques Chrétien
Born: 1 February 1879, Paris
Died: 6 February 1956, Washington, D.C.
Known for: Anamorphic lens, Hypergonar, CinemaScope

Henri Jacques Chrétien (February 1, 1879 – February 6, 1956) was a French astronomer and optical engineer, and an inventor in the fields of astronomy and cinematography. He was a co-inventor of the Ritchey–Chrétien telescope design used by most modern professional telescopes (including the Hubble Space Telescope), and of the anamorphic lens system that became CinemaScope, for which he received an Academy Award.

Astronomy

Trained as an astronomer, Chrétien worked at the Nice Observatory and later became professor of astrophysics at the Sorbonne. With George Willis Ritchey he developed, around 1910–1927, the aplanatic two-mirror telescope design — the Ritchey–Chrétien configuration — that eliminates coma and is used by nearly all large research telescopes built since. He also founded the Institut d'Optique in Paris and promoted the professional training of optical engineers in France.

Anamorphic lens and CinemaScope

In the 1920s Chrétien developed the Hypergonar, an anamorphic lens that squeezed a wide image onto standard film, intended originally for military tank periscopes and later adapted to cinema. In 1952 he licensed the process to 20th Century Fox, which launched it as CinemaScope; the process premiered with The Robe (1953) and transformed widescreen filmmaking. Chrétien received an Academy Award (Scientific or Technical, Class II) in 1954 for his work.

Legacy

The lunar crater Chrétien is named after him, and the American Astronomical Society's Chrétien International Research Grants commemorate his name.
