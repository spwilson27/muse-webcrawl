Vanishing twin

A vanishing twin, also known as twin resorption, is a fetus in a multigestation pregnancy that dies in the uterus and is then partially or completely reabsorbed by the mother or by the other fetus or fetuses. The phenomenon typically occurs in the first trimester of pregnancy and is detected far more often now that early ultrasound examination is routine.

Early ultrasound can reveal a second gestational sac that later disappears.

Occurrence

Studies using early ultrasound suggest that vanishing twin syndrome occurs in a substantial proportion of twin pregnancies — estimates range from roughly 10 to 40 percent of pregnancies that begin as twins, depending on when the first scan is performed. Because conception of twins is itself more common than twin births, the vanishing twin is thought to explain part of the difference between the rate of twin conceptions and the rate of twin deliveries.

Causes and mechanisms

The loss of one twin is usually attributed to chromosomal abnormalities in the non-surviving embryo or to problems with implantation and placental development. The dead fetal tissue may be reabsorbed by the mother's body, compressed into the membranes of the surviving twin's sac (sometimes visible at delivery as a fetus papyraceus), or absorbed by the surviving twin's developing body.

Clinical significance

When the loss occurs early in the first trimester, the prognosis for the surviving twin is generally excellent, and the pregnancy usually proceeds as a normal singleton pregnancy. Losses later in pregnancy, particularly in monochorionic twins who share a placenta, carry greater risks, including neurological injury to the survivor. The phenomenon can also affect prenatal screening tests: DNA from the vanished twin may persist in the mother's blood for a time and produce misleading results on non-invasive prenatal testing. In rare cases of absorption, the surviving individual may be a chimera, carrying two distinct sets of DNA.

Detection

The vanishing twin is most often identified when an early ultrasound shows two gestational sacs or two heartbeats, and a later scan shows only one fetus. Mild vaginal bleeding or cramping may accompany the loss, but frequently there are no symptoms at all.
