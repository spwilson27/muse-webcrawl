Benjamin Disraeli, 1st Earl of Beaconsfield (1804–1881) was a British statesman, Conservative politician and writer who twice served as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom. He played a central role in the creation of the modern Conservative Party.

Early life and career

Born in London to a Sephardic Jewish family, baptised into the Church of England at twelve, Disraeli first won fame as a novelist — Vivian Grey, Coningsby and Sybil. Elected to Parliament in 1837, he rose in opposition to Robert Peel over the Corn Laws.

Premierships

As Chancellor of the Exchequer and then briefly Prime Minister in 1868, he passed the Reform Act 1867, greatly expanding the electorate. In his second ministry (1874–1880) he enacted social reforms, bought shares in the Suez Canal, made Queen Victoria Empress of India, and represented Britain at the Congress of Berlin in 1878.

Legacy

Disraeli's rivalry with William Ewart Gladstone defined Victorian politics. His "One Nation" conservatism and imperial vision shaped British politics for generations.
