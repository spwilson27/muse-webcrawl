Nurse practitioner

A nurse practitioner (NP) is an advanced practice registered nurse who is educated and trained to assess patient needs, order and interpret diagnostic and laboratory tests, diagnose disease, and formulate and prescribe medications and treatment plans. Nurse practitioners work in a wide range of settings, including hospitals, clinics, private practices, schools, and community health centres.

Education and training

Nurse practitioners typically complete a Bachelor of Science in Nursing, gain clinical experience as registered nurses, and then earn a graduate degree — either a Master of Science in Nursing or a Doctor of Nursing Practice (DNP). Training includes advanced coursework in pharmacology, pathophysiology, and health assessment, plus hundreds of hours of supervised clinical practice. Graduates must pass a national certification examination in a population focus such as family, paediatrics, adult-gerontology, or psychiatric-mental health.

Scope of practice

The scope of NP practice varies by jurisdiction. In some regions nurse practitioners practise independently with full authority to diagnose, prescribe, and manage treatment; in others they work under collaborative agreements with physicians. Studies comparing NP and physician care in primary care settings have generally found comparable quality and patient satisfaction outcomes for many common conditions.

History

The nurse practitioner role was established in the United States in 1965, when Loretta Ford and Henry Silver created the first NP programme at the University of Colorado to address a shortage of primary care providers, especially for children and underserved communities. The role subsequently spread internationally, with programmes in Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom, and other countries.
