Cain and Abel

[Image placeholder: Cain slaying Abel, a detail from a medieval illuminated manuscript]

In the biblical Book of Genesis, Cain and Abel are the first two sons of Adam and Eve. Cain, the firstborn, was a farmer, and his brother Abel was a shepherd. The brothers made sacrifices to God, but God favoured Abel's sacrifice instead of Cain's. Cain then murdered Abel, whereupon God punished Cain by condemning him to a life of wandering. Cain then dwelt in the land of Nod, where he built the first city and fathered the line of descendants ending with Lamech.

The narrative (Genesis 4)

Abel, a keeper of sheep, offered God the firstborn of his flock and their fat portions; Cain, a tiller of the ground, offered fruit of the soil. God "had regard" for Abel's offering but not for Cain's, and warned the angry Cain that sin was crouching at his door. In the field, Cain rose up against his brother and killed him — the first murder in the Bible. Asked "Where is Abel your brother?", Cain replied with the famous line "Am I my brother's keeper?" God cursed Cain from the ground, yet set a mark on him to protect him from vengeance, decreeing sevenfold punishment on anyone who killed him.

Cain settled east of Eden in Nod ("wandering"). His wife bore Enoch, after whom Cain named a city. His descendants are credited with the origins of tent-dwelling herding, music (Jubal) and metalworking (Tubal-cain). Eve later bore Seth, described as compensation for Abel.

Interpretations and legacy

Jewish, Christian and Islamic traditions all retell the story (in the Qur'an, the sons of Adam are unnamed but the account is closely parallel). Commentators have debated why Abel's offering was accepted — the quality of the sacrifice, the faith of the offerer (as Hebrews 11 suggests), or divine freedom. Cain has been read variously as a symbol of jealousy, of settled farmers in tension with nomadic herders, and, in later folklore, as the ancestor of monsters such as Grendel in Beowulf. The story gave English the phrase "raise Cain" and indelible lines in literature from Byron's Cain to Steinbeck's East of Eden.
