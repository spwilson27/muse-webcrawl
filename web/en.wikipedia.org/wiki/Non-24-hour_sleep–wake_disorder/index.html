Non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder

Non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder (also known as non-24, free-running sleep disorder, or, in its older name, hypernychthemeral syndrome) is a circadian rhythm sleep disorder in which a person's biological clock runs on a cycle longer than 24 hours — typically 24.2 to 25 hours — and fails to entrain to the light–dark cycle. Sleep onset and waking drift progressively later each day, cycling around the clock over a period of weeks.

Population affected

The disorder is most common in totally blind people who lack light perception and therefore cannot synchronise their suprachiasmatic nucleus to the day; a substantial fraction of the totally blind population is affected. It also occurs, more rarely, in sighted people, in whom the causes are less clear and may involve an unusually long intrinsic circadian period or reduced sensitivity to light cues.

Symptoms

When the internal clock is misaligned with social schedules, patients experience insomnia at night and excessive daytime sleepiness; when it happens to align, sleep is normal. The cycling pattern causes recurring periods of severe disruption to work, school and social life, and is frequently misdiagnosed as depression or a primary sleep disorder. Diagnosis uses sleep diaries and actigraphy over several weeks to reveal the characteristic daily drift.

Treatment

Treatment aims to entrain the clock to 24 hours. Timed melatonin, given at a consistent clock time, is a mainstay, including for blind patients for whom light therapy is ineffective. Tasimelteon, a melatonin receptor agonist, was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2014 specifically for non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder — the first drug approved for the condition. Structured schedules and, for sighted patients, bright-light exposure on waking may help.
