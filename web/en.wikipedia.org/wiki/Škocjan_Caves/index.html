Škocjan Caves (Slovene: Škocjanske jame; Italian: Grotte di San Canziano) is a cave system in southwestern Slovenia, in the Karst Plateau region that gave the science of karst studies its name. The caves are considered one of the most significant underground phenomena in the world and were inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage list in 1986.

Description

The caves are formed by the Reka River, which sinks underground near the village of Škocjan and flows through a vast underground canyon before re-emerging some 34 km away near the Adriatic coast as the Timavo springs. The underground canyon — up to about 146 metres high and 120 metres wide — is among the largest known anywhere. Visitors cross the river on the Cerkvenik Bridge, some 45 metres above the water, between the collapsed dolines of Mala and Velika, two enormous sinkholes that open the canyon to the sky.

Features and archaeology

The system includes the Silent Cave (Tiha jama), notable for its stalactites and stalagmites, and Tominc Cave, where human remains and artefacts document occupation from the Mesolithic through the Iron Age. The caves support distinctive underground fauna and rare plant species in the dolines' unusual microclimate, and were designated a Ramsar wetland of international importance in 1999.

Exploration and tourism

Systematic exploration began in the 19th century, when local cavers and researchers mapped much of the system, and tourism dates back to the early 1800s. Today the caves are managed by the Škocjan Caves Regional Park and receive around 100,000 visitors per year on guided tours.
