Ricci decomposition

In the mathematical fields of Riemannian and pseudo-Riemannian geometry, the Ricci decomposition is a way of breaking up the Riemann curvature tensor of a Riemannian or pseudo-Riemannian manifold into pieces with simpler transformation properties. It is the splitting of the Riemann tensor into three parts: the Weyl tensor, the Ricci part, and the scalar part. This decomposition is the geometric analogue of decomposing a matrix into its trace, symmetric trace-free, and antisymmetric parts, and it underlies much of the modern understanding of curvature in general relativity and conformal geometry.

Statement of the decomposition

On an n-dimensional manifold, the Riemann tensor Rabcd can be written as a sum of the Weyl tensor Cabcd, terms built from the Ricci tensor Rab, and a term built from the Ricci scalar R. Schematically:

Rabcd = Cabcd + (Ricci terms) + (scalar terms)

The fully explicit formula in index notation is:

Rikℓm = Cikℓm + 2/(n−2) (gi[ℓ Rm]k − gk[ℓ Rm]i) + 2R/(n(n−1)) gi[ℓ gm]k

where square brackets denote antisymmetrisation over the enclosed indices. An alternative formulation uses the Schouten tensor, a rescaled combination of the Ricci tensor and scalar, which makes the decomposition take a compact Kulkarni–Nomizu product form.

The three parts

Scalar part

The scalar part is determined entirely by the Ricci scalar R, the total (average) curvature at a point. It describes the part of the curvature akin to that of a space of constant curvature.

Ricci part

The Ricci part is determined by the trace-free portion of the Ricci tensor, sometimes called the traceless Ricci tensor Sab. In general relativity, through the Einstein field equations, this part is linked to the distribution of matter and energy at the point in question: it is the curvature produced directly by local matter.

Weyl part

The Weyl tensor Cabcd is the completely trace-free part of the Riemann tensor. It is unchanged under conformal rescalings of the metric (hence "conformal curvature tensor") and describes the tidal, shape-distorting part of the curvature—the part that persists in vacuum, where the Ricci tensor vanishes. In four dimensions the Weyl tensor vanishes identically in three or fewer dimensions, and in a four-dimensional vacuum spacetime the whole curvature is carried by the Weyl tensor, which describes gravitational waves and the tidal field of distant masses.

Physical and geometric significance

The decomposition separates curvature into parts with distinct sources and behaviour. The scalar and Ricci parts respond to matter present at the point; the Weyl part represents the free gravitational field, capable of propagating through empty space. The decomposition is also fundamental in the study of special holonomy, the Petrov classification of spacetimes (which classifies the algebraic structure of the Weyl tensor), and the analysis of the Einstein equations as an initial-value problem.
