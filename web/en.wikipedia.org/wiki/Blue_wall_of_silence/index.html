Blue wall of silence

The blue wall of silence, also known as the blue code of silence, the blue shield and the thin blue line code, is the informal code among police officers not to report on a colleague's misconduct, errors or crimes, and not to cooperate fully when questioned about another officer's conduct. When it operates, officers who witness wrongdoing remain silent or perjure themselves rather than testify against fellow officers.

History and investigations

In the United States, the phenomenon was documented by successive commissions: the Knapp Commission (1970–72) investigating corruption in the New York City Police Department — famous for Frank Serpico's testimony — and the Mollen Commission (1992–94) both found a pervasive code of silence protecting corrupt officers. Comparable findings emerged in other countries, including inquiries in the United Kingdom and Australia (the Wood Royal Commission in New South Wales). Researchers attribute the code to the solidarity and mutual dependence of police work, distrust of external scrutiny, and fear of ostracism or retaliation against "whistleblowers".

Countermeasures and debate

Reforms intended to break the wall include independent civilian review bodies, body-worn cameras, duty-to-intervene and duty-to-report rules, and protections for whistleblowing officers. Police unions and officers' advocates respond that loyalty within a dangerous profession is often misread as a conspiracy of silence, and that most officers support removing those who commit crimes. The concept features prominently in debates over police reform, particularly following high-profile killings in which bystander officers failed to intervene.
