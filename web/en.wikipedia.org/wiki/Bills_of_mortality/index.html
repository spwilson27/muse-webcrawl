Bills of mortality

A weekly bill of mortality for London during the Great Plague of 1665.

The bills of mortality were weekly mortality statistics published in London and other English cities from the sixteenth to the nineteenth centuries. They recorded burials and baptisms by parish and attributed causes of death, and they are among the earliest regular systems of vital statistics in Europe.

History

London's bills began sporadically in the 1590s during plague outbreaks and became a regular weekly publication in the early 1660s, produced by the Company of Parish Clerks. Causes of death were reported by "searchers" — often elderly women employed by parishes — who viewed corpses and questioned households, classifying deaths under headings ranging from plague, consumption and fever to such archaic categories as "teeth", "rising of the lights" and "frighted".

Graunt's analysis

In 1662 the merchant John Graunt published Natural and Political Observations … upon the Bills of Mortality, a pioneering work of demography and statistics. Analysing decades of London bills, Graunt estimated the city's population, noted the regular excess of male over female births, and constructed an early form of the life table.

Legacy

The bills were most famously consulted during the Great Plague of London (1665–66), when Londoners watched the weekly plague death counts rise and fall. They were superseded in the nineteenth century by the Registrar General's returns following the Births and Deaths Registration Act 1836, but remain a key primary source for historical demography and the history of public health.
