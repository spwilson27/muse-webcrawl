Double feature

The double feature, also known as a double bill, is a phenomenon in the film industry in which a movie theatre exhibits two films for the price of one. The format arose from earlier variety-style programming in which a feature was accompanied by shorts, cartoons, and newsreels. To use the term today, the exhibiting theatre must advertise both films prominently; in practice the second film of the pair is usually a low-budget B movie. The term "double feature" is also used in television and streaming for back-to-back related programming, and metaphorically in other sports and entertainment contexts.

History

Double features became standard in American cinemas during the Great Depression, when studios and exhibitors competed on value amid the block booking system: theatres signing for a studio's A pictures were required to take its B movies as well. One film, the A feature, received top billing and percentage-of-gross rental, while the B movie played second for a flat fee. The practice underpinned the careers of Poverty Row studios and series films such as the Charlie Chan and Mr. Moto pictures.

Decline

The 1948 Supreme Court decision in United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc. dismantled block booking and studio theatre ownership, and television drew audiences away; by the 1960s most theatres had shifted to single features. Drive-in theatres preserved double (and triple) features longest, often with exploitation films. Occasional revivals occur — the 2007 Grindhouse release paired two films with fake trailers in homage, and the 2023 "Barbenheimer" phenomenon saw audiences self-programming double features of Barbie and Oppenheimer.
