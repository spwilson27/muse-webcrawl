Dactylography

Dactylography (also spelled dactylographie) is the study of fingerprints as a method of identification. The term derives from the Greek daktylos (finger) and graphē (writing). In its strict sense it denotes the engraving or carving of designs on finger rings — dactyliography — but in modern usage, particularly in French and Spanish forensic literature (dactiloscopia/dactyloscopie), it is used for the scientific examination of friction-ridge patterns.

Fingerprint identification

Fingerprints form in the womb and, except for changes in size, remain unchanged throughout life; no two people, including identical twins, share the same prints. Classification systems developed by Francis Galton and Edward Henry divide patterns into arches, loops and whorls, enabling large collections to be filed and searched. The Henry system, adopted in British India in the 1890s and then by Scotland Yard, underpinned the first fingerprint bureaus, and fingerprint evidence was first used to secure a murder conviction in Argentina in 1892 under Juan Vucetich's system.

Modern practice

Latent prints recovered at crime scenes are compared with inked or electronically scanned ten-print records; automated fingerprint identification systems (AFIS) search databases of millions of records. Despite its long history, the discipline has been subject to scientific scrutiny over the objectivity of visual comparison, and professional standards now emphasise documentation and blind verification.
