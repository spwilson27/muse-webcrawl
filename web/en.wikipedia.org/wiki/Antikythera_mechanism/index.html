The Antikythera mechanism is an ancient Greek hand-powered orrery, described as the oldest example of an analogue computer, used to predict astronomical positions and eclipses decades in advance. It was recovered in 1901 from the Antikythera shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera. The device, housed in a wooden case about the size of a shoebox, contained more than 30 meshing bronze gears with teeth as fine as those of much later clockwork.

Function

The mechanism modelled the irregular orbit of the Moon using an ingenious pin-and-slot gear pair, displayed the Metonic and Saros cycles, predicted solar and lunar eclipses, and kept track of the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic games. Inscriptions on its plates served as an instruction manual. Nothing of comparable mechanical complexity is known again until the astronomical clocks of medieval Europe, some fourteen centuries later.

Study

Derek de Solla Price's research in the 1970s established its gearing, and twenty-first-century imaging — including X-ray computed tomography by the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project — has allowed working reconstructions and a fuller reading of its inscriptions. Its date is generally placed in the late second century BC, and researchers have proposed links to the astronomy of Rhodes and to Archimedes' planetarium tradition.
