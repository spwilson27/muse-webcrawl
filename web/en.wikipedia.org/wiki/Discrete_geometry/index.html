Discrete geometry

Discrete geometry and combinatorial geometry are branches of geometry that study combinatorial properties and constructive methods of discrete geometric objects. Most questions in discrete geometry involve finite or discrete sets of basic geometric objects, such as points, lines, planes, circles, spheres, polygons, and so forth. The subject focuses on the combinatorial properties of such objects, such as how they intersect one another, or how they may be arranged to cover a larger object.

Topics

Major themes include polytopes and abstract polytopes; packing, covering, and tiling problems, including the Kepler conjecture on sphere packing (proved by Thomas Hales) and circle packing; rigidity and flexibility of frameworks; incidence geometry and the Szemerédi–Trotter theorem; arrangements of hyperplanes; lattice point geometry and Pick's theorem; triangulations and the Delaunay triangulation; and discrete differential geometry. The field has close ties to convex geometry, computational geometry, graph theory, and the theory of error-correcting codes and sphere packings in coding theory.

History

Although polyhedra and tessellations were studied for millennia, discrete geometry coalesced as a field in the twentieth century through the work of mathematicians such as László Fejes Tóth, H. S. M. Coxeter, and Paul Erdős, who posed many of its characteristic combinatorial problems. The journal Discrete & Computational Geometry, founded in 1986, reflects the field's modern identity and its synergy with computer science, where questions about arrangements, convex hulls, and geometric data structures have algorithmic importance.

Applications

Results in discrete geometry feed into crystallography, materials science, robotics and motion planning, computer graphics, geographic information systems, and the design of codes and networks.
