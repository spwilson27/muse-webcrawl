Color field painting

Color field painting is a style of abstract painting that emerged in New York City during the 1940s and 1950s. It was inspired by European modernism and closely related to abstract expressionism, though many of its leading practitioners distanced themselves from the gestural, emotional qualities of action painting. Color field painting is characterised by large fields of flat, solid colour spread across or stained into the canvas, creating areas of unbroken surface and a unified composition.

Color field works envelop the viewer in large, unified areas of colour.

Origins and characteristics

The term was coined by the critic Clement Greenberg, who championed the style. Artists such as Mark Rothko, Barnett Newman, and Clyfford Still sought to create paintings that were purely visual and emotional in effect, free of representation, symbolism, or visible brushwork. Rothko's soft-edged rectangles, Newman's "zips" (vertical bands dividing fields of colour), and Still's jagged expanses are hallmark forms.

Techniques

Helen Frankenthaler developed the "soak-stain" technique, pouring thinned paint onto unprimed canvas so that the colour fused with the support. Morris Louis and Kenneth Noland adopted and extended this method, pouring or rolling rivulets and targets of colour. The staining technique eliminated the distinction between figure and ground and emphasised the flatness of the picture plane, a quality Greenberg prized as central to modernist painting.

Legacy

Color field painting influenced later movements including post-painterly abstraction, lyrical abstraction, and minimalism. Its emphasis on optical sensation, scale, and the physical presence of the canvas helped define postwar American art, and its works remain central to museum collections of modern art.
