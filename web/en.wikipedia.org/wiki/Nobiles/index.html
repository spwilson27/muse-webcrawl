Nobiles

The nobiles (Latin: nobilis, "well-known, notable", plural nobiles) were the aristocratic governing families of the Roman Republic. The nobilitas emerged after the Conflict of the Orders as an oligarchy of patrician and leading plebeian families whose ancestors had held the consulship or other curule magistracies. A man whose family had no consular ancestor but who reached the consulship himself was known as a novus homo ("new man").

Formation

After plebeians gained access to the consulship (formalised by the Licinian-Sextian laws of 367 BC and subsequent measures), political power coalesced around a relatively small circle of families — the gentes maiores such as the Cornelii, Aemilii, Fabii, Claudii and Valerii, together with prominent plebeian houses like the Licinii and Caecilii Metelli. Elections, wealth, military glory, networks of clients, and marriage alliances maintained their dominance of the Senate and the higher magistracies through the middle and late Republic.

Nobilitas and the new men

The prestige of the nobiles rested on the imagines — wax masks of ancestors who had held curule office, displayed in the atria of their houses and carried in funeral processions. Outsiders rarely broke in: famous novi homines included Cato the Elder, Gaius Marius and Cicero, who remarked on the obstacles facing a new man. In modern scholarship the term's exact scope is debated — some historians restrict nobiles to descendants of consuls, others use it more broadly for the senatorial aristocracy.

Under the Empire

With the advent of the Principate, the old nobility retained social prestige but lost political independence; the emperor's favour, rather than ancestry and election, increasingly determined careers, and many Republican noble houses died out during the first century AD.
