Mosel is one of Germany's 13 official wine regions (Anbaugebiete), following the course of the Moselle river from the Luxembourg border to its confluence with the Rhine at Koblenz, together with its tributaries the Saar and Ruwer. Until 2007 the region was called Mosel-Saar-Ruwer. It is famed above all for light, intensely aromatic Riesling wines grown on some of the steepest vineyards in the world.

Geography and viticulture

The region's vineyards cling to slopes of blue and red Devonian slate at gradients of up to 70 degrees; the Bremmer Calmont, at about 68 degrees, is often cited as the steepest vineyard in Europe. Slate soils retain the sun's heat and drain freely, while the river reflects light and moderates the cool northern climate, allowing late ripening. Harvesting is almost entirely by hand, and yields of classified vineyards (VDP.Grosse Lage) are strictly limited.

Wines

Mosel Rieslings are typically low in alcohol, high in acidity, and range from bone-dry (trocken) through off-dry Kabinett and Spätlese to the lusciously sweet Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese, and ice wines harvested from frozen grapes. Famous single vineyards include the Wehlener Sonnenuhr, Bernkasteler Doctor, Piesporter Goldtröpfchen, and Erdener Prälat. The region also produces Elbling (historically dominant), Müller-Thurgau, and increasing amounts of Pinot Noir (Spätburgunder).

History

Viticulture here dates to Roman times; the poet Ausonius praised the Moselle valley in the 4th century AD. Monasteries, especially Cistercian houses, developed the great vineyard sites through the Middle Ages, and the region's reputation was cemented in the 19th century when its Rieslings ranked among the most expensive wines in the world.
