Human skin color

Human skin color ranges from the darkest brown to the lightest hues. Differences in skin color among individuals are caused by variation in pigmentation, which is the result of genetics (inherited from one's biological parents), exposure to the sun, disorders, or a combination of these. The pigment melanin, produced by melanocytes in the skin, is the primary determinant of color.

Evolution

Skin color is an example of adaptive evolution: populations native to regions of intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation near the equator evolved darker skin, which protects against UV damage and folate depletion, while populations at higher latitudes evolved lighter skin, which facilitates vitamin D synthesis in low-UV conditions. This produces the well-documented gradient of skin color with latitude.

Genetics

Skin pigmentation is a polygenic trait influenced by many genes affecting the amount, type and distribution of melanin. Genes such as MC1R (associated with red hair and fair skin) and SLC24A5 (a major contributor to lighter skin in Europeans) have been identified through genome-wide studies.

Variation and tanning

Within any population there is substantial variation, and an individual's skin can darken through tanning in response to UV exposure, or lighten seasonally. Disorders of pigmentation include albinism, vitiligo and melasma.

Social significance

Skin color has been used historically as a basis for social classification and discrimination, though genetic variation in skin color does not correspond to discrete biological "races" — variation is continuous and clinal.
