The Year 2000 problem, also known as the Y2K problem or millennium bug, was a class of computer bugs arising from the practice of representing years with only two digits. Programs that assumed the twentieth century would interpret the year "00" as 1900 rather than 2000, potentially causing failures in date-sensitive systems — banking, utilities, government records, and embedded controllers — when the calendar rolled over at midnight on 1 January 2000.

Background

In the 1960s and 1970s, computer memory and storage were expensive and scarce, and programmers routinely saved space by storing years as two digits, as on punched cards. The convention persisted in vast amounts of legacy software, particularly COBOL programs in finance and government. As 2000 approached, experts warned that date arithmetic, sorting, and comparisons would break: a program subtracting 99 from 00, for instance, might compute a negative age or declare a newborn a centenarian.

Remediation

Governments and corporations mounted an enormous remediation effort in the late 1990s, auditing code, expanding date fields, and using "windowing" techniques that interpreted two-digit years within a sliding pivot range. Programmers such as Bob Bemer had warned of the issue decades earlier; figures like Peter de Jager popularized it in the 1990s. Estimates of the global cost commonly exceed US$300 billion.

The rollover and aftermath

When 1 January 2000 arrived, serious disruptions were few: some slot machines, monitoring systems, and government programs malfunctioned, but no catastrophic infrastructure failures occurred. Whether this reflected the success of remediation or exaggerated fears has been debated ever since. Related later problems, such as the Year 2038 problem in Unix timekeeping, continue to be addressed.
