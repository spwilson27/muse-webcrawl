Fuke Zen

Fuke Zen (Japanese: 普化宗, Fuke-shū) was a distinct school of Japanese Zen Buddhism that emerged in the Kamakura period and flourished during the Edo period. It traced its lineage to the eccentric Tang-dynasty Chinese monk Puhua (Japanese: Fuke), a contemporary and friend of Linji Yixuan, founder of the Rinzai school. Puhua was remembered in legend for wandering about ringing a handbell to proclaim enlightenment.

Komusō and the shakuhachi

The followers of Fuke Zen were the komusō ("monks of emptiness"), wandering mendicant monks recognisable by the deep woven basket hats (tengai) that concealed their faces and by their playing of the shakuhachi bamboo flute. Rather than chanting sutras, the komusō practised suizen — "blowing Zen" — treating the solo flute pieces called honkyoku as a form of meditation and a path to enlightenment. Many komusō were masterless samurai (rōnin) who had taken vows after the pacification of Japan under the Tokugawa shogunate.

History and suppression

Under the Tokugawa, the Fuke school received official recognition and its monks were granted the exclusive right to play the shakuhachi religiously and to travel freely — a privilege that led to abuses, including espionage for the shogunate. After the Meiji Restoration, the new government abolished the Fuke sect in 1871 and banned the komusō, suspecting them as potential spies; the shakuhachi survived as a musical instrument, and the honkyoku repertory was preserved by later players and revived within modern Zen practice.
