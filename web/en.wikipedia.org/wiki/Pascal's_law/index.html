Pascal's law

Pascal's law (also Pascal's principle) is a principle in fluid mechanics stating that a pressure change at any point in a confined, incompressible fluid is transmitted throughout the fluid such that the same change occurs everywhere. The principle was established by the French mathematician and physicist Blaise Pascal in the seventeenth century and is set out in his posthumously published treatise on the equilibrium of liquids (1663).

Statement and explanation

For a fluid at rest in a closed container, pressure applied to one part of its boundary is transmitted undiminished to every other part. This is a consequence of the fact that a static fluid cannot support shear stress: pressure at a point in a fluid at rest is the same in all directions, and any imposed pressure change propagates through the whole volume (apart from small variations with depth due to gravity, which follow the hydrostatic equation).

Applications

Hydraulic force multiplication
RelationF₂ = F₁ × (A₂ / A₁)
DevicesHydraulic press, car brake systems, lifts, jacks

The best-known application is the hydraulic press: a force F₁ applied to a small piston of area A₁ creates a pressure that acts on a larger piston of area A₂, producing a multiplied force F₂ = F₁·(A₂/A₁), at the cost of a proportionally smaller displacement of the large piston. The same principle underlies hydraulic brakes, lifts, jacks, and heavy machinery. Pascal also demonstrated the related effect with his famous "barrel experiment," in which water poured into a long thin vertical tube bursts a sturdy barrel — showing that pressure depends on the height of the fluid column, not its total weight.
