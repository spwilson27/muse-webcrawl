Rudder

Rudder
Type: Steering control surface
Used on: Ships, boats, aircraft, submarines
Position: Aft, typically behind the propeller

A rudder is a primary control surface used to steer a ship, boat, submarine or aircraft. It operates by redirecting the flow of water (or air) past the hull or fuselage, thereby imparting a turning moment to the craft.

Boat and ship rudders

On watercraft the rudder is a flat, vertically hinged plate mounted at the stern, usually directly behind the propeller so that the propeller's slipstream flows over it, increasing its effectiveness at low speeds. Turning the rudder deflects the water flow, pushing the stern sideways and swinging the bow in the opposite direction of the rudder's deflection. Early steering used a side-mounted steering oar — the origin of the word "starboard" (steerboard side). The pintle-and-gudgeon sternpost rudder appeared in China by the 1st century AD and in medieval Europe by the 12th–13th centuries.

Rudder types include spade, balanced and semi-balanced rudders, skeg-mounted rudders, and transom-hung rudders on small boats. On ships the rudder is moved by a steering gear under control of the wheel or autopilot, acting on the rudder stock.

Aircraft rudders

In aircraft, the rudder is the movable surface on the trailing edge of the vertical stabiliser. It controls yaw — rotation about the vertical axis — and is used chiefly to counter adverse yaw and to coordinate turns, rather than to turn the aircraft by itself. It is operated by the pilot's rudder pedals.
