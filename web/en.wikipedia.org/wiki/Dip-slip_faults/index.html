Dip-slip faults

Dip-slip faults are inclined fractures in the Earth's crust in which the displacement of the rock blocks is primarily along the dip direction of the fault plane — that is, the blocks move up or down the slope of the fault, rather than sideways as in strike-slip faults. Dip-slip faults are divided by their sense of motion into normal faults and reverse (and thrust) faults, and they accommodate the vertical component of crustal deformation that builds mountain ranges, rift valleys and sedimentary basins.

Normal faults

In a normal fault, the block above the fault plane (the hanging wall) moves down relative to the block below (the footwall). Normal faulting is caused by extensional (tensional) stresses that lengthen the crust, and is characteristic of rift systems such as the East African Rift and the Basin and Range Province. Repeated normal faults produce horsts and grabens — uplifted blocks and down-dropped valleys — and tilted fault blocks.

Reverse and thrust faults

In a reverse fault, the hanging wall moves up relative to the footwall, shortening and thickening the crust under compression. A thrust fault is a reverse fault with a shallow dip, typically less than 45 degrees; large thrusts can transport sheets of rock (nappes) tens of kilometres over younger strata. Reverse faulting generates many of the world's largest earthquakes, including megathrust events on subduction zones such as the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan.

Terminology

The vertical separation on a dip-slip fault is the throw and the horizontal component is the heave. Faults with comparable strike-slip and dip-slip components are called oblique-slip faults. The dip-slip classification dates from the systematic fault nomenclature developed by 19th- and early 20th-century geologists such as Reid and Anderson, whose theory of faulting (1951) related fault type to the orientation of the principal stresses.
