Angiotensin

ClassPeptide hormone
SystemRenin–angiotensin system
FormsAngiotensin I, II, III, IV
EffectsVasoconstriction, aldosterone release, thirst

Angiotensin is a peptide hormone that causes vasoconstriction and an increase in blood pressure. It is part of the renin–angiotensin system, which regulates blood pressure and fluid balance. The liver produces and releases the precursor protein angiotensinogen; the enzyme renin, released by the kidneys in response to low blood pressure or low sodium, cleaves it to form angiotensin I, which is in turn converted by angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) to the physiologically active angiotensin II.

Angiotensin II

Angiotensin II (Asp-Arg-Val-Tyr-Ile-His-Pro-Phe) acts mainly through AT1 receptors to constrict blood vessels, stimulate the release of aldosterone from the adrenal cortex (increasing sodium and water reabsorption in the kidney), provoke thirst and the release of antidiuretic hormone, and enhance sympathetic activity. Further cleavage yields angiotensin III and IV, which retain some activity, and the counter-regulatory peptide angiotensin-(1-7), produced by ACE2, which tends to vasodilate.

Clinical relevance

Drugs that interrupt the system — ACE inhibitors such as lisinopril and angiotensin II receptor blockers such as losartan — are mainstays in the treatment of hypertension, heart failure and diabetic kidney disease.
