Morton's fork

Morton's fork is a type of false dilemma in which contradictory observations lead to the same conclusion. It is named after John Morton, Lord Chancellor and Archbishop of Canterbury under Henry VII of England, who is said to have devised a method of extracting funds from the king's subjects: those who lived modestly must be saving money and could therefore afford to pay, while those who lived extravagantly were evidently rich and could also afford to pay. Either way, the subject paid.

Origin

The traditional attribution of the argument to Morton (c. 1420–1500) appears in later accounts of Henry VII's fiscal exactions, particularly in Francis Bacon's history of the reign. Modern historians note that the saying may be apocryphal, and Morton's reputation as a tax-gatherer was heavily propagandised under the Tudors; nevertheless the phrase entered the language as the archetype of a "heads I win, tails you lose" dilemma.

Usage

The term is used in logic and rhetoric for an argument in which the same conclusion is drawn from either of two opposed premises, and more loosely for any choice between two equally unpleasant alternatives — analogous to "damned if you do, damned if you don't," a "Catch-22," or being "between a rock and a hard place." It also appears in contract bridge, where the Morton's fork coup presents a defender with a choice in which either play loses a trick.
