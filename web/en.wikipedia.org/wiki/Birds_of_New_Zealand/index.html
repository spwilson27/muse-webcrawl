Birds of New Zealand

RegionNew Zealand and its outlying islands
Notable speciesKiwi, kākāpō, kea, moa (extinct), takahē, tūī
EndemismVery high; many endemic families and genera
Major threatsIntroduced mammalian predators, habitat loss

The birds of New Zealand evolved in the isolation of an archipelago with no native land mammals (except bats), producing an avifauna of exceptional endemism and specialisation. Many species became flightless, filled niches elsewhere occupied by mammals, and grew to great size; many are now extinct or endangered because of hunting and introduced mammalian predators.

Flightless birds

New Zealand's most famous birds are its flightless ratites: the five species of kiwi (national symbol of New Zealand) and the moa, nine species of giant flightless birds, the largest reaching about 3.6 metres in height, hunted to extinction by Māori by about 1500. The kākāpō, a nocturnal flightless parrot, and the takahē, a large flightless rail once presumed extinct and rediscovered in 1948, survive only under intensive conservation management.

Other groups

The islands host endemic songbirds such as the tūī, kererū (New Zealand pigeon), fantail, and the wattlebirds; the kea and kākā, alpine and forest parrots; the world's only truly alpine parrot, the kea; numerous seabirds, including albatrosses and petrels breeding in the subantarctic islands; and shorebirds such as the endangered black stilt. The extinct Haast's eagle, the largest eagle known, preyed on moa.

Conservation

Since human arrival roughly 700 years ago, more than 50 bird species have become extinct, and many survivors are threatened by rats, stoats, possums, and cats. Conservation measures include transferring populations to predator-free offshore islands, creating mainland sanctuaries such as Zealandia in Wellington, intensive breeding programmes, and the national Predator Free 2050 initiative aiming to eradicate the worst introduced predators.
