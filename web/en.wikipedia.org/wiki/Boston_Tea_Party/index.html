Date16 December 1773
LocationGriffin's Wharf, Boston, Province of Massachusetts Bay

The Boston Tea Party was an American political protest on 16 December 1773, in which members of the Sons of Liberty and other colonists boarded three ships of the British East India Company in Boston Harbour and threw 342 chests of tea overboard. The protest targeted the Tea Act of 1773 and the principle of "taxation without representation". It was a pivotal escalation on the road to the American Revolution.

[ Image: Lithograph of colonists disguised as Native Americans dumping tea into Boston Harbour ]

Background

The Tea Act granted the financially troubled East India Company the right to sell tea directly in the colonies, undercutting colonial merchants while preserving the Townshend duty on tea — which colonists saw as Parliament asserting a right to tax them without their consent. In other ports, pressure forced tea ships to turn back; in Boston, Governor Thomas Hutchinson refused to let the ships Dartmouth, Eleanor and Beaver leave without unloading, setting a deadline of 17 December for payment of the duties.

The event

On the evening of 16 December, after a mass meeting at Old South Meeting House failed to win a reprieve, a group of men — many thinly disguised as Mohawk Indians — boarded the three ships at Griffin's Wharf. Over about three hours they broke open 342 chests and dumped some 92,000 pounds of tea, worth roughly £9,659, into the harbour, taking care to damage nothing else and even replacing a broken padlock.

Aftermath

London responded with the Coercive Acts — called the Intolerable Acts in America — closing the port of Boston, curtailing Massachusetts self-government and quartering troops. Rather than isolating Boston, the measures united the colonies, prompted the First Continental Congress in 1774, and helped set the stage for the outbreak of war at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. The name "Tea Party" only became standard in the 1820s–30s; the modern American political movement of 2009 borrowed the name for its own protest symbolism.
