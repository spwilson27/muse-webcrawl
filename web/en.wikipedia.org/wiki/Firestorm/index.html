Firestorm

A firestorm is a conflagration which attains such intensity that it creates and sustains its own wind system. It is most commonly a natural phenomenon, created during some of the largest bushfires and wildfires, although it has also been produced by deliberate bombing of cities. As the fire consumes oxygen and fuel, rising hot air pulls in fresh air at ground level from all directions, creating fierce inward winds that further fan the flames, and the convection column may spawn fire whirls and a pyrocumulonimbus cloud.

Mechanism

A firestorm develops when a large area burns nearly simultaneously and heat release overcomes ambient winds. Temperatures in the core can exceed 1,000 °C. The inrushing winds, which can reach storm force, prevent the fire from spreading outward at ground level but make survival inside the affected area almost impossible through radiant heat, carbon monoxide and oxygen depletion.

Historical examples

The Great Peshtigo Fire of 1871 in Wisconsin exhibited firestorm behaviour and remains the deadliest wildfire in American history. During the Second World War, the incendiary bombing of Hamburg in July 1943 (Operation Gomorrah) generated a firestorm that killed tens of thousands. Dresden in February 1945 and Tokyo in March 1945 likewise experienced firestorms caused by air raids. The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were followed in Hiroshima's case by a firestorm some hours after the blast. In the modern era, firestorm dynamics have been documented in large wildfires such as those in Canberra in 2003 and the 2017 and 2021 fire seasons in western North America and the Mediterranean.

Distinction from mass fires

Not every mass fire is a firestorm: the term is reserved for fires whose circulation is dominated by the buoyancy of their own convection column, drawing air inward toward a single burning region rather than being driven across a landscape by external winds.
