Cryptococcosis is a fungal infection caused by yeasts of the genus Cryptococcus, principally Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii. Infection is usually acquired by inhaling spores or desiccated yeast cells from the environment — C. neoformans is associated with soil contaminated by bird, especially pigeon, droppings, while C. gattii is associated with certain trees such as eucalypts.

Clinical features

In healthy people infection is often asymptomatic or limited to a mild pulmonary illness. In people with weakened immunity — notably advanced HIV/AIDS, but also organ transplant recipients and others on immunosuppressive therapy — the fungus may disseminate, most seriously to the central nervous system, causing cryptococcal meningitis with headache, fever, confusion and, untreated, coma and death. Skin, bone and other organs can also be involved. Cryptococcal meningitis is a leading cause of meningitis deaths among people living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on microscopy (the yeasts have a characteristic wide capsule, visible with India ink), culture, and detection of cryptococcal antigen in blood or cerebrospinal fluid; antigen lateral-flow tests are widely used for screening people with low CD4 counts. Severe disease is treated with induction therapy using amphotericin B combined with flucytosine, followed by fluconazole consolidation and maintenance; raised intracranial pressure in meningitis is managed with repeated therapeutic lumbar punctures. The WHO included C. neoformans in the critical group of its 2022 fungal priority pathogens list.
