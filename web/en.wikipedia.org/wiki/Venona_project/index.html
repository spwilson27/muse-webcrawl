Venona project

The Venona project was a United States counterintelligence programme initiated during World War II by the United States Army's Signal Intelligence Service (later absorbed into the National Security Agency). It ran from 1 February 1943 until 1 October 1980 and involved the cryptanalysis of messages sent by the intelligence agencies of the Soviet Union, principally the NKVD, KGB and GRU.

Background

Soviet intelligence messages were enciphered with a theoretically unbreakable one-time pad system. However, wartime pressure led the Soviets to reuse some one-time pad pages, creating a fatal vulnerability. Working at Arlington Hall, cryptanalysts including Genevieve Grotjan, who found the first break, and Meredith Gardner, the project's principal linguist-analyst, gradually exploited this reuse to read thousands of messages sent between 1942 and 1945.

Findings

The decrypts revealed extensive Soviet espionage in the United States and allied countries, including penetration of the Manhattan Project. Venona traffic contributed to identifying agents such as Klaus Fuchs and helped corroborate information about the Rosenberg spy ring; Julius Rosenberg's guilt was indicated in the cables, while Ethel Rosenberg's role appeared minor in them. Code names in the traffic — some 349 Americans were identified as having a covert relationship with Soviet intelligence — were painstakingly matched to real identities. Because the material could not be revealed without compromising the source, it was withheld even from President Truman, and evidence derived from it generally could not be used in court.

Disclosure

The project's existence was publicly acknowledged and the decrypts released beginning in 1995, following advocacy by Senator Daniel Patrick Moynihan and others. Historians have since used Venona to reassess Cold War espionage controversies, including the cases of Alger Hiss (tentatively identified with the code name ALES) and Harry Dexter White.
