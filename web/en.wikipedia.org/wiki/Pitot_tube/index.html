Pitot tube

A pitot tube (also pitot probe) is a pressure instrument used to measure fluid flow velocity. Invented by the French engineer Henri Pitot in the early 18th century, it consists of a tube pointing directly into the flow; the fluid is brought to rest at its opening, so the measured stagnation pressure can be compared with the static pressure to compute speed via Bernoulli's principle.

Principle of operation

For an incompressible flow, Bernoulli's equation gives the dynamic pressure as q = p0 − p, where p0 is the stagnation pressure at the pitot opening and p is the ambient static pressure, often measured by separate static ports. The flow speed is then v = √(2q/ρ), with ρ the fluid density. A combined pitot-static tube packages both sensing elements in one probe. At high subsonic and supersonic speeds, compressibility corrections are applied.

Aviation use

On aircraft, pitot tubes feed the airspeed indicator and other air data instruments. Blockage is a serious hazard: icing of pitot tubes contributed to accidents including Air France Flight 447 (2009), in which ice crystals temporarily obstructed the probes and airspeed readings became unreliable; birthing of the incident chain also involved crew response. Pitot covers, drains, and electrical heating mitigate these risks, and tubes are checked in pre-flight inspections.

Other uses

Pitot tubes measure gas velocities in ducts and HVAC systems, water flow in pipes and open channels, and wind speed in wind tunnels. In motorsport, arrays of pitot rakes map airflow over Formula One cars during testing.
