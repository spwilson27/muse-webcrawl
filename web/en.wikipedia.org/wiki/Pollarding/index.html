Pollarding

Type: Pruning system

Purpose: Wood production, fodder, tree size control

Pollarding is a pruning system in which the upper branches of a tree are removed, promoting the growth of a dense head of foliage and branches. The tree is cut back to a framework of stubs — typically two to three metres above the ground — and new shoots regrow from dormant buds around the cut points, forming the characteristic knobbly "knuckles" of a mature pollard.

Purpose and history

Pollarding has been practised in Europe since medieval times, and probably since antiquity. Cutting above the reach of grazing livestock allowed trees to produce repeated crops of poles, firewood and leafy fodder while animals grazed beneath them in wood-pasture. Unlike coppicing, in which shoots are cut at ground level, pollarded regrowth is protected from browsing animals. Regular cutting also greatly extends a tree's life, and ancient pollards are among the oldest living trees in many landscapes.

Technique

A young tree is first cut when it reaches the desired height, and the regrowth is then harvested on a cycle of a few years, depending on the intended product. If the cycle lapses for too long, the heavy regrowth can tear away from the old cuts, so lapsed pollards are dangerous to restore to the cycle and may need staged reduction. Species commonly pollarded include willow, lime (linden), plane, oak, beech, hornbeam, poplar and ash.

Modern use

Today pollarding is widely used in urban forestry and street planting to keep trees at a fixed size, control root growth and reduce shading and leaf litter. It is also valuable for conservation, since old pollards support rich communities of fungi, invertebrates and hole-nesting birds.
