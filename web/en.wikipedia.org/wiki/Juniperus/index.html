Juniperus is a genus of coniferous trees and shrubs in the cypress family Cupressaceae, commonly known as junipers. Depending on taxonomic opinion, between 60 and 75 species are recognised, distributed widely across the Northern Hemisphere from the Arctic south to tropical Africa and Central America, with the highest diversity in western North America and eastern Asia.

Description

Junipers range from low prostrate shrubs to trees up to 40 m tall. The leaves are of two forms: needle-like juvenile leaves and scale-like adult leaves, often both on the same plant. They are usually dioecious (male and female cones on separate plants). The female cones are distinctive, with fleshy scales fused into a berry-like structure — the juniper berry — which is eaten by birds that disperse the seeds.

Notable species

Juniperus communis, the common juniper, has one of the largest ranges of any woody plant and supplies the culinary berries used to flavour gin. Other notable species include J. virginiana (eastern red cedar), J. chinensis, J. scopulorum, J. oxycedrus (cade juniper), the incense-producing J. thurifera, and J. procera, the tallest juniper, of East African mountains.

Uses

Juniper wood is durable, aromatic and resistant to decay; it is used for fence posts, chests, and (from eastern red cedar) for pencils and moth-repellent "cedar" chests. Many species and hundreds of cultivars are planted as ornamentals and for bonsai. Juniper foliage can cause "cedar apple rust" in orchards, and pollen of some species is a potent allergen.
