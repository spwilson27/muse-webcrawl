Sir Muhammad Iqbal (9 November 1877 – 21 April 1938) was a philosopher, poet and politician in British India whose Urdu and Persian poetry is considered among the finest of the modern era. Known in Pakistan as Allama Iqbal ("the Scholar"), the Poet of the East (Shair-e-Mashriq) and the Thinker of Pakistan (Musawwir-e-Pakistan), he is credited with inspiring the Pakistan Movement: in his presidential address to the Muslim League at Allahabad in 1930 he articulated the vision of a separate Muslim state in north-western India.

Life and thought

Born in Sialkot to a Kashmiri family, Iqbal studied philosophy at Government College, Lahore, then at Cambridge and Munich, where he completed a doctorate on Persian metaphysics in 1908. He practised law in Lahore while rising to fame as a poet. His early patriotic verse, including "Tarana-e-Hindi" ("Sare Jahan se Accha"), later gave way to a philosophy centred on khudi — selfhood — the cultivation of the individual ego in creative tension with God and community, expounded in Asrar-e-Khudi ("The Secrets of the Self", 1915), the first of his Persian mathnawis.

Iqbal sought a revival of Islamic civilisation through a dynamic, ijtihad-based reinterpretation of the faith, set out in his lectures The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam (1930). Politically he moved from Indian nationalism toward Muslim separatism, presiding over the Muslim League's 1930 session and urging, in letters to Muhammad Ali Jinnah, the creation of a consolidated Muslim state. He died in Lahore nine years before Pakistan's creation in 1947, and is honoured there as its ideological founder; his tomb stands beside the Badshahi Mosque.
