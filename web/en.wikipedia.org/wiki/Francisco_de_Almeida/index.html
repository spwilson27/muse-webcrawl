Francisco de Almeida

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dom Francisco de Almeida (Portuguese pronunciation: [fɾɐ̃ˈsiʃku dɨ aɫˈmɐjdɐ]; c. 1450 – 1 March 1510) was a Portuguese nobleman, soldier, and explorer. He distinguished himself as a counsellor to King John II and later in the wars against the Moors and in the conquest of Granada in 1492. In 1505 he was appointed the first governor and viceroy of the Portuguese State of India (Estado da Índia). Almeida is credited with establishing Portuguese hegemony in the Indian Ocean with his victory at the naval Battle of Diu in 1509.

Viceroy of India

Sailing from Lisbon in March 1505 with a fleet of 22 ships, Almeida built a chain of fortresses along the East African coast and in India — at Kilwa, Sofala, Angediva, Cannanore, and Cochin — pursuing a "blue-water" policy of commanding the sea lanes rather than occupying territory. His son Lourenço de Almeida explored Ceylon but was killed at the Battle of Chaul (1508) by the combined Mamluk and Gujarati fleets, a loss that devastated his father. In February 1509 Almeida destroyed that alliance at the Battle of Diu, one of the most decisive naval battles of the age, avenging his son and securing Portuguese naval supremacy in the Indian Ocean for a century.

Death

Almeida resisted handing over office to his successor, Afonso de Albuquerque, imprisoning him for a time before finally relenting in late 1509. On the return voyage to Portugal, Almeida stopped near the Cape of Good Hope for water and was killed on 1 March 1510 in a skirmish with the indigenous Khoikhoi at the beach near Table Bay — an end later chroniclers found ignominious for so great a commander.
