Sexual orientation

Sexual orientation describes patterns of romantic and sexual attraction.

Sexual orientation is an enduring pattern of romantic or sexual attraction (or a combination of these) to persons of the opposite sex or gender, the same sex or gender, or to both sexes or more than one gender. These attractions are generally subsumed under heterosexuality, homosexuality, and bisexuality, while asexuality (the lack of sexual attraction to others) is sometimes identified as the fourth category.

These categories are aspects of the more nuanced nature of sexual identity and terminology. For example, people may use other labels, such as pansexual or polysexual, or none at all. According to the American Psychological Association, sexual orientation "also refers to a person's sense of identity based on those attractions, related behaviors, and membership in a community of others who share those attractions". Androphilia and gynephilia are terms used in behavioral science to describe sexual orientation as an alternative to a gender binary conceptualization.

Definitions and distinctions

Sexual orientation and sexual identity

Sexual orientation is traditionally defined as including heterosexuality, bisexuality, and homosexuality, while asexuality is considered the fourth category by some researchers and has been defined as the absence of a traditional sexual orientation. An asexual person has little or no sexual attraction to people of either sex. It has been debated whether it is a sexual orientation, an argument against it, or not part of it.

Most definitions of sexual orientation include a psychological component, such as the direction of one's erotic desires, or a behavioral component, which focuses on the sexual behavior and the sex or gender of one's partner(s). Some people prefer to simply follow a person's self-definition or identity.

Gender, sex, and sexual orientation

Early writers on sexual orientation usually understood it to be intrinsically linked to the subject's own sex. For example, it was thought that a typical female-bodied person who is attracted to female-bodied persons would have masculine attributes, and vice versa. This understanding was shared by most prominent theorists of sexual orientation from the mid-19th to early 20th century, such as Karl Heinrich Ulrichs, Richard von Krafft-Ebing, Magnus Hirschfeld, Ellis, Freud, and many others.

By the second half of the 20th century, gender identity was increasingly seen as a phenomenon distinct from sexual orientation. Transgender and cisgender people may be attracted to men, women, or both, although the prevalence of different orientations varies in the two populations.

Measurement and dimensions

The Kinsey scale, created by Alfred Kinsey and colleagues in 1948, ranges from 0 (exclusively heterosexual) to 6 (exclusively homosexual), with an additional category X for those with no socio-sexual contacts or reactions. Kinsey reported that a significant minority of people did not fit neatly into exclusive categories. Later models, such as Fritz Klein's Klein Sexual Orientation Grid, assess orientation across multiple dimensions and over time.

Causes

The exact causes of sexual orientation are not known. Research over several decades has demonstrated that sexual orientation ranges along a continuum, from exclusive attraction to the opposite sex to exclusive attraction to the same sex. Biologists have pointed to a combination of genetic, hormonal, and environmental influences. No single factor has been identified, and there is no evidence that parenting or early childhood experiences play a role in the development of sexual orientation. Major mental health organizations state that sexual orientation results from a complex interaction of biological and environmental factors and is not a choice.

Sexual orientation change efforts

Sexual orientation change efforts (SOCE) are methods used in attempts to change the sexual orientation of non-heterosexual people to heterosexuality. Such efforts have been rejected by every major medical and psychological organization in the United States, which have adopted policy statements warning that there is no evidence that sexual orientation can be changed through therapy, and that attempts may be harmful.

Demography

Reliable data on the size of the gay and lesbian population are of value to public policymakers. Surveys in various countries have found that roughly 2–11% of adults report some same-sex attraction or behavior, with figures varying by methodology and by the dimension measured (attraction, behavior, or identity). Younger cohorts in many Western countries report higher rates of non-heterosexual identification than older cohorts.

Law and politics

As of the 2020s, same-sex marriage is legally performed and recognized in more than thirty countries. Conversely, laws in a number of countries, primarily in Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia, criminalize consensual same-sex sexual activity, with penalties ranging from imprisonment to death. Antidiscrimination protections based on sexual orientation exist in many jurisdictions.
