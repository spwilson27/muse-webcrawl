Perennation

In botany, perennation is the ability of an organism — typically a plant — to survive from one growth season to the next, especially under unfavourable conditions such as winter cold or summer drought. A plant that does so is a perennial, in contrast to annuals, which complete their life cycle in one season and die, and biennials, which live for two seasons.

Perennating organs

Plants survive adverse seasons through specialised storage organs that hold food reserves and dormant buds. These perennating organs include bulbs (as in onions and tulips), corms (gladiolus, crocus), rhizomes (iris, ginger), tubers (potato, dahlia), and runners or stolons. Danish botanist Christen Raunkiær classified plants by the position of these buds during the unfavourable season — above ground in phanerophytes (trees and shrubs), at the surface in hemicryptophytes, or below ground in geophytes (cryptophytes).

Significance

Perennation underlies much of agriculture and horticulture: fruit trees, asparagus, rhubarb, and pasture grasses all yield harvests over many years from a single planting, and recent research aims to breed perennial grain crops, such as intermediate wheatgrass (Kernza), to reduce erosion and input costs compared with annual cereals.
