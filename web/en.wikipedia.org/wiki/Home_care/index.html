Home care

Home care supports people in their own homes

Home care (also referred to as domiciliary care, social care, or in-home care) is supportive care provided in the home. Care may be provided by licensed healthcare professionals who provide medical treatment needs or by professional caregivers who provide daily assistance to help ensure the activities of daily living (ADLs) are met. In-home medical care is often and more accurately referred to as home health care or formal care.

Home care services support adults, older people and children with a wide range of needs: older people who need help to remain independent, people of any age living with physical or learning disabilities, people recovering from surgery or illness, and people with long-term conditions such as dementia.

Types of care

Services range from non-medical support — help with washing, dressing, cooking, cleaning, shopping, mobility, and companionship — to skilled nursing care such as wound dressing, injections, catheter care, and rehabilitation therapy. Care may be delivered through short daily visits ("homecare visits"), overnight care, or live-in care in which a caregiver resides in the home. Hospice and palliative care can also be delivered at home for people at the end of life.

Provision and payment

Home care is provided by a mixture of public services, private agencies, voluntary organisations, and informal carers (family and friends), the latter providing the majority of care in most countries. In the United States, home health care following hospitalisation is partly funded by Medicare under strict conditions; in the United Kingdom, domiciliary care is arranged by local authorities subject to needs and means assessments, as well as purchased privately. Staffing shortages, low pay, and the training and regulation of home care workers are recurring policy concerns, as demand rises with ageing populations.
