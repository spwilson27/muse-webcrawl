A testicle, or testis (plural testes), is the male gonad in most animals, including humans. It is homologous to the female ovary. The functions of the testicles are to produce both sperm and androgens, primarily testosterone. In most placental mammals, including humans, the two testes descend into the scrotum, which keeps them at a temperature slightly below core body temperature favourable to spermatogenesis.

Structure

Each testis is an oval organ roughly 4–5 cm long in adult humans, enclosed in a tough fibrous capsule, the tunica albuginea. Internally it is divided into some 250–300 lobules, each containing tightly coiled seminiferous tubules where sperm are produced. Sertoli cells within the tubules support developing spermatozoa, while Leydig cells in the interstitium secrete testosterone. Sperm pass through the rete testis and efferent ductules into the epididymis, where they mature and are stored, before entering the vas deferens. The spermatic cord carries the vas, vessels and nerves; the cremaster and dartos muscles adjust testicular position for temperature regulation.

Development

The testes develop in the abdomen from the genital ridge and normally descend through the inguinal canal into the scrotum before or shortly after birth, guided by the gubernaculum. Failure to descend (cryptorchidism) is associated with infertility and increased risk of testicular cancer and is usually corrected surgically in childhood.

Clinical significance

Conditions affecting the testicle include testicular torsion (a surgical emergency), epididymitis and orchitis (inflammation), hydrocele, varicocele, and testicular cancer, the most common cancer in young adult men in many countries. Testicular self-examination is commonly recommended for early detection of lumps.

In other animals

Most male mammals have two external testes in a scrotum, though some groups (elephants, monotremes, cetaceans) retain internal testes. Testis size relative to body mass varies with mating system and sperm competition.
