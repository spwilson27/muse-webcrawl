Poʻouli

The poʻouli (Melamprosops phaeosoma)

The poʻouli (Melamprosops phaeosoma), or black-faced honeycreeper, is an extinct species of passerine bird endemic to the island of Maui in Hawaii. It was one of the most recently discovered Hawaiian birds, first identified by science only in 1973, and one of the most recently to disappear.

Description and discovery

The poʻouli was a stocky honeycreeper about 14 cm long, brown above and pale below, with a striking black mask across the face, a short tail, and a stout bill suited to feeding on native tree snails, its principal prey. It was discovered in 1973 by students from the University of Hawaii in the Koʻolau Forest Reserve on the north-eastern slopes of Haleakalā, at elevations of roughly 1,400–1,700 metres – the last surviving member of a lineage distinct enough to be placed in its own genus, Melamprosops.

Decline and extinction

At discovery the population was estimated at perhaps 100–200 birds, but it declined rapidly owing to habitat degradation by feral pigs, predation by rats and mongooses, competition and disease (avian malaria and pox) spread by introduced mosquitoes, and the collapse of its native snail prey. By 1997 only three individuals were known, in separate home ranges. A plan to translocate one bird to bring two together failed; one bird was captured in September 2004 for captive breeding but died that November – the last known poʻouli. Surveys found no others, and the species was formally declared extinct (the IUCN listing and the US Fish and Wildlife Service's 2021 delisting of the bird as extinct confirmed the assessment).
