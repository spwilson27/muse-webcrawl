Intrinsically disordered protein

Intrinsically disordered protein
AbbreviationIDP (regions: IDR)
PropertyNo fixed 3D structure under physiological conditions
Enriched inCharged and polar residues (P, E, S, Q, K); depleted in hydrophobics
ExamplesTau, α-synuclein, p53 transactivation domain, Sic1

An intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) is a protein that lacks a fixed or ordered three-dimensional structure under physiological conditions, existing instead as a dynamic ensemble of conformations. Many proteins contain intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) alongside folded domains. Disorder is especially common in eukaryotes: computational predictions suggest that a large fraction of eukaryotic proteins contain disordered segments of 30 residues or more, whereas fully disordered proteins are rarer in bacteria and archaea.

Properties and functions

Disordered sequences are typically rich in charged and proline residues and poor in the bulky hydrophobic residues that drive folding. Disorder suits proteins to signalling and regulation: IDPs bind many partners with high specificity but modest affinity, often folding on binding (coupled folding and binding), present short linear motifs and sites for post-translational modification in accessible form, and act as flexible linkers and entropic springs. They are central to the formation of biomolecular condensates by liquid–liquid phase separation, such as nucleoli and stress granules. Because experiments such as X-ray crystallography capture only ordered structure, disorder was long overlooked; NMR spectroscopy, SAXS and single-molecule methods now characterise IDP ensembles, and prediction tools (IUPred, PONDR) estimate disorder from sequence.

Disease relevance

Several aggregation-prone IDPs are implicated in neurodegenerative disease — tau in Alzheimer's disease, α-synuclein in Parkinson's disease, and huntingtin in Huntington's disease — where misfolding into amyloid is pathological. The tumour suppressor p53 has disordered terminal regions central to its many interactions. IDPs were historically considered "undruggable", but small molecules and stabilisers targeting their transient pockets and ensembles are an active research area. The database DisProt curates experimentally verified disordered regions.
