Kabaka is the title of the king of Buganda, the largest of the traditional kingdoms within modern Uganda. The Baganda people trace the line of kabakas back to Kato Kintu in the 14th century; the royal tombs at Kasubi in Kampala, where several kabakas are buried, are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Role and history

Historically the kabaka was an absolute ruler who combined political, military and religious authority, governing through a hierarchy of chiefs, clan heads (bataka) and the Lukiiko (parliament) chaired by the Katikkiro (prime minister). Under the 1900 Buganda Agreement with the British Protectorate, the kabaka retained constitutional status within colonial Uganda.

The title's modern history has been dramatic. Mutesa II, kabaka from 1939, became Uganda's first president at independence in 1962, but fled into exile in 1966 when Prime Minister Milton Obote's troops stormed his palace at Mengo. The kingdoms were abolished in 1967; Idi Amin returned Mutesa's remains for burial in 1971. President Yoweri Museveni's government restored the cultural institutions in 1993, and Mutesa's son, Ronald Muwenda Mutebi II, was crowned as kabaka — as a cultural and ceremonial leader without political power, though the monarchy remains politically influential. In 2009, riots erupted in Kampala when the government blocked the kabaka from visiting Kayunga District.

Notable kabakas

  • Kato Kintu — legendary founder of the dynasty.
  • Muteesa I (r. 1856–1884) — opened Buganda to Arab traders and European explorers and missionaries.
  • Mwanga II (r. 1884–1897) — ordered the execution of the Uganda Martyrs; deposed and exiled.
  • Daudi Cwa II (r. 1897–1939) — first kabaka raised as a Christian.
  • Mutesa II (r. 1939–1969) — last pre-republican kabaka and first President of Uganda.
