Glass–Steagall legislation

The Glass–Steagall legislation is the collective name for the provisions of the U.S. Banking Act of 1933 — principally its sections 16, 20, 21 and 32 — that erected a legal wall between commercial banking and investment banking in the United States. Sponsored by Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry B. Steagall, the provisions prohibited deposit-taking banks from underwriting and dealing in securities (with exceptions for U.S. government obligations and certain municipal bonds), barred securities firms from accepting deposits, and outlawed shared officers and directors between member banks and securities companies.

Historical context

The legislation emerged from the wreckage of the 1929 crash and the ensuing banking panics, in which roughly 9,000 American banks failed between 1930 and 1933. Hearings by chief counsel Ferdinand Pecora documented how banks' securities affiliates had sold dubious bonds to the public and lent to support the prices of their own underwritings. Glass, a former Treasury Secretary and a principal author of the Federal Reserve Act, had long argued that banks' involvement in securities speculation diverted credit from commerce and agriculture.

Key provisions

  • Section 16 restricted national banks' securities activities to purchasing investment securities for their own account, chiefly government and high-grade municipal issues.
  • Section 20 prohibited Federal Reserve member banks from affiliating with firms "engaged principally" in underwriting or dealing in securities.
  • Section 21 made it unlawful for securities underwriters to simultaneously take deposits.
  • Section 32 prohibited interlocking directorates and officer positions between member banks and securities firms.

Repeal and the modern debate

Beginning in the 1980s, Federal Reserve and Comptroller interpretations progressively diluted the wall, authorising "Section 20 subsidiaries" to underwrite an expanding range of securities. The Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act (1999) repealed sections 20 and 32, legalising financial holding companies that combine banking, securities and insurance — the structure exemplified by Citigroup. In the wake of the 2008 financial crisis, commentators and politicians across the spectrum debated reinstatement; the 2010 Dodd–Frank Act instead adopted the Volcker Rule's narrower ban on proprietary trading, leaving the surviving parts of Glass–Steagall (sections 16 and 21) in force.
