Ceremonial ship launching

Ceremonial ship launching is the ritual surrounding the transfer of a vessel from the slipway where it was built into the water for the first time. The ceremony typically includes naming the ship, blessings or religious invocations, speeches, and the breaking of a bottle — traditionally champagne — against the hull by a sponsor, a custom intended to bring good fortune to the vessel and its crew.

History

Launching rituals are ancient: Babylonian, Greek, and Roman sources describe offerings made when new ships took to the water, and Viking tradition associated launches with sacrifices. In early modern Europe, launches of naval vessels became state occasions; in Britain members of the royal family or senior officials stood on the launching platform, and wine was poured or a silver cup thrown into the sea. The custom of breaking a bottle against the bow became widespread in the late 19th century, with champagne becoming the customary liquid; in the United States, water or other substitutes were used during Prohibition.

The ceremony

The ship is traditionally named aloud by its sponsor — in modern naval practice often a woman with a connection to the vessel or its name — who says words such as "I name this ship…" before breaking the bottle. In the United States Navy the ceremony is called a christening and launching when the two are combined. Superstition holds that a bottle that fails to break is an ill omen. In some traditions, such as in Japan, axes cut the lines holding the ship while ceremonial decorations and tethered balloons mark the occasion; in India a coconut may be broken against the hull.

Methods of launching

Physically, most large ships are launched stern-first down inclined slipways on greased ways, checked by drag chains once afloat; others are launched sideways, particularly on the Great Lakes, or floated out of dry docks and building basins. After launching, the vessel is completed afloat in a process called fitting out, followed by sea trials and commissioning.
