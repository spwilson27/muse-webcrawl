Celastrus scandens, commonly called American bittersweet, is a woody climbing vine of the family Celastraceae native to central and eastern North America. It grows in rich, dry soils of woods, thickets, and hillsides from Quebec and Manitoba south to Oklahoma and Georgia.

Description

The vine can climb 6–20 metres by twining around trees and shrubs. Leaves are alternate, ovate to oblong, finely toothed, and pointed at the tip. The species is dioecious: small greenish-white flowers appear in terminal panicles at the ends of branches — the key feature distinguishing it from the invasive Oriental bittersweet (Celastrus orbiculatus), whose flowers and fruit sit in the leaf axils. The orange-yellow capsules split in autumn to expose seeds wrapped in crimson arils, and the fruiting branches are traditionally collected for winter decoration. All parts of the plant are considered poisonous to humans and livestock, although birds eat the fruit.

Ecology and conservation

American bittersweet is declining in parts of its range. It is outcompeted and genetically swamped by Oriental bittersweet, with which it hybridises; the hybrids are fertile and often more vigorous. Mislabelled nursery stock — invasive plants sold as the native species — has worsened the problem. Conservation guidance recommends planting only verified native stock and removing Oriental bittersweet where it threatens native populations.

Uses

Indigenous peoples of North America used preparations of the plant medicinally, including for rheumatism and as an emetic, and the colourful fruiting stems remain popular in floral arrangements. The berries are a winter food source for birds such as wild turkey and grouse.
