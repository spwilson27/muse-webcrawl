Fumie

A fumie (踏み絵, fumi-e, "stepping-on picture") was a likeness of Jesus or Mary, usually a bronze plaque, onto which suspected Christians in Tokugawa Japan were required to step in order to prove they were not members of the banned religion. The ritual, called e-fumi, formed part of the shogunate's suppression of Christianity in the 17th and 18th centuries.

Practice

After Christianity was prohibited in Japan and missionaries expelled, officials—especially in Nagasaki, with its history of Christian communities—developed annual ceremonies in which residents were made to trample fumie before witnesses. Refusal or hesitation exposed "hidden Christians" (Kakure Kirishitan) to arrest, torture or execution. The practice began around 1629 and continued, with varying intensity, into the 19th century; it was formally abandoned for Japanese subjects in the later Edo period but was still applied symbolically to Dutch traders at Dejima for some time.

Legacy

Surviving fumie, worn smooth by centuries of feet, are preserved in Japanese museums and are powerful artefacts of religious persecution. The ritual features prominently in Shūsaku Endō's novel Silence (1966) and its film adaptations, which explore the moral dilemma of apostasy under duress.
