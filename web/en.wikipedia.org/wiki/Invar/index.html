Invar


Invar is prized for its near-zero thermal expansion.

Invar is a nickel–iron alloy notable for its uniquely low coefficient of thermal expansion. It usually consists of about 36% nickel and 64% iron, and its name refers to its dimensional invariability with temperature.

Discovery and properties

The alloy was discovered in 1896 by the Swiss physicist Charles Édouard Guillaume, whose work on nickel–iron alloys earned him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1920 — the only Nobel awarded for metallurgical research. Near room temperature Invar's thermal expansion coefficient is roughly 1–2 parts per million per kelvin, about a tenth that of ordinary steel, due to a magnetovolume effect that compensates normal lattice expansion. The anomaly disappears above its Curie temperature of about 230 °C.

Applications

Invar has been used in precision instruments, pendulum rods and balance wheels for clocks and watches, surveyor's tapes, seismometer frames, shadow masks in cathode-ray tubes, moulds for composite aircraft parts, and tanks and piping for liquefied natural gas. Related alloys such as Super Invar and the iron–nickel–cobalt alloy Kovar serve in electronics and glass-to-metal seals.
