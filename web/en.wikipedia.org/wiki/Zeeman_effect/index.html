Zeeman effect

The Zeeman effect is the splitting of a spectral line into several components in the presence of a static magnetic field. It was first observed by the Dutch physicist Pieter Zeeman in 1896 and explained by Hendrik Lorentz, with whom Zeeman shared the 1902 Nobel Prize in Physics. The effect provided early evidence for the quantisation of angular momentum and, through the "anomalous" Zeeman patterns, led to the discovery of electron spin.

Normal and anomalous effects

In the normal Zeeman effect, a spectral line splits into three components (a triplet) when viewed perpendicular to the field, or two when viewed along it; Lorentz's electron theory explained this using the orbital motion of electrons, with a shift Δν = eB/(4πme) for neighbouring components. Many lines instead show more complex patterns—the anomalous Zeeman effect—which classical theory could not explain. Quantum mechanics accounts for both through the magnetic moment of the electron: the energy shift is ΔE = μB gJ mJ B, where gJ is the Landé g-factor incorporating both orbital and spin contributions.

Related effects

At very strong fields the pattern reverts to a normal triplet (the Paschen–Back effect). The analogous splitting in an electric field is the Stark effect. George Ellery Hale used the Zeeman effect in 1908 to make the first measurement of a magnetic field beyond Earth, in sunspots.

Applications

Zeeman splitting is used to measure magnetic fields in the Sun, stars, interstellar clouds and laboratory plasmas, and in analytical chemistry (Zeeman background correction in atomic absorption spectroscopy). The related interaction underlies techniques from NMR and MRI to atomic clocks and magnetometry.
