Mingqi

Mingqi (Chinese: 明器 or 冥器, míngqì, "spirit objects" or "brilliant objects") are Chinese burial goods — models and effigies placed in tombs to serve the deceased in the afterlife. They include pottery figures of servants, soldiers, officials, entertainers and animals, as well as miniature models of houses, watchtowers, granaries, stoves, wells, carts, and other furnishings of daily life.

History

The practice is rooted in beliefs that the dead required the apparatus of life in the tomb. From the Han dynasty (206 BCE – 220 CE), mingqi in painted earthenware largely replaced the burial of real retainers and goods of full value, a substitution endorsed by Confucian ritual thought, which prescribed effigies that were deliberately not usable by the living. Production became highly elaborate in the Tang dynasty, when large, glazed (sancai) figures of camels, horses, grooms, and court ladies filled the tombs of the elite around Xi'an and Luoyang. The custom continued, in changing forms, through later dynasties.

Significance

Because tombs were often sealed and undisturbed, mingqi preserve detailed evidence of architecture, costume, agriculture, technology, and social hierarchy — from Han farmstead models with working yards and livestock to Tang depictions of Silk Road merchants and musicians. Famous archaeological finds rich in mingqi include the tomb complexes of the Tang imperial family and, in a distinct category of full-scale sculpture, the Terracotta Army of the First Emperor.
