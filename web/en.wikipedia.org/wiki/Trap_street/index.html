Trap street

A trap street is a fictitious street inserted into a map by its publisher for the purpose of detecting plagiarism or copyright infringement. If the invented street subsequently appears in a rival's map, this is evidence that the rival copied the original rather than surveying the area independently. Trap streets are also known as paper streets, phantom streets or copyright traps, and are one example of a broader class of fictitious entries used in reference works.

Use and limitations

Trap streets are generally inconspicuous minor roads, often ending blindly or bearing an innocuous invented name, placed where mistakes are unlikely to mislead users seriously. Their legal value varies: in the United States, courts have held that facts themselves are not copyrightable, so copying factual data such as street locations may not infringe, although deliberately copied fictitious entries have figured in copyright disputes over compilations. Mapmakers have sometimes been caught out when their own trap streets came from another publisher's work, and phantom settlements such as Agloe, New York — invented by mapmakers — have occasionally become real places.

Related practices

Similar devices include fictitious entries in dictionaries and encyclopedias (such as the invented word "esquivalience" in the New Oxford American Dictionary), phantom dictionary headwords known as Mountweazels, and fictitious telephone-directory listings used to detect copying of directories.
