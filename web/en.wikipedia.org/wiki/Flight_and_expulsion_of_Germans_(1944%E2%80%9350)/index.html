Flight and expulsion of Germans (1944–1950)

LocationCentral and Eastern Europe

The flight and expulsion of Germans during and after the Second World War (1944–1950) was the largest forced migration of a single ethnic group in modern history. Between 12 and 14 million ethnic Germans fled or were expelled from their homes in Central and Eastern Europe, including the former eastern territories of Germany, the Sudetenland in Czechoslovakia, and long-standing German communities in Poland, Hungary, Yugoslavia and Romania.

Background and course

As the Red Army advanced in 1944–45, millions of German civilians fled westward, often in harsh winter conditions and amid violence. After the war, the Potsdam Agreement sanctioned the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary, but in practice many expulsions were carried out brutally, with internment, forced labour, expropriation and large numbers of deaths from violence, disease and deprivation. Estimates of deaths vary widely and remain contested among historians.

Aftermath

The expellees (Vertriebene) were absorbed into post-war West and East Germany and Austria, where their integration was a major social and political issue. Expellee organisations campaigned for recognition and for a "right to the homeland," and the memory of the expulsions remains a sensitive topic in German relations with Poland and the Czech Republic.
