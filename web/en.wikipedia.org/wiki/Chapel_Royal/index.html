Chapel Royal

The Chapel Royal is an establishment of the Royal Household of the United Kingdom, formally a department responsible for providing pastors, musicians and liturgical services to the sovereign. It is not a single building: the term refers both to the body of clergy and musicians — the chaplains, the choir of gentlemen and children, and the organists — and, by extension, to the chapels in royal palaces where they serve, principally the Chapel Royal at St James's Palace in London and the Chapel Royal at Hampton Court Palace.

History

The Chapel Royal traces its origins to the itinerant body of priests and singers who attended the English monarch in the Middle Ages. By the reign of Henry VIII it had become a fixed and celebrated institution; its choir was regarded as among the finest in Europe, and composers such as Thomas Tallis and William Byrd served as Gentlemen of the Chapel Royal. In 1542 Henry VIII granted Byrd and Tallis a monopoly on printed music in recognition of the chapel's standing.

Under Elizabeth I and the Stuart kings the Chapel Royal was a centre of English church music. Its musicians included Orlando Gibbons and Henry Purcell, who was organist of the chapel. The choir traditionally consisted of gentlemen (adult singers, including countertenors, tenors and basses) and children of the chapel, educated at the monarch's expense.

The Chapel Royal today

Today the Chapel Royal is headed by a Dean — by convention the Bishop of London — and a Sub-Dean. Its choir, based at St James's Palace, sings at services attended by members of the Royal Family, at state occasions, and at royal weddings, funerals and commemorations. The choir of the Chapel Royal at Hampton Court, which has its own musical establishment, sings regular Sunday services in the Tudor chapel there.

Other chapels royal

Scotland historically had its own Chapel Royal, associated with the Order of the Thistle and the chapel at Holyrood. In Canada, two chapels have been designated "chapel royal" (Mohawk Chapel in Brantford and Christ Church, Tyendinaga), a distinction connected with the Crown's historic alliance with the Mohawk people.
