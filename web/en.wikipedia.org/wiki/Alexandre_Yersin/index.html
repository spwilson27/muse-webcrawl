Alexandre Émile John Yersin (22 September 1863 – 1 March 1943) was a Swiss and French physician and bacteriologist of the Pasteur Institute. He is remembered chiefly for identifying, during the Hong Kong epidemic of 1894, the bacterium that causes bubonic plague, later named Yersinia pestis in his honour, and for spending half a century developing medicine, agriculture and education in Vietnam.

Early career

Born near Lake Geneva, Yersin studied medicine in Lausanne, Marburg and Paris. At the Pasteur Institute he worked with Émile Roux on diphtheria, contributing to the discovery of the diphtheria toxin (1888). Restless in the laboratory, he shipped out as a doctor with the Messageries Maritimes and reached French Indochina in 1890.

Exploration and the plague bacillus

Yersin led expeditions through the largely unmapped interior of Vietnam, crossing the Annamite range to the Mekong. In June 1894, sent to Hong Kong during the third plague pandemic, he set up a straw-hut laboratory beside the hospital where Shibasaburo Kitasato's better-equipped team was working. Within days Yersin isolated from buboes a Gram-negative bacillus, demonstrated it in cadavers and in rodents, and established its identity as the plague agent — the discovery priority generally credited to him today. He later developed, with colleagues at Paris, an anti-plague serum.

Life in Vietnam

Settling at Nha Trang, Yersin founded a Pasteur Institute there (1895), opened Indochina's first medical school at Hanoi (1902) and directed it, introduced rubber trees (Hevea brasiliensis) and cinchona (quinine) plantations at altitude stations he founded, including Hon Ba, and acclimatised numerous crops and livestock. He identified the site that became the hill station of Da Lat. Revered locally as "Ông Năm" (Uncle Five), he remained in Vietnam through the Second World War years and died at Nha Trang in 1943; his grave there is still tended and honoured.
