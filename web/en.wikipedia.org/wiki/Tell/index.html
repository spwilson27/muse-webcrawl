Tell

A tell (from Arabic tall, "hill" or "mound") is an artificial hill created by many generations of people living and rebuilding on the same spot. Over centuries, successive settlements produce a mound of accumulated debris, collapsed mud-brick structures, refuse, and burials. Tells are a characteristic archaeological site type of the ancient Near East and parts of South and Southeast Europe.

Formation

When a settlement is abandoned or destroyed, its mud-brick and timber buildings decay or are levelled, and a new settlement is built on the flattened remains. Repeated over hundreds or thousands of years, this raises the site well above the surrounding plain. Stratigraphic excavation of a tell can therefore reveal a sequence of occupation layers, each layer holding artefacts, architecture, and organic remains datable relative to the layers above and below.

Distribution and examples

Tells are found across Mesopotamia, the Levant, Anatolia, Egypt, and the Balkans, and comparable mounds exist elsewhere (for example, the terps of the Netherlands and the settlement mounds of South Asia). Famous examples include Tell Brak and Uruk in Mesopotamia, Megiddo and Jericho in the Levant, Çatalhöyük in Anatolia, and Hisarlik, the site of Troy. In Mesopotamia many great cities began as tells, and the Arabic word appears in countless place names across the region, alongside the equivalent Turkish höyük and Hebrew tel.

Archaeological significance

Because their strata preserve long, ordered sequences, tells have been central to establishing chronologies of the Neolithic, Chalcolithic, and Bronze and Iron Ages in the Near East. Excavation is typically conducted through step trenches on the mound's slope and horizontal excavation of individual layers on its summit.
