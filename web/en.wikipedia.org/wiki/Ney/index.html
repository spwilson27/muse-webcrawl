The ney (Persian: نی; Arabic: ناي; Turkish: ney) is an end-blown flute that figures prominently in the music of the Middle East, North Africa and Central Asia, especially Persian, Turkish, Arabic and Azerbaijani music. One of the oldest instruments still in use — flutes of this general type appear in ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian art and the instrument has been played continuously for some 4,500–5,000 years — the ney is traditionally made from a hollow segment of giant cane, with five or six finger holes in front and one thumb hole in back.

Construction and technique

The player blows across the sharp rim at the top of the tube, holding the instrument obliquely. In the Turkish tradition a mouthpiece of horn, ivory or plastic called a başpare protects the lips and shapes the tone. Neys come in many sizes tuned to different pitches, named in Turkish practice after their tonic (for example kız ney, mansur). Because the cane is not perfectly cylindrical, intonation depends on embouchure and breath; skilled players can produce the microtones of the makam and dastgah modal systems, and the instrument has a range of about two and a half octaves. Its breathy, plaintive timbre makes it a favoured solo instrument and a symbol in poetry.

Cultural significance

The ney holds a special place in the Mevlevi Sufi order, whose whirling ceremonies (sema) it accompanies; the master ney player is the neyzen, and the chief neyzen (neyzenbaşı) has an honoured position. Rumi's Masnavi opens with the famous "Song of the Reed" — "Listen to the reed, how it tells a tale, complaining of separations" — in which the ney, cut from its reed-bed, becomes a metaphor for the soul exiled from God. In Persian and Turkish classical music the ney is a principal melodic instrument; in Arabic music it is a standard member of the takht ensemble.
