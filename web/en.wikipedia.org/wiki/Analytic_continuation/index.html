Analytic continuation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In complex analysis, a branch of mathematics, analytic continuation is a technique to extend the domain of definition of a given analytic function. Analytic continuation often succeeds in defining further values of a function, for example in a new region where an infinite series representation in terms of which it is initially defined becomes divergent.

Definition

Suppose f is an analytic function defined on a nonempty open set U in the complex plane ℂ. If V is a larger open set containing U, and F is an analytic function defined on V such that F(z) = f(z) for all z ∈ U, then F is called an analytic continuation of f. By the identity theorem for holomorphic functions, such a continuation is unique wherever it exists.

Applications

A classic example is the Riemann zeta function, initially defined by a series converging only for Re(s) > 1, but extendable by analytic continuation to a meromorphic function on the whole complex plane with a single pole at s = 1. Similarly, the gamma function extends the factorial beyond the values given by its defining integral. Step-by-step continuation along paths of overlapping disks gives rise to the notion of a germ and leads to multi-valued functions, such as the complex logarithm and square root, whose branching is formalised by the theory of Riemann surfaces. The monodromy theorem guarantees that continuation along paths in a simply connected domain yields a single-valued result.

Natural boundaries

Continuation is not always possible to every point: some functions possess a natural boundary — a curve dense with singularities — beyond which no analytic continuation exists. Lacunary (gap) power series, such as Σ z2n, provide standard examples, having the unit circle as a natural boundary.

History

The technique was developed in the nineteenth century, notably by Karl Weierstrass, who used power-series continuation (his "chain of disks") as a foundation for the theory of analytic functions, and by Bernhard Riemann, whose geometric approach connected continuation with multi-valued functions and their surfaces.
