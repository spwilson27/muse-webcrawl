Piast dynasty

The Piast dynasty was the first historical ruling dynasty of Poland. The first documented Polish monarch was Duke Mieszko I (c. 930–992). The Piasts ruled Poland—initially as dukes, then as kings—from its formation as a state in the 10th century until 1370, when the senior line ended with Casimir III the Great. Cadet branches, notably the Silesian Piasts, survived much longer: the last legitimate Piast, George William of Legnica, died in 1675. The dynasty takes its name from the legendary peasant-founder Piast the Wheelwright, said to be the ancestor of Mieszko's father, Siemomysł.

Origins and early rulers

The Piasts arose among the Polans of Greater Poland, centred on Gniezno. Mieszko I accepted baptism in 966, bringing Poland into Latin Christendom. His son Bolesław I the Brave expanded the realm and was crowned the first King of Poland in 1025. After a crisis in the 1030s, Casimir I the Restorer rebuilt the state; Bolesław II and Bolesław III Wrymouth followed. Bolesław III's testament of 1138 divided Poland among his sons, beginning nearly two centuries of fragmentation into Piast duchies.

Fragmentation and reunification

During the fragmentation period, Piast dukes ruled provinces including Silesia, Mazovia, Greater Poland, and Lesser Poland, competing with one another and with foreign powers. Władysław I the Elbow-high reunified much of the kingdom and was crowned in 1320. His son Casimir III the Great (r. 1333–1370) reformed law, administration, and the economy, founded the University of Kraków (1364), and doubled the kingdom's territory by annexing Red Ruthenia—fame summarised in the saying that he "found Poland built of wood and left it built of stone."

Cadet branches

The Silesian Piasts, descended from Władysław II the Exile, multiplied into lines ruling duchies such as Legnica, Wrocław, Głogów, and Cieszyn, many falling under the Bohemian crown. The Mazovian line survived until 1526, when Mazovia was incorporated into the Polish Crown. The Cieszyn and Legnica-Brzeg lines persisted under Habsburg overlordship until the 17th century.

Legacy

As Poland's founding dynasty, the Piasts occupy a central place in Polish national memory. Their eagle remains Poland's coat of arms, and rulers such as Mieszko I, Bolesław the Brave, and Casimir the Great are commemorated on Polish currency and in the names of institutions.
