Walter Bendix Schönflies Benjamin (15 July 1892 – 26 September 1940) was a German Jewish philosopher, cultural critic and essayist. An eclectic thinker, combining elements of German idealism, Romanticism, Western Marxism and Jewish mysticism, Benjamin made enduring and influential contributions to aesthetic theory, literary criticism and historical materialism.

Benjamin was closely associated with the Frankfurt School, and maintained formative friendships with, among others, Bertolt Brecht and Gershom Scholem. He was also related by marriage (through his cousin) to Hannah Arendt, who popularised his work in the United States after his death.

Life

Benjamin was born in Berlin into a prosperous assimilated Jewish family. He studied philosophy at the University of Freiburg and later in Berlin, Munich and Bern, completing his doctorate in 1919 with a dissertation on the concept of criticism in German Romanticism. His attempt at a Habilitation, The Origin of German Tragic Drama (Ursprung des deutschen Trauerspiels, 1928), was rejected by the University of Frankfurt, effectively closing an academic career to him; he thereafter worked as a freelance writer, critic and translator, translating Baudelaire and Proust into German.

After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, Benjamin fled to Paris, where he lived in poverty, supported in part by the Institute for Social Research. In September 1940, attempting to escape from France into Spain en route to the United States, he was threatened with being handed back to the Gestapo at the Spanish border town of Portbou, and took his own life with morphine on the night of 25–26 September 1940. His companions were allowed to cross the following day.

Work

Benjamin's most widely read essay is "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935), which argues that technologies of reproduction such as photography and film dissolve the "aura" of the unique artwork, with ambiguous political consequences. His posthumously published "Theses on the Philosophy of History" (1940) contains the famous image of the Angelus Novus — the angel of history blown backward into the future by the storm of progress. His unfinished magnum opus, The Arcades Project (Passagenwerk), is a vast constellation of quotations and reflections on nineteenth-century Paris, the "capital of the nineteenth century".

Other major works include the essay collections One-Way Street (1928) and Illuminations (published posthumously, 1955, edited by Arendt), the autobiographical Berlin Childhood around 1900, and essays on Baudelaire, Kafka, Proust and the storyteller Nikolai Leskov.
