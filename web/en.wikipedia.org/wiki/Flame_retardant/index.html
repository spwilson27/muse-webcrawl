Flame retardant

Flame retardants

ClassesHalogenated, organophosphorus, mineral (e.g. aluminium hydroxide), nitrogen-based, intumescent
ApplicationsElectronics, furniture foam, textiles, construction materials, transport
Mode of actionGas-phase radical quenching, char formation, endothermic decomposition, fuel dilution

Flame retardants are a diverse group of chemicals and materials-system additives that are applied to, or incorporated into, manufactured materials — such as plastics, textiles, foams, and surface finishes — to inhibit, suppress, or delay ignition and the spread of fire. The term refers to the function rather than a single chemical class: flame retardants may act in the gas phase by interrupting combustion radical reactions, in the condensed phase by promoting protective char, or physically by cooling and diluting fuel gases.

Classes and mechanisms

Halogenated flame retardants, including brominated compounds such as polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs) and tetrabromobisphenol A, release halogen radicals that quench the high-energy radicals sustaining a flame. Organophosphorus compounds act mainly by char promotion in the solid phase and by radical quenching in the gas phase. Mineral retardants such as aluminium hydroxide and magnesium hydroxide decompose endothermically, releasing water that cools the substrate and dilutes flammable gases; they are used in large loadings in cables and construction products. Intumescent systems swell on heating to form an insulating charred foam layer, protecting steel and timber structures.

Applications

Flame retardants are used to meet fire-safety standards in television and computer housings, printed circuit boards, upholstered furniture and mattresses, children's sleepwear and other textiles, building insulation and roofing, wire and cable insulation, and vehicle and aircraft interiors. Demand expanded greatly from the 1970s as synthetic polymers displaced natural materials and as flammability regulations were introduced.

Health and environmental concerns

Several brominated flame retardants have proved to be persistent, bioaccumulative, and toxic; PBDEs and hexabromocyclododecane have been listed under the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants and phased out in many jurisdictions. Additive (non-bonded) retardants can migrate out of products into household dust, a significant exposure route, especially for children. Regulatory responses have combined chemical-specific bans with reform of flammability standards — notably the revision of California's Technical Bulletin 117 in 2013, which allowed furniture to meet smoulder-resistance tests without added retardant chemicals — and research continues into greener phosphorus, silicon, and bio-based alternatives.
