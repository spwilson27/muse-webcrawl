Carangidae

The Carangidae are a family of ray-finned fishes known as the jacks, pompanos, jack mackerels, runners and trevallies. The family contains about 150 species in roughly 30 genera, distributed throughout the tropical and subtropical waters of the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific Oceans. Many are fast-swimming predators of the open sea and are of great importance to commercial and sport fisheries.

Characteristics

Carangids are typically silvery, laterally compressed fishes with deeply forked caudal fins, narrow caudal peduncles often bearing paired keels, and two dorsal fins — the first spinous and depressible into a groove, the second long and soft-rayed. The lateral line is frequently armed with enlarged scales called scutes toward the tail. Body shapes range from the deep, disc-like pompanos to the fusiform jack mackerels. Most species have small, conical teeth and feed on smaller fishes, crustaceans and squid.

Distribution and habitat

The family is marine and circumtropical. Some species are oceanic and highly migratory; others frequent reefs, bays and estuaries, and juveniles of several species shelter among jellyfish or floating debris. A few species enter brackish or even fresh water for short periods.

Important genera and species

  • Caranx — the trevallies, including the giant trevally (C. ignobilis) prized by anglers.
  • Trachurus — jack mackerels and horse mackerels, major food fishes.
  • Seriola — amberjacks and yellowtails, including the Japanese amberjack farmed for sushi.
  • Trachinotus — pompanos, esteemed table fish.
  • Naucrates ductor — the pilot fish, famous for accompanying sharks and ships.
  • Remora-like echeneids were formerly placed here but are now in their own family; the cobia and dolphinfishes are close relatives in Carangiformes.

Fisheries

Carangids support major fisheries worldwide: horse mackerels are taken in huge quantities by purse-seine and trawl fleets, while trevallies, amberjacks and pompanos command high prices fresh. The family also sustains valuable recreational fisheries. Some large predators may accumulate ciguatera toxin and should be eaten with caution in affected regions.
