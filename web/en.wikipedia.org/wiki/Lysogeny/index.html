Lysogeny

Lysogeny, or the lysogenic cycle, is one of the two cycles of viral reproduction used by temperate bacteriophages, the other being the lytic cycle. In lysogeny, the phage genome is incorporated into the host bacterium's chromosome (or maintained as a plasmid) and replicated along with it as a prophage, without destroying the host cell. The bacterium carrying a prophage is called a lysogen.

Mechanism

After infecting a host cell, a temperate phage such as lambda phage "chooses" between the lytic and lysogenic pathways, a decision governed by regulatory proteins such as the cI repressor and Cro. In the lysogenic state the repressor keeps the phage's lytic genes switched off, and the prophage is passively copied at each cell division. Under stress conditions — for example, DNA damage activating the host SOS response — the repressor is cleaved, the prophage is excised, and the phage enters the lytic cycle, producing new virions and lysing the host.

Significance

Prophages can alter the properties of their host bacteria, a phenomenon called lysogenic conversion. Several bacterial toxins, including those causing diphtheria, cholera and botulism, are encoded by prophage genes. Lysogeny is also an important mechanism of horizontal gene transfer among bacteria.
