Eurocentrism

Eurocentrism (also Eurocentricity or Western-centrism) is a worldview centred on Western civilisation or a biased view that favours it over non-Western civilisations. In scholarship the term denotes the practice — conscious or unconscious — of treating European history, values and categories as the universal standard against which other societies are measured, and of attributing modernity's origins almost entirely to Europe.

History of the concept

Although its roots lie in the colonial era, the term gained prominence in the late twentieth century through critics such as Samir Amin, whose Eurocentrism (1988) argued that the ideology presented capitalism's European emergence as the natural culmination of world history, and Edward Said, whose Orientalism (1978) examined Western representations of Asia and the Middle East. Historians have pointed to Eurocentric periodisation (the "Middle Ages" applied globally), maps that enlarge Europe, and narratives that treat non-European peoples as peoples without history.

Debates

Scholars such as Andre Gunder Frank, Kenneth Pomeranz and Jack Goody have argued that Europe's divergence from Asia came late and owed much to colonial extraction and contingencies such as coal and American land, challenging accounts of inherent European superiority. Defenders of traditional narratives respond that some features of European development — scientific institutions, legal systems, competitive states — were genuinely distinctive, and that the critique can slip into reverse caricature. The concept remains central to postcolonial studies, development theory and debates about curricula.
