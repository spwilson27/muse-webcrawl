The trolley problem is a series of thought experiments in ethics and psychology. In the classic scenario, a runaway tram (trolley) is barreling down a track toward five people who will be killed if it continues. You may pull a lever to divert it onto a side track, where it will kill one person instead. The question: is it morally permissible—or obligatory—to pull the lever?

History

The problem was introduced by philosopher Philippa Foot in 1967 and extensively analysed by Judith Jarvis Thomson, who coined the term. Most respondents judge diverting the trolley permissible, but in Thomson's "footbridge" variant—where one must push a large stranger off a bridge to stop the trolley—most judge the intervention impermissible, even though the outcome (one dies, five live) is identical.

Explanations and significance

Proposed explanations appeal to the doctrine of double effect (foreseen harm versus intended harm), the distinction between doing and allowing harm, and deontological constraints on using a person as a means. The problem has become a standard test case for ethical theories and a model scenario in debates about the programming of self-driving cars, and it has been studied experimentally across cultures in projects such as the Moral Machine.
