Big wave surfing is a discipline of surfing in which surfers paddle into, or are towed onto, waves of exceptional size — generally at least 20 feet (6.2 m) measured on the Hawaiian scale, and in the extreme cases approaching or exceeding 80–100 feet on the face. It is among the most dangerous branches of the sport, combining the risks of drowning, impact with the reef or ocean floor, and prolonged hold-downs under successive waves.

A big wave surfer on a towering ocean wave.

History

Surfers began deliberately seeking larger waves in Hawaii in the 1950s and 1960s, riding spots such as Makaha and Waimea Bay on the North Shore of Oahu. Specialized long, narrow boards called "guns" were developed for speed and control. In the 1990s, Laird Hamilton and others pioneered tow-in surfing, using personal watercraft to tow surfers onto waves too large and fast to catch by paddling, opening breaks such as Jaws (Peahi) on Maui.

Techniques and safety

Two main approaches exist: paddle-in surfing, in which the surfer catches the wave under their own power, and tow-in surfing, in which a jet ski whips the surfer into position. Modern big wave safety relies on teams of watercraft, spotters, and inflatable flotation vests that can rapidly lift a surfer to the surface. Breath-hold training and underwater "hold-down" drills are standard preparation.

Famous breaks

Nazaré in Portugal, where an underwater canyon funnels Atlantic swells toward the shore, has produced the largest waves ever ridden; Sebastian Steudtner rode a wave measured at 86 feet there in 2020, and an even larger ride was certified in 2024. Other renowned venues include Mavericks off Northern California, Jaws on Maui, Teahupo'o in Tahiti, Shipstern Bluff in Tasmania, and the remote Cortes Bank seamount off California.

Records and competition

World records for the largest wave surfed are certified by Guinness World Records based on measurements submitted by the World Surf League. Big wave competition includes events such as the Eddie Aikau Invitational at Waimea Bay, held only in years when conditions reach the required threshold, and the WSL Big Wave Awards. Fatalities, including those of Mark Foo at Mavericks in 1994 and Todd Chesser in 1997, underscore the discipline's dangers.
