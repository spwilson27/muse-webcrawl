Commonhold

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Commonhold is a system of interdependent freehold property ownership in England and Wales, introduced by the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002. It allows a building to be divided into units — typically flats — each owned outright on a freehold basis by a unit-holder, while the common parts are owned and managed by a commonhold association, a company limited by guarantee of which every unit-holder is a member.

Structure

Each commonhold development is governed by a commonhold community statement, a standard-form document setting out the rights and obligations of unit-holders, the percentage contributions each unit pays toward maintenance, and rules for meetings and dispute resolution. There is no landlord and no expiring lease: the unit-holder's title is a freehold estate, registered at the Land Registry, and can be sold or mortgaged like any other freehold.

History and uptake

Commonhold was modelled on strata title and condominium systems used in Australia, Canada, and the United States, and was intended to replace long leasehold for flats — a tenure criticised for wasting assets as terms shorten and for landlord–tenant conflicts. Uptake was extremely low: fewer than twenty commonhold developments were created in the system's first two decades, because mortgage lenders, developers, and existing leaseholders had little incentive to convert.

Reform

The Law Commission reviewed commonhold in 2020 and recommended reforms to make conversion easier and the system more attractive. Subsequent UK governments have proposed reviving commonhold as the default tenure for new flats as part of wider leasehold reform.
