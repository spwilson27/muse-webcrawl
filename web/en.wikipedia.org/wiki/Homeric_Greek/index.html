Homeric Greek

Homeric Greek is the form of the Ancient Greek language used in the Iliad and Odyssey attributed to Homer, and in the related Homeric Hymns. It is an artificial literary dialect — never exactly anyone's spoken language — based mainly on the Ionic dialect with substantial Aeolic elements, archaisms preserved by the demands of the dactylic hexameter, and forms from different periods accumulated over centuries of oral composition.

Features

Homeric Greek preserves many features lost in classical Attic: uncontracted vowels, alternative case endings (such as genitive singular in -oio and dative plurals in -essi), a wider use of the infinitive, optional augment on past-tense verbs, and traces of the lost consonant digamma (ϝ), whose absence still affects the metre. Vocabulary includes formulaic epithets ("swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered Dawn") that served the oral poet's composition in performance — the basis of the oral-formulaic theory developed by Milman Parry and Albert Lord.

Relation to other dialects

The epic dialect's mixed character reflects its history: an Aeolic phase of the tradition is thought to have preceded the Ionic phase in which the poems took their surviving form. Because Homer stood at the head of Greek literature, Homeric forms continued to influence later epic and elevated poetry for a thousand years, and learning Homeric Greek remains the traditional entry point to the epics in classical education.
