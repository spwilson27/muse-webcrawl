Constitutional crisis

A constitutional crisis is a problem or conflict in the function of a government that the political constitution or other fundamental governing law is perceived to be unable to resolve. Such crises may arise from a contradiction between constitutional provisions, from a law's ambiguity in an unprecedented situation, from a branch of government refusing to accept the authority of another, or from attempts to violate or circumvent the constitution itself.

Causes

Typical triggers include disputes over succession to executive office; refusal by an executive to enforce laws or court judgments; conflicts between a head of state and a legislature over the formation or dismissal of a government; secession of a region; disputed elections; and emergency powers being used beyond their intended scope. A constitutional crisis differs from an ordinary political controversy in that the accepted rules for resolving the dispute are themselves in question, so the normal functioning of government is impaired or threatened.

Examples

Notable constitutional crises include the secession crisis leading to the American Civil War; the 1975 Australian constitutional crisis, in which the Governor-General dismissed Prime Minister Gough Whitlam after the Senate blocked supply; the Watergate scandal in the United States, which tested executive privilege and ended with President Nixon's resignation in 1974; and repeated crises over government formation in countries with reserve powers held by a monarch or president. In monarchies, crises have also arisen over royal assent and abdication, such as the British abdication crisis of 1936.

Resolution

Constitutional crises are resolved through some combination of judicial interpretation, negotiated political settlement, constitutional amendment, fresh elections, or — in the worst cases — force. Their outcomes often set durable precedents that reshape the constitutional order they threatened.
