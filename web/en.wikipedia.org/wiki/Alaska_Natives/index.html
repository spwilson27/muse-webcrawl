Alaska Natives (also known as Native Alaskans, Alaskan Indians, or Indigenous Alaskans) are the Indigenous peoples of Alaska, comprising a diverse range of cultures and language groups. They include the Iñupiat, Yup'ik, Cup'ik, Aleut (Unangax̂), Eyak, Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, and numerous Athabascan peoples of the interior.

Groups

Alaska Native cultures are commonly grouped by region. In the far north and northwest are the Iñupiat; in the southwest, the Yup'ik and Cup'ik; along the Aleutian chain, the Unangax̂ (Aleut); in the interior, the Athabascan peoples; and in the southeast panhandle, the Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian. Each group has its own language, traditions and subsistence practices, historically centred on hunting, fishing and gathering, including whaling among the Iñupiat and salmon fishing throughout much of the state.

History

Indigenous peoples have inhabited Alaska for more than 12,000 years. Russian colonisation beginning in the eighteenth century brought profound disruption, particularly to the Aleut and Alutiiq peoples. Alaska was purchased by the United States in 1867. For much of the following century, Alaska Natives lacked clear legal title to their ancestral lands.

The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA) of 1971 settled aboriginal land claims, conveying nearly 44 million acres of land and nearly one billion dollars to newly created Alaska Native regional and village corporations. It remains the largest Indigenous land claims settlement in United States history. The Alaska Native corporation system continues to play a major role in the state's economy and in Native self-governance.

Languages and culture

Alaska is home to at least twenty distinct Indigenous languages, many of them endangered. Revitalisation efforts, immersion schools and Native-language programmes have grown in recent decades. Subsistence hunting and fishing remain central to rural Alaska Native life and identity, and are protected in part under federal law.

Contemporary issues

Alaska Natives face ongoing challenges including the impacts of climate change on coastal villages threatened by erosion and permafrost thaw, rural infrastructure needs, and debates over subsistence rights, resource development and tribal sovereignty.
