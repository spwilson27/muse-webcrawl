Sod

Sod, also known as turf, is a layer of grass and the part of the soil beneath it held together by its roots. It is harvested and sold in rolls or slabs to establish lawns quickly — "instant lawns" — on sports pitches, golf courses, gardens and landscaping projects, where a mature grass surface is needed faster than seed can provide. Sod is also used to repair worn areas of pitches and to stabilise soil against erosion.

Production

Sod farms grow selected turfgrass cultivars on prepared fields for roughly ten to eighteen months, with regular mowing, fertilisation and irrigation. At harvest, specialised machines cut strips of grass with a uniform thin layer of soil and roots — typically about 1–2 cm of soil — which are rolled or stacked for transport. Because sod is perishable once cut, it is usually laid within a day or two of harvest.

Installation and care

Sod is laid on levelled, cleared soil, with strips staggered like brickwork and edges butted tightly to avoid gaps and drying. Fresh sod must be kept moist and is usually watered deeply for the first two weeks while roots knit into the soil beneath ("tacking down"); a lawn that can no longer be lifted by a gentle tug has established. Compared with seeding, sod gives immediate cover and can be used within weeks, at higher cost per unit area.

Other uses and meanings

Historically, blocks of turf (sods) were used as building material — for roofs and walls in Iceland, Scandinavia and on the North American prairies, where settlers built "sod houses" (soddies) for want of timber. In Ireland and Scotland, "sods" of peat cut for fuel are more commonly called turf, a usage distinct from lawn turf. Sod is also laid to repair erosion on embankments and to stabilise bare soil on construction sites.
