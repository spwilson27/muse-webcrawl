Congressional Review Act

The Congressional Review Act (CRA) is a 1996 United States law that gives Congress a procedure to overturn federal agency regulations by joint resolution. It was enacted as part of the Contract with America Advancement Act of 1996.

CRA
EnactedMarch 29, 1996
Codified5 U.S.C. §§ 801–808

Procedure

Agencies must submit rules to Congress and the GAO before they take effect. Congress then has 60 legislative/session days to introduce a joint resolution of disapproval, which benefits from expedited, filibuster-proof Senate procedures. If both chambers pass it and the President signs it (or a veto is overridden), the rule is void and the agency may not reissue a rule that is "substantially the same" without new legislation.

Use

Only one rule — OSHA's ergonomics standard — was overturned in the law's first two decades, in 2001. In 2017, the Republican Congress and President Donald Trump used the CRA to repeal 14 late Obama-era rules; further uses followed in later administrations, typically in the window after a change of party control.

Criticism and debate

Supporters see the CRA as restoring legislative accountability over the administrative state; critics argue the "substantially the same" bar creates regulatory uncertainty and that expedited repeal bypasses deliberation and scientific review.
