The Q source (also known as Q document, or simply Q, from the German Quelle, "source") is a hypothetical written collection of Jesus's sayings that is believed to form part of the common material found in the Gospel of Matthew and the Gospel of Luke but absent from the Gospel of Mark. Q is a central component of the two-source hypothesis, the most widely accepted solution to the Synoptic problem: Mark was written first and used by both Matthew and Luke, who independently supplemented it with sayings from Q and with material unique to each (the "M" and "L" sources).

Content and reconstruction

Because no copy of Q has ever been found, its contents are reconstructed by comparing the material Matthew and Luke share — roughly 200 verses, mostly teachings: the Sermon on the Mount/Sermon on the Plain, the Lord's Prayer, the Beatitudes, John the Baptist's preaching, the temptation sayings and many parables. Q appears to have had little narrative framework and, strikingly, no account of Jesus's death and resurrection — leading scholars to characterise its community as a "sayings gospel" movement, comparable in genre to the Gospel of Thomas discovered at Nag Hammadi in 1945. The International Q Project published a critical edition, The Critical Edition of Q (2000), attempting to fix the text and wording verse by verse.

History of the hypothesis

The idea developed in 19th-century German scholarship: Schleiermacher noted Papias's reference to a sayings collection by Matthew, and in 1838 Christian Hermann Weisse proposed that Matthew and Luke used both Mark and a sayings source. Heinrich Holtzmann formalised the two-source theory in 1863, and by the early 20th century the siglum "Q" was standard.

Criticism

Q's existence is disputed by proponents of rival solutions to the Synoptic problem — notably the Farrer hypothesis (Mark first; Matthew used Mark; Luke used both), defended by scholars such as Mark Goodacre, which dispenses with Q as an unnecessary hypothesis. Others argue that some "Q" material may have circulated orally, or that Luke's use of Matthew (or vice versa) explains the agreements. Nevertheless, Q remains the majority position in New Testament scholarship.
