Latin Empire

The Latin Empire, also known as the Latin Empire of Constantinople and officially the Empire of Romania (Latin: Imperium Romaniae), was a feudal crusader state founded by the leaders of the Fourth Crusade after the sack of Constantinople in 1204. Established on lands taken from the Byzantine Empire, it lasted from 1204 until 1261, when the Byzantines of the Empire of Nicaea retook Constantinople. The Latin Empire was intended to replace the Byzantine Empire as the recognised Roman Empire in the east, with a Catholic emperor in place of the Orthodox one, but it never controlled the whole of the Byzantine territory and struggled throughout its existence with hostile Greek successor states and the Bulgarian Empire.

Establishment

In March 1204 the crusader and Venetian leaders agreed the Partitio Romaniae, dividing the Byzantine Empire among themselves. After the sack of the city on 13 April 1204, Count Baldwin of Flanders was elected emperor and crowned on 16 May in Hagia Sophia. Venice received three-eighths of the former empire, including a string of ports and islands; crusader barons received fiefs — the Kingdom of Thessalonica under Boniface of Montferrat, and, arising somewhat later, the Duchy of Athens, the Principality of Achaea and the Duchy of the Archipelago.

History

The empire was fragile from the start. Emperor Baldwin I was captured by the Bulgarians at the Battle of Adrianople in 1205 and died in captivity. His brother Henry of Flanders (r. 1206–1216) was an able ruler who reconciled many Greeks and checked Bulgarian power, but after his death the empire entered decline. Three Greek successor states — the Empire of Nicaea in Anatolia, the Empire of Trebizond, and the Despotate of Epirus — competed to recover Constantinople. Thessalonica fell to Epirus in 1224; Nicaea under John III Vatatzes steadily squeezed the Latin territories. Under Baldwin II the empire shrank to Constantinople itself, kept alive by western subsidies — Baldwin pawned the Crown of Thorns to Venetian bankers (later redeemed by Louis IX of France) and stripped lead from palace roofs for coin. On 25 July 1261 a small Nicaean force under Alexios Strategopoulos slipped into the nearly undefended city, and Michael VIII Palaiologos was crowned, restoring the Byzantine Empire.

Aftermath

Baldwin II and his heirs retained the imperial title in exile and continued to grant away (already lost) lands. Several Latin states in Greece survived much longer: the Principality of Achaea and the Duchy of Athens lasted into the 15th century, and Venetian and Genoese island possessions endured until the Ottoman conquests — some until the 18th century. The Latin occupation left a lasting legacy of Greek–western mistrust that still colours relations between the Orthodox and Catholic churches.
