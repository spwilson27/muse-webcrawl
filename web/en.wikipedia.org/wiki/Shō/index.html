Shō

The shō (笙) is a Japanese free-reed mouth organ used as a standard instrument in gagaku, the ancient court music of Japan. It consists of 17 slender bamboo pipes of graduated length fitted into a lacquered wooden wind chamber shaped like a resting phoenix, from which the instrument also takes the poetic name hō (鳳, "phoenix").

Description

Fifteen of the seventeen pipes bear small metal free reeds; two pipes are silent and symmetrical, included for visual balance. The player blows into a mouthpiece while covering finger holes, and in gagaku the shō typically sounds clusters of tones called aitake — chords of five or six notes that shift slowly as the melody progresses, giving the music its characteristic ethereal texture, often likened to light shining through the heavens. To keep moisture from stopping the reeds, the instrument is warmed over a charcoal brazier before and during performance.

History

The shō derives from the Chinese sheng, introduced to Japan by the 8th century; exquisitely preserved examples survive in the Shōsō-in treasure house at Nara. In the 20th century composers such as Tōru Takemitsu and, outside Japan, John Cage wrote new works for the instrument, and the virtuoso Mayumi Miyata helped establish it as a contemporary solo instrument.
