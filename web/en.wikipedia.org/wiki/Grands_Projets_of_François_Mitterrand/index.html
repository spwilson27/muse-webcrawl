The Grands Projets of François Mitterrand (also known as the Grands Travaux) were a series of monumental architectural projects undertaken in Paris during the presidency of François Mitterrand, who served as President of France from 1981 to 1995. Conceived as a modern equivalent of the works of Haussmann under Napoleon III, the projects were intended to leave a lasting cultural legacy, celebrate the bicentenary of the French Revolution in 1989, and reassert Paris as a world capital of culture.

Background

Mitterrand, the first Socialist president of the Fifth Republic, took a personal interest in architecture and urbanism. Shortly after his election in 1981 he launched a programme of public works, several of which had been initiated or contemplated by his predecessors. Many were organised through open international design competitions, in keeping with a tradition established by Georges Pompidou's Centre Pompidou in the 1970s.

The projects

Musée d'Orsay (1986)

The conversion of the Gare d'Orsay, a Beaux-Arts railway station on the Left Bank, into a museum of nineteenth-century art. The interior was redesigned by the Italian architect Gae Aulenti, and the museum opened in December 1986.

Institut du Monde Arabe (1987)

Designed by Jean Nouvel, the Arab World Institute on the banks of the Seine is noted for its south façade of photosensitive apertures ("moucharabiehs") that open and close with the light, a modern reinterpretation of traditional Arabic latticework.

Louvre Pyramid (1989)

The most controversial of the projects, the glass Louvre Pyramid designed by I. M. Pei serves as the main entrance to the Louvre Museum in the Cour Napoléon. Inaugurated in 1989, it formed part of the "Grand Louvre" programme, which also removed the Ministry of Finance from the Richelieu wing and created the underground Carrousel du Louvre.

Grande Arche de la Défense (1989)

A hollow cube of white marble, 110 metres high, designed by the Danish architect Johann Otto von Spreckelsen, completing the historical axis of Paris from the Louvre through the Arc de Triomphe to the business district of La Défense. It was inaugurated in July 1989 for the bicentenary and the G7 summit.

Opéra Bastille (1989)

A new opera house on the Place de la Bastille, designed by the Uruguayan-Canadian architect Carlos Ott, intended as a "popular" counterpart to the Palais Garnier. It opened on 13 July 1989, the eve of the bicentenary.

Bercy and the Ministry of Finance (1989)

The new Ministry of the Economy and Finance at Bercy, designed by Paul Chemetov, vacated the Louvre's Richelieu wing. The nearby Palais Omnisports de Paris-Bercy (1984) was an early work of the period.

Parc de la Villette and Cité des Sciences (1986)

The redevelopment of the former slaughterhouse district of La Villette in north-east Paris included the Cité des Sciences et de l'Industrie — Europe's largest science museum, incorporating the Géode IMAX theatre — and Bernard Tschumi's deconstructivist Parc de la Villette with its red "folies".

Bibliothèque nationale de France (1996)

The last and largest of the projects, the new national library in Tolbiac (Paris Rive Gauche), designed by Dominique Perrault, consists of four 79-metre towers shaped like open books around a sunken garden. It opened in December 1996, after Mitterrand left office, and bears his name.

Criticism and legacy

The projects were criticised for their cost, their monumental character — opponents dubbed Mitterrand "Tontonkhamon" (a play on Tutankhamun and "Tonton", his nickname) — and for the president's personal role in choosing designs. Nevertheless, they transformed large areas of eastern Paris, and several, notably the Louvre Pyramid and the Grande Arche, have become landmarks. The programme is often compared with the building campaigns of earlier French rulers and is credited with reviving the tradition of state patronage of contemporary architecture in France.
