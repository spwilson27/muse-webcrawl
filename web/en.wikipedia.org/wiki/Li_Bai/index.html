Li Bai (Chinese: 李白; pinyin: Lǐ Bái; 701–762), courtesy name Taibai (太白), art name Qinglian Jushi (青蓮居士, "Lotus Householder"), was a Chinese poet acclaimed as one of the greatest and most important poets of the Tang dynasty and indeed of Chinese poetry generally. He is celebrated as a "poet immortal" (詩仙), and together with Du Fu is regarded as one of the two greatest Chinese poets. Around 1,000 of his poems survive.

Life

Li Bai's birthplace is uncertain; his family may have originated in Jiangyou, Sichuan, though some scholars argue he was born in Suyab in Central Asia, where his ancestors had been exiled. He spent his youth in Sichuan, left at about 25, and wandered widely through China — up the Yangtze, through the lake country and to the great cities — drinking, writing poetry and seeking patrons. In 742 he was summoned to the court of Emperor Xuanzong at Chang'an and appointed to the Hanlin Academy, but court intrigues forced his departure within two years; he famously is said to have made the powerful eunuch Gao Lishi remove his boots.

He resumed his wanderings, meeting Du Fu in 744 — the start of one of the most celebrated friendships in literature. During the An Lushan Rebellion he became involved with the Prince of Yong's rival court, was imprisoned for treason in 757 and exiled to Yelang (in modern Guizhou), though he was pardoned en route. He died in 762 at Dangtu, in the home of his relative Li Yangbing. Legend holds that he drowned trying to embrace the moon's reflection in the Yangtze River while drunk in a boat.

Poetry

Li Bai's verse is marked by extravagant imagination, Taoist imagery, romantic celebration of wine and nature, and mastery of both old-style (gushi) and regulated verse. His most famous poem, "Quiet Night Thoughts" (靜夜思), is known by virtually every Chinese schoolchild: "Before my bed, the bright moonlight…" Other celebrated works include "The Difficulty of the Shu Road", "Liangfu Yin" and "Bring in the Wine" (將進酒). Ezra Pound's versions in Cathay (1915), based on Ernest Fenollosa's notes, introduced Li Bai (as "Rihaku") to English-language readers and influenced modernist poetry.
