Kinematic chain

A kinematic chain is an assembly of rigid bodies connected by joints that provides a mathematically modelled system, used in engineering and robotics to describe the motion of mechanisms. Each body is called a link and each joint constrains the relative motion between links. The concept was formalised in the 19th century by Franz Reuleaux.

Degrees of freedom

The mobility, or number of degrees of freedom, of a kinematic chain can be computed using criteria such as the Grübler–Kutzbach criterion, which relates the number of links, the number of joints and the freedoms allowed by each joint. A planar four-bar linkage, for example, has one degree of freedom.

Open and closed chains

In an open kinematic chain, such as a serial robot arm, the links form a sequence in which each joint position affects those beyond it. In a closed chain, the links form one or more loops, as in a four-bar linkage or a parallel manipulator such as the Stewart platform. Robot arms are typically modelled as open chains, and the study of their position and orientation is divided into forward kinematics and inverse kinematics.

Applications

Kinematic chains underpin the analysis of machinery, vehicle suspensions, biomechanics of human and animal limbs, and computer animation skeletons. Denavit–Hartenberg parameters provide a standard convention for assigning coordinate frames to the links of a chain.
