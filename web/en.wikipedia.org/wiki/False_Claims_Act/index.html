False Claims Act

The False Claims Act (FCA), 31 U.S.C. §§ 3729–3733, is an American federal law that imposes liability on persons and companies (typically federal contractors) who defraud governmental programs. Enacted in 1863, it is sometimes called the Lincoln Law because it was signed by President Abraham Lincoln to combat fraud by suppliers of the Union Army during the Civil War — including contractors who sold lame horses, faulty rifles and rancid rations.

Qui tam provisions

The FCA's distinctive feature is its qui tam provision, which allows private persons (relators — in practice, usually whistleblowers such as employees) to file suit on behalf of the government. The suit is filed under seal while the Department of Justice decides whether to intervene; if the case succeeds, the relator receives a share (generally 15–30%) of treble damages and civil penalties recovered. The government may also settle or dismiss a qui tam action over the relator's objection, a power upheld by the Supreme Court in United States ex rel. Polansky v. Executive Health Resources (2023).

Scope and impact

Liability requires knowingly presenting a false or fraudulent claim for payment, or making a false record material to such a claim; the Supreme Court's decision in Universal Health Services v. Escobar (2016) endorsed the "implied false certification" theory under a demanding materiality standard. Strengthened by amendments in 1986, the FCA has become the federal government's primary civil anti-fraud tool, recovering tens of billions of dollars, mostly in healthcare and defence procurement cases. Many U.S. states have parallel false claims statutes.
