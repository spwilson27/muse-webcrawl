Fartlek

Fartlek, which means "speed play" in Swedish, is a running training method that blends continuous training with interval training. Developed in Sweden in the 1930s by coach Gösta Holmér, fartlek involves varying one's pace throughout a run, alternating between fast segments and slow recovery jogs, traditionally according to the terrain and the runner's impulse rather than a stopwatch or fixed structure.

History

Holmér, coach of the Swedish cross-country team, devised fartlek around 1937 after Sweden's poor results against Finland, seeking a workout that combined speed and endurance. Training outdoors over natural terrain, athletes surged up hills, coasted down, and sprinted between landmarks. The method helped Swedish runners such as Gunder Hägg and Arne Andersson to world records in the 1940s and influenced interval and tempo training worldwide.

Practice

A typical fartlek session begins with an easy warm-up jog, followed by repeated cycles of hard effort — for example a sprint to a distant tree or lamppost — and easy running until partially recovered, finishing with a cool-down. Unlike formal interval training, distances, repetitions and rest are unstructured and governed by feel, which devotees praise for developing pacing judgement and reducing monotony. Modern structured variants ("Swedish fartlek", "Polish fartlek") impose timed blocks while retaining the emphasis on varied pace.

Benefits

Fartlek stresses both aerobic and anaerobic energy systems, improves speed endurance and mental adaptability to race surges, and can be performed anywhere without a track. It remains a staple of distance-running training from school cross-country to elite marathon preparation.
