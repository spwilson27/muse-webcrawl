Kings' sagas

The kings' sagas preserve much of what is known of early Scandinavian history.

Kings' sagas (Old Norse: konungasögur) are Old Norse sagas which principally tell of the lives of the early kings of Norway and Denmark. They were composed in Iceland and Norway between the 12th and 14th centuries, drawing on earlier histories, skaldic poetry, and oral tradition.

Principal works

The best-known collection is Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla (c. 1230), which narrates the history of the Norwegian kings from the mythical Yngling dynasty down to 1177. Other important kings' sagas include Morkinskinna, Fagrskinna, the Ágrip af Nóregskonungasögum, the separate sagas of Óláfr Tryggvason and St Óláfr, and Sverris saga, which tells of King Sverre of Norway. Danish material is represented by works such as Knýtlinga saga.

Historical value

Although written centuries after many of the events they describe, the kings' sagas are a principal source for Scandinavian history before the 13th century. The skaldic verses embedded in them, composed by court poets close to the events, are considered especially reliable evidence.
