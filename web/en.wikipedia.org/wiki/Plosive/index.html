Plosive

In phonetics, a plosive, also known as a stop, is a consonant sound produced by completely blocking the flow of air in the vocal tract and then releasing it, often with a burst. During the closure, air pressure builds up behind the obstruction; when the closure is released, the air escapes in a small explosion of sound.

Types

The six plosives most common in the world's languages are the voiceless stops [p], [t], and [k] and their voiced counterparts [b], [d], and [ɡ], articulated at the bilabial, alveolar (or dental), and velar places of articulation respectively. Other places yield the palatal [c ɟ], uvular [q ɢ], and glottal [ʔ] stops. Plosives may be aspirated, as in the initial sound of English pin [pʰ], or unaspirated, as in spin [p]. In some positions English plosives are unreleased or pre-glottalised.

Terminology

"Stop" refers to the oral closure phase and is the broader term — nasal consonants like [m] are also oral stops (but with nasal airflow). "Plosive" properly describes an oral stop with a pulmonic release burst; the two terms are often used interchangeably in practice.
