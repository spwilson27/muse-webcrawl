Product differentiation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FieldEconomics, marketing
Key theoristsEdward Chamberlin, Harold Hotelling, Michael Porter

In economics and marketing, product differentiation (or simply differentiation) is the process of distinguishing a product or service from others to make it more attractive to a particular target market. This involves differentiating it from competitors' products as well as from a firm's own other products. The concept was proposed by Edward Chamberlin in his 1933 Theory of Monopolistic Competition.

Types

Horizontal differentiation occurs when consumers have different preferences and products differ in characteristics that are not objectively better or worse (e.g. flavours or colours). Vertical differentiation occurs when products differ in quality and consumers, given equal prices, would agree on the ranking. In practice most differentiation mixes both: products differ in physical attributes, availability and location, accompanying services, and subjective image created by branding and advertising.

Effects

Differentiation gives firms a degree of market power: because buyers perceive products as imperfect substitutes, a firm can raise its price above marginal cost without losing all sales. It is one of Michael Porter's generic competitive strategies, contrasted with cost leadership and focus. Successful differentiation can build brand loyalty and reduce price competition, though it also raises costs and can be imitated by rivals. Hotelling's law models spatial differentiation, predicting that competing sellers may cluster together ("minimum differentiation") under some conditions.
