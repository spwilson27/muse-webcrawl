Guide dog

Guide dogs (colloquially known in the US as seeing-eye dogs) are assistance dogs trained to lead blind or visually impaired people around obstacles. Although dogs can be trained to navigate various obstacles, they cannot comprehend colours such as red and green on traffic lights; instead, the handler judges when it is safe to cross by listening to traffic. The handler directs the dog using commands and knowledge of the route, while the dog is trained in "intelligent disobedience" — refusing a command that would lead the pair into danger, such as stepping in front of a moving vehicle.

History

References to service animals date back centuries, but systematic guide-dog training began in Germany during and after the First World War, to assist soldiers blinded in combat; the first school was opened by Gerhard Stalling in Oldenburg in 1916. In the United States, Dorothy Harrison Eustis founded The Seeing Eye in 1929 in Morristown, New Jersey — the source of the American term "seeing eye dog". In Britain, the Guide Dogs for the Blind Association was founded in 1934. Guide dog organisations are now coordinated internationally by the International Guide Dog Federation (founded 1989).

Breeds and training

The most common breeds are Labrador Retrievers, Golden Retrievers and crosses of the two, valued for their temperament, intelligence and trainability; German Shepherds and Standard Poodles are also used. Puppies are typically raised by volunteer puppy-raisers for their first year, then undergo several months of formal training at a guide dog school before being matched with a handler, with whom they train together. A guide dog's working life is usually about 8–10 years, after which it retires, often remaining with the handler as a pet.

Access rights

In many countries, guide dogs are legally permitted to accompany their handlers into public places, transport and businesses that would otherwise exclude animals — for example under the Americans with Disabilities Act in the United States and the Equality Act 2010 in the United Kingdom. Handlers ask that the public not pet or distract a dog wearing a harness, as it is working.
