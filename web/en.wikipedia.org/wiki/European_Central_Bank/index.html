European Central Bank

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The European Central Bank (ECB) is the central bank for the euro, administering monetary policy for the eurozone's member states. Established by the Treaty of Amsterdam in 1998 and headquartered in Frankfurt, it forms, together with the national central banks of all EU member states, the European System of Central Banks; the Governing Council (Executive Board plus eurozone governors) sets monetary policy, with price stability as its primary objective under the Treaties.

Role and crises

Modelled partly on the Bundesbank's stability culture and treaty-guaranteed independence, the ECB was designed for a monetary union without fiscal union — the asymmetry the euro-area crisis (2010–12) exposed, when President Mario Draghi's "whatever it takes" pledge (July 2012) and the (never-used) Outright Monetary Transactions programme calmed sovereign-bond markets, and later asset-purchase programmes (from 2015) imported quantitative easing over internal dissent. The ECB also became the eurozone's banking supervisor (Single Supervisory Mechanism, 2014). German constitutional challenges to its bond-buying (the 2020 Weiss judgment of the Federal Constitutional Court) dramatise the tension between the ECB's expanding crisis role and its narrow treaty mandate — the defining institutional drama of European integration in the 21st century.
