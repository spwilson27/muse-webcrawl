Algebraic variety

Algebraic varieties are the central objects of study in algebraic geometry, a sub-field of mathematics. Classically, an algebraic variety is defined as the set of solutions of a system of polynomial equations over the real or complex numbers. Modern definitions generalise this notion, making it intrinsic and applicable over arbitrary fields.

Affine varieties

An affine variety is the set of common zeros, in affine space over an algebraically closed field k, of a collection of polynomials. By Hilbert's Nullstellensatz there is a correspondence between affine varieties and radical ideals of the polynomial ring, which makes the coordinate ring of a variety a central algebraic invariant. Examples include the twisted cubic in three-space and the cone defined by x² + y² − z² = 0.

Projective varieties

A projective variety is a subset of projective space defined by homogeneous polynomial equations. Projective varieties are "complete" in a geometric sense, which makes their theory cleaner: for instance, Bézout's theorem counts intersections exactly in projective space. The Grassmannian and flag varieties are important examples.

Morphisms and dimension

Maps between varieties given by polynomials (or rational functions, for rational maps) are called morphisms. The dimension of a variety is the dimension of its coordinate ring as a Krull dimension; curves are one-dimensional varieties, surfaces two-dimensional, and so on. A variety is smooth at a point if the Jacobian criterion holds there; singular points are a major focus of resolution-of-singularities theory.

Abstract varieties and schemes

In the mid-twentieth century, André Weil, Oscar Zariski and Alexander Grothendieck rebuilt the foundations: varieties became abstract spaces glued from affine pieces, equipped with the Zariski topology, and Grothendieck's more general notion of a scheme extended the theory further. Varieties remain the geometric heart of the subject, with applications from number theory — elliptic curves are smooth projective curves of genus one — to string theory.
