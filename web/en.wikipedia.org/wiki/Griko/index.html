Griko

Griko, sometimes spelled Grico, is the dialect of the Greek language spoken by the Griko people in the Bovesia region of Calabria and in the Grecìa Salentina area of Apulia, in southern Italy. Together these enclaves in Magna Graecia preserve the last remnants of a once much wider Greek-speaking territory in the Italian peninsula.

Origins

Scholars debate whether Griko descends chiefly from the Doric Greek of ancient Magna Graecia colonists, reinforced during the Byzantine period (6th–11th centuries), or mainly from Byzantine Greek of the medieval era. Its phonology and vocabulary show heavy influence from the neighbouring Romance varieties — Salentino and Calabrian — as well as Italian.

Present status

Griko is severely endangered. UNESCO classifies it as severely endangered; fluent speakers are now mostly elderly, and estimates put the number who can speak it at under 20,000, with many more having passive knowledge. The Italian state formally recognizes the Griko communities under Law 482 of 1999 on historical linguistic minorities. Cultural associations, festivals (such as La Notte della Taranta, which features Griko songs), and local teaching programmes attempt to sustain it.

Sample

Griko retains words of great antiquity alongside Romance loans. Its folk music tradition, including laments and dance songs accompanied by tamburello, is one of the most visible expressions of Griko identity in Salento.
