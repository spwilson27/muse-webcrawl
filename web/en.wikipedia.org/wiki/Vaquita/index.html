Vaquita

The vaquita (Phocoena sinus) is a species of porpoise endemic to the northern end of the Gulf of California (Sea of Cortez) in Mexico. Averaging about 150 cm (4.9 ft) in length for females, it is the smallest living cetacean. It is also the most endangered: its population, estimated at about 600 in 1997 and fewer than 20 by the late 2010s, has been driven to the brink of extinction by incidental capture in gillnets, above all nets set illegally for the totoaba fish, whose swim bladders are trafficked for the Chinese market.

Biology

The vaquita has a dark ring around each eye, dark lips, and a tall, slightly falcate dorsal fin relative to its size. It lives in shallow, turbid lagoons and coastal waters, feeding on fish, squid and crustaceans, and using echolocation to navigate. Females calve about every other year, generally in spring.

Conservation

Mexico has banned gillnet fishing in the vaquita's habitat and established a refuge area, with enforcement support from the Mexican Navy and conservation groups that remove illegal nets. A 2017 attempt to bring vaquitas into temporary human care (VaquitaCPR) was abandoned after a captured animal died. Acoustic monitoring surveys in the 2020s detected roughly a dozen individuals, including calves, indicating the species persists but remains critically endangered; its survival depends on eliminating gillnet mortality entirely.
