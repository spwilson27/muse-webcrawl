Vicksburg campaign

DateDecember 29, 1862 – July 4, 1863
LocationMississippi and Louisiana
ResultUnion victory
Union commanderUlysses S. Grant
Confederate commanderJohn C. Pemberton

The Vicksburg campaign was a series of maneuvers and battles in the Western Theater of the American Civil War directed against Vicksburg, Mississippi, a fortress city that dominated the last Confederate-controlled section of the Mississippi River. The Union victory there and at Port Hudson in Louisiana gave the Union complete control of the river and split the Confederacy in two.

The campaign is widely studied as one of the masterpieces of military maneuver in American history. Major General Ulysses S. Grant's Army of the Tennessee, after a frustrating winter of failed bayou expeditions north of the city, crossed the Mississippi below Vicksburg in late April 1863 and conducted a daring campaign through the interior of Mississippi.

The final offensive

After running the Vicksburg batteries with transports protected by Admiral David Dixon Porter's gunboats, Grant crossed at Bruinsburg on April 30, 1863. Rather than secure a long supply line back to the river, Grant cut loose and lived substantially off the land, striking inland. In a span of about three weeks his army fought and won five battles:

  • Port Gibson (May 1)
  • Raymond (May 12)
  • Jackson (May 14) — the state capital was taken, separating Pemberton from General Joseph E. Johnston's relief force
  • Champion Hill (May 16) — the decisive battle of the campaign
  • Big Black River Bridge (May 17)

Grant then besieged Vicksburg. Two assaults (May 19 and May 22) were repulsed with heavy loss, after which Grant settled into a formal siege. Confederate supplies dwindled, civilian morale collapsed under constant shelling, and on July 4, 1863, Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton surrendered the city and some 29,000 men. The surrender, combined with Lee's defeat at Gettysburg on July 3, marked the turning point of the war. President Lincoln later said, "The Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea."

Significance

The campaign cemented Grant's reputation and led to his appointment as general-in-chief of all Union armies in 1864. Control of the Mississippi isolated Texas, Arkansas, and Louisiana from the rest of the Confederacy, cutting off a vital source of men, food, and supplies.
