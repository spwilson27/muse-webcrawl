Guillain–Barré syndrome

SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsMuscle weakness beginning in the legs, ascending paralysis
Usual onsetDays to weeks after infection
CausesAutoimmune reaction, often post-infectious

Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is a rapid-onset muscle weakness caused by the immune system damaging the peripheral nervous system. It is named after French neurologists Georges Guillain and Jean Alexandre Barré, who described it in 1916.

Signs and symptoms

The syndrome typically begins with weakness and tingling in the feet and legs that spreads upward to the arms and upper body. In severe cases the paralysis can affect the respiratory muscles, requiring mechanical ventilation. Autonomic dysfunction, including dangerous fluctuations in blood pressure and heart rhythm, is common. Deep tendon reflexes are reduced or absent.

Causes

GBS is an autoimmune disorder in which antibodies cross-react with gangliosides on peripheral nerves, often triggered by a preceding infection. The most common antecedent is Campylobacter jejuni gastroenteritis; other triggers include influenza, cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr virus, and Zika virus.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on clinical features, cerebrospinal fluid showing raised protein with a normal white cell count (albuminocytological dissociation), and nerve conduction studies. There is no cure, but intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange speed recovery. With modern intensive care, mortality is around 3–7%, and most patients recover substantially, though recovery may take months to years.
