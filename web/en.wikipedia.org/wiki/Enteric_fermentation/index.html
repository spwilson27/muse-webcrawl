Enteric fermentation

Enteric fermentation
Process typeDigestive (microbial fermentation)
Main productMethane (CH₄), volatile fatty acids
Principal hostsRuminants: cattle, sheep, goats, buffalo
Climate relevanceMajor anthropogenic methane source

Enteric fermentation is a digestive process by which microorganisms in the gut of an animal break down and ferment carbohydrates and other feed components, producing volatile fatty acids the animal can absorb, along with hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and methane. The methane is expelled mainly by eructation (belching). The process is most developed in ruminants — cattle, sheep, goats, buffalo, and deer — whose forestomach (the rumen) houses a dense microbial community.

Process

In the rumen, bacteria, protozoa, and fungi ferment cellulose and other plant material that the animal's own enzymes cannot digest. Excess hydrogen produced by fermentation is consumed by methanogenic archaea, which convert it to methane. A dairy cow may produce several hundred litres of methane per day. Smaller amounts of enteric methane also come from non-ruminant livestock such as pigs and horses, whose fermentation occurs in the hindgut.

Emissions and mitigation

Enteric fermentation is one of the largest anthropogenic sources of methane, a potent greenhouse gas, and is a major share of the emissions attributed to agriculture and livestock production globally. Proposed mitigation strategies include improved feed quality and precision feeding, feed additives such as 3-nitrooxypropanol (3-NOP) and red seaweed (Asparagopsis) that inhibit methanogenesis, selective breeding for lower-emitting animals, and vaccines against rumen methanogens.
