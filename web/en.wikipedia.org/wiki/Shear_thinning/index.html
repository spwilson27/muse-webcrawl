Shear thinning

Shear thinning is the non-Newtonian behaviour in which a fluid's apparent viscosity decreases under increasing shear strain. Such fluids are also called pseudoplastic and are described approximately by a power-law relation in which the flow behaviour index is less than one. The effect is usually reversible: viscosity recovers when the shearing stops, though some materials additionally show time-dependent thixotropy.

Mechanism and examples

In polymer solutions and melts, shear aligns and disentangles long molecules, reducing resistance to flow. In suspensions, particles that are randomly oriented at rest orient themselves with the flow. Everyday examples include ketchup, which resists pouring until the bottle is shaken or squeezed; blood, whose viscosity falls in small vessels; paint, formulated to flow under the brush but stay on the wall; and many cosmetics, inks and drilling muds.

Applications

Shear thinning is exploited in injection moulding and extrusion, where material flows easily under the high shear in a nozzle yet holds its shape afterwards; in lubricants; in food processing; and in biomedical engineering, where hydrogels that shear thin can be injected through a needle and then re-solidify in tissue. It contrasts with shear thickening (dilatancy), shown by concentrated cornstarch suspensions.
