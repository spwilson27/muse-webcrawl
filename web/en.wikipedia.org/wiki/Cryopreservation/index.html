Cryopreservation or cryoconservation is a process where biological material — cells, tissues, or whole organs — is preserved by cooling to very low sub-zero temperatures, typically −80 °C using solid carbon dioxide or −196 °C using liquid nitrogen. At these low temperatures, enzymatic and chemical activity that might cause damage is effectively stopped, allowing material to be stored for long periods.

Methods

Two broad strategies dominate. Slow freezing cools specimens gradually, allowing water to leave cells osmotically so that damaging intracellular ice crystals do not form. Vitrification, by contrast, uses high concentrations of cryoprotective agents and ultra-rapid cooling to turn the specimen into an amorphous, glass-like solid without ice formation at all.

Cryoprotective agents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), glycerol, ethylene glycol and propylene glycol are added to reduce ice formation and osmotic shock, though they can themselves be toxic at high concentrations and must be removed or diluted during thawing.

Applications

Cryopreservation is routine in reproductive medicine for sperm, oocytes and embryos; in cell biology for banking cell lines; in transfusion medicine and stem cell therapy for hematopoietic stem cells; and in conservation biology for seeds and genetic material of endangered species. Whole-organ cryopreservation remains an unsolved challenge because of thermal stress, ice formation and the difficulty of perfusing cryoprotectants evenly.

Naturally occurring cryopreservation

Some organisms survive freezing naturally. The wood frog (Rana sylvatica) tolerates having much of its body water frozen, using glucose as a cryoprotectant. Certain bacteria, notably Deinococcus radiodurans, and microscopic animals such as tardigrades can survive extremely low temperatures.

History

The cryoprotective effect of glycerol was discovered in 1949 by Christopher Polge and colleagues, enabling the freezing of spermatozoa. Human embryos were first successfully frozen, thawed and implanted in the 1980s, and vitrification of oocytes became widespread in the 2000s.
