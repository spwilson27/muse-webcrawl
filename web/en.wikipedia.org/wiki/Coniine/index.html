Coniine

FormulaC8H17N
Molar mass127.23 g/mol
AppearanceColourless oily liquid

Coniine is a poisonous chemical compound, an alkaloid present in and isolable from poison hemlock (Conium maculatum), the plant that was used to execute Socrates in 399 BC. It is also found in the yellow pitcher plant and fool's parsley. Coniine is a neurotoxin that disrupts the nervous system and can cause death by respiratory paralysis; it is toxic to humans and livestock.

Poison hemlock (Conium maculatum), the source of coniine.

Chemistry

Coniine is 2-propylpiperidine, a colourless, oily liquid with a penetrating mousy odour that darkens on exposure to air and light. It was the first alkaloid whose structure was determined (by August Wilhelm von Hofmann) and the first to be synthesised, by Albert Ladenburg in 1886. The naturally occurring form is chiefly the (S)-enantiomer.

Pharmacology and toxicology

Coniine acts on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, first stimulating and then blocking neuromuscular transmission, in a manner similar to curare. Poisoning begins with burning in the mouth, nausea and tremor, progressing to ascending muscular paralysis; consciousness is characteristically retained while breathing fails, matching Plato's account in the Phaedo of Socrates' death. Treatment is supportive, with mechanical ventilation until the toxin is cleared.
