Tlingit


Totem poles are emblematic of Tlingit art and clan crests

The Tlingit (also spelled Tlinkit) are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast of North America, native to the temperate rainforest archipelago of what is now Southeast Alaska and northwestern British Columbia. Their language, Lingít, means "People of the Tides". Some 14,000 Tlingit people live in the region today, with communities including Juneau, Sitka, Haines, and Yakutat.

Society and culture

Traditional Tlingit society is matrilineal, organized into two moieties — Raven (Yéil) and Eagle/Wolf (Ch'aak'/Gooch) — subdivided into clans that own crests, stories, songs, and territories. The potlatch (ku.éex') remains the ceremonial heart of social life, validating marriages, names, and memorial obligations. Tlingit artists are renowned for formline carving, totem poles, Chilkat weaving, and silverwork. Subsistence has centred on salmon, halibut, herring, and sea mammals, supplemented by berries and game.

History

Tlingit clans fiercely resisted Russian colonization, destroying the Russian fort at Sitka in 1802 before the decisive 1804 Battle of Sitka. After the Alaska Purchase of 1867, Tlingit people faced pressure on their land and culture; the twentieth century saw organized land-claims work culminating in the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, under which the Sealaska Corporation was formed. Language revitalization efforts for Lingít are ongoing.
