Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media is a 1988 book by Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky. It argues that the mass media of the United States "serve to mobilize support for the special interests that dominate the state and private activity," functioning as a propaganda system even without overt censorship or conspiracy. The title alludes to the phrase "the manufacture of consent," originally used by Walter Lippmann.

The propaganda model

The book's central thesis, the propaganda model, holds that news is shaped by five editorial "filters":

  1. The size, concentrated ownership, owner wealth, and profit orientation of the dominant mass-media firms.
  2. Advertising as the primary income source of the media.
  3. Reliance on information provided by government, business, and "experts" funded and approved by them.
  4. "Flak" — negative responses and pressure used to discipline the media.
  5. Anticommunism as a national ideology and control mechanism (later editions generalise this to the "war on terror" and similar ideological frames).

According to the model, these filters determine which events become news and how they are framed, marginalising dissenting views without direct state coercion.

Case studies

Herman and Chomsky support the model with paired case studies: the dramatically different coverage of "worthy" and "unworthy" victims (for example, a murdered Polish priest versus murdered religious workers in U.S.-backed states in Latin America); coverage of elections in El Salvador and Guatemala versus Nicaragua; and what they argue was the media's role in legitimising the Indochina wars. A chapter examines the media's treatment of the alleged KGB-Bulgarian plot to assassinate Pope John Paul II.

Reception

The book has been highly influential in media studies and among media critics, and it was adapted into a 1992 documentary film, Manufacturing Consent: Noam Chomsky and the Media. Critics have disputed its case-study methods, its measures of media coverage, and whether structural bias fully explains the patterns it documents. Herman and Chomsky revisited the model in later prefaces, arguing that the filters had intensified with media consolidation.
