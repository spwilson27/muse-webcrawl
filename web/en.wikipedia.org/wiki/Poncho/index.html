TypeOuter garment

A poncho (Spanish pronunciation: [ˈpontʃo]; from Quechua punchu) is a type of outer garment designed to keep the body warm, or if made from a watertight material, to keep the wearer dry during rain. It consists of a single large sheet of fabric with an opening at the centre for the head, sometimes with a hood. Ponchos have been used by the Native American peoples of the Andes and Patagonia since pre-Hispanic times.

History

The poncho is thought to have originated in the Andean cultures of South America, where finely woven woollen ponchos were worn by peoples such as the Paracas and later the Inca. After the Spanish conquest, the garment spread throughout Latin America and became associated with the gauchos of the pampas and the huasos of Chile. Ponchos were also adopted by outdoorsmen and soldiers in North America during the 19th century.

During the American Civil War, both Union and Confederate soldiers used rubberised ponchos as rain gear and ground sheets. The United States military developed the design further, and ponchos remain standard issue rain protection in many armed forces.

Types

Traditional ponchos are woven from sheep's wool or alpaca fibre and often decorated with stripes and geometric patterns. Modern variants include lightweight nylon rain ponchos, disposable plastic ponchos sold at sporting events, and fashion ponchos made from knitted fabrics.

In popular culture

The poncho became an iconic garment in cinema through the "Man with No Name" character played by Clint Eastwood in Sergio Leone's Dollars Trilogy of Spaghetti Western films in the 1960s.
