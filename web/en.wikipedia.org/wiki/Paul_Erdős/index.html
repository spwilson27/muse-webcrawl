Paul Erdős

Paul Erdős (Hungarian: Erdős Pál; 26 March 1913 – 20 September 1996) was a Hungarian mathematician. He was one of the most prolific mathematicians and producers of mathematical conjectures of the 20th century. Erdős pursued and proposed problems in discrete mathematics, graph theory, number theory, mathematical analysis, approximation theory, set theory, and probability theory.

Life

Erdős was a child prodigy in Budapest who earned his doctorate in 1934, the year he gave a simple proof of Bertrand's postulate. After postdoctoral work in Manchester and Princeton he adopted a famously itinerant life, owning no home and travelling from university to university with a suitcase of belongings, collaborating with mathematicians worldwide and announcing that "a mathematician is a machine for turning coffee into theorems". He published some 1,500 papers with 511 co-authors, more than any other mathematician.

Work

With Alfréd Rényi he founded the study of random graphs; with Atle Selberg he gave an elementary proof of the prime number theorem (1949); the Erdős–Kac theorem linked number theory to probability. He offered cash prizes for problems he posed, ranging from $10 to several thousand dollars, many still open. His name attaches to hundreds of concepts, theorems, and conjectures.

Erdős number

The Erdős number measures collaborative distance from Erdős in the graph of mathematical co-authorship: his co-authors have Erdős number 1, their other collaborators 2, and so on. The concept reflects his central place in 20th-century mathematics. He died of a heart attack in Warsaw while attending a conference in 1996.
