Silk Road transmission of Buddhism

The Silk Road transmission of Buddhism describes the spread of Mahayana Buddhism from India into Central Asia and China along the trade routes known collectively as the Silk Road, beginning around the 2nd century BCE to the 1st century CE and continuing for many centuries. From China, Buddhism later spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, while Central Asian transmission also influenced Tibet.

Early transmission

Buddhist monks travelled with merchant caravans through the Kushan Empire and the oasis kingdoms of the Tarim Basin, such as Khotan, Kashgar, Turfan, and Kucha. Missionary translators — including Parthian, Sogdian, Khotanese, and Kuchean monks — rendered Sanskrit and Prakrit texts into Chinese. Famous translator-monks include An Shigao (2nd century CE), Lokakṣema, Dharmarakṣa, and Kumārajīva (344–413), whose translations profoundly shaped East Asian Buddhism.

Pilgrims and texts

Chinese pilgrims such as Faxian (337–422) and Xuanzang (602–664) journeyed west to India in search of authentic scriptures, returning with hundreds of texts and detailed travel accounts. Their journeys illustrate the two-way flow of people, art, and ideas along the routes: Greco-Buddhist artistic styles from Gandhara travelled east, while Chinese innovations later moved west.

Decline

The expansion of Islam into Central Asia from the 8th century onward gradually displaced Buddhism in the western Tarim Basin, and the sea routes increasingly supplemented the overland transmission to China. Nevertheless, the legacy of the Silk Road transmission defines the Mahayana traditions still practised across East Asia.
