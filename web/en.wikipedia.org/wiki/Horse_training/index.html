Horse training

Horse training refers to a variety of practices that teach horses to perform certain behaviors when commanded to do so by humans. Horses are trained to be ridden, driven, and handled for everyday care, and for specialized work and sport, from racing and dressage to police and therapeutic riding.

Principles

Most training relies on learning theory: habituation to stimuli, desensitization to frightening objects, and operant conditioning. The most widely used technique is negative reinforcement — applying a cue or pressure and releasing it the instant the horse responds correctly. Positive reinforcement, such as food rewards and clicker training, is also used. Punishment is considered counterproductive by most modern trainers because it can produce fear and conflict behaviors.

Stages

Young horses are first handled ("halter broken"), taught to lead, stand for grooming and hoof care, and loaded into trailers. "Starting" or "breaking in" introduces saddle and bridle, then a rider, usually beginning on the longe line or in a round pen. Further schooling teaches the aids — seat, leg, and rein cues — and discipline-specific skills such as jumping, reining patterns, or harness work.

Approaches

Traditional methods, codified since Xenophon's On Horsemanship (c. 355 BC), emphasize systematic gymnastic schooling, as in classical dressage. The natural horsemanship movement, popularized in the late 20th century by figures such as Monty Roberts and Pat Parelli, stresses reading equine body language and working in a round pen using pressure-and-release and "join-up". Critics of both camps debate welfare questions including rollkur (hyperflexion) in dressage and the use of bits and whips. Racehorse training, by contrast, focuses on fitness conditioning as much as behavior.
