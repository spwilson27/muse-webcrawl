Lexical hypothesis

The lexical hypothesis (also known as the sedimentation hypothesis) is a thesis in personality psychology holding that the most important and socially relevant individual differences in human personality will, over time, become encoded in natural language as single words — adjectives and other descriptive terms. In its strong form it proposes that a language's vocabulary of personality descriptors contains, in effect, a complete taxonomy of the ways people differ, and that by harvesting all such terms and studying their patterns of co-occurrence, psychologists can discover the fundamental structure of personality. The lexical hypothesis is the theoretical basis of the lexical approach that produced the Big Five personality traits.

History

The idea is usually traced to Francis Galton, who in 1884 reported that he had searched a dictionary for words describing character and concluded that language had registered the distinctions people actually observe. Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert carried out the programme in earnest in 1936, extracting 17,953 person-descriptive terms from Webster's dictionary, of which roughly 4,500 referred to stable traits. Raymond Cattell reduced this list and applied factor analysis, arriving at his sixteen personality factors. Later researchers — notably Warren Norman, and Lewis Goldberg, who coined the term "Big Five" — reanalysed similar lexical data and converged on five broad dimensions: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism.

Assumptions and criticism

The hypothesis rests on two assumptions: first, that most significant personality differences are noticed by lay observers; and second, that those differences important enough to be talked about will crystallise into words. Critics note that the lexical approach can only find traits that a given speech community has lexicalised: studies in different languages have not always recovered exactly five factors (some find six, as in the HEXACO model, which adds honesty-humility), and harmful, rare, or socially unspeakable attributes may be missing from the vocabulary. Defenders respond that cross-language lexical studies are the best available check on the universality of trait structure, and that the rough convergence of results across languages supports the approach.

Use

Beyond the Big Five, lexical methods have been used to map the structure of emotions, attitudes, values, and mental-health symptoms, and to compare how personality is described in different cultures. The hypothesis is therefore a foundational premise of trait psychology and of psycholexical research in general.
