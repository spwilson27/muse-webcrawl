Spanish missions in Mexico

The Spanish missions in Mexico are a series of religious outposts established by Spanish Catholic Franciscans, Jesuits, Dominicans, and Augustinians to spread the Christian doctrine among the local Native Americans during the colonial era of New Spain. Beginning shortly after the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521, missions became a central institution of Spanish colonization, combining evangelization with the reorganization of indigenous communities into settled towns under Spanish rule.

Beginnings

In 1524, twelve Franciscan friars known as the "Twelve Apostles of Mexico" arrived in New Spain under Martín de Valencia and began mass baptisms and the construction of churches, often on the sites of pre-Hispanic temples. Augustinians arrived in 1533 and Dominicans in 1526, each order working in distinct regions. Friars learned indigenous languages such as Nahuatl and Purépecha, compiled grammars and dictionaries, and founded schools for indigenous elites.

Sierra Gorda and the frontier

On the northern frontier, missions served as instruments for pacifying and settling semi-nomadic peoples. In the 18th century, Junípero Serra worked in the Sierra Gorda missions of Querétaro — Santiago de Jalpan, Santa María del Agua de Landa, San Francisco del Valle de Tilaco, Nuestra Señora de la Luz de Tancoyol, and San Miguel Concá — whose ornate mestizo-baroque facades are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. From these bases, Serra later moved to Alta California.

Jesuit missions of the northwest

Jesuits, led by figures such as Eusebio Francisco Kino, established missions across Sinaloa, Sonora, and Baja California from the late 16th century until their expulsion from Spanish territories in 1767. Kino founded missions including Nuestra Señora de los Dolores and San Xavier del Bac, mapped the region, and proved Baja California was a peninsula, not an island.

Decline and secularization

After Mexican independence in 1821, the mission system declined; missions were secularized, their lands distributed, and many churches became parish churches. Today numerous mission churches remain active places of worship and major examples of Mexican colonial architecture, including the Franciscan missions of the Sierra Gorda and the missions of the Popocatépetl slopes, inscribed in 1994 among the earliest Mexican World Heritage listings.
