The electricity sector in Canada has played a defining role in the country's economic and regional development. Canada is one of the world's largest producers of electricity and among the largest per-capita consumers, and it is a major net exporter to the United States. Generation is dominated by hydroelectricity, which supplies roughly 60 per cent of output — Canada is the world's second- or third-largest hydro producer — followed by nuclear power concentrated in Ontario, natural gas, coal (being phased out), wind and a growing share of solar.

Organisation

Under the Canadian constitution, electricity is mainly a provincial responsibility, and most provinces are served by vertically integrated, publicly owned Crown corporations, including Hydro-Québec (the largest utility in North America by generating capacity), BC Hydro and Manitoba Hydro. Alberta operates a deregulated wholesale market and Ontario a hybrid market run by the Independent Electricity System Operator (IESO), with Ontario Power Generation and Bruce Power supplying most nuclear output from the CANDU reactor fleet. Interprovincial trade is modest compared with extensive north–south interconnections to the US grids, and Atlantic Canada is linked by subsea cables such as the Maritime Link.

Issues and transition

Canada's electricity is among the cleanest of large economies, roughly 80 per cent non-emitting, and federal policy targets a net-zero grid. Challenges include electrifying transport and heating in a cold climate, building interprovincial transmission, retiring coal (federal regulations require conventional coal phase-out by 2030), refurbishing nuclear plants, and Indigenous partnership and ownership in new projects. Landmark developments include Quebec's James Bay Project, Newfoundland's Muskrat Falls, and Ontario's coal phase-out completed in 2014 — one of the largest climate actions of its kind in North America.
