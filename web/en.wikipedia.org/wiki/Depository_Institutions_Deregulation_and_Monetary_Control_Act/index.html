The Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 (DIDMCA, Pub. L. 96-221) is a United States federal financial statute passed in 1980 and signed into law by President Jimmy Carter on 31 March 1980. It is considered one of the most significant banking laws of the late 20th century, beginning the deregulation of the American financial industry.

Provisions

  • Monetary control: it subjected all depository institutions — not only Federal Reserve member banks — to the Federal Reserve's reserve requirements, and in return gave them access to Fed services such as the discount window and check clearing, which the Fed was required to price explicitly.
  • Deregulation of interest rates: it established the Depository Institutions Deregulation Committee to phase out, over six years, the Regulation Q ceilings on deposit interest rates.
  • NOW accounts: it authorised interest-bearing negotiable order of withdrawal (NOW) accounts nationwide.
  • Deposit insurance: it raised federal deposit insurance coverage from $40,000 to $100,000 per account.
  • Thrift powers: it expanded the lending powers of savings and loan associations and savings banks, and pre-empted state usury ceilings on certain loans.

Background and effects

The act responded to the crisis of "disintermediation": with market interest rates in the late 1970s far above the Regulation Q ceilings, depositors moved funds to money market mutual funds, squeezing banks and thrifts. By deregulating deposit rates and broadening thrift powers, the act helped institutions compete, but critics argue it also contributed to the risk-taking that produced the savings and loan crisis later in the 1980s, addressed by the Garn–St Germain Act of 1982 and subsequent reforms.
