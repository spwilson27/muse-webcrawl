Town gas is a gaseous fuel manufactured from coal by carbonisation in retorts, distributed through municipal pipe networks from the early nineteenth century until it was replaced by natural gas in the mid-to-late twentieth century. It is also called coal gas, though that term can also refer to gas produced by other processes.

History

William Murdoch first lit his Cornish home with coal gas in 1792, and the Gas Light and Coke Company, chartered in London in 1812, built the first public gasworks supplying street lighting. The technology spread rapidly through industrial cities in Britain, Europe, and North America, supplying lighting, heating, and cooking. Gasworks became fixtures of the urban landscape, with their distinctive telescopic gasholders.

Composition and hazards

Town gas typically contained roughly half hydrogen and a third methane, but also a significant proportion of toxic carbon monoxide. This made leaks and deliberate inhalation dangerous; the high carbon monoxide content was implicated in many accidental deaths and suicides, and the switch to non-toxic natural gas in Britain during the 1967–1977 conversion programme is credited with removing a common method of suicide. By-products of manufacture included coal tar and coke, both valuable industrial feedstocks.

Replacement

The discovery of large natural gas fields (in the North Sea for Britain, and earlier in the United States) led to wholesale conversion of distribution networks and appliances. In Britain, some 40 million appliances were converted over a decade. Manufactured gas continues to be supplied in a few places, notably Hong Kong, where "town gas" remains the commercial name of the hydrogen-rich gas produced from naphtha.
