Main lobe

In a radio antenna's radiation pattern, the main lobe, or main beam, is the lobe containing the direction of maximum radiation power. In most antennas the main lobe is much larger than the other lobes, called side lobes, which radiate in unwanted directions; a smaller lobe opposite the main lobe is termed the back lobe.

Characteristics

The width of the main lobe is commonly described by the half-power beamwidth — the angle between the directions at which the radiated power falls to half its maximum value. A narrow main lobe corresponds to high antenna directivity and gain. Antenna design generally aims to concentrate as much power as possible in the main lobe and to suppress side lobes, since side lobes waste power, can cause or receive interference, and in radar systems can create false targets or vulnerability to jamming.

Related concepts

The term is also used in optics and acoustics, for example for the central maximum of a diffraction pattern, where the main lobe is flanked by subsidiary maxima separated by nulls. The shape and width of the main lobe are determined by the aperture of the antenna or optical system: larger apertures produce narrower main lobes.
