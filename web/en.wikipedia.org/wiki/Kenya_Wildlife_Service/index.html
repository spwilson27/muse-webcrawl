Kenya Wildlife Service

KWS rangers with Mount Kilimanjaro in the background

The Kenya Wildlife Service (KWS) is a Kenyan state corporation responsible for the conservation and management of wildlife and national parks in Kenya. Established in 1989 and operational from 1990, it manages Kenya's system of national parks, national reserves, sanctuaries and marine protected areas, and enforces wildlife law throughout the country.

History

KWS was created under the Wildlife (Conservation and Management) Amendment Act of 1989 to replace the previous Wildlife Conservation and Management Department, amid a crisis of elephant and rhino poaching in the 1980s. Its first director, the palaeoanthropologist Richard Leakey, dramatically professionalised the service and its armed anti-poaching units, coinciding with Kenya's public burning of ivory stockpiles in 1989. Elephants and rhinos subsequently recovered substantially.

Responsibilities

The service manages more than twenty terrestrial national parks — including Amboseli, Tsavo East and West, Lake Nakuru, Meru and Nairobi National Park — plus marine parks, sanctuaries and the Nairobi Safari Walk and orphanage. Its duties include anti-poaching operations, human–wildlife conflict mitigation, veterinary response, wildlife translocation, research, and tourism management within parks.

Challenges

KWS operates under pressure from drought, habitat loss to settlement and infrastructure, and renewed poaching demand for ivory and rhino horn. It is principally funded by park tourism revenue, government appropriations and donor support, and works with private and community conservancies, which now protect a large share of Kenya's wildlife outside state parks.
