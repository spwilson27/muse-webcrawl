Ambassadors of the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ambassadors of the United States are persons nominated by the president of the United States to serve as the country's official representatives to foreign nations, international organizations, and as ambassadors-at-large. Under the Appointments Clause of the United States Constitution, a nominee for ambassador is confirmed by the United States Senate before being commissioned, unless a recess appointment is made.

Appointment and tenure

Ambassadors serve "at the pleasure of the president", and by tradition submit letters of resignation when a new administration takes office. Roughly 30 percent of ambassadorial appointments are political appointees, often major campaign donors or associates of the president, while about 70 percent are career members of the United States Foreign Service. Political appointees are concentrated in Western Europe and other low-security-risk posts, while career diplomats predominate in hardship and high-risk assignments.

Before taking up a post, an ambassador-designate must be accepted (agrément) by the receiving state, and presents letters of credence to the host country's head of state upon arrival.

Duties

An ambassador heads a United States embassy and serves as chief of mission, directing the work of U.S. government agencies in the host country, protecting American citizens abroad, promoting trade, and reporting political and economic developments to the Department of State.
