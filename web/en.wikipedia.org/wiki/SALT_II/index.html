SALT II (Strategic Arms Limitation Talks II) was a bilateral treaty between the United States and the Soviet Union intended to limit strategic nuclear weapons. It was signed by U.S. President Jimmy Carter and Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev in Vienna on 18 June 1979, after seven years of negotiations. Although the U.S. Senate never ratified the treaty — Carter withdrew it from consideration after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 — both superpowers broadly observed its terms until the United States formally abandoned it in 1986.

Terms

Building on the SALT I Interim Agreement of 1972, SALT II set equal aggregate ceilings: 2,400 strategic nuclear delivery vehicles (ICBMs, SLBMs and heavy bombers) for each side, falling to 2,250, with sub-ceilings on MIRVed systems — including a limit of 1,320 MIRVed ballistic missile launchers and heavy bombers with cruise missiles, and 1,200 MIRVed ballistic missile launchers. It also limited the number of warheads per missile type, banned new ICBM types beyond one per side, and included verification provisions relying on "national technical means" such as reconnaissance satellites.

Ratification controversy and aftermath

In the United States the treaty was attacked from the right as too permissive and was entangled with a controversy over a Soviet combat brigade in Cuba. Carter withdrew it in January 1980 following the invasion of Afghanistan. The Reagan administration criticised SALT II as "fatally flawed" but continued informal compliance until May 1986, when the United States exceeded its limits and Reagan announced the treaty would no longer be observed. SALT II was eventually superseded by the START I treaty of 1991, which mandated actual reductions.
