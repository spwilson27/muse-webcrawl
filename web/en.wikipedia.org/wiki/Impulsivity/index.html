Impulsivity

Impulsivity is a tendency to act on urges, desires, or whims with little forethought about consequences, displaying behaviour characterised by haste, risk-taking, and poor planning. In psychology it is considered a core feature of many conditions, including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), bipolar disorder, substance use disorders, and certain personality disorders, but it also exists as a normal personality trait varying across the general population.

Impulsive behaviour involves acting quickly without weighing long-term outcomes.

Components

Researchers generally divide impulsivity into several facets. Ernest Barratt's influential model distinguishes attentional impulsiveness (difficulty focusing), motor impulsiveness (acting without thinking), and non-planning impulsiveness (preference for the present over the future). The UPPS model adds negative and positive urgency (acting rashly under strong emotion), lack of premeditation, lack of perseverance, and sensation seeking. Behavioural tasks such as delay-discounting tests — in which people choose between a smaller immediate reward and a larger later one — measure related tendencies.

Brain basis

Impulsivity is associated with the balance between reward circuits centred on the ventral striatum and dopamine signalling, and control circuits in the prefrontal cortex. Immaturity of prefrontal control in adolescence is thought to explain teenagers' heightened risk-taking, and damage or dysfunction in these systems can produce disinhibited behaviour.

Clinical significance

High impulsivity predicts problems ranging from gambling and substance abuse to aggression and self-harm, and it is a risk factor for suicidal behaviour. Treatments include cognitive behavioural therapies that build planning and emotion-regulation skills, and medications that stabilise mood or treat underlying disorders. Moderate impulsivity, however, is not purely pathological: it can accompany spontaneity, quick decision-making, and creativity.
