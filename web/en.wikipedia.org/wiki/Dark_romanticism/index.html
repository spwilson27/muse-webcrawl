Dark romanticism

Dark romanticism is a literary subgenre of Romanticism characterised by gloomy, pessimistic themes: a fascination with evil, sin, guilt, madness, the supernatural and the grotesque. Where mainstream Romanticism and Transcendentalism emphasised the goodness of humanity and nature, dark romantics saw human beings as prone to sin and self-destruction, and nature as a dark, decaying and mysterious force.

Principal figures

The movement is most associated with three 19th-century American writers: Edgar Allan Poe, Nathaniel Hawthorne and Herman Melville. Poe's tales of horror and his poem "The Raven" exemplify the genre; Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter and his short stories explore inherited guilt and hidden sin in Puritan New England; Melville's Moby-Dick confronts evil and obsession through Captain Ahab's monomaniacal quest.

Themes and legacy

Recurring motifs include haunted houses and ruins, doubles, premature burial, forbidden knowledge and unreliable narrators. Dark romanticism grew partly out of the European Gothic novel of Horace Walpole, Ann Radcliffe and Mary Shelley, and in turn foreshadowed later horror fiction, Symbolism and the Decadent movement.
