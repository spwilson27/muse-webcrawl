Georgian Military Road

Length 212 km (132 mi)
From Tbilisi, Georgia
To Vladikavkaz, Russia
Highest point Jvari (Cross) Pass, 2,379 m (7,805 ft)

The Georgian Military Road (Georgian: საქართველოს სამხედრო გზა, Sakartvelos samkhedro gza; Russian: Военно-Грузинская дорога, Voyenno-Gruzinskaya doroga) is the historic name for the major route through the Caucasus Mountains from Georgia to Russia. The 212-kilometre (132 mi) road runs between Tbilisi, Georgia, and Vladikavkaz, Russia, following the valleys of the Terek and Aragvi rivers across the watershed of the Greater Caucasus at the Jvari (Cross) Pass, 2,379 metres above sea level. It is part of the international E117 route and is today Georgia's only land connection with Russia.

History

The route has been known since antiquity and is described by Strabo. After the Treaty of Georgievsk (1783) placed eastern Georgia under Russian protection, the Russian military improved the track; the modern road was built by the Russian army between 1799 and 1863, at enormous cost in labour by soldiers and conscripted Georgian peasants. Following the Russian annexation of Georgia in 1801, the road became the main artery for Russian expansion into the Caucasus, and it figured in the Caucasian War. The name "Military Road" dates from 1809.

The road is celebrated in Russian literature: Pushkin travelled it in 1829, Lermontov set part of A Hero of Our Time along it, and it appears in Tolstoy's The Cossacks and works by Alexander Dumas.

Route

From Tbilisi the road passes the ancient capital Mtskheta, the Ananuri fortress complex overlooking the Aragvi, the ski resort of Gudauri, and a travertine mineral spring, before climbing by switchbacks to the Cross Pass. The Georgian side descends to the village of Stepantsminda (Kazbegi), dominated by Mount Kazbek (5,047 m) and the Gergeti Trinity Church. The road then enters the Darial Gorge, the "Gate of the Caucasus," past the Russian border crossing at Larsi/Verkhny Lars, and descends to Vladikavkaz in North Ossetia.

The road is subject to avalanches, landslides, and heavy snow, and the border crossing has periodically closed during tensions between Russia and Georgia, notably between 2006 and 2010, and for long queues of trucks in subsequent years.
