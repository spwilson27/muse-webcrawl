Henry Hobson Richardson (29 September 1838 – 27 April 1886) was an American architect, best known for his work in a style that became known as Richardsonian Romanesque. He is regarded as one of the founders of a distinctively American architecture, and in a famous 1885 poll his Trinity Church in Boston was voted the finest building in the United States.

Education and early career

Born on a Louisiana plantation, Richardson studied at Harvard and then at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, one of the first Americans to do so. The Civil War delayed his return; he began practice in New York in 1865 and formed a partnership with Charles D. Gambrill. His design for Trinity Church, Boston (1872–77), made his reputation: a massive, earth-hued Romanesque composition in Copley Square with a central crossing tower.

Mature work

Richardson's mature buildings are characterised by heavy rusticated stonework, broad round arches, deep window reveals, and sculptural massing. Major works include the Allegheny County Courthouse and Jail in Pittsburgh, the Marshall Field Wholesale Store in Chicago (a key precursor of commercial architecture), Sever Hall at Harvard, the Winn Memorial Library at Woburn, the Ames Gate Lodge, and the Glessner House in Chicago, completed after his death. He also designed influential suburban railway stations and shingled country houses. Despite dying at 47 of kidney disease, he trained a generation of architects, and his style spread nationwide for courthouses, libraries, churches, and stations.
