The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 (IRA) is a United States federal law which aims to reduce the federal government budget deficit, lower prescription drug prices, and invest in domestic clean energy production while promoting environmental justice. It was passed by the 117th United States Congress and signed into law by President Joe Biden on August 16, 2022. It is a budget reconciliation bill sponsored by Senators Chuck Schumer and Joe Manchin, a scaled-down successor to the proposed Build Back Better Act.

Provisions

The law directed roughly $369 billion (later estimates, including uncapped tax credits, ran considerably higher) toward energy security and climate change — the largest climate investment in American history — through tax credits for electric vehicles, renewable electricity, battery storage, and clean-energy manufacturing. On healthcare, it empowered Medicare to negotiate prices for certain drugs for the first time, capped Medicare out-of-pocket drug costs at $2,000 per year, and capped insulin at $35 per month for Medicare beneficiaries. Revenue provisions included a 15% corporate minimum tax on large companies' book income, a 1% excise tax on stock buybacks, and increased funding for the Internal Revenue Service.

Legislative history

After months of stalled negotiations, Manchin and Schumer announced a surprise agreement on 27 July 2022. The Senate passed the bill 51–50 on 7 August, with Vice President Kamala Harris casting the tie-breaking vote, and the House followed 220–207 on 12 August; no Republican in either chamber voted for it.

Effects and later changes

Analyses projected the Act would cut US greenhouse gas emissions to roughly 40% below 2005 levels by 2030 and modestly reduce the deficit. Its clean-energy credits spurred a wave of announced battery and solar factories, mostly in Republican-held districts. In 2025, the Trump administration and congressional Republicans enacted legislation (the "One Big Beautiful Bill") that repealed or phased out many of the IRA's clean-energy credits, though its Medicare drug-negotiation provisions largely remained.
