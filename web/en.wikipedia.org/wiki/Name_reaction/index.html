Name reaction

A name reaction (or named reaction) is a chemical reaction named after its discoverer(s) or developer(s). Well-known examples include the Grignard reaction, the Diels–Alder reaction, the Wittig reaction, the Aldol reaction, and the Suzuki coupling. Name reactions are a standard shorthand in organic chemistry, allowing chemists to communicate complex transformations concisely.

History

The practice of naming reactions after individuals developed alongside organic chemistry in the 19th and 20th centuries. Some reactions carry the names of two or more chemists (e.g. the Fischer–Tropsch process, the Mannich reaction draws on earlier work), while others are named after the institution or place of discovery. Occasionally, the eponym is not the first discoverer but the person who popularized or generalized the reaction.

Usage

Textbooks of organic synthesis organize large numbers of name reactions; standard reference works catalog several hundred. Modern naming sometimes shifts toward descriptive systematic names, but eponymous reactions remain deeply embedded in the literature and in examinations of the discipline.

Criticism

Name reactions have been criticized because eponyms give little information about the transformation itself, can be difficult for students to memorize, and may obscure the contributions of less famous researchers. Nevertheless, they persist because of their brevity and wide recognition among practitioners.
