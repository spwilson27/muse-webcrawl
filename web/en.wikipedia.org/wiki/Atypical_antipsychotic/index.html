Atypical antipsychotic

The atypical antipsychotics (AAP), also known as second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs), are a group of antipsychotic drugs largely introduced after the 1970s and used to treat psychiatric conditions. Some atypical antipsychotics have received regulatory approval for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism, and as adjunctive treatment in major depressive disorder.

Pharmacology

Atypical antipsychotics generally combine antagonism at dopamine D2 receptors with antagonism at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, though the precise receptor profiles vary widely between agents. Clozapine, the prototype, is an effective drug with low propensity for extrapyramidal side effects, but carries a risk of agranulocytosis requiring blood monitoring. Other widely used agents include risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole (a dopamine partial agonist), ziprasidone, amisulpride, paliperidone, lurasidone, and cariprazine.

Uses

The primary indication is schizophrenia; many agents are also used for acute mania and maintenance treatment in bipolar disorder, and some as adjuncts in major depressive disorder. Clozapine remains uniquely effective for treatment-resistant schizophrenia.

Side effects

Compared with typical (first-generation) antipsychotics, the atypicals generally cause fewer extrapyramidal symptoms and less tardive dyskinesia, but several cause significant metabolic adverse effects: weight gain, dyslipidaemia, and increased risk of type 2 diabetes, most prominently with clozapine and olanzapine. Other effects can include sedation, orthostatic hypotension, QT prolongation, hyperprolactinaemia (especially risperidone), and, in elderly patients with dementia, an increased risk of stroke and death that prompted regulatory warnings.

History

Clozapine was synthesized in 1958 and introduced in Europe in the 1970s; withdrawn after cases of agranulocytosis, it was reintroduced in 1989 with monitoring requirements after trials showed superior efficacy in treatment-resistant illness. Its success spurred development of the other second-generation agents in the 1990s and 2000s.
