Adolf Eichmann

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Adolf Eichmann testifying in the glass booth at his trial in Jerusalem, 1961
Adolf Eichmann testifying in the glass booth at his trial in Jerusalem, 1961

Otto Adolf Eichmann (19 March 1906 – 1 June 1962) was a German-Austrian SS officer and one of the chief organisers of The Holocaust. As head of the Reich Main Security Office section dealing with Jewish affairs and evacuation, he organised the identification, assembly and deportation of millions of Jews to ghettos and extermination camps. Captured in Argentina by Mossad agents in 1960, he was tried in Israel, convicted of crimes against humanity and hanged in 1962.

Career in the SS

Eichmann joined the Nazi Party and SS in 1932 and rose in the SD as its 'Jewish expert', promoting forced emigration from Vienna after the Anschluss (1938) through the Central Office for Jewish Emigration — a model of bureaucratic efficiency in expulsion. He attended the Wannsee Conference (1942), prepared its minutes, and thereafter managed the railway logistics of deportation across Europe, notoriously organising the deportation of some 437,000 Hungarian Jews to Auschwitz in 1944.

Escape and capture

Eichmann fled to Argentina in 1950 with a Red Cross passport in the name Ricardo Klement, living quietly in Buenos Aires. Tipped off by the German prosecutor Fritz Bauer via a survivor's family, Mossad abducted him on 11 May 1960 and flew him to Israel, provoking an Argentine protest at the UN but global fascination.

Trial and legacy

His televised trial in Jerusalem (1961), presided over amid testimony from over a hundred survivors, ended in conviction and the only judicial execution in Israel's history. Hannah Arendt's reportage, Eichmann in Jerusalem, coined the phrase 'the banality of evil' for Eichmann's self-portrait as a mere bureaucrat — a characterisation later scholarship has complicated, showing a committed, ideological antisemite who boasted of his role.
