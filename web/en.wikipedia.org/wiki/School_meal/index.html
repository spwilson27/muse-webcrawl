School meal

TypePublic welfare / nutrition programme
ProvidersGovernments, municipalities, charities
BeneficiariesSchoolchildren
ExamplesNational School Lunch Act (US), Midday Meal Scheme (India)

A school meal (or school lunch or school dinner) is a meal provided to students at school, typically during the middle of the day. School meals are served free of charge or at a reduced price in many countries, and are considered an important tool of public health, education policy and social welfare, improving children's nutrition, concentration and attendance.

History

Organised school feeding began in the 19th century. In Britain, charitable societies began providing meals to poor schoolchildren in the 1860s, and the Education (Provision of Meals) Act 1906 allowed local authorities to fund them. In the United States, school lunch programmes expanded in the early 20th century and were placed on a permanent federal footing by the National School Lunch Act of 1946. Japan developed a school lunch system (kyūshoku) after the Second World War that became a model of nutritional education.

Programmes by country

India's Midday Meal Scheme, launched in 1995 and now known as PM-POSHAN, is one of the largest school feeding programmes in the world, serving well over 100 million children cooked meals on school days. Finland and Sweden provide free school meals to all pupils as a universal entitlement. In England, the introduction of Universal Infant Free School Meals in 2014 extended free lunches to all children in their first three years of primary school. In the United States, the National School Lunch Program serves tens of millions of children daily, with free and reduced-price meals for low-income families.

Nutrition standards

Many school meal programmes are governed by nutritional standards intended to limit salt, sugar and saturated fat and to require fruit, vegetables and whole grains. In the United States, standards were significantly strengthened by the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010. Campaigners such as chef Jamie Oliver in Britain drew public attention to the quality of school dinners in the 2000s.

Effects and debate

Research generally finds that school meal programmes improve food security, diet quality and educational outcomes, particularly for disadvantaged children, and reduce stigma when provided universally. Debates continue over cost, food quality, cultural appropriateness, and whether provision should be universal or means-tested.
