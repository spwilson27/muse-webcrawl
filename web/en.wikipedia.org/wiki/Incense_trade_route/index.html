Incense trade route

The incense trade route (also called the Incense Route) was an ancient network of major land and sea trading routes linking the Mediterranean world with sources of incense, spices, and other luxury goods in eastern and southern Arabia, East Africa, and India. Its most famous section was the overland caravan route that carried frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia (modern Yemen and Oman) northward through Mecca and the Nabataean city of Petra to the Mediterranean ports of Gaza and Damascus. The route flourished from roughly the 7th century BC to the 2nd century AD.

History

Frankincense and myrrh — aromatic resins tapped from trees growing only in southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa — were essential to temple ritual, funerary practice, and medicine throughout Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, and Rome, and commanded prices comparable to gold. South Arabian kingdoms such as Saba (Sheba), Ma'in, Qataban, and Hadhramaut grew rich controlling production and the desert crossing; the Queen of Sheba's legendary embassy to Solomon is associated with this trade. From the 1st century BC the Nabataeans, from their capital at Petra, dominated the northern caravan trade and levied tolls and protection fees.

The route

The overland route ran some 2,000 km (about 1,240 miles) from the frankincense-growing regions of Dhofar and Hadhramaut through Shabwah and the great oasis and dam city of Marib, then north along the western edge of Arabia through Najran, Mecca, and Hegra (Mada'in Salih) to Petra, whence caravans continued to Gaza on the Mediterranean or to Damascus. The journey took roughly 62 days and was organised in stages between fortified wells and caravanserais. Pliny the Elder described the route and its tolls in detail, noting that charges were levied at every stage by priests, royal officials, and guards. Sea routes complemented the caravan trade: the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (1st century AD) describes monsoon sailing between Arabian and Red Sea ports and India.

Decline

From the 1st–2nd centuries AD, direct monsoon sea trade between Roman Egypt and India, described in the Periplus, increasingly bypassed the overland route and its middlemen. Later, the spread of Christianity reduced demand for incense in pagan ritual, while political fragmentation in South Arabia and the silting and collapse of irrigation systems hastened the decline; the route had largely faded by late antiquity.

World Heritage

Four Nabataean desert cities on the route's northern section — Haluza, Mamshit, Avdat, and Shivta — were inscribed by UNESCO in 2005 as the "Incense Route – Desert Cities in the Negev" World Heritage Site, and the Land of Frankincense sites in Oman (including Shisr/Ubar and the ports of Khor Rori and Al-Baleed) are likewise listed.
