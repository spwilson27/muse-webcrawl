Tidal island

Tidal island
DefinitionLand connected to the mainland at low tide and cut off at high tide
AccessCauseway, sandbar, or tombolo exposed at low water
Famous examplesMont-Saint-Michel (France); Lindisfarne and St Michael's Mount (England); Cramond Island (Scotland)

A tidal island is a piece of land that is connected to the mainland at low tide, when it can be reached on foot or by vehicle, but is entirely surrounded by water and cut off at high tide. The connection is typically a natural sandbar or tombolo, or an artificial causeway, often built so that it is submerged by each high tide.

Characteristics

Tidal islands occur where the tidal range is large enough to expose the connecting strip of land for part of each tidal cycle. Visitors must time crossings carefully: many tidal islands post official safe-crossing times, and coastguard services regularly rescue people caught by the returning tide. Some causeways have refuge towers or raised boxes along their length for those trapped mid-crossing.

Notable examples

  • Mont-Saint-Michel, Normandy, France — the abbey island in a bay with some of Europe's largest tidal ranges; historically reached across the sands at low tide.
  • Lindisfarne (Holy Island), Northumberland, England — site of the seventh-century monastery raided by Vikings in 793, reached by a tidal causeway from the mainland.
  • St Michael's Mount, Cornwall, England — the Cornish counterpart of Mont-Saint-Michel, with a castle and a causeway from Marazion.
  • Cramond Island, in the Firth of Forth near Edinburgh, Scotland, known for its anti-landing defences and the rows of concrete pylons beside its causeway.
  • Burgh Island, Devon, and Hilbre Island, Wirral, England; Bar Island, Maine, and other examples occur worldwide.

Hazards

The chief danger of tidal islands is the speed of the incoming tide, which across flat sands can advance faster than a person can walk. Mud and quicksand on tidal flats add to the risk, and many causeways display warnings and tide tables at both ends.
