White Paper of 1939

The White Paper of 1939 was a policy statement issued by the British government, led by Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain, in reaction to the 1936–1939 Arab revolt in Palestine. Presented to Parliament on 17 May 1939 by Colonial Secretary Malcolm MacDonald, it rejected the Peel Commission's proposal to partition Mandatory Palestine and instead envisaged a single independent Palestinian state, governed jointly by Arabs and Jews, within ten years.

Provisions

The paper limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over the following five years, after which further immigration would depend on Arab consent. It restricted the sale of Arab land to Jews in much of the country and declared that Britain's obligations to the Jewish national home under the Balfour Declaration had been fulfilled, envisaging an independent Palestine state in which Arabs and Jews shared government.

Reactions

The Jewish Agency and Zionist organisations rejected the paper as a betrayal of the Mandate, and it was denounced by David Ben-Gurion; Jewish immigration nevertheless continued illegally (Aliyah Bet). Arab reactions were mixed: the paper met several demands of the Arab Higher Committee regarding immigration and land, but some leaders objected that independence was deferred and conditional. The Permanent Mandates Commission of the League of Nations concluded unanimously that the policy was not in accordance with the Mandate.

Consequences

Coming on the eve of the Second World War and the Holocaust, the immigration restrictions had grave consequences for Jewish refugees seeking to flee Europe. After the war the paper remained a focus of Zionist opposition and armed revolt against the Mandate; Britain referred the Palestine question to the United Nations in 1947, leading to the 1947 partition plan and the end of the Mandate in 1948.
