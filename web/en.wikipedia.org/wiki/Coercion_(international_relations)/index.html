Coercion (international relations)

Coercive instruments range from sanctions to blockades and limited force

Field: International relations; strategic studies

Key theorist: Thomas Schelling

Related: Compellence, deterrence, coercive diplomacy

In international relations, coercion is the use of threats, sanctions or limited force by one state (or coalition) to persuade another state to change its behaviour — to stop doing something it is doing, or to start doing something it is not. The modern theory of coercion derives largely from Thomas Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict (1960) and Arms and Influence (1966), which analysed "the power to hurt" as bargaining power: violence and deprivation are most effective held in reserve, as threats that give the adversary a choice.

Compellence and deterrence

Schelling distinguished deterrence — persuading an adversary not to do something — from compellence — persuading it to actively change ongoing behaviour. Compellence is generally held to be harder: it requires the target to visibly act, often under a deadline, and can engage prestige and domestic politics. Political scientist Alexander George developed the related concept of coercive diplomacy, a strategy of graduated threats and incentives that seeks to resolve crises short of war, with variants including the ultimatum, the "try-and-see" approach, and gradual escalation ("turning the screw").

Instruments and success conditions

Coercive instruments include economic sanctions and embargoes, diplomatic isolation, cyber operations, shows of force, blockades, no-fly zones and limited air or missile strikes. Scholarship — notably Robert Pape's work on air power and sanctions — suggests coercion succeeds most often when demands are clear and limited, threats are credible, the target is offered a face-saving exit ("carrots" alongside "sticks"), and the coercer can credibly promise that compliance ends the punishment. Coercion frequently fails or backfires when demands touch the target regime's survival, when the target can shift costs onto its population, or when multiple senders issue conflicting signals.

Examples

Frequently studied cases include the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962), often treated as successful compellence/deterrence bargaining; NATO's 1999 Kosovo campaign, debated as coercive air war; and long-running sanctions regimes against Iran and North Korea, which illustrate both the leverage and the limits of economic coercion.
