Cluniac reforms

The Cluniac reforms (also called the Benedictine reform) were a series of changes within medieval monasticism in the Western Church focused on restoring the traditional monastic life, encouraging art and care for the poor, and enforcing celibacy and the elimination of simony. The movement began within the Benedictine order at Cluny Abbey in Burgundy, founded in 910 by William I, Duke of Aquitaine, and grew into the first great religious order network of medieval Europe.

The abbey church of Cluny was once the largest in Christendom.

Origins

Cluny's foundation charter gave the abbey an unusual freedom: it was placed directly under the pope and exempt from interference by local bishops and lay lords. Under a succession of long-lived, able abbots — Berno, Odo, Odilo, and Hugh of Semur — Cluny imposed strict observance of the Rule of Saint Benedict, lavish liturgy, and intercessory prayer for the dead. Dependent priories (eventually more than a thousand houses across France, Spain, Italy, and England) were governed centrally from Cluny, with their priors appointed by its abbot. The great church of Cluny III, begun in 1088, was the largest church in Christendom until St Peter's in Rome.

Influence

The Cluniac ethos — church independence from lay control, clerical celibacy, and suppression of simony — prepared the way for the Gregorian Reform of the later eleventh century. In the twelfth century the austere Cistercian order, led by Bernard of Clairvaux, reacted against Cluniac wealth and liturgical elaboration even while spreading Cluny's reforming ideals still further.
