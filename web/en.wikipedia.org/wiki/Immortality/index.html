Immortality

Immortality is the concept of eternal life — the ability to live forever, or an endless existence. It has been a central theme of human religion, myth, and philosophy since antiquity, taking forms from the biological immortality of certain organisms to spiritual immortality of the soul, resurrection of the dead, and the modern scientific pursuit of life extension. What constitutes immortality varies: some traditions attribute it only to gods, others hold that the human soul is inherently immortal, while materialists generally treat death as final.

The ouroboros, a serpent eating its own tail, symbolizes eternity and cyclical renewal.

In religion and myth

The Epic of Gilgamesh, among the oldest surviving works of literature, turns on its hero's failed quest for eternal life. Greek heroes could win a kind of immortality through fame (kleos), while the gods themselves were deathless. Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions teach rebirth in samsara, with liberation (moksha or nirvana) as escape from the cycle — a state beyond both life and death. Judaism and especially Christianity and Islam teach resurrection and an afterlife; Christianity proclaims eternal life as a gift of God through Christ. Chinese Daoism developed elaborate practices, from alchemy to meditation, aimed at becoming a xian, an immortal.

Biological immortality

Some organisms show negligible senescence: the hydrozoan Turritopsis dohrnii can revert from its medusa stage to a polyp, potentially cycling indefinitely; hydra show no measurable aging; and certain trees, such as bristlecone pines, live for millennia, though they can still die from injury or disease. No complex animal is known to be truly immune to death. In humans, aging research studies telomeres, cellular senescence, and regenerative medicine, while cryonics preserves bodies at low temperature in the hope of future revival — a practice whose feasibility remains unproven.

Philosophical debate

Philosophers have questioned whether immortality would even be desirable. Bernard Williams argued, in his essay on the Makropulos case, that an endless life would inevitably become tedious or dissolve personal identity, echoing Karel Čapek's play about a woman who lives 342 years and longs for death. Others, such as John Martin Fischer, defend the desirability of an indefinitely extended life filled with repeatable pleasures. The prospect of mind uploading and digital immortality raises further questions about whether a copy of a mind would be the original person.
