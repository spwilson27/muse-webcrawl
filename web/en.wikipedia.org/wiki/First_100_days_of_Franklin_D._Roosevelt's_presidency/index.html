First 100 days of Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency

The first 100 days of Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency began on 4 March 1933, when Roosevelt was inaugurated as the 32nd president of the United States in the depths of the Great Depression. In a special session of Congress lasting until 16 June 1933, Roosevelt pushed through an unprecedented burst of legislation — the core of the early New Deal — aimed at relief, recovery and reform. The phrase "first hundred days" has since become the standard benchmark by which the beginnings of later presidencies are judged.

The banking crisis

Roosevelt took office as the American banking system was collapsing. On 5 March he proclaimed a national bank holiday, closing all banks, and on 9 March Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act, allowing sound banks to reopen under federal supervision and giving the Treasury power over those that could not. In his first fireside chat on 12 March Roosevelt explained the measures, and when banks reopened, deposits exceeded withdrawals, restoring confidence.

Major legislation

The Hundred Days produced a remarkable sequence of laws, among them:

  • The Emergency Banking Act and the Glass–Steagall Act (Banking Act of 1933), which created federal deposit insurance through the FDIC and separated commercial from investment banking.
  • The Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), employing young men in conservation work.
  • The Federal Emergency Relief Act, funding direct relief to the unemployed.
  • The Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA), paying farmers to reduce production.
  • The Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), a public corporation for flood control, navigation and electricity in the Tennessee Valley.
  • The Securities Act of 1933, requiring truthful disclosure in the sale of securities.
  • The National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA), creating the National Recovery Administration and the Public Works Administration.
  • The Economy Act, cutting federal salaries and veterans' payments, and the Beer–Wine Revenue Act, legalising the sale of beer and light wine ahead of the repeal of Prohibition.

The Brain Trust and Congress

Roosevelt relied on a circle of advisers known as the "Brain Trust" — including Raymond Moley, Rexford Tugwell and Adolf Berle — and on cabinet members such as Frances Perkins and Harold Ickes. The Democratic Congress, desperate for action, passed his bills with little debate and few changes; the banking bill was passed by the House after less than an hour's discussion, from a single copy read aloud.

Legacy

The Hundred Days established the modern activist presidency and the expectation that a new president arrives with a programme ready to enact. Historians continue to debate the economic effectiveness of the measures, several of which — the NIRA and parts of the AAA — were later struck down by the Supreme Court, but the period's political and institutional legacy, from deposit insurance to the TVA, proved durable.
