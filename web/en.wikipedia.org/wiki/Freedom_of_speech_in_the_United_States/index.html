Freedom of speech in the United States

In the United States, freedom of speech and expression is strongly protected from government restrictions by the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, many state constitutions, and state and federal laws. The Supreme Court of the United States has recognised several categories of speech that are given lesser or no protection by the First Amendment, and has recognised that governments may enact reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions on speech.

The First Amendment's constitutional right of free speech, which applies to state and local governments under the incorporation doctrine, prevents only government restrictions on speech, not restrictions imposed by private individuals or businesses unless they are acting on behalf of the government. However, laws may restrict the ability of private businesses and individuals from restricting the speech of others, such as employment laws that prevent employers from preventing employees from discussing their own interests.

History

Freedom of speech in British America was shaped by the trial of John Peter Zenger in 1735 and by English common law. After independence, the First Amendment was ratified in 1791. The Sedition Act of 1798 provoked an early controversy over its scope. During the First World War, the Supreme Court upheld convictions for anti-war speech in cases such as Schenck v. United States (1919), where Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. introduced the "clear and present danger" test. Doctrine shifted over the twentieth century toward broader protection, culminating in Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), which protects advocacy of violence or illegality unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it.

Types of speech

Political speech occupies the core of First Amendment protection. Commercial speech receives intermediate protection under the Central Hudson test. The Supreme Court has held that the First Amendment generally prohibits viewpoint discrimination and most content-based restrictions, which are subject to strict scrutiny. Categories historically treated as unprotected or less protected include obscenity, defamation, fraud, incitement, fighting words, true threats and speech integral to criminal conduct.

Free speech zones and public forums

Under public forum doctrine, government property is classified as traditional public forums, designated public forums, or nonpublic forums, with the level of permissible regulation varying accordingly. Reasonable time, place and manner restrictions must be content-neutral, narrowly tailored to a significant government interest, and leave open ample alternative channels of communication.
