John Broadus Watson (January 9, 1878 – September 25, 1958) was an American psychologist who popularized the scientific theory of behaviorism, establishing it as a psychological school. Through his research on animal behavior, human child-rearing, and advertising, he helped change psychology from a study of consciousness to a science of observable behavior.

Behaviorism

In his 1913 lecture "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It"—often called the behaviorist manifesto—Watson argued that psychology should study only observable behavior, discarding introspection and speculation about mental states. He emphasized environment over heredity, famously claiming that, given a dozen healthy infants, he could train any one of them to become any type of specialist regardless of innate talents.

Little Albert experiment

With Rosalie Rayner, Watson conducted the 1920 "Little Albert" experiment, conditioning an infant to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud noise, and showing the fear generalized to other furry objects. The study is now regarded as ethically unacceptable by modern standards.

Later career

A scandal over his affair with Rayner forced his resignation from Johns Hopkins in 1920. Watson moved into advertising, rising to a vice-presidency at the J. Walter Thompson agency, where he applied behaviorist principles to consumer marketing. He continued to write popular works on child-rearing, including Psychological Care of Infant and Child (1928).
