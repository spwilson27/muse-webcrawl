Microchip implant (animal)

A microchip implant is an identifying integrated circuit placed under the skin of an animal. The chips, about the size of a large grain of rice, use passive radio-frequency identification (RFID) technology: they carry no battery and are read when a scanner passes over them, transmitting a unique identification number linked to records in a pet recovery database.

Use in pets

Microchipping is widely used for dogs, cats and other companion animals to reunite lost pets with their owners. Implantation, usually between the shoulder blades, is done with a hypodermic needle and requires no anaesthesia. Several countries, including the United Kingdom (for dogs, since 2016) and many others, make microchipping compulsory, and chips are commonly required for international pet travel under schemes such as the EU Pet Travel Scheme.

Standards and problems

The ISO 11784/11785 standards specify a 15-digit code structure and the FDX-B transmission protocol used in most of the world; in the United States several incompatible frequencies were historically used, though modern scanners are typically universal. Chips can occasionally migrate from the implant site, and the system's usefulness depends on owners keeping their contact details current in the registry. Chips are also used for livestock, laboratory animals, zoo animals and wildlife research, and for marking valuable items such as racehorses.
