Alfred Dreyfus (9 October 1859 – 12 July 1935) was a French artillery officer of Jewish ancestry whose wrongful conviction for treason in 1894 became the centre of the Dreyfus affair, the bitter political and social crisis that divided the French Third Republic for more than a decade. Fully exonerated in 1906, he was restored to the army and served in the First World War.

Early life

Born in Mulhouse, Alsace, to a prosperous textile-manufacturing family, Dreyfus opted for French nationality after Germany's annexation of Alsace in 1871 and pursued a military career. A graduate of the École Polytechnique, he rose to captain and was appointed a trainee on the General Staff in Paris — one of very few Jewish officers to reach it.

The affair

In 1894 a cleaning woman recovered from the German embassy a torn memorandum (the bordereau) offering French military secrets. Suspicion fell on Dreyfus largely because of his background; on the basis of a disputed handwriting identification and secret evidence withheld from the defence, a court-martial convicted him unanimously. He was publicly degraded in January 1895 — his insignia torn off and sword broken — and deported for life to Devil's Island in French Guiana, where he was held in harsh solitary confinement. Evidence pointing to the real author, Major Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy, was uncovered by Lt-Colonel Georges Picquart in 1896 and suppressed; Émile Zola's open letter J'accuse…! (January 1898) forced the case into the open. A retrial at Rennes in 1899 again found Dreyfus guilty "with extenuating circumstances", and he accepted a presidential pardon. Only in 1906 did the Court of Cassation annul the verdict entirely and declare him innocent.

Later life

Reinstated as a major and made a Chevalier of the Legion of Honour, Dreyfus served in the First World War, including at Verdun and the Chemin des Dames, retiring as a lieutenant-colonel. He died in Paris in 1935 and was buried at Montparnasse Cemetery.
