Naresuan the Great (Thai: สมเด็จพระนเรศวรมหาราช; 1555/56 – 1605) was the 18th king of the Ayutthaya Kingdom and the ruler of Siam from 1590 until his death in 1605. Naresuan is one of Thailand's most revered monarchs, celebrated for freeing Siam from Burmese domination after the fall of Ayutthaya in 1569.

Early life and hostage years

Naresuan was the son of Maha Thammaracha, the governor of Phitsanulok who became a vassal king under the Burmese Toungoo Empire. As a child, Naresuan was taken to Pegu (Bago) as a royal hostage, where he was educated in Burmese military arts alongside Burmese princes. He returned to Siam in 1571 and governed Phitsanulok as viceroy, earning a reputation as an able commander in campaigns against Cambodia and the Lao kingdoms.

Restoration of independence

In 1584, after a rupture with the Burmese crown prince, Naresuan formally renounced Siamese allegiance to Burma at Kraeng. Over the following decade he repelled a series of large-scale Burmese invasions. The decisive moment came in 1593 at the Battle of Nong Sarai, where — according to Thai tradition — Naresuan killed the Burmese crown prince Mingyi Swa in single combat from elephant-back, a duel commemorated in Thai art, literature, and film.

Reign

As king, Naresuan went on the offensive, campaigning into lower Burma and Tenasserim and extending Siamese influence over the Shan states and Lan Na. He reformed the kingdom's military organization, promoted trade with foreign merchants including the Dutch and Portuguese, and strengthened Ayutthaya as a commercial port. He died in 1605 while campaigning in the north and was succeeded by his brother Ekathotsarot.

Legacy

Naresuan is honoured throughout Thailand with monuments — most famously the Don Chedi memorial at Suphan Buri — and a major film series, The Legend of King Naresuan, retells his life. The Royal Thai Armed Forces Day, observed on 18 January, marks the traditional date of the elephant duel.
