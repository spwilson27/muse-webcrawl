Thatcherism

Thatcherism is the political and economic ideology associated with British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher, leader of the Conservative Party from 1975 to 1990 and Prime Minister from 1979 to 1990. It combined free-market economic policies — deregulation, privatisation of state-owned industries, reduced direct taxation, monetarist control of inflation and curbs on trade union power — with a strong central state in areas such as law and order and defence, and an assertive nationalism in foreign policy.

Origins and ideas

Thatcherism drew on classical liberal and neoliberal thought, particularly the work of Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman, transmitted to British politics through think tanks such as the Institute of Economic Affairs and the Centre for Policy Studies (co-founded by Thatcher and Keith Joseph). It rejected the post-war consensus of Keynesian demand management and the mixed economy, holding that state intervention had caused Britain's economic decline.

In government

Key policies included the sale of council houses under the "Right to Buy", the privatisation of industries including British Telecom, British Gas, British Airways, electricity, water and steel, the defeat of the miners' strike of 1984–85, tax reforms, and the replacement of domestic rates with the Community Charge ("poll tax"), which proved deeply unpopular and contributed to Thatcher's fall in 1990.

Debate and legacy

Supporters credit Thatcherism with reviving the British economy, curbing inflation and restoring national confidence, symbolised by victory in the Falklands War (1982). Critics associate it with mass unemployment in industrial regions, deindustrialisation, rising inequality and social division; Thatcher's 1987 remark that "there is no such thing as society" remains controversial. Her successors, from John Major to Tony Blair's New Labour, retained much of the economic settlement, leading some commentators to describe Thatcherism's legacy as a lasting transformation of British politics.
