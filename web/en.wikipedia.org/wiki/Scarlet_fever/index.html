Scarlet fever

Specialty: Infectious disease
Symptoms: Sore throat, fever, red rash, "strawberry tongue"
Cause: Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes)
Treatment: Antibiotics (e.g. penicillin)

The scarlet rash and "strawberry tongue" typical of the disease.

Scarlet fever, also known as scarlatina, is an infectious disease caused by toxin-producing strains of group A Streptococcus bacteria (Streptococcus pyogenes), the same organism responsible for strep throat. It occurs most often in children and young adolescents, typically alongside or following a streptococcal throat infection.

Signs and symptoms

The illness usually begins with fever, sore throat, headache and swollen lymph nodes. Within a day or two a characteristic fine, red, sandpaper-like rash appears, starting on the trunk and spreading outward; the area around the mouth often remains pale (circumoral pallor), and creases such as those of the elbow show deeper red lines (Pastia's lines). The tongue may appear first white-coated and then bright red — the "strawberry tongue". After the fever subsides, the skin often peels, especially on the hands and feet.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical and can be confirmed with a throat swab, rapid antigen test or culture. Scarlet fever is treated with antibiotics, typically penicillin or amoxicillin, which shorten the illness, reduce contagiousness and prevent most complications. Untreated infection can lead to rheumatic fever, kidney inflammation (post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis), ear infections and abscesses.

Epidemiology and history

Scarlet fever was once a leading cause of death in children, with severe epidemics in the 19th century; its severity declined markedly even before antibiotics, for reasons not fully understood, though occasional resurgences, as in the United Kingdom and East Asia in the 2010s, have been reported. There is no vaccine; prevention relies on hygiene and prompt treatment of cases.
