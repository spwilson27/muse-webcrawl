Elevation

The elevation of a geographic location is its height above or below a fixed reference point, most commonly a reference geoid, a mathematical model of the Earth's sea level as an equipotential gravitational surface. The term elevation is mainly used when referring to points on the Earth's surface, while altitude or geopotential height is used for points above the surface, such as an aircraft in flight or a spacecraft in orbit, and depth is used for points below the surface.

Measurement

Elevation is measured in metres or feet above mean sea level, relative to a vertical datum. Traditional surveying established elevations by levelling from tide-gauge benchmarks; modern determinations use the Global Positioning System, whose raw heights are referenced to an ellipsoid and must be corrected by a geoid model (such as EGM96) to give elevations comparable to sea level. The highest point on Earth is the summit of Mount Everest, at 8,848.86 metres (29,031.7 ft) as jointly announced by China and Nepal in 2020; the lowest land point is the shore of the Dead Sea, about 430 metres below sea level.

Maps and GIS

On topographic maps, elevation is represented by contour lines joining points of equal height, by hypsometric tinting, and by spot heights and benchmarks. In geographic information systems, elevation over an area is stored as a digital elevation model (DEM), either as a raster grid or a triangulated irregular network, and underpins analysis of watersheds, visibility, and terrain slope. Elevation data for most of the Earth's land surface are available from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission.

Effects

Elevation strongly influences climate—air temperature falls with height at an average lapse rate of about 6.5 °C per kilometre—and human physiology, where low oxygen pressure at high altitude causes altitude sickness unless acclimatization occurs. Political and athletic preparations for events at high-altitude venues, such as Mexico City, famously take elevation into account.
