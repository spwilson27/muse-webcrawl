The Egyptian language is an Afro-Asiatic language that was spoken in ancient Egypt. It has the longest documented history of any language, attested in writing from about 3400 BC until the 17th century AD, when its latest stage, Coptic, ceased to be a living vernacular. Coptic survives today as the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church.

Stages

Egyptologists traditionally divide the language into stages. Archaic Egyptian (before ~2600 BC) is known from early labels and inscriptions. Old Egyptian (~2600–2000 BC) is the language of the Pyramid Texts. Middle Egyptian (~2000–1300 BC) became the classical literary standard, used for monumental and literary texts for the rest of pharaonic history. Late Egyptian (~1300–700 BC), the vernacular of the New Kingdom, appears in letters, tales, and administrative documents. Demotic (~650 BC – 5th century AD) was both a stage of the language and its cursive script. Coptic, written in a Greek-derived alphabet, is the final stage, emerging in the first centuries AD.

Writing systems

Egyptian was written in hieroglyphs for monumental inscriptions, in the cursive hieratic script for everyday documents on papyrus, and later in demotic. The writing system combined logograms (signs standing for words), phonograms (signs standing for one, two, or three consonants), and determinatives (silent signs clarifying meaning). Vowels were not written, so the pronunciation of ancient Egyptian is reconstructed only approximately, partly through Coptic.

Decipherment

Knowledge of the scripts was lost in late antiquity. The discovery of the Rosetta Stone in 1799 — bearing the same decree in hieroglyphs, demotic, and Greek — enabled Jean-François Champollion to announce the decipherment of hieroglyphs in 1822, founding modern Egyptology.

Grammar

Egyptian is a synthetic language with a typically Afro-Asiatic root-and-pattern morphology. Earlier stages favour verb–subject–object word order; the verbal system relies heavily on a suffix conjugation and, in later stages, on auxiliary constructions. Nouns distinguish masculine and feminine gender and singular, dual, and plural number.
