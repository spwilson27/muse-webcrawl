Wage theft

Wage theft is the failure to pay workers the full wages to which they are legally entitled. It is a form of wealth transfer from low-income working people to employers and shareholders. Wage theft can take many forms, including:

  • Failure to pay overtime
  • Failure to pay the minimum wage
  • Misclassification of employees as independent contractors
  • Illegal deductions from pay
  • Failure to pay for all hours worked, including work performed "off the clock"
  • Confiscation of tips
  • Failure to pay final wages to departing employees
  • Failure to provide legally mandated meal and rest breaks (or pay in lieu)

Scope

Studies suggest wage theft is widespread and amounts to billions of dollars annually in the United States alone. A landmark 2009 study by the National Employment Law Project, surveying low-wage workers in New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles, found that 26% had been paid less than the minimum wage in the previous week, and 76% of those who had worked overtime had not received the legally required overtime premium. A 2017 study by the Economic Policy Institute estimated that minimum-wage violations alone cost affected workers an average of $64 per week — roughly $3,300 per year — totalling about $15 billion annually across the ten most populous US states.

Some analyses have compared the aggregate scale of wage theft unfavourably with all other forms of property crime combined, noting that reported wage theft exceeds the total value of burglaries, robberies, and motor-vehicle thefts reported to police in the United States, while enforcement resources devoted to it are far smaller.

Enforcement and remedies

In the United States, wage claims may be pursued through the Wage and Hour Division of the Department of Labor, state labour agencies, or private litigation under the Fair Labor Standards Act and state wage-and-hour laws. Critics argue that penalties are often too low to deter violations and that workers — particularly immigrants and low-wage workers — fear retaliation for filing claims. Several states and cities have responded with stronger statutes, treble-damage provisions, and dedicated wage-theft enforcement units.
