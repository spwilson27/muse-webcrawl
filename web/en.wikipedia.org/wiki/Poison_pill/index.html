Poison pill

Shareholder rights plan
TypeAnti-takeover defence
MechanismFlip-in / flip-over rights
OriginUnited States, early 1980s
Key caseMoran v. Household International (1985)

A poison pill is a type of defensive tactic used by a corporation's board of directors against a takeover. In the field of mergers and acquisitions, shareholder rights plans are used to deter hostile takeovers: once a hostile bidder acquires a threshold percentage of the target's shares, existing shareholders (other than the bidder) gain the right to buy additional shares at a steep discount, massively diluting the bidder's stake and making the acquisition prohibitively expensive.

A shareholder rights plan dilutes a hostile acquirer's stake once a trigger threshold is crossed.

History

The poison pill was invented in the early 1980s by takeover lawyer Martin Lipton of Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz. It was first used successfully in 1982, when corporate raider T. Boone Pickens targeted General American Oil; the target's board issued preferred shares bearing special voting rights designed to defeat the bid. The Delaware Supreme Court upheld the validity of rights plans in Moran v. Household International, Inc. (1985), confirming that directors could adopt such plans as a preventive measure.

Variants

Under a flip-in plan, if an acquirer crosses the ownership threshold, all shareholders except the acquirer may buy shares at a deep discount. Under a flip-over plan, target shareholders gain the right to buy the acquirer's shares at a discount after a merger. A related device, the "dead-hand" pill, allows only directors who were in office when the plan was adopted to redeem it, though such provisions have been struck down in Delaware and New York.

Criticism and use

Critics argue poison pills entrench incumbent management and suppress shareholder value by blocking premium bids, while proponents contend they give boards bargaining power to negotiate higher prices or seek alternative buyers. Rights plans became famous again in 2022 when Twitter's board adopted one in response to Elon Musk's unsolicited bid, and in high-profile cases involving companies such as Netflix (against Carl Icahn) and Airgas (against Air Products).
