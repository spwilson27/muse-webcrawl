Dentin

Dentin (also spelled dentine) is a calcified tissue of the body and, along with enamel, cementum, and pulp, is one of the four major components of teeth. It is usually covered by enamel on the crown and cementum on the root and surrounds the entire pulp. By weight, 70% of dentin consists of the mineral hydroxyapatite, 20% is organic material, and 10% is water. In appearance it is yellow-hued and softer than enamel.

Structure

Dentin is secreted by odontoblasts, whose cell bodies line the pulp chamber while their processes extend into microscopic channels called dentinal tubules that radiate outward through the dentin. Unlike enamel, dentin is a living tissue that continues to form throughout life: primary dentin forms before root completion, secondary dentin forms slowly afterward, and tertiary (reparative) dentin forms in response to stimuli such as caries or attrition.

Function and sensitivity

Dentin supports the brittle enamel above it and cushions the tooth during chewing. Because its tubules communicate with the pulp, exposed dentin can be sensitive to hot, cold, sweet, or tactile stimuli; the hydrodynamic theory attributes dentin hypersensitivity to fluid movement within the tubules stimulating pulpal nerves.

Clinical significance

Once tooth decay breaches the enamel it spreads more rapidly through the softer dentin. Developmental disorders such as dentinogenesis imperfecta produce weak, discolored dentin and teeth prone to wear and fracture.
