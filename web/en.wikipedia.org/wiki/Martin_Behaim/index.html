Martin Behaim

Martin Behaim (6 October 1459 – 29 July 1507), also known as Martin von Behaim and by the Portuguese form Martinho da Boémia, was a German textile merchant and cartographer in the service of King John II of Portugal. He is best known for the Erdapfel, the oldest surviving terrestrial globe, which he made in Nuremberg in 1492 — before news of Columbus's first voyage reached Europe.

Life

Born in Nuremberg into a merchant family, Behaim worked in the Flanders cloth trade before travelling to Lisbon around 1480. He claimed to have studied navigation and astronomy and to have sailed on Portuguese voyages down the West African coast, possibly with Diogo Cão. Modern historians doubt some of his claims, but he was knighted and served on the Portuguese junta of mathematicians advising on navigation.

The Erdapfel

Returning to Nuremberg in 1490, Behaim directed the construction of a globe about 51 cm in diameter, painted by Georg Glockendon from Behaim's designs. It shows the world as understood in 1492: Eurasia and Africa recognisable but with a greatly elongated Asia, no Americas, and mythical islands scattered in the ocean where the New World would soon be found. The globe is preserved in the Germanisches Nationalmuseum, Nuremberg, and is inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World register.
