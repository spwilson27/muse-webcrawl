Polonium-210 (210Po, also historically called radium F) is an isotope of polonium. It undergoes alpha decay to stable lead-206 with a half-life of 138.376 days, the longest of all naturally occurring polonium isotopes. First identified in 1898 by Marie and Pierre Curie during their discovery of polonium, it is a member of the uranium-238 decay chain, formed from the decay of bismuth-210.

Properties and production

Polonium-210 is one of the most radiotoxic substances known: a single gram emits 166 terabecquerels of alpha radiation and generates about 140 watts of heat, enough to make a sealed sample glow. Because alpha particles are stopped by skin or paper, the isotope is comparatively harmless outside the body but extremely dangerous if inhaled or ingested, where it irradiates internal tissues at close range. It occurs naturally in trace amounts in uranium ores and in tobacco, and accumulates in small quantities in the environment from radon decay. Industrial production, mainly in Russia, uses neutron irradiation of bismuth-209 in nuclear reactors.

Uses

Applications include static-eliminators in industry, neutron sources when mixed with beryllium, and radioisotope thermoelectric generators and heat sources for spacecraft—polonium heaters were used on the Soviet Lunokhod lunar rovers.

Poisoning

Polonium-210 gained worldwide notoriety in November 2006, when former Russian intelligence officer Alexander Litvinenko was fatally poisoned with it in London; a British public inquiry concluded the poisoning was an operation of the Russian FSB probably approved at the highest level. In 2012, claims that Yasser Arafat had been poisoned with polonium-210 were investigated but produced inconclusive and contested results.
