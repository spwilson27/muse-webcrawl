Grigori Yefimovich Rasputin (21 January 1869 – 30 December 1916) was a Russian mystic and holy man who befriended the family of Nicholas II, the last Emperor of Russia, and gained considerable influence in the imperial court. A Siberian peasant from the village of Pokrovskoye, he made a pilgrimage to Mount Athos and, after arriving in St Petersburg in 1903–1905, presented himself as a starets (elder) and healer.

Influence at court

Rasputin's decisive point of entry was the imperial couple's desperation over their son, the Tsarevich Alexei, who suffered from haemophilia. On occasions when doctors could not stop the boy's bleeding, Rasputin's prayers — and his instruction to keep doctors away — appeared to coincide with Alexei's recovery, winning the absolute trust of Empress Alexandra. His influence grew during the First World War, when Nicholas took personal command of the army in 1915, leaving Alexandra effectively governing with Rasputin's counsel; ministers were appointed and dismissed at his prompting, which, combined with rumours of debauchery and treason, fatally discredited the monarchy.

Assassination

On the night of 29–30 December 1916, Rasputin was murdered by a group of conservative nobles led by Prince Felix Yusupov, the Tsar's nephew-in-law, together with Grand Duke Dmitri Pavlovich and the politician Vladimir Purishkevich. According to Yusupov's account, Rasputin survived poisoned cakes and wine and a first shooting before being shot again and thrown into the Malaya Nevka River, where his body was recovered days later; the autopsy indicated drowning was not the cause of death, though the legend of his superhuman resistance to death took permanent hold. He was buried at Tsarskoye Selo; after the February Revolution his body was exhumed and burned.

Legacy

Rasputin remains one of the most mythologised figures of the late Russian Empire — the "Mad Monk" of popular culture, subject of songs, films and a famous 1978 disco hit by Boney M. Historians debate the real extent of his political influence, while agreeing that the scandal surrounding him hastened the collapse of Romanov rule, which fell barely two months after his death.
