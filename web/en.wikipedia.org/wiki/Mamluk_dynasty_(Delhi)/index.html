The Mamluk dynasty (Persian: سلطنت مملوک), also known as the Slave dynasty or Ghulam dynasty, was the first dynasty to rule the Delhi Sultanate, governing much of northern India from 1206 to 1290. It was founded by Qutb al-Din Aibak, a Turkic mamluk (slave-soldier) general of the Ghurid ruler Muhammad of Ghor, who seized the Ghurid territories in India after his master's assassination in 1206.

The dynasty is notable because several of its sultans had themselves begun life as slaves, rising through military service to the throne. Its rulers consolidated Muslim rule in the Gangetic plain and repelled the Mongol invasions that devastated much of Asia.

History

Qutb al-Din Aibak (1206–1210)

Aibak, purchased as a slave and raised to high command under Muhammad of Ghor, was left in charge of the Indian conquests. After 1206 he ruled from Lahore and then Delhi, though he never formally assumed the title of Sultan and struck no coins in his own name. He began construction of the Qutb Minar in Delhi and the Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque. He died in 1210 after a fall from his horse during a game of polo (chaugan) at Lahore.

Iltutmish (1211–1236)

Aibak's son-in-law and former slave Iltutmish defeated a rival claimant and moved the capital firmly to Delhi. He is regarded as the real consolidator of the Sultanate: he received formal investiture from the Abbasid Caliph of Baghdad in 1229, introduced a standard silver coin (the tanka) and copper jital, organised the iqta system of land assignments, and formed a corps of elite slave officers known as the Turkan-e-Chahalgani ("the Forty"). He completed the Qutb Minar and successfully refused asylum to the Khwarezmian prince Jalal al-Din, thereby avoiding a direct clash with Genghis Khan in 1221.

Razia Sultana (1236–1240)

Iltutmish nominated his daughter Razia as his successor — an extraordinary step. Razia Sultana ruled effectively for nearly four years, discarding the veil, appearing in public, and leading armies, before she was overthrown by the Turkish nobility and killed in 1240. She remains the only woman to have ruled Delhi in the Sultanate period.

The Forty and Balban (1240–1287)

After Razia's fall, a succession of weak sultans was dominated by the Forty. In 1266 Ghiyas ud din Balban, himself a former slave of Iltutmish and a member of the Forty, took the throne. Balban destroyed the power of the Forty, imposed a rigid court ceremonial modelled on Persian kingship, built a chain of forts against the Mongols, and restored order in the Doab and around Delhi. His death in 1287, shortly after the death of his heir Muhammad Khan in battle against the Mongols, gravely weakened the dynasty.

Decline and fall

Balban's grandson Muiz ud din Qaiqabad was paralysed by a stroke, and in 1290 the aged Khalji commander Jalaluddin Firuz Khalji seized power, ending the Mamluk dynasty and founding the Khalji dynasty.

Culture and architecture

The dynasty introduced Perso-Islamic architecture to northern India on a large scale. Surviving monuments include the Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque, the Qutb Minar, the tomb of Iltutmish, and Balban's tomb in Mehrauli, which contains one of the earliest true arches in India.
