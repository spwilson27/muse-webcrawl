Italian war crimes

Italian occupation forces in Yugoslavia during the Second World War.

Italian war crimes were crimes committed by the armed forces of the Kingdom of Italy during the colonial wars of the late 19th and early 20th centuries and during the Second World War, principally in Libya, Ethiopia, the Balkans, and Greece.

Colonial campaigns

During the pacification of Libya (1923–1932), Italian forces under Rodolfo Graziani used concentration camps in Cyrenaica in which tens of thousands of Libyans died. In the Second Italo-Ethiopian War (1935–36), Italy employed mustard gas against Ethiopian soldiers and civilians in violation of the Geneva Protocol, and after an assassination attempt on Graziani in 1937, reprisals in Addis Ababa killed thousands; the massacre at Debre Libanos monastery followed.

Second World War

In occupied Yugoslavia and Greece, Italian forces carried out reprisal killings, deportations, and internment in camps such as Rab and Gonars, where Slovene and Croat internees died in large numbers from starvation and disease. In Greece, massacres such as that at Domenikon in 1943 were committed by Italian troops. Italian forces also participated in the deportation of Jews from their occupation zones after September 1943, though before the armistice Italian authorities had often refused German demands to hand over Jews in areas under their control.

Aftermath

Unlike German and Japanese leaders, few Italian commanders were prosecuted after the war. Italy was not required to extradite suspected war criminals, and the subject was long overshadowed by the popular image of italiani brava gente — "Italians, good people" — a national myth historians have since subjected to critical scrutiny. Ethiopia and other states pursued compensation claims for decades.
