Deployable structure

A deployable structure is a structure that can change shape or volume, transforming from a compact, folded configuration to an expanded, predetermined form. Deployability is valuable wherever a structure must be transported efficiently and erected quickly — in spaceflight, disaster relief, temporary architecture, and medicine.

Mechanisms and types

  • Scissor-hinge (pantograph) structures — grids of paired bars pivoted at their centres, popularised by engineers such as Félix Escrig and Emilio Pérez Piñero, used for deployable roofs, bridges, and shelters.
  • Foldable plate and origami structures — rigid panels joined by hinges that fold flat following patterns such as the Miura fold, used for maps, solar arrays, and packaging.
  • Tensegrity and cable-strut systems — networks of struts and tensioned cables that stiffen as they deploy.
  • Membrane and inflatable structures — surfaces deployed and stiffened by internal pressure, such as inflatable antennas and habitats.
  • Coilable masts and tape-spring booms — slender elements that flatten and coil for storage and spring back to shape, standard on satellites.

Applications

The most demanding applications are in space: solar arrays, antennas such as mesh reflectors, and sunshields (the James Webb Space Telescope's multi-layer sunshield and segmented mirror are celebrated examples) must unfold reliably after launch. On Earth, deployable systems serve as emergency shelters and hospitals, retractable stadium roofs, rapidly erected military bridges, and minimally invasive medical devices such as expandable stents.
