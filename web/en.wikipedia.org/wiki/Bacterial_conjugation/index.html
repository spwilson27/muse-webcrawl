Bacterial conjugation

Conjugation

TypeHorizontal gene transfer
Discovered1946, by Joshua Lederberg and Edward Tatum
Key structuresSex pilus, conjugative plasmid, type IV secretion system

Bacterial conjugation is the transfer of genetic material between bacterial cells by direct cell-to-cell contact or by a bridge-like connection between two cells. It is one of the three principal mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer in bacteria, the others being transformation and transduction. Conjugation is a major route by which genes for antibiotic resistance spread through bacterial populations.

History

Conjugation was discovered in 1946 by Joshua Lederberg and Edward Tatum, who showed that genetic recombination could occur in Escherichia coli. William Hayes and others later established that transfer is unidirectional, from a donor (male) cell to a recipient (female) cell, and identified the fertility factor (F factor) responsible.

Mechanism

In the classic F-plasmid system of E. coli:

  1. The donor cell (F+) produces a sex pilus, encoded by the plasmid's tra genes, which attaches to a recipient (F−) cell and retracts, drawing the cells together.
  2. A mating bridge forms. A nick is made at the origin of transfer (oriT) of the plasmid, and one strand of the plasmid DNA is transferred to the recipient in a 5′ to 3′ direction by a rolling-circle mechanism.
  3. Complementary strands are synthesised in both cells, so the donor retains a copy of the plasmid and the recipient becomes F+.

Transfer of a complete F plasmid takes roughly 2 minutes; transfer of an entire integrated chromosome (in Hfr strains) takes about 100 minutes and is usually interrupted before completion.

Hfr and F′ strains

When the F plasmid integrates into the chromosome, the resulting Hfr (high frequency of recombination) strain transfers chromosomal genes to recipients at high frequency; the order and timing of gene transfer allowed early mapping of the E. coli chromosome in minutes. Imprecise excision of an integrated F plasmid produces an F′ plasmid carrying neighbouring chromosomal genes.

Significance

Conjugative plasmids — including R plasmids carrying multiple antibiotic-resistance genes, virulence plasmids, and degradative plasmids — can cross species and even genus boundaries. Conjugation therefore has major consequences for medicine (spread of resistance in pathogens such as MRSA and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales), agriculture, and microbial evolution. In Agrobacterium tumefaciens, a conjugation-like system transfers T-DNA from the Ti plasmid into plant cells, a process exploited in plant genetic engineering.
