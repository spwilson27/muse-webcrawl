Marine Mammal Protection Act

The Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA) is a United States federal law enacted on 21 October 1972, the first legislation anywhere to mandate an ecosystem-based approach to marine resource management. The Act prohibits, with certain exceptions, the "taking" of marine mammals — whales, dolphins, porpoises, seals, sea lions, walruses, manatees, dugongs, sea otters, and polar bears — in US waters and by US citizens on the high seas, as well as the importation of marine mammals and marine mammal products into the United States.

Provisions

The MMPA imposed a general moratorium on the taking and importation of marine mammals and established that populations should be maintained at, or restored to, their optimum sustainable population levels. Management authority is divided: the National Marine Fisheries Service (NOAA Fisheries) is responsible for cetaceans and most pinnipeds, while the US Fish and Wildlife Service manages walruses, sea otters, polar bears, and manatees. The independent Marine Mammal Commission provides oversight and scientific advice.

Permits and exceptions

The Act allows exceptions through permits for scientific research, public display, and enhancement of species survival; incidental taking authorizations for commercial fisheries and other activities; and exemptions for Alaska Natives, who may take marine mammals for subsistence and for creating authentic native handicrafts. Amendments in 1988, 1992, and 1994 added programmes addressing interactions with commercial fisheries, including the tuna–dolphin issue, and introduced the Potential Biological Removal framework for stock assessment.

Impact

Under the MMPA, many US marine mammal populations have recovered significantly, including gray whales, northern elephant seals, and California sea lions, while others, such as the North Atlantic right whale and the vaquita (in Mexico), remain of grave concern. The Act's import provisions have been influential internationally, pressing other nations to adopt comparable protections as a condition of access to US seafood markets.
