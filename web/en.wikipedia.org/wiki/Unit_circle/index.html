Unit circle

In mathematics, a unit circle is a circle of radius one centered at the origin of a coordinate system. In the Euclidean plane it is the set of points satisfying

x² + y² = 1

The unit circle is fundamental in trigonometry because the coordinates of a point reached by moving an angle θ from the positive x-axis are (cos θ, sin θ). Thus cosine gives the horizontal coordinate and sine gives the vertical coordinate. The tangent may be understood as the ratio sin θ / cos θ, subject to cos θ ≠ 0.

Angles and periodic functions

Angles are naturally measured in radians on the unit circle: an angle of θ radians corresponds to an arc of length θ. A full revolution is 2π radians. Adding or subtracting multiples of 2π returns to the same point, explaining the periodicity of sine and cosine. Quadrants determine the signs of coordinates and hence the signs of the trigonometric functions.

Complex numbers

In the complex plane, points on the unit circle have absolute value one and may be written eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ, an identity known as Euler's formula. Multiplication by such a number rotates another complex number by θ without changing its magnitude. The unit circle is therefore the circle group, an important example in geometry, analysis and algebra.

Uses

Unit-circle diagrams are used to derive values for common angles, identities such as sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, symmetry properties of trigonometric functions, and solutions to trigonometric equations. General circles and periodic motion can be reduced to the unit case by scaling and translation.
