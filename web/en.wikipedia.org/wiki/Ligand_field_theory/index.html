Ligand field theory

Ligand field theory (LFT) describes the bonding, orbital arrangement, and other characteristics of coordination complexes. It represents an application of molecular orbital theory to transition metal complexes. A transition metal ion has nine valence atomic orbitals — consisting of five (n)d, one (n+1)s, and three (n+1)p orbitals. These orbitals are of appropriate energy to form bonding interaction with ligands.

The LFT analysis is highly dependent on the geometry of the complex, but most explanations begin by describing octahedral complexes, where six ligands coordinate to the metal. Other complexes can be described by reference to crystal field theory.

History

Ligand field theory was developed during the 1930s and 1940s by combining the principles of molecular orbital theory and crystal field theory to describe the bonding in coordination complexes more completely than either predecessor theory could alone. Hans Bethe published the foundations of crystal field theory in 1929, and John Hasbrouck van Vleck developed the ideas further in the 1930s. Leslie Orgel was among those who applied ligand field theory extensively in the 1950s, and his 1960 textbook An Introduction to Transition-metal Chemistry: Ligand-field Theory popularized the subject.

Comparison to crystal field theory

Crystal field theory (CFT) treats the interaction between the metal ion and the ligands as a purely electrostatic, point-charge interaction. It successfully explains the splitting of the metal d orbitals and the origins of colour and magnetism in many complexes, but it cannot account for the spectrochemical series ordering of ligands, in particular why neutral ligands such as carbon monoxide are such strong field ligands while hydroxide is weak. Ligand field theory incorporates covalent bonding through molecular orbital considerations, allowing it to rationalize these trends.

High-spin and low-spin complexes

The arrangement of ligands around a metal ion splits the five d orbitals into sets of different energy. Whether electrons pair up in the lower set or occupy the higher set depends on the magnitude of the splitting relative to the pairing energy, giving rise to low-spin and high-spin configurations respectively, with distinct magnetic and spectroscopic properties.
