David Ogilvy

This article is about the advertising executive. For other uses, see David Ogilvy (businessman).

David Mackenzie Ogilvy CBE (23 June 1911 – 21 July 1999) was a British advertising tycoon, founder of Ogilvy & Mather, and known as the "Father of Advertising." Having worked as a chef, door-to-door salesman, social worker, and farmer, he founded his New York agency in 1948 at the age of 37 and built it into one of the world's largest advertising groups, famous for campaigns for Hathaway shirts, Rolls-Royce, and Dove.

Career

Born in West Horsley, Surrey, Ogilvy attended Fettes College and won a scholarship to Oxford, which he left without a degree. He apprenticed as a chef in Paris, sold Aga cookers door-to-door in Scotland (writing a sales manual still admired today), worked for his brother at the London agency Mather & Crowther, studied American advertising on a fellowship, worked for George Gallup's research institute, and farmed in Pennsylvania. During the Second World War he served in British intelligence in Washington.

In 1948 he founded Hewitt, Ogilvy, Benson & Mather in New York with $6,000. His "Man in the Hathaway Shirt" (1951) — a model wearing an eyepatch — made the agency's name, and his 1958 Rolls-Royce headline, "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock," became perhaps the most famous advertisement ever written. He insisted on research, long copy, and respect for the consumer: "The customer is not a moron. She is your wife."

Books and legacy

His books Confessions of an Advertising Man (1963) and Ogilvy on Advertising (1983) remain staples of the profession. He retired to the Château de Touffou in France. In 1989 his agency became part of WPP.
