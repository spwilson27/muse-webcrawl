Herman Webster Mudgett (May 16, 1861 – May 7, 1896), better known as Dr. Henry Howard Holmes or H. H. Holmes, was an American con artist and serial killer active between 1891 and 1894. He is often called one of America's first documented serial killers. Most of his crimes took place in Chicago around the time of the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition.

Early life

Mudgett was born in Gilmanton, New Hampshire, into a prosperous farming family. He graduated from the University of Michigan's medical school in 1884, during which period he stole and mutilated cadavers to commit insurance fraud. He moved to Chicago in 1886 and took work at a pharmacy in Englewood, whose owner later disappeared; Holmes acquired the business and the property.

The "Murder Castle"

Across from the pharmacy, Holmes built a three-story building, completed in 1892, with shops on the ground floor and rooms above. Later newspaper accounts dubbed it the "Murder Castle" and embellished it with claims of secret passages, soundproof rooms, gas pipes, trapdoors, a kiln and dissecting tables. In fact the building's upper floors served as apartments and offices, and some of Holmes's victims — employees, lovers and guests — were killed there, typically for insurance money or to silence them. The building was gutted by fire in 1895 and later demolished; a post office now stands on the site.

Pitezel murders and arrest

With his accomplice Benjamin Pitezel, Holmes ran a scheme in which Pitezel would fake his death in Philadelphia to collect a $10,000 life insurance payout. Instead Holmes killed Pitezel and then murdered three of Pitezel's children — Alice, Nellie and Howard — while travelling with the family. Insurance investigator and Pinkerton detective work led to Holmes's arrest in Boston in November 1894.

Trial and execution

Holmes was tried in Philadelphia for Pitezel's murder, convicted, and sentenced to death. He confessed to 27 murders in a sensational (and partly fabricated) paid confession. He was hanged at Moyamensing Prison on 7 May 1896. Nine murders are generally confirmed. His crimes were popularised for modern audiences by Erik Larson's 2003 book The Devil in the White City.
