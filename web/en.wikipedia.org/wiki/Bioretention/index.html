Bioretention

A bioretention cell (rain garden) treating car park runoff.

Bioretention is the process in which contaminants and sedimentation are removed from stormwater runoff. The main objective of the bioretention cell is to attenuate peak runoff and to remove stormwater runoff pollutants. Stormwater is firstly directed into the treatment area, which consists of a grass buffer strip, ponding area, organic layer or mulch layer, planting soil, and plants.

Design

A bioretention cell — often called a rain garden when small — is a shallow landscaped depression. Runoff ponds on the surface (typically 15–30 cm deep) and infiltrates through an engineered soil medium, usually a sandy loam with some organic matter, over a gravel drainage layer. An underdrain may discharge treated water to a storm sewer where native soils infiltrate poorly. Plants, ideally deep-rooted natives, maintain soil permeability and take up nutrients.

Pollutant removal

Filtration, adsorption, plant uptake and microbial processes remove suspended solids, phosphorus, nitrogen, heavy metals, oils and pathogens. Well-designed cells typically remove a large fraction of sediment and metals; nitrogen removal improves when a saturated (internal water storage) zone is included to promote denitrification.

History and use

Bioretention was developed in the early 1990s in Prince George's County, Maryland, as a low-impact development (LID) technique and is now a standard stormwater best management practice in the United States, Australia (where it is central to water-sensitive urban design) and elsewhere. Maintenance needs include removing accumulated sediment, replacing mulch, and clearing inlets.
