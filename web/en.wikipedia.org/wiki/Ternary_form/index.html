Ternary form

Ternary form, also called song form, is a three-part musical structure consisting of an opening section (A), a contrasting middle section (B), and a return of the opening material (A), commonly notated ABA. It is one of the most fundamental forms in Western music, found in everything from folk songs and hymns to large-scale symphonic movements.

The three sections of ternary form: A–B–A.

Characteristics

In a typical ternary form, sections A and B are each self-contained and often correspond to binary structures in their own right. The middle section contrasts with the outer ones in key (frequently moving to the dominant, relative major or minor, or subdominant), melody, mood or texture. The final A section may be repeated exactly or varied, and is often abbreviated.

Examples

The classic example is the minuet and trio of the Classical symphony and string quartet: minuet (A), trio (B), and minuet da capo (A). The Baroque da capo aria — standard in the operas and cantatas of Handel and Bach — is likewise ternary, with the repeat of the A section customarily ornamented by the singer. Many marches, including those of John Philip Sousa, follow a ternary scheme with a contrasting trio.

Compound ternary form

When each of the three sections is itself in ternary (or binary) form, the result is a compound ternary structure, as in the scherzo-and-trio movements of Beethoven and later composers.
