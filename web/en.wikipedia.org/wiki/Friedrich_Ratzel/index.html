Friedrich Ratzel

Friedrich Ratzel (30 August 1844 – 9 August 1904) was a German geographer and ethnographer whose works laid the foundations of human geography and of the modern study of the relationship between people and territory. He is known for the concept of Lebensraum (living space) and for his influential Anthropogeographie (1882–1891) and Politische Geographie (1897).

Biography

Ratzel was born in Karlsruhe and studied zoology at Heidelberg, where he was strongly influenced by Darwin's theory of evolution. He worked as a journalist and travelled widely, including a long tour of North America and Cuba in 1873–1875, whose accounts of the landscapes and peoples he encountered shaped his later scholarship.

Geographical thought

As professor at the Technical University of Munich and later the University of Leipzig, Ratzel argued that human societies could be understood through their interaction with the natural environment. His Völkerkunde (The History of Mankind) surveyed peoples around the world. His theory of the state as an organism competing for space became influential, though later regimes distorted his ideas for expansionist ends.

Legacy

Ratzel's ideas were transmitted to American geography through his student Ellen Churchill Semple, and to political thought through Karl Haushofer and Rudolf Kjellén. Modern geographers regard him as a central, if contested, founder of human geography and geopolitics.
