Parallel projection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In descriptive geometry and technical drawing, a parallel projection is a projection of an object in three-dimensional space onto a fixed plane, known as the projection plane or image plane, where the rays, known as lines of sight or projection lines, are parallel to each other. It is a basic tool in descriptive geometry. The projection is called orthographic if the rays are perpendicular (orthogonal) to the image plane, and oblique if they are not.

A parallel projection is a particular case of projection in mathematics and graphical projection in technical drawing. Parallel projections can be seen as a perspective projection where the center of projection is at an infinite distance from the projection plane. The concept has numerous applications in engineering drawing, architecture, and computer graphics.

Overview

Parallel projections are defined by three elements: the object being projected, the projection plane, and the family of parallel projection lines. When the image plane is not perpendicular to the viewing direction, lengths in the image can be foreshortened depending on orientation. Parallel projections preserve parallelism: two parallel lines in the object remain parallel in the image, a property that perspective projections do not share.

Because parallel projections preserve relative proportions along parallel lines, they are well suited to working drawings from which measurements can be taken directly, which is why they dominate engineering and architectural practice.

Orthographic projection

An orthographic projection is a parallel projection in which the projection lines are perpendicular to the projection plane. Important subtypes include the multiview projection, in which the object is rotated to align its principal faces with the projection plane (giving plan, front, and side views), and the axonometric projection, in which the object is rotated so that multiple faces are visible in a single view.

Axonometric projection

Axonometric projections are classified as isometric, dimetric, or trimetric, according to how many of the object's axes are foreshortened equally. In an isometric projection, all three axes are foreshortened by the same amount and appear at 120 degrees to one another. Axonometric views are widely used in technical illustration and in video games.

Oblique projection

In oblique projections, the projection lines strike the plane at an angle other than 90 degrees. The face of the object parallel to the projection plane is shown in its true shape, while receding edges are drawn at an angle, commonly 30, 45, or 60 degrees. Cavalier projection uses full-length receding edges, while cabinet projection halves them to reduce visual distortion.

Properties

Parallel projections have several useful properties: parallelism is preserved; ratios of lengths along parallel lines are preserved; and the projection of a circle lying in a plane parallel to the image plane remains a circle of the same radius. Circles in other planes project to ellipses. The chief limitation is the lack of depth cues: distant objects appear the same size as near ones, which can make parallel projections ambiguous without additional views.
