Zellige

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Zellige (Arabic: الزليج, romanized: zillīj; also zelij, zillij) is a style of mosaic tilework made from individually hand-chiseled glazed terracotta tiles set into a plaster base to form elaborate geometric patterns. It is characteristic of Moroccan architecture and, more broadly, of the Islamic architecture of the Maghreb and al-Andalus.

Technique

Craftsmen first produce large glazed tiles in a palette of colors — traditionally cobalt blue, turquoise, green, yellow, and white among others. Each small piece, or tessera, is then cut and chiseled by hand with a sharp hammer to a precise shape and beveled edge. Master craftsmen (maâlems) assemble the pieces face-down from memory or from pattern drawings, fitting them into panels of interlocking stars, polygons, and arabesques whose geometry reflects the Islamic tradition of aniconic decoration.

History

The art developed in the medieval Islamic west and reached its height under the Marinid dynasty of Morocco (13th–15th centuries) and the Nasrids of Granada. Masterpieces survive in the Alhambra in Spain and in the madrasas of Fez, such as the Bou Inania and Al-Attarine madrasas, where zellige covers walls, fountains, floors, and minarets. Fez remains the preeminent center of production, and the craft is still taught through apprenticeship and used in Moroccan buildings today.
