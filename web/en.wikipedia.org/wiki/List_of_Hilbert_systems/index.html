List of Hilbert systems

In mathematical logic, a Hilbert system (or Hilbert-style deductive system) is a formal system in which proofs are built principally from axioms and a small number of inference rules, typically modus ponens. This article lists axiomatizations of classical propositional logic in the Hilbert style, beginning with the pioneering systems of Gottlob Frege and David Hilbert and their collaborators.

Frege's system

Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) contained the first complete axiomatization of classical propositional logic, with six axioms using implication and negation, and modus ponens (along with substitution) as its rule of inference.

Hilbert and Ackermann

The system in Hilbert and Ackermann's Grundzüge der theoretischen Logik (1928) used axioms for disjunction, conjunction-free implication and negation, with substitution and modus ponens. Hilbert's earlier lectures (1917–1922, with Paul Bernays) developed related axiom sets.

Łukasiewicz and other classic sets

Jan Łukasiewicz found elegant three-axiom systems for implication and negation, e.g. (1) ((p → q) → ((q → r) → (p → r)?) in historical presentations and, most famously, the set: p → (q → p); (p → (q → r)) → ((p → q) → (p → r)); and (¬p → ¬q) → (q → p). Other single- and multi-axiom systems were given by Hilbert, Bernays, Russell and Whitehead (Principia Mathematica), Rosser, Tarski and others.

Implicational systems

For the purely implicational fragment of classical logic, systems typically use the two axioms K: p → (q → p) and S: (p → (q → r)) → ((p → q) → (p → r)), which correspond to the K and S combinators of combinatory logic. Meredith found a single axiom for classical implication with modus ponens.

Systems for other logics

Hilbert systems have also been given for intuitionistic logic, modal logics (such as the standard systems for K, T, S4 and S5), relevance logics and first-order predicate calculus, where quantifier axioms and the rule of generalisation are added. Hilbert systems contrast with natural deduction and sequent calculi, which have more rules but fewer axioms.
