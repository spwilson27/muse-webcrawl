Makoko is an informal settlement in Lagos, Nigeria, famous for the portion of the community built on stilts over the Lagos Lagoon, which has earned it the nickname "the Venice of Africa". Located off the mainland near the Third Mainland Bridge and the University of Lagos, Makoko was founded in the nineteenth century by Egun fishing communities, whose descendants and newer migrants from across Nigeria and neighbouring Benin and Togo now live in the settlement. Population estimates vary enormously, from under 100,000 to a quarter of a million people.

Life and economy

The waterborne part of Makoko consists of wooden houses raised on stilts above the lagoon, served by a network of canals travelled by canoe. Fishing and fish smoking are the mainstay of the economy, alongside timber milling on the shore, petty trade, and work in the wider Lagos economy. Services are rudimentary: most homes lack piped water, electricity comes from generators or informal connections, and sanitation drains directly into the lagoon, contributing to poor health outcomes. Community organisations, churches, mosques, and informal schools provide much of what the state does not.

Eviction attempts and demolition

Because Makoko occupies valuable waterfront land and is classified as informal, its residents have repeatedly faced forced eviction. In July 2012 the Lagos State government issued a 72-hour quit notice to the waterfront communities and demolished homes on the lagoon, displacing thousands; a community leader was shot dead during the operation, after which demolitions were suspended amid public outcry and litigation. Further demolition and eviction threats have recurred, and rights groups continue to press for upgrading rather than clearance.

Makoko Floating School

Makoko gained worldwide attention through the Makoko Floating School, a three-storey floating structure of timber and bamboo designed by architect Kunlé Adeyemi and his firm NLÉ, completed in 2013 as a prototype for climate-adaptive building in African coastal cities. The school won international design awards but collapsed during heavy rain in June 2016 (without casualties) after falling out of regular use, prompting debate about iconic projects versus durable infrastructure.
