Phronesis


Phronesis (Ancient Greek: φρόνησις) is practical wisdom — the intellectual virtue of deliberating well about what to do, central to Aristotle's ethics.

Phronesis (Ancient Greek: φρόνησις, romanized: phrónēsis) is a type of wisdom or intelligence relevant to practical action. Commonly translated as "practical wisdom" or "prudence", it is the virtue of practical thought in Aristotle's philosophy, discussed in Book VI of the Nicomachean Ethics.

Aristotle's account

Aristotle distinguishes phronesis from sophia (theoretical wisdom, concerned with necessary and eternal truths) and from techne (craft knowledge, aimed at producing an end outside the action itself). Phronesis concerns action (praxis) whose end is good action itself: it is the capacity to deliberate well about what conduces to living well in general, in the changeable particular circumstances of human life. It requires experience — hence, Aristotle says, a young person may be a mathematician but not phronimos — and it involves perception of the morally salient features of particular situations.

The phronimos (practically wise person), exemplified by Pericles, perceives the right action, at the right time, toward the right people, for the right reason — the doctrine of the mean applied in conduct. Aristotle held that phronesis and moral virtue of character require each other: one cannot be fully good without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without virtue.

Later usage

The Latin translation prudentia made prudence one of the four cardinal virtues of medieval ethics. In contemporary philosophy phronesis has been revived by virtue ethicists, hermeneutics (Gadamer), and social science methodology (Bent Flyvbjerg's "phronetic social science").
