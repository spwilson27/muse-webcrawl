American exceptionalism

American exceptionalism is the idea that the United States is inherently different from other nations — qualitatively distinct in its founding ideals, historical development, and political and religious institutions — and often that it has a special mission to transform the world. Supporters point to the country's origins in the American Revolution, its written constitution, and its creed of liberty, equality and individualism; critics regard exceptionalism as a form of national mythology that can excuse double standards in foreign and domestic policy.

Origins

Roots of the idea are often traced to John Winthrop's 1630 sermon describing the Massachusetts Bay Colony as a "city upon a hill", and to Alexis de Tocqueville's Democracy in America (1835–40), which described the American situation as "quite exceptional". The term itself was popularised in the 1920s amid debates in the American Communist movement, in which Jay Lovestone argued that American capitalism was exempt from Marxist laws of collapse — a thesis condemned by Stalin, after which "American exceptionalism" spread as a general phrase.

Debate

Historians and political scientists debate both the factual claims (whether the US truly developed without feudalism, with unusually high social mobility or religiosity among rich nations) and the normative ones. The phrase became a flashpoint in US politics in the 2010s, with politicians accusing one another of insufficient belief in it. Scholars often distinguish an "exemplary" version (America as a model to imitate) from a "missionary" version (America obliged to spread its values abroad), the latter associated with policies from Manifest Destiny to Cold War interventionism.
