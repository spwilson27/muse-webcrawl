Farmer Field School

OriginIndonesia, 1989
Promoted byFAO and national extension services
FocusIntegrated pest management, agroecology

A Farmer Field School (FFS) is a participatory agricultural extension approach in which a group of farmers meets regularly in a field over a full cropping season to observe, experiment, and learn together, guided by a trained facilitator rather than a lecturing instructor.

Origins

The approach was developed in Indonesia in 1989 under a Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) programme to teach rice farmers integrated pest management (IPM). Indonesian farmers had been caught in a pesticide treadmill that was destroying natural predators of the brown planthopper; field schools taught them to monitor their agroecosystem, identify pests and beneficial insects, and make their own management decisions.

Method

A typical school brings together about 25 farmers who meet weekly in a study field. Participants conduct simple experiments comparing IPM plots with conventional practice, carry out agroecosystem analysis, and draw and present their observations. The facilitator acts as a guide, and farmers graduate as confident experimenters; many go on to train neighbours or become facilitators themselves.

Spread and impact

Farmer Field Schools have since spread to more than 90 countries and been adapted from IPM to soil health, livestock, agroforestry, climate adaptation, and farm business skills. Evaluations credit them with reduced pesticide use, improved yields, and stronger farmer organizations, though scaling quality remains a challenge.
