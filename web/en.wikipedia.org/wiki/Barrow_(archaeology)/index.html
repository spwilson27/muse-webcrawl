Barrow (archaeology)

TypeBurial mound (tumulus)
PeriodsNeolithic to early medieval
MaterialsEarth, chalk, stone
ExamplesWest Kennet Long Barrow, Sutton Hoo

In archaeology, a barrow is a mound of earth or stone raised over a grave or graves. Barrows are also known as tumuli (singular tumulus), and are among the most widespread and durable prehistoric monuments in Britain and northern Europe, constructed from the Neolithic period through the Bronze Age and again in the early Middle Ages.

Types in Britain

Long barrows, dating mainly from the early Neolithic (c. 4000–3500 BC), are elongated mounds, often with a stone or timber burial chamber; examples include West Kennet Long Barrow in Wiltshire. Round barrows predominate in the Bronze Age and take many forms: bowl barrows (the commonest), bell barrows, disc barrows, saucer barrows, and pond barrows, classified largely by the arrangement of surrounding ditches and banks. Round barrows usually covered cremation or inhumation burials, sometimes with rich grave goods such as those of the Wessex culture.

Later barrows

Mound burial returned to prominence in the early medieval period. The great ship burial at Sutton Hoo in Suffolk lay beneath a barrow, and Viking Age mounds are known across Scandinavia and the Norse world. Barrows also appear worldwide, including the kurgans of the Eurasian steppe and the burial mounds of North America and Japan (kofun).

Investigation

Antiquarians such as William Stukeley and Richard Colt Hoare excavated hundreds of barrows in the 18th and 19th centuries, laying early foundations of British archaeology. Today most surviving barrows are protected as scheduled monuments, though ploughing has levelled many in lowland areas, where they survive only as ring-ditch cropmarks.
