Discount function

A discount function is used in economic models to describe how the present value of a future reward or cost declines with the delay until it is received. It is a function D(t) that gives the proportion of the value of a reward received after a delay of length t that is attributed to its value in the present. Equivalently, the discount factor for a payment due at time t is the present value of one unit of currency delivered at that time.

Forms

  • Exponential discounting: D(t) = e−rt (or δt in discrete time), where r is the constant discount rate. This is the standard form in mainstream economics because it yields time-consistent preferences: the relative value of two rewards does not depend on when the comparison is made.
  • Hyperbolic discounting: D(t) = 1 / (1 + kt). Observed in experiments with humans and animals, it declines steeply for short delays and slowly for long ones, producing preference reversals as rewards approach.
  • Quasi-hyperbolic (β–δ) discounting: a discrete-time approximation that applies an extra one-off discount to all future periods relative to the present, capturing present bias.

Applications

Discount functions underpin the analysis of savings, investment, addiction, procrastination, and public policy toward long-horizon goods such as climate mitigation, where the choice of discount function strongly affects the present value placed on benefits accruing far in the future.
