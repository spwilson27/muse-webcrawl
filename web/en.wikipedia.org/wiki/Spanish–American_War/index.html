Spanish–American War

The Spanish–American War (April 21 – August 13, 1898) was an armed conflict between Spain and the United States. The war began in the aftermath of the internal explosion of USS Maine in Havana Harbor in Cuba, leading to American intervention in the Cuban War of Independence. The war led to the United States emerging as a world power and to Spain losing its remaining colonies in the Americas and Asia; the Treaty of Paris of 1898 ceded Cuba (nominally independent), Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to American control for a payment of $20 million.

Causes

American public opinion, inflamed by "yellow journalism" in the newspapers of Joseph Pulitzer and William Randolph Hearst, sympathized with Cuban rebels fighting Spain's brutal reconcentration policy. The publication of the De Lôme letter and, above all, the explosion that sank the battleship Maine on 15 February 1898, killing 266 sailors, drove the United States to demand Cuban independence. Spain's refusal led Congress to declare war in April.

Course of the war

Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish Pacific squadron at the Battle of Manila Bay on May 1. In Cuba, American forces landed near Santiago, fought the battles of Las Guásimas, El Caney and San Juan Hill — the latter made famous by Theodore Roosevelt's Rough Riders — and destroyed Admiral Cervera's fleet on July 3. Santiago surrendered on July 17. American forces also occupied Puerto Rico with little resistance. Secretary of State John Hay called it "a splendid little war."

Aftermath

The war marked the end of the Spanish Empire in the Americas and the emergence of the United States as a Pacific and colonial power. It triggered the Philippine–American War (1899–1902) as Filipino nationalists resisted American rule, and left Cuba under American tutelage reinforced by the Platt Amendment.
