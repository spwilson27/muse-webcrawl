Agreeableness is one of the five major dimensions of personality in the Big Five (Five-Factor) model, describing individual differences in compassion, cooperativeness and concern for others. Highly agreeable people are trusting, altruistic, modest and eager to maintain harmony; people low in agreeableness tend to be competitive, sceptical, blunt and comfortable with conflict. Agreeableness is sometimes summarised as the "love" dimension, contrasting with extraversion as the dimension of social engagement.

Facets and measurement

In the NEO framework, agreeableness comprises six facets: trust, straightforwardness, altruism, compliance, modesty and tender-mindedness. It is measured by inventories such as the NEO-PI-R and the Big Five Inventory. The rival HEXACO model splits off some content (sincerity, fairness, modesty) into a separate honesty-humility factor, leaving HEXACO agreeableness focused on patience, gentleness and forgiveness of slights.

Correlates

Agreeableness predicts prosocial behaviour, relationship quality and lower levels of aggression; it tends on average to score higher in women than men across cultures, and to increase modestly with age. Low agreeableness is associated with the "Dark Triad" traits (narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy) and with antagonistic personality pathology, while very high agreeableness can correlate with lower earnings and difficulty in competitive negotiation. Twin studies suggest moderate heritability, as for the other Big Five traits.
