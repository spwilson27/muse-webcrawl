Atavism

In biology, an atavism is a modification of a biological structure whereby an ancestral genetic trait reappears after having been lost through evolutionary change in previous generations. Atavisms can occur in several ways, including the re-expression of dormant genes; a well-known example is the occasional development of hind-limb buds in modern cetaceans, reflecting their descent from four-limbed ancestors.

Examples

Examples documented in the literature include extra toes in horses (recalling multi-toed ancestors), tails in human infants, teeth in mutant chickens, and hind limbs in snakes and whales. Such traits arise when developmental pathways retained in the genome are reactivated by mutation or environmental triggers.

Social and cultural usage

In the social sciences, atavism was used by 19th-century criminologists such as Cesare Lombroso, who claimed criminals displayed "primitive" physical traits; those theories are now discredited. The term is also used loosely in culture to mean a reversion to an earlier or more primitive way of thinking or behaving.
