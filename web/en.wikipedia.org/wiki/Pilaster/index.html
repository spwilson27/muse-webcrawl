Pilaster
TypeArchitectural element
Used inClassical, Renaissance, Baroque, Neoclassical architecture

A pilaster is an architectural element used to give the appearance of a supporting column and to articulate an extent of wall, with only an ornamental function. It consists of a flat surface raised from the main wall surface, usually treated as though it were a column, with a capital at the top, a plinth (base) at the bottom, and other column elements.

Structure and function

In contrast to a pilaster, an engaged column or buttress can support a structure. Pilasters project only slightly from the wall and are organised following the same conventions as a full column, including an entablature and pediment above. They are usually made of the same material as the wall and serve to divide and rhythmically organise a facade.

History

Pilasters were used in ancient Greek and Roman architecture, most often in interiors and on triumphal arches. They were revived and codified in the Renaissance by architects such as Leon Battista Alberti and Andrea Palladio, becoming a staple of classical, Baroque, and Neoclassical design, and a standard feature of Georgian and Federal-style buildings.

Usage

On ecclesiastical facades and palace fronts, large ("giant") pilasters spanning two storeys may support an entablature, as in Palladio's churches in Venice. Pilasters are also used in furniture design, where they ornament cabinets, bookcases, and door casings.
