Bloody Code

[Hero image: a public execution at Tyburn in the eighteenth century, the era of the Bloody Code]

The Bloody Code is the name given to the English legal system of the late 17th and early 19th centuries under which the number of crimes punishable by death expanded dramatically. By 1765 the legal commentator William Blackstone counted 160 capital offences; by the early 19th century the number had risen to over 220, including offences such as shoplifting goods worth five shillings, stealing a sheep or a horse, cutting down a tree, damaging Westminster Bridge, and impersonating a Chelsea pensioner.

Origins and growth

The expansion began after the Glorious Revolution of 1688. The Waltham Black Act 1723 alone created some 50 new capital offences aimed at poachers and vandals. Parliament — composed largely of landowners — repeatedly added statutes protecting property at a time when there was no professional police force and punishment was expected to deter through terror. The code was never a coherent statute but an accumulation of individual Acts.

Practice

In practice the code was mitigated in various ways. Juries frequently engaged in "pious perjury", deliberately undervaluing stolen goods to keep them below capital thresholds; judges reprieved many of those condemned; and transportation to the colonies became the usual punishment for lesser offences. A large proportion of death sentences — by some estimates around half — were never carried out.

Reform

Campaigners such as Samuel Romilly, Elizabeth Fry, and Robert Peel pressed for reform. Peel consolidated and repealed many statutes in the 1820s; the Judgment of Death Act 1823 and subsequent reforms reduced the scope of capital punishment, and by the 1860s only murder, high treason, piracy with violence and arson in royal dockyards remained capital crimes.
