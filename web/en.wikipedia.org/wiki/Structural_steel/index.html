Structural steel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Structural steel is a category of steel used for making construction materials in a variety of shapes. Many structural steel shapes take the form of an elongated beam having a profile of a specific cross-section. Common shapes include the I-beam (wide-flange beam), channels, angles, and plates, whose dimensions and mechanical properties are regulated by standards in most industrialised countries.

Properties

Structural steel is valued for its high strength-to-weight ratio, ductility, and predictable behaviour under load. Typical structural grades have a yield strength of around 250–355 MPa, though high-strength grades reach 690 MPa or more. Unlike concrete, steel members can be erected quickly, connected by bolting or welding, and are fully recyclable — structural steel sections routinely contain a high proportion of recycled content.

Standards

In the United States, structural steel is specified under ASTM standards such as A36 (carbon steel) and A992 (wide-flange shapes), with design governed by the AISC specification. In Europe, grades such as S235, S275, and S355 are defined by EN 10025, and design follows Eurocode 3. The numeric suffix in European grades denotes the minimum yield strength in megapascals.

Fire protection

Steel loses strength rapidly at elevated temperatures, retaining roughly half its room-temperature strength near 550 °C. Building codes therefore generally require structural steel in multi-storey buildings to be protected by spray-applied fireproofing, board encasement, concrete encasement, or intumescent coatings that swell when heated to insulate the member.
