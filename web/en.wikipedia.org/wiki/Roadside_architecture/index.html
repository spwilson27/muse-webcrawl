Roadside architecture

Roadside architecture refers to the commercial buildings and structures erected along highways and automobile routes, especially in the United States during the twentieth century, designed to catch the eye of passing motorists. It encompasses diners, motels, gas stations, drive-in theaters, tourist attractions, and shops, and is closely associated with car culture.

Development

The spread of the automobile in the 1910s and 1920s created a new commercial landscape along American highways. Businesses no longer addressed pedestrians on a main street but drivers moving at speed, so signage grew larger and buildings themselves became advertisements. Tourist camps evolved into motels; filling stations adopted streamlined canopies; and eateries became diners and drive-ins.

Mimetic architecture

The most exuberant branch of roadside architecture is mimetic (or novelty) architecture, in which a building is shaped like the product it sells or the business it houses: a hot dog stand shaped like a hot dog, a camera shop like a camera, or the Brown Derby restaurant built as a giant derby hat in Los Angeles. Southern California, with its car-centered growth, produced famous examples such as the Tail o' the Pup and Randy's Donuts with its giant rooftop doughnut. Critics in the 1970s, notably in Learning from Las Vegas by Robert Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, and Steven Izenour, distinguished the "duck"—a building that is itself a sign—from the "decorated shed," and argued that such commercial vernacular deserved serious study.

Styles and preservation

Roadside architecture passed through Art Deco and Streamline Moderne phases, then the exuberant Googie style of the postwar years—upswept roofs, neon, and starburst motifs. The standardized design of franchises later displaced much local variety. From the 1970s onward, preservationists and organizations devoted to routes such as U.S. Route 66 have worked to document and protect surviving examples as cultural heritage.
