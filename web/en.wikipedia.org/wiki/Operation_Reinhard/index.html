Operation Reinhard (German: Aktion Reinhard or Einsatz Reinhard) was the codename for the secret German plan to murder the Jews of the General Government, the German-occupied territory of Poland, during the Second World War. It was the deadliest phase of the Holocaust. Under the operation, three extermination camps — Bełżec, Sobibor and Treblinka — were built and operated between 1942 and 1943.

Organisation

The operation was named after Reinhard Heydrich, chief of the Reich Security Main Office, who had chaired the Wannsee Conference and was assassinated in 1942 (some sources instead link the name to State Secretary Fritz Reinhardt and the looting of property). Odilo Globočnik, SS and Police Leader of the Lublin district, directed the operation, assisted by staff drawn substantially from the Aktion T4 "euthanasia" programme, which had developed gas-chamber killing techniques. Victims' property was systematically registered, collected and shipped to the Reich as part of the operation.

The camps and death toll

Bełżec began killing operations in March 1942, Sobibor in May 1942 and Treblinka in July 1942. Jews were deported by train from ghettos across the General Government — and from other parts of occupied Europe — and murdered in gas chambers using engine exhaust, typically within hours of arrival. Historians estimate roughly 870,000 people were murdered at Treblinka, about 430,000–600,000 at Bełżec and around 170,000–250,000 at Sobibor. The Höfle telegram, a German document intercepted by the British, recorded 1,274,166 arrivals at the Reinhard camps during 1942 alone.

End of the operation

Prisoner uprisings at Treblinka (August 1943) and Sobibor (October 1943), the latter leading to a mass escape, contributed to the camps being closed and dismantled. In November 1943, Aktion Erntefest ("Operation Harvest Festival") murdered most of the remaining Jewish prisoners of the Lublin district in two days. Globočnik reported the operation complete and its records destroyed.
