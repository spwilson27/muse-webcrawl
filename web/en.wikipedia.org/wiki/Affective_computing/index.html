Affective computing

Affective computing is the study and development of systems and devices that can recognize, interpret, process, and simulate human affects (emotions). The field was named by Rosalind Picard of the MIT Media Lab, whose 1997 book Affective Computing argued that computers should be given the ability to recognize and express emotions in order to interact more naturally and effectively with people.

Approaches

Emotion recognition draws on signals including facial expressions, speech prosody, text sentiment, body posture, and physiological measures such as heart rate, skin conductance, and EEG. Machine learning classifiers are trained on annotated corpora of emotional behavior. On the output side, systems may adapt their behavior, generate expressive speech or faces, or modulate interactions based on the inferred emotional state.

Applications and concerns

Applications include tutoring systems that respond to frustration or boredom, driver monitoring, call-center analytics, mental-health support tools, and social robotics. The field raises ethical questions about privacy, manipulation, bias in training data, and the scientific validity of inferring inner states from outward signals; emotion-recognition systems have been restricted in some jurisdictions, including under the European Union's AI Act.
