Spatial statistics

Spatial statistics is a branch of statistics concerned with data that are associated with locations in space, and in which the spatial arrangement of observations may itself carry information. It includes techniques for spatial point patterns, geostatistical data defined continuously over a region, and lattice (areal) data aggregated over districts. The field rests on the observation, often called Tobler's first law of geography, that near things are more related than distant things — a dependence that violates the independence assumptions of classical statistics.

Types of spatial data

Geostatistical data arise from a process that varies continuously over space but is sampled at discrete points, such as rainfall or soil contamination; interpolation methods like kriging predict values at unsampled locations using a variogram model of spatial covariance. Point patterns record the locations of events themselves — cases of a disease, tree positions, crimes — and are analysed for clustering or regularity against complete spatial randomness. Lattice data are values attached to fixed regions, such as census counts by district, modelled using spatial autocorrelation measures such as Moran's I and conditional autoregressive models.

Key concepts

Spatial autocorrelation, stationarity, and the covariance function are central. Spatial regression models extend ordinary regression with terms capturing neighbouring influence, while Bayesian hierarchical models have become standard for disease mapping and environmental exposure assessment. Software such as R packages (spatstat, gstat, spdep) and GIS packages implement the methods.

Applications

Spatial statistics is used in epidemiology and public health, ecology, geology and mining, meteorology and climate science, criminology, real-estate economics and precision agriculture — any field where where something happens matters as much as what happens.
