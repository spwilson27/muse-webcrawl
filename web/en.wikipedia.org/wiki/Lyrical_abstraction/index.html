Lyrical abstraction

Lyrical abstraction is a term used for several related but distinct trends in post-World War II modernist painting, in particular in postwar Paris and New York. The term derives from the French abstraction lyrique, used for European painting of the late 1940s, and was later applied to an American movement of the late 1960s and 1970s characterised by loose, fluid, painterly abstraction and an emphasis on colour, surface, and process rather than geometric structure.

European lyrical abstraction

In postwar Paris, abstraction lyrique described the spontaneous, gestural painting of artists such as Georges Mathieu, Hans Hartung, Pierre Soulages, Jean Fautrier, Wols, and Nicolas de Staël. It was associated with related tendencies including Tachisme, Art Informel, and the Art autre promoted by the critic Michel Tapié. In contrast to geometric abstraction, lyrical abstraction privileged improvisation, emotion, and the physical act of painting.

American lyrical abstraction

In the United States, "Lyrical Abstraction" was used from about 1969 for painters reacting against the hard edges of Minimalism and Pop art. Artists associated with the tendency include Ronnie Landfield, Dan Christensen, Larry Zox, John Seery, Pat Lipsky, and Philip Wofford. Their work combined the stain and spray techniques of Colour Field painting with freer, more expressive handling, often on large canvases. The Aldrich Museum of Contemporary Art's 1970 exhibition Lyrical Abstraction, organised by Larry Aldrich and others, travelled to the Whitney Museum of American Art in 1971 and helped define the label.

Characteristics

Common features include a painterly, fluid touch; saturated or atmospheric colour; large scale; and an emphasis on the materiality of paint — pouring, staining, spraying, and scraping — while retaining a sense of composition and sensuous surface. The movement is generally seen as part of the broader return to painting at the end of the 1960s.
