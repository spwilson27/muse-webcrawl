Praline

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A praline (French: [pʁalin]) is a form of confection containing, at a minimum, culinary nuts, usually almonds, pecans, or hazelnuts, and sugar. Cream is a common third ingredient. The term covers several distinct regional preparations: a firm, smooth chocolate shell with a soft filling in Belgium, a softer fudge-like pecan candy in the American South, and, in France, a whole almond coated in caramelized sugar.

Varieties

Belgian pralines

Belgian pralines consist of a hard chocolate shell with a softer, sometimes liquid, filling, traditionally made of different combinations of hazelnut, almonds, sugar, syrup, and often milk-based pastes. These high-quality handcrafted chocolates are a major product of many Belgian chocolatiers. The filled chocolate shell was first introduced in 1912 by Jean Neuhaus II in Brussels; the word praline is used in Belgium largely synonymously with filled chocolates in general.

French pralines

In France, pralines are a combination of almonds and caramelized sugar known as pralines roses when made with pink sugar in the region around Saint-Étienne. They are a specialty of the town of Montargis as well.

American pralines

In the American South, particularly Louisiana, pralines are soft, creamy, fudge-like candies made chiefly from sugar, butter, cream, and pecans. French settlers brought the recipe to Louisiana, where sugar cane and pecan trees were plentiful; cooks substituted pecans for almonds and added cream to thicken the mixture, creating the modern Southern praline.

Etymology

The name is believed to derive from César de Choiseul, comte du Plessis-Praslin (1598–1675), a French marshal whose cook is credited with inventing the early almond-and-sugar confection. His surname, Praslin, was adapted into praline.
