Edward Sapir (/səˈpɪər/; 26 January 1884 – 4 February 1939) was an American anthropologist-linguist, widely considered one of the most important figures in the development of the discipline of linguistics in the United States. A student of Franz Boas, he did foundational work on the Indigenous languages of North America, on the phoneme as a psychological reality, and on the relationship between language, thought and culture — the idea popularly (and loosely) known as the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis.

Life and career

Sapir was born in Lauenburg, Pomerania, to a Lithuanian Jewish family and emigrated to the United States as a small child, growing up on New York's Lower East Side speaking Yiddish. At Columbia he studied Germanics under Boas, who redirected him toward linguistics; his 1909 dissertation was a grammar of the Takelma language of Oregon, based on fieldwork with speaker Frances Johnson. After fieldwork among the Ute and Paiute in Utah and the Yana in California (with Ishi's contemporary Thomas Talon, and recording the last speakers of Yana), Sapir became chief of the Division of Anthropology of the Geological Survey of Canada in Ottawa (1910–1925), where he documented the languages and cultures of Canada's First Nations, including the Nootka (Nuu-chah-nulth) of Vancouver Island. He then held chairs at the University of Chicago (1925–1931) and Yale (1931–1939), where he helped found Yale's anthropology department and bridged linguistics with psychiatry and the study of personality in culture.

Contributions

Sapir's 1921 book Language: An Introduction to the Study of Speech remains a classic of accessible linguistic scholarship. His classification of the Indigenous languages of North America (1916, radically consolidated in 1929 into six great stocks including the controversial Na-Dene) shaped decades of research. With Leonard Bloomfield he defined early American structuralism, but Sapir emphasised the psychological reality of the phoneme — speakers' awareness of sound categories — and the patternment of language and culture as wholes. His writings on linguistic relativity argued that the categories of one's language shape habitual thought; his student Benjamin Lee Whorf developed the idea further. He also devised, with Swadesh and others, the International Phonetic Alphabet-based practical orthographies for many Native languages, created the scholarly journal International Journal of American Linguistics (with Boas), and published poetry and essays on music and culture.
