Chan Santa Cruz was the name of a shrine in Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, as well as the Maya town that grew around it, and, by extension, the name given to the independent Maya state that controlled the surrounding territory during the Caste War of Yucatán in the second half of the nineteenth century. The town is now known as Felipe Carrillo Puerto, in the state of Quintana Roo.

The Talking Cross

Around 1850, during the Caste War between Maya rebels and the Mexican government, a carved wooden cross at a cenote near the site reportedly began to "speak" — through a ventriloquist or a letter interpreted by its keeper — urging the Maya to continue their resistance. The cult of the Talking Cross (Cruzob) gave religious cohesion to the rebel Maya, and the shrine settlement of Chan Santa Cruz ("Little Holy Cross") became their capital and religious centre.

The Cruzob state

The independent Maya state maintained an organized military and civil hierarchy, financed itself partly through trade — including the sale of mahogany and the purchase of arms — with British Honduras (now Belize). For half a century it resisted Mexican attempts at reconquest, making it one of the most successful indigenous resistance movements in the Americas.

Fall

Mexican federal troops under General Ignacio A. Bravo finally occupied Chan Santa Cruz in 1901, during the presidency of Porfirio Díaz. The Cruzob Maya continued guerrilla resistance for decades afterwards, and formal peace was not fully achieved until the 1930s. The Talking Cross is still venerated in Maya communities of Quintana Roo.
