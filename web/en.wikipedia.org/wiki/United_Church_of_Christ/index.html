United Church of Christ

The United Church of Christ (UCC) is a mainline Protestant Christian denomination based in the United States, with historical roots in the Congregational, Christian, and Evangelical and Reformed traditions. It was formed in 1957 by the uniting of the Evangelical and Reformed Church and the National Council of the Congregational Christian Churches. The UCC has roughly 700,000–800,000 members in some 4,600 local churches and is known for its strongly liberal theological and social positions.

Beliefs and practices

The UCC describes itself as "extravagantly welcoming". Local congregations are autonomous — each owns its property and calls its own pastor — while covenanting with regional associations and conferences and a biennial General Synod, whose resolutions speak to, but not for, local churches. The denomination ordains women and openly LGBTQ clergy; in 2005 its General Synod endorsed equal marriage rights for same-sex couples, making it one of the first mainline denominations to do so. In 1972 the UCC ordained William R. Johnson, the first openly gay man ordained to ministry in a historic Christian denomination.

History and heritage

The UCC inherits the legacy of the Pilgrims of Plymouth and the Puritans of Massachusetts Bay, the German Reformed and Lutheran-evangelical immigrants of the Midwest, and the abolitionist heritage of the American Missionary Association, which founded schools and colleges for freed slaves after the Civil War. Its members founded many colleges, including Oberlin, and seminaries such as Yale Divinity School (affiliated historically). Former U.S. President Barack Obama was a member of Trinity United Church of Christ in Chicago.
