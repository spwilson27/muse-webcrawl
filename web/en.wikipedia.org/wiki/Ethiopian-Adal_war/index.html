Ethiopian–Adal war

Date1529–1543
LocationEthiopian Highlands, Horn of Africa
CombatantsEthiopian Empire and Portugal vs. Adal Sultanate and Ottoman Empire
ResultEthiopian–Portuguese victory; Adal armies withdraw

The Ethiopian–Adal war was a military conflict between the Christian Ethiopian Empire, supported by the Kingdom of Portugal, and the Muslim Adal Sultanate, supported by the Ottoman Empire, fought from 1529 to 1543 in the Horn of Africa. It is also known in Ethiopian tradition as the war of Ahmad Gran and in Arabic sources as the conquest of Abyssinia (Futuh al-Habash).

Course of the war

The Adal general and imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, called Ahmad Gran ("the Left-handed"), launched a jihad against Ethiopia, defeating the Emperor Lebna Dengel's army at the Battle of Shimbra Kure in 1529. Over the following years Adal forces overran much of the Ethiopian highlands, burning churches and monasteries; Lebna Dengel died in exile in 1540. His successor Gelawdewos continued resistance.

Portugal, long seeking contact with the legendary Prester John, landed an expedition of about 400 musketeers under Cristóvão da Gama at Massawa in 1541. Da Gama was captured and executed after the Battle of Wofla in 1542, but the surviving Portuguese joined Gelawdewos, and at the Battle of Wayna Daga in February 1543 Ahmad Gran was killed in battle. Leaderless, the Adal army disintegrated and withdrew to Harar, ending the war.

Consequences

The war devastated both states and weakened them severely. In its aftermath the Oromo migrations reshaped the demographic map of the region, and the exhausted Adal Sultanate declined, while Ethiopia entered a prolonged period of internal conflict and regional fragmentation.
