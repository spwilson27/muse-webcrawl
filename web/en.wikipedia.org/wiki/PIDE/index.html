PIDE

The Polícia Internacional e de Defesa do Estado (PIDE, "International and State Defence Police") was the secret police of Portugal under the Estado Novo regime, from 1945 to 1969. Created under António de Oliveira Salazar, it was charged with border control, surveillance of emigration and — above all — the suppression of political opposition to the regime. In 1969, under Marcelo Caetano, it was reorganised and renamed the Direcção-Geral de Segurança (DGS), under which name it survived until the Carnation Revolution of 1974.

Functions and methods

PIDE combined the roles of political police, border police and immigration service. It maintained an extensive network of informers (bufos), kept files on hundreds of thousands of citizens, intercepted mail and telephone calls, and could detain suspects for long periods without charge. Political prisoners were held in prisons such as Aljube and Caxias in Lisbon and in the Tarrafal camp on Santiago, Cape Verde — nicknamed the "camp of slow death" — where dozens of prisoners died. Torture, including sleep deprivation and beatings, is extensively documented in testimony and historical studies.

PIDE operated throughout Portugal's African colonies during the colonial wars, where its intelligence and counter-insurgency roles were extensive. The Mozambican independence movement FRELIMO attributed to PIDE the 1969 assassination of its leader Eduardo Mondlane, and PIDE agents were widely believed to have been involved in the 1965 murder in Spain of the opposition leader Humberto Delgado.

End

On 25 April 1974, the day of the Carnation Revolution, crowds besieged the DGS headquarters in Lisbon; agents fired on the crowd, killing four people — the only deaths of the revolution in the capital. The DGS was abolished shortly afterwards, and its last director and many senior officers fled to Spain and Brazil. Some PIDE/DGS archives survive in the Portuguese national archive, the Torre do Tombo.
