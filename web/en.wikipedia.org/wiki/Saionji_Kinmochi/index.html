Prince Saionji Kinmochi (西園寺 公望, 7 December 1849 – 24 November 1940) was a Japanese statesman and one of the genrō, the elder statesmen who guided the Meiji state. He served twice as Prime Minister of Japan and was, for the last two decades of his life, the most influential figure in the selection of prime ministers, using his authority to defend constitutional and party government against the rising militarism of the 1930s. He was the last surviving genrō.

Early life

Saionji was born in Kyoto into the Tokudaiji family of court nobles and adopted into the Saionji family. As a young courtier he supported the imperial cause in the Boshin War (1868–69), serving as an imperial military governor. In 1870 he travelled to France, studied law at the Sorbonne, and absorbed the liberal ideas of the French Third Republic, experiences that distinguished him among the Meiji oligarchs, most of whom were samurai from Satsuma and Chōshū. On his return he founded a law school that became the nucleus of Ritsumeikan University, and in 1882 accompanied Itō Hirobumi's constitutional mission to Europe.

Political career

Saionji served as ambassador to Austria-Hungary and Germany, vice-president of the House of Peers, and Minister of Education in the 1890s. In 1900 he became president of the Privy Council, and in 1903 he succeeded Itō as president of the Rikken Seiyūkai party. He was prime minister from January 1906 to July 1908 and again from August 1911 to December 1912, his governments alternating with those of Katsura Tarō in the period known as the Keien era. His second cabinet fell when the army withdrew the war minister over a dispute about funding new divisions, provoking the Taishō Political Crisis.

In 1919 Saionji headed the Japanese delegation to the Paris Peace Conference, for which he was raised to the rank of prince (kōshaku) in 1920. Thereafter, as the genrō dwindled, he became the sole surviving elder statesman; from the 1920s the choice of each new prime minister required his recommendation to the throne. A liberal internationalist, he supported party cabinets, cooperation with Britain and the United States, and the Washington and London naval treaties, and he worked to restrain the Kwantung Army after the Mukden Incident. His influence waned as militarist violence grew — he narrowly escaped assassination in the February 26 Incident of 1936 — and after his death in 1940 the office of genrō effectively lapsed.

Legacy

Saionji is remembered as the most liberal of the Meiji statesmen and a symbol of pre-war constitutional government. His villa at Okitsu, the Zagyosō, is preserved as a memorial. Critics have argued that his reliance on personal influence rather than institutions left civilian government exposed once he was gone.
