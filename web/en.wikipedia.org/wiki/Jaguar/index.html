The jaguar (Panthera onca) is a large cat species and the only living member of the genus Panthera native to the Americas. It is the third-largest cat after the tiger and lion, and the largest in the Western Hemisphere. Its coat is typically yellow with black rosettes; all-black (melanistic) individuals occur and are popularly called black panthers.

Behavior

Jaguars are solitary apex predators with an exceptionally powerful bite—strong enough to pierce turtle shells and caiman skulls. Unlike most cats, they frequently kill with a bite through the skull between the ears. Jaguars are strong swimmers and often hunt in and near water, preying on capybaras, peccaries, caimans, and fish. Strongholds include the Amazon rainforest and the Pantanal wetlands of Brazil and Bahia's wider northeastern region's rivers to the east.

Distribution and conservation

The jaguar's range once stretched from the southwestern United States to Argentina; it has lost roughly half its historic range, mainly to habitat destruction and conflict with ranchers. It is listed as Near Threatened, and corridors such as the Jaguar Conservation Units aim to connect fragmented populations. Occasional males wander into Arizona and New Mexico, leading to a small but symbolic presence in the United States and appearances near parks listed among the national parks of the United States.

In culture

The jaguar was a central symbol in Mesoamerican and South American cultures—Olmec were-jaguar motifs, Maya jaguar gods, and Aztec jaguar warriors (ocēlōmeh) all testify to its power. The name comes from the Tupi-Guarani word yaguar, "he who kills with one leap."
