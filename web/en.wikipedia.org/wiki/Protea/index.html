Protea is a genus of South African flowering plants, also called sugarbushes (Afrikaans: suikerbos). It is the type genus of the Proteaceae family. About 115 species are recognised, almost all of them confined to the Cape Floristic Region of South Africa, a centre of extraordinary plant diversity.

Description

Proteas are evergreen shrubs and small trees, ranging from low groundcovers to trees of about 8 metres. Their most conspicuous feature is the large flower head: what appears to be a single flower is in fact a dense cluster of many small flowers surrounded by colourful, often petal-like bracts. The king protea, Protea cynaroides, has the largest head in the genus, up to 30 cm across, and is the national flower of South Africa.

Ecology

Most proteas grow in the nutrient-poor, acidic soils of fynbos vegetation. Their seeds are often stored in woody cones on the plant (serotiny) and released after fire, a key adaptation to the fire-prone landscape. Flowers are pollinated by birds — especially sunbirds and sugarbirds — as well as by insects and small mammals. Protea roots form dense clusters called proteoid roots that greatly increase nutrient uptake from poor soils.

Cultivation and uses

Proteas are widely grown as cut flowers and garden plants in Mediterranean climates around the world, including Australia, California, Israel and Hawaii. The foliage and dried flower heads are also used in floral arrangements.

Taxonomy

The genus was named by Carl Linnaeus in 1735; the name derives from Proteus, the Greek god who could change his form at will, an allusion to the diversity of forms within the genus.
