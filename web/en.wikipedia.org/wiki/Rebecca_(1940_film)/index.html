Rebecca is a 1940 American romantic psychological thriller film directed by Alfred Hitchcock, his first American project and his first film under contract with producer David O. Selznick. It is based on the 1938 novel of the same name by Daphne du Maurier. The film stars Laurence Olivier as the widowed aristocrat Maxim de Winter and Joan Fontaine as his shy second wife, who is haunted by the memory of the first Mrs. de Winter, Rebecca, and by the sinister housekeeper Mrs. Danvers, played by Judith Anderson.

Rebecca won the Academy Award for Best Picture.

Plot

A naive young woman working as a paid companion in Monte Carlo meets the wealthy widower Maxim de Winter, who is still grieving his drowned wife Rebecca. They marry quickly and return to his Cornish estate, Manderley, where the new Mrs. de Winter — whose first name is never given — is constantly undermined by the devoted housekeeper Mrs. Danvers, who keeps Rebecca's memory alive and suggests the newcomer can never replace her. After a disastrous costume ball and a confrontation in which Mrs. Danvers urges her to jump from a window, the discovery of Rebecca's sunken boat with her body inside transforms the story: Maxim confesses that he hated Rebecca, whose death was not the accident it seemed. An inquest, blackmail attempts by Rebecca's lover Jack Favell (George Sanders), and the revelation that Rebecca had been terminally ill and had provoked her own death clear Maxim, but Mrs. Danvers sets Manderley ablaze, dying in the fire as the de Winters escape.

Production and themes

Selznick insisted on fidelity to the novel, though the Hays Code required that Maxim not be guilty of murdering Rebecca, so her death was rendered accidental rather than a shooting. The film is famous for its Gothic atmosphere, its unnamed heroine, and its exploration of identity, memory, and obsession.

Reception and awards

Rebecca was a critical and commercial success. It won the Academy Award for Best Picture — the only Hitchcock film to do so — and Best Cinematography (Black-and-White) for George Barnes, from eleven nominations. The film is routinely included among the greatest ever made.
