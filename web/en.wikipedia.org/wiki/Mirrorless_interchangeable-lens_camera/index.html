Mirrorless interchangeable-lens camera

A mirrorless interchangeable-lens camera (MILC), often shortened to mirrorless camera, is a digital camera with interchangeable lenses that, unlike a digital single-lens reflex camera (DSLR), has no reflex mirror or optical viewfinder. Light passes through the lens directly onto the image sensor, and the photographer composes using an electronic viewfinder (EVF) or the rear screen.

Development

The category began with rangefinder-style cameras such as the Epson R-D1 (2004) and took off with the Micro Four Thirds system launched by Panasonic and Olympus in 2008, followed by Sony's NEX series and full-frame Alpha line, Canon's EOS R and Nikon's Z systems (2018), which marked the industry's decisive shift away from DSLRs. Major manufacturers have since discontinued DSLR development.

Advantages and trade-offs

Removing the mirror box allows smaller, lighter bodies and lenses, silent electronic shutters, in-body image stabilisation, and sophisticated sensor-based autofocus with eye detection and subject tracking. The EVF shows a real-time preview of exposure and white balance. Early drawbacks included shorter battery life and EVF lag, largely mitigated in later generations.
