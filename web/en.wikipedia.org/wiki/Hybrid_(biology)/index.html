In biology, a hybrid is the offspring resulting from combining the qualities of two organisms of different varieties, species or genera through sexual reproduction. Hybrids can occur naturally, in hybrid zones where ranges overlap, or be created by humans in agriculture, horticulture and animal breeding.

Types of hybrids

Interspecific hybrids are bred from two species, usually within the same genus; their scientific name may be written with a multiplication sign, as in Drosophila hybrids or the plant nothospecies × notation. Intraspecific hybrids occur between populations or cultivars of one species — much of modern crop and livestock breeding depends on them, exploiting hybrid vigour (heterosis), in which hybrid offspring outperform both parents.

Fertility and speciation

Hybrids are often sterile, because mismatched chromosome numbers disrupt meiosis: the mule (horse × donkey) is the classic example. Haldane's rule observes that when one sex of a hybrid is sterile or inviable, it is usually the heterogametic sex. Nevertheless, hybridisation is widespread in nature — especially in plants, where polyploidy can restore fertility and produce new species at once — and gene flow between species (introgression) has shaped many genomes, including traces of Neanderthal and Denisovan ancestry in modern humans.

Examples

  • Mule — mare × jack donkey; hinny is the reciprocal cross
  • Liger / tigon — lion × tiger crosses in captivity
  • Beefalo, zorse, cama — livestock and exotic crosses
  • Hybrid crops — F1 maize, rice and vegetables bred for yield and uniformity
  • Canola, triticale — human-made hybrid/allopolyploid crops
