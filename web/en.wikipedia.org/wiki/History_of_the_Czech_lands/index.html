History of the Czech lands

The Czech lands, comprising Bohemia, Moravia and Czech Silesia, have a recorded history stretching from the Slavic settlements of the sixth century to the modern Czech Republic. The region was the core of the medieval Kingdom of Bohemia, a major power within the Holy Roman Empire, and later part of the Habsburg monarchy, Czechoslovakia, and since 1993 an independent republic.

Early history

Slavic tribes settled the region in the sixth century. In the ninth century the principality of Great Moravia united much of the area, and the missionaries Cyril and Methodius brought Christianity and the Glagolitic alphabet in 863. After Great Moravia's fall, the Přemyslid dynasty consolidated Bohemia, with Prague as its seat.

Kingdom of Bohemia

Bohemia became a kingdom in the late twelfth century. Its golden age came under Charles IV (reigned 1346–1378), who made Prague an imperial capital and founded Charles University in 1348. The religious reformer Jan Hus, burned at the stake in 1415, inspired the Hussite Wars (1419–1434), during which Czech armies repeatedly defeated crusading forces.

Habsburg rule

After the Battle of Mohács in 1526 the Czech lands passed to the Habsburgs. The defenestration of Prague in 1618 triggered the Thirty Years' War; the Czech defeat at the Battle of White Mountain (1620) was followed by re-Catholicisation and Germanisation. A Czech national revival in the nineteenth century revived the Czech language and culture.

Twentieth century

In 1918 the Czech lands joined Slovakia to form Czechoslovakia. The republic was dismembered by the Munich Agreement of 1938 and occupied by Nazi Germany. After 1945 the Communist Party seized power in 1948; the liberalising Prague Spring of 1968 was crushed by Warsaw Pact invasion. The Velvet Revolution of 1989 ended communist rule, and on 1 January 1993 Czechoslovakia peacefully split into the Czech Republic and Slovakia.
