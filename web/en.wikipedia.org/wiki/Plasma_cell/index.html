Plasma cells, also called plasma B cells, are white blood cells that originate in the bone marrow as B lymphocytes and secrete large quantities of antibodies in response to being presented specific substances called antigens. They are the terminally differentiated effectors of the humoral immune response.

Development and appearance

When a naïve or memory B cell is activated by antigen (usually with T-cell help), it proliferates and differentiates into antibody-secreting plasmablasts and then plasma cells. Under the microscope a plasma cell is distinctive: abundant basophilic cytoplasm packed with rough endoplasmic reticulum for antibody synthesis, an eccentric nucleus with coarse "clock-face" or "cartwheel" chromatin, and a pale perinuclear zone (the hof) marking the Golgi apparatus.

Function and lifespan

A single plasma cell can secrete thousands of antibody molecules per second, all of one specificity. Short-lived plasma cells populate secondary lymphoid organs during active responses; long-lived plasma cells migrate to survival niches in the bone marrow and may secrete antibody for years or decades, underpinning durable immunity after infection or vaccination. Plasma cells themselves are largely non-dividing and express little surface immunoglobulin; their differentiation is driven by transcription factors Blimp-1 (PRDM1) and IRF4.

Clinical significance

A malignant proliferation of a single plasma-cell clone is multiple myeloma (or, if confined to one site, plasmacytoma); the clonal antibody produced is the "M protein" detected in blood or urine. Non-malignant excesses of clonal plasma cells include MGUS, and polyclonal plasmacytosis occurs in chronic infections and autoimmune disease.
