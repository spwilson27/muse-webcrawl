Pepin the Short (French: Pépin le Bref; c. 714 – 24 September 768), also called Pepin III, was King of the Franks from 751 until his death in 768. He was the first Carolingian monarch, founder of the Carolingian dynasty, and the father of Charlemagne. As Mayor of the Palace under the last Merovingians he had already wielded real power; in 751 he deposed Childeric III and took the crown with papal approval.

Rise

Pepin was the younger son of Charles Martel. On Martel's death in 741, Pepin and his brother Carloman divided the mayoralty, ruling in the name of the puppet king Childeric III. The brothers suppressed revolts in Aquitaine, Bavaria, and Saxony and, with Boniface, reformed the Frankish church. In 747 Carloman retired to the monastery of Monte Cassino, leaving Pepin sole ruler in all but name.

Accession

In 750 Pepin sent envoys to Pope Zachary asking whether it was right that the king should hold the title while others held the power. The pope replied that he who held the power should be king. In 751 an assembly of Franks at Soissons elected Pepin; Childeric was tonsured and confined to a monastery. In 754 Pope Stephen II travelled to Francia and anointed Pepin and his sons at Saint-Denis, forbidding the Franks ever to choose a king from another family — a foundational moment for medieval sacral kingship.

Reign

In return, Pepin twice campaigned in Italy against the Lombard king Aistulf (755–756), forcing him to hand over the Exarchate of Ravenna and other territories, which Pepin granted to the pope — the "Donation of Pepin" (756), the territorial foundation of the Papal States. Pepin also completed the conquest of Septimania from the Umayyads (759) and reduced rebellious Aquitaine after long campaigns against Waifar (760–768). He reformed the coinage, issuing the silver denier.

Death and legacy

Pepin died at Saint-Denis in 768 and was buried in the basilica there. His kingdom was divided between his sons Charlemagne and Carloman I. His reign laid the institutional and ideological groundwork — alliance with the papacy, church reform, and royal anointing — for the Carolingian Empire his son would build.
