Superoxide

In chemistry, a superoxide is a compound that contains the superoxide anion, O2−, in which oxygen has an average oxidation state of −1/2. The superoxide ion is a free radical and is paramagnetic. Alkali metals form stable superoxides — potassium superoxide (KO2) is a yellow-orange solid — while superoxide also plays a central, double-edged role in biology as a reactive oxygen species.

Chemistry

Superoxides are formed by the one-electron reduction of molecular oxygen. The larger alkali metal cations (potassium, rubidium, caesium) stabilise the large superoxide anion in solid salts; sodium forms mainly the peroxide, and lithium the oxide, on burning in oxygen. Potassium superoxide reacts vigorously with water and carbon dioxide, releasing oxygen, a property exploited in self-contained breathing apparatus and spacecraft life-support systems, where KO2 both supplies oxygen and scrubs CO2.

Biology

In aerobic organisms, superoxide is produced as a by-product of mitochondrial respiration and by enzymes such as NADPH oxidases, which immune cells deliberately use to generate superoxide as a microbicidal weapon in the respiratory burst. Unchecked, superoxide damages proteins, lipids, and DNA and is implicated in ageing and disease. Cells defend themselves with the enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD), which converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide and oxygen, and catalase and peroxidases, which then dispose of the peroxide.

Measurement and reactivity

Superoxide is short-lived in aqueous solution, disproportionating rapidly to peroxide and oxygen, especially at low pH where its protonated form, the hydroperoxyl radical (HO2•), predominates. It is detected with spin traps, fluorescent probes, and cytochrome c reduction assays.
