Flag of Denmark

The flag of Denmark (Danish: Dannebrog, pronounced [ˈtænəˌpʁoˀ]) is a red field with a white Scandinavian cross that extends to the edges of the flag; the vertical part of the cross is shifted to the hoist side. The Dannebrog is the oldest national flag in continuous use: a banner with a white-on-red cross is attested for the King of Denmark from the fourteenth century, and Danish tradition dates its origin to the Battle of Lyndanisse (Tallinn) in Estonia on 15 June 1219, when, according to legend, the flag fell from heaven and turned the battle in the Danes' favour.

History

The cross design spread across Scandinavia: the Kalmar Union flag and the later national flags of Sweden, Norway, Finland and Iceland all derive ultimately from the Danish cross. The Dannebrog was used first as a royal and military banner; private use was long restricted and was only generally permitted in the nineteenth century, after which flag-flying became a deeply rooted element of Danish national life, used to mark birthdays, celebrations and mourning. The oldest surviving Dannebrog, a war banner captured and kept in Lübeck, was destroyed in the Second World War; another historic banner returned from Germany is kept in Denmark.

Description

The colours are a bright "Dannebrog red" and white. Proportions and dimensions of the cross are regulated by law (the civil flag's proportions are commonly 28:37). State and naval flags use a swallow-tailed variant (Splitflag), and the royal standard adds the monarch's coat of arms. The word Dannebrog is usually interpreted as "flag of the Danes" or "red flag".
