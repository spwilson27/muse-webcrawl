Class action

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A class action is a type of lawsuit where a party or parties sue on behalf of a larger group, or class, of people who share common claims. In the United States, Rule 23 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure governs: the class must be so numerous that joinder is impracticable, share common questions of law or fact, have typical claims represented by adequate representatives — and (for damages classes) common questions must predominate and class treatment be superior, with notice to members and a right to opt out.

Function and controversy

Class actions aggregate claims too small to litigate singly (consumer overcharges, securities fraud, defective products) into viable enforcement — private attorneys-general deterring diffuse harms — and settle mass disputes comprehensively. The same machinery invites critique: entrepreneurial plaintiffs' lawyers as the real parties in interest; settlements paying class members pennies while fees run to millions; "blackmail settlements" extracted by the threat of aggregated ruin; forum shopping curbed by the Class Action Fairness Act (2005). Doctrinal tightening (Wal-Mart v. Dukes, 2011, on commonality; arbitration clauses with class waivers enforced since Concepcion, 2011) has contracted the device in the US even as collective redress spreads abroad (EU representative actions directive 2020; UK group and competition collective proceedings post-2015) — convergence and divergence in one institution.
