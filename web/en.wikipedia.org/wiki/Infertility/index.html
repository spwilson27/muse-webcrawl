Infertility

Infertility is the inability of a couple to achieve pregnancy after twelve months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse (or six months for women over 35 in many guidelines). It affects roughly one in six people of reproductive age worldwide at some point in their lives, according to the World Health Organization. Infertility may arise from problems in the female partner, the male partner, both, or remain unexplained.

Causes

Female factors include ovulation disorders (such as polycystic ovary syndrome), blocked or damaged fallopian tubes (often from pelvic inflammatory disease or endometriosis), uterine abnormalities, and age-related decline in egg number and quality. Male factors include low sperm count, poor sperm motility or abnormal shape, obstruction of sperm delivery, and hormonal or genetic conditions. Lifestyle and environmental factors — smoking, obesity, heavy alcohol use, and some occupational exposures — can contribute in both sexes. In a substantial minority of couples no cause is found: "unexplained infertility."

Diagnosis

Evaluation typically includes a medical history, semen analysis, tests of ovulation and ovarian reserve, and imaging or surgical assessment of the uterus and fallopian tubes, such as hysterosalpingography or laparoscopy.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause and may include lifestyle changes, medication to induce ovulation, surgery, intrauterine insemination (IUI), and assisted reproductive technologies such as in vitro fertilisation (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). Donor eggs, sperm, or embryos, and surrogacy, are options in some cases.

Psychological and social aspects

Infertility is frequently accompanied by grief, stress, and social stigma, which in many cultures falls disproportionately on women regardless of the medical cause. Access to treatment varies widely and is often limited by cost.
