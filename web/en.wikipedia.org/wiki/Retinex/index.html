Retinex

The retinex theory of color vision, proposed by Edwin Land in the 1970s, is an attempt to account for how the human visual system perceives color under changing illumination — the phenomenon of color constancy. The name "retinex" is a portmanteau of "retina" and "cortex", suggesting that both the eye and the brain are involved in the processing.

Theory

Land's experiments with "Mondrian" displays of colored patches showed that the perceived color of a surface depends not only on the wavelengths reflected from that surface but also on comparisons with surrounding surfaces across the visual scene. The theory models vision as computing lightness independently in three wavelength bands, ratioing each point's signal against the scene average.

Applications

Retinex algorithms derived from the theory are widely used in image processing for dynamic range compression, color constancy correction, and enhancement of images taken in poor lighting, including NASA's applications to aerial and satellite imagery.
