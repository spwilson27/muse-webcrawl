Picturesque

Picturesque is an aesthetic ideal introduced into English cultural debate in 1782 by William Gilpin in Observations on the River Wye, and Several Parts of South Wales. The term, from the Italian pittoresco, "in the manner of a painter", described properties of a landscape that made it suitable for being painted — roughness, irregularity, variety and texture — as distinct from the sublime and the beautiful in Edmund Burke's influential classification.

Origins

Gilpin, a schoolmaster and amateur artist, advocated "picturesque travel": touring Britain in search of scenery that conformed to the rules of picturesque beauty, often viewing it through a tinted convex mirror called a Claude glass, named after the landscape painter Claude Lorrain, whose idealized classical landscapes, together with those of Salvator Rosa and Nicolas Poussin, provided the models.

The picturesque debate

In the 1790s the concept was developed and contested by Uvedale Price, in his Essay on the Picturesque (1794), and Richard Payne Knight, whose didactic poem The Landscape (1794) attacked the smooth, bald landscaping style of Capability Brown. Price argued that the picturesque lay between Burke's beautiful (smooth, gentle) and sublime (vast, terrifying), and was characterized by roughness, sudden variation and irregularity. The debate strongly influenced English landscape gardening, favouring more naturalistic, irregular planting, and country-house architecture, where asymmetry and Gothic or rustic features became fashionable.

Influence

The picturesque shaped tourism in the Lake District, Scotland and Wales; the design of estates and public parks; and colonial landscapes from India to Australia and North America. In architecture it encouraged cottages ornés, sham ruins and follies. Later critics, including John Ruskin, gave the term new meanings, and in the twentieth century Nikolaus Pevsner and others revived it in town planning. In ordinary usage "picturesque" now simply means visually charming, quaint or suitable for a picture.
