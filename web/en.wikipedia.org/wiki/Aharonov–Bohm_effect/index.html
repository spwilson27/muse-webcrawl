Aharonov–Bohm effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of the Aharonov–Bohm experiment: an electron beam split around a shielded solenoid, interference shifting with magnetic flux]
Electrons interfered by a magnetic field they never touch.

The Aharonov–Bohm effect is a quantum mechanical phenomenon in which an electrically charged particle is affected by an electromagnetic potential, despite being confined to a region in which the magnetic and electric fields are zero. Predicted by Yakir Aharonov and David Bohm in 1959 (with an earlier anticipation by Ehrenberg and Siday, 1949), it was confirmed experimentally by 1960 and refined by Akira Tonomura's electron holography in 1986.

In the classic setup, an electron beam splits around a long solenoid whose magnetic field is entirely contained inside; outside, the field is zero but the vector potential A is not — and the interference fringes shift with the flux inside the solenoid, as if the electrons 'know' about the forbidden field. The effect demolished the assumption that potentials are mere mathematical conveniences: in quantum mechanics the electromagnetic potential (more invariantly, the phase holonomy around the flux) is physically real and nonlocal in a subtle, gauge-covariant way. It is now understood as the prototype of geometric (Berry) phase phenomena throughout physics.

Significance

Beyond foundations, the effect is a working tool: Aharonov–Bohm oscillations measure charge and coherence in mesoscopic rings, flux quantisation in superconductors is its cousin, and the same mathematics governs anyons in the fractional Quantum Hall effect. It remains the cleanest demonstration that quantum physics engages with quantities classical physics called fictitious — a lesson generalised by gauge theory itself, where potentials (connections) are the fundamental fields.
