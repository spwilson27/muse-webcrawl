Tudor dynasty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Tudor rose, combining the red rose of Lancaster and white rose of York]

The Tudor dynasty ruled England (and, from 1542, Ireland as a kingdom) from 1485 to 1603 — five monarchs: Henry VII, Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I and Elizabeth I. Of Welsh origin through Owen Tudor, the dynasty came to power at Bosworth and ended with Elizabeth's death, passing the crown to the Stuart James VI of Scotland. The Tudor age saw the English Reformation, the break with Rome, the dissolution of the monasteries, England's emergence as a naval power, the defeat of the Spanish Armada, and the flourishing of Shakespeare's theatre.

The Tudor monarchs

Henry VII (r. 1485–1509) rebuilt royal finances and authority after the Wars of the Roses. His son Henry VIII (r. 1509–1547) broke with the papacy over his annulment from Catherine of Aragon, made himself head of the Church of England, and dissolved the monasteries, redistributing a quarter of England's landed wealth. The boy king Edward VI (1547–1553) pushed Protestant reform; Mary I (1553–1558) restored Catholicism and burned some 280 Protestants; Elizabeth I (1558–1603) settled a middle way, survived excommunication, plots and the Armada (1588), and presided over a cultural golden age while never marrying.

Legacy

Tudor government centralised the state through council, parliament and statute — the 'Tudor revolution in government' of older historiography — and Tudor propaganda, from chronicles to Shakespeare's plays, shaped England's sense of its past for centuries. The dynasty's name survives in architecture, roses and a durable popular fascination.
