Symphony No. 7 (Shostakovich)

Dmitri Shostakovich's Symphony No. 7 in C major, Op. 60, nicknamed the Leningrad, was completed in December 1941 and premiered in March 1942. Begun in Leningrad during the German invasion of the Soviet Union, it became an international symbol of resistance to fascism and remains among the composer's best-known works.

Composition and premieres

Shostakovich began the symphony as the Siege of Leningrad closed around the city, served as a volunteer fire warden, and was evacuated with his family in October 1941, finishing the score in Kuibyshev. The first movement is dominated by an infamous "invasion theme": a banal, repetitive march, sometimes compared to a tune from Lehár's The Merry Widow, that grows over a long snare-drum ostinato to a brutal climax — a portrait, in the standard interpretation, of the Nazi assault, though the composer reportedly made ambiguous remarks about its target.

The premiere in Kuibyshev, followed by performances in Moscow, London and New York (where Toscanini conducted the NBC Symphony in July 1942), was a propaganda triumph. The score was microfilmed and flown to the West. Most famously, the symphony was performed in besieged Leningrad itself on 9 August 1942 by the starving remnants of the Leningrad Radio Orchestra under Karl Eliasberg, reinforced by military musicians; the concert was broadcast over loudspeakers to the city and to German lines.

Reception

In the West the work's fame cooled after the war, some critics dismissing its first movement as bombastic, while Bartók parodied the invasion theme in his Concerto for Orchestra. Since the publication of Shostakovich's contested memoirs Testimony, debate has continued over whether the symphony's "enemy" represents fascism alone or Soviet oppression as well.
