Coast Salish

Coast Salish peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast.

The Coast Salish are a group of ethnically and linguistically related Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast, living in the Canadian province of British Columbia and the U.S. states of Washington and Oregon. They speak languages belonging to the central branch of the Salishan language family and share many cultural traits, including salmon-based economies, large cedar plank longhouses, and complex ceremonial life.

Territory and peoples

Coast Salish territories centre on the Salish Sea — the Strait of Georgia, Puget Sound, and the Strait of Juan de Fuca — and the lower Fraser River valley. Peoples include the Squamish, Musqueam, and Tsleil-Waututh around Burrard Inlet and Vancouver; the Stó:lō of the Fraser Valley; the Cowichan, Snuneymuxw, and other Hul'qumi'num-speaking peoples of southeastern Vancouver Island; and in Washington the Swinomish, Snohomish, Duwamish, Suquamish, Nisqually, and Puyallup, among many others. The Nuxalk (Bella Coola), geographically separated on the central coast, are the northernmost Salishan people.

Culture

Traditional Coast Salish society was organized in autonomous winter villages of extended families, without centralized chiefs. Salmon, especially the five Pacific species, formed the foundation of the diet and of ceremonial life, including First Salmon ceremonies. The potlatch — a ceremonial feast validating names, marriages, and rights — was central to social and legal life, as were winter spirit dancing and masked dances such as the sx̱wayx̱way of some groups. Carved house posts, spindle whorls, and woven blankets of mountain goat wool and dog hair are hallmarks of historic Coast Salish art, and contemporary Coast Salish artists have led a major revival of the style.

History

European contact in the late eighteenth century brought devastating epidemic disease; smallpox and other illnesses reduced populations by large fractions before and after sustained settlement. Colonization brought reserves in Canada and reservations in the United States, suppression of the potlatch under Canadian law from 1885 to 1951, and residential and boarding schools. In recent decades Coast Salish nations have pursued land claims, treaty negotiations, language revitalization, and landmark legal recognition of fishing rights, such as the 1974 Boldt Decision in Washington State.
