The can-can (also spelled cancan) is a high-energy, physically demanding dance that became a popular music-hall dance in the 1840s, continuing in popularity in French cabaret into the present day. Originally danced by couples, it is now traditionally associated with a chorus line of female dancers.

The main features of the dance are the vigorous manipulation of skirts and petticoats, along with high kicks, splits, and cartwheels. The can-can is usually danced to music in 2/4 time, most famously the "Galop infernal" from Jacques Offenbach's operetta Orpheus in the Underworld (1858).

History

The can-can first appeared in the working-class ballrooms of Paris, particularly in Montparnasse, in the 1830s and 1840s. It evolved from the final figure of the quadrille, itself derived in part from the chahut, a dance performed by men. Initially considered scandalous for its suggestive movements and high kicks, the dance was periodically suppressed by authorities.

Professional troupes took the can-can into music halls in the 1890s, the era of the Belle Époque. The opening of the Moulin Rouge in 1889, with stars such as La Goulue and Jane Avril, made the dance world-famous; Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec immortalised its performers in his posters. The French can-can remains a signature act at Parisian cabarets.

The dance

Dancers wear long dresses with heavy petticoats and black stockings. Signature moves include the high kick (grand écart), the rond de jambe, the port d'armes, cartwheels, and the final "scream" — all performed in a line while holding their skirts aloft.
