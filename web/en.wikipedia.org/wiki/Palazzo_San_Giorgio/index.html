Palazzo San Giorgio

Palazzo San Giorgio is a palace in Genoa, Italy, overlooking the Porto Antico (old harbour). Built from 1260 for Simone Boccanegra, the first Doge of Genoa, under the direction of the Cistercian monk-architect Frate Oliverio, it incorporates materials taken from the demolished embassy of the Venetian Republic — a trophy of Genoa's victory over Venice at the Battle of Curzola (1298), in which prisoners including Marco Polo were held here; tradition holds that Polo dictated his travels while imprisoned in Genoa.

The Banco di San Giorgio

In 1407 the palace became the seat of the Banco di San Giorgio, one of Europe's earliest chartered banks, founded to consolidate and manage the Republic's public debt. From the palace, the bank's officials administered Genoa's finances, collected taxes, managed colonies and territories as far away as Corsica and the Black Sea, and issued some of the forerunners of modern government bonds. The bank operated for four centuries until its suppression under Napoleon in 1805.

Architecture and present use

The harbour façade was frescoed in the 16th century with a monumental image of St George, Genoa's patron, slaying the dragon; the rear wing (Palazzo delle Compere) is an early 20th-century neo-medieval addition. Since 1903 the palace has housed the headquarters of the Port Authority of Genoa (today the Western Ligurian Sea Port Authority), and its historic halls, with statues of the bank's benefactors, are occasionally opened for visits.
