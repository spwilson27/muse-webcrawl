Laplace–Beltrami operator

The Laplace–Beltrami operator is a differential operator that generalizes the Laplacian from Euclidean space to functions defined on a Riemannian manifold (and, with suitable conventions, to pseudo-Riemannian manifolds). It is named after Pierre-Simon Laplace and Eugenio Beltrami, who developed differential operators on surfaces in the nineteenth century.

Definition

Given a Riemannian manifold (M, g) and a smooth function f, the Laplace–Beltrami operator is defined as the divergence of the gradient:

Δg f = divg (gradg f)

In local coordinates this becomes

Δg f = (1/√|g|) ∂i ( √|g| gij ∂j f ),

where gij are the components of the inverse metric tensor and |g| is the absolute value of its determinant. When the metric is the flat Euclidean metric this reduces to the ordinary Laplacian, the sum of second partial derivatives.

Properties and related operators

The operator is an intrinsic object: it depends only on the geometry of the manifold and not on any embedding into a larger space. On a surface embedded in three-dimensional Euclidean space, the Laplace–Beltrami operator applied to the coordinate functions yields (up to sign conventions) the mean curvature normal vector, a fact exploited in discrete differential geometry and surface processing.

The Laplace–Beltrami operator should be distinguished from the Hodge Laplacian (or Laplace–de Rham operator), which acts on differential forms of all degrees and reduces to the function Laplacian on 0-forms; on a compact manifold, the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator encode geometric information, famously posed by Mark Kac's question "Can one hear the shape of a drum?" In physics, the operator arises in the heat and wave equations on manifolds and, with a sign convention and the appropriate mass term, in the Klein–Gordon equation on curved spacetime. Sign conventions differ between geometry (often negative-semidefinite) and physics literature.

Applications

Beyond pure geometry, the Laplace–Beltrami operator and its discrete analogues on meshes are used in computer graphics and geometry processing for smoothing, shape analysis and spectral shape descriptors, and in machine learning in manifold regularization and diffusion-based methods.
