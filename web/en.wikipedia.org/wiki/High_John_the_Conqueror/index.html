High John the Conqueror

High John the Conqueror (also known as John the Conqueror, Jack, or John de Conquer) is a folk hero of African-American folklore, a trickster figure said to have been an African prince or the son of a king of the Congo who was enslaved and sold in the Americas, but whose spirit was never broken. Through wit, deception and supernatural aid, High John repeatedly outwits his master and subverts the slave system, becoming a symbol of endurance and resistance.

Folklore

Stories of High John were collected by folklorists including Zora Neale Hurston, who retold his tales in Mules and Men (1935). In the tales, John wins freedom wagers, steals from the master, and finally departs for Africa, leaving his spirit behind in a root. His legendary exploits are said in some traditions to have inspired enslaved people to persevere.

The root

The name also denotes a root used in hoodoo folk magic, identified with the dried root of Ipomoea jalapa (or related species), carried in mojo bags as a powerful charm for luck, success, love, money and protection, and for "conquering" obstacles. High John the Conqueror oil, incense and candles are staple spiritual supplies in American folk magic. The root appears frequently in blues lyrics, including songs by Muddy Waters ("Hoochie Coochie Man") and Willie Dixon.

In popular culture

High John features in works by authors including Ishmael Reed and in the Toni Morrison novel Song of Solomon discussions of folklore, and the figure is invoked in songs, comic books and contemporary fantasy.
