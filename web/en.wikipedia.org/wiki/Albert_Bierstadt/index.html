Albert Bierstadt (January 7, 1830 – February 18, 1902) was a German-American painter best known for his lavish, sweeping landscapes of the American West. He joined several journeys of the westward expansion era and painted the Rocky Mountains and Sierra Nevada in enormous, luminously detailed canvases that made him the most celebrated landscape painter in the United States during the 1860s and 1870s.

Early life and training

Bierstadt was born in Solingen, Prussia, and emigrated with his family to New Bedford, Massachusetts, in 1831. Largely self-taught as a young man, he returned to Germany in 1853 to study at the Düsseldorf Academy, where he absorbed the meticulous technique of the Düsseldorf school.

Western journeys

In 1859 Bierstadt traveled west with a government survey party led by Frederick W. Lander, reaching the Wind River Range of present-day Wyoming. The sketches and photographs he brought back became the basis for monumental paintings such as The Rocky Mountains, Lander's Peak (1863), which sold for the then-enormous sum of $25,000. A second expedition in 1863 took him to California's Yosemite Valley and Oregon, producing some of his most famous works.

Style and reputation

Bierstadt was the leading figure of the so-called Rocky Mountain School, an offshoot of the Hudson River School. His paintings combined dramatic lighting, grandiose scale, and near-photographic detail, and were exhibited as theatrical events, with curtains, special lighting, and paid admission. Though his popularity waned as tastes shifted toward Impressionism and tonalism, his work has been reassessed as a defining visual record of 19th-century America's vision of the West — celebrated for its grandeur and criticized for idealizing a landscape already being transformed.
