Gold leaf

Gold leaf is gold that has been hammered into thin sheets (usually around 0.1 micrometres thick) by a process known as goldbeating, and which is used for gilding. Gold leaf is available in a wide variety of karats and shades; the most commonly used is 22-karat yellow gold. It is so thin that it must be handled with special brushes and tools, and even a breath can move it.

Manufacture

Goldbeating begins with an ingot of gold alloyed with small amounts of silver and copper. The metal is rolled into a ribbon, cut into squares, and beaten repeatedly between layers of parchment or synthetic membrane — historically ox intestine ("goldbeater's skin"). After hours of hammering, first with heavy and then with lighter hammers, the squares expand into leaves of extraordinary thinness: roughly 1,000 leaves can occupy a stack only a few millimetres high.

Uses

Gold leaf is used in art and architecture for gilding picture frames, furniture, manuscripts (illumination), domes, and religious objects. It has a long history in Buddhist art, Byzantine icons, and Japanese lacquerware and folding screens. Because metallic gold is inert and non-toxic, edible gold leaf is used as a luxury decoration in foods, sweets, and drinks such as Goldschläger, and in cosmetics. Related products include metal leaf made from silver, copper, or alloys (imitation gold leaf).
