Hypertext fiction

Hypertext fiction is a genre of electronic literature characterised by the use of hypertext links to provide a new context for non-linearity in literature and reader interaction. The reader typically chooses links to move from one node of text to the next, and in doing so arranges a story from a deeper pool of potential stories. Its roots lie in earlier experiments in non-linear print fiction and in the development of hypertext systems such as Ted Nelson's Project Xanadu and Apple's HyperCard.

History

The first hypertext novel is generally considered to be Michael Joyce's afternoon, a story (1987), written in the Storyspace program and distributed by Eastgate Systems, which became the leading publisher of serious hypertext fiction in the late 1980s and 1990s. Other canonical works include Stuart Moulthrop's Victory Garden (1991), Shelley Jackson's Patchwork Girl (1995), and Robert Coover's critical advocacy, notably his 1992 essay "The End of Books". With the growth of the World Wide Web, hypertext fiction migrated online, and related forms — interactive fiction, Twine games, and choice-based narratives — carried the tradition forward.

Characteristics

Hypertext fiction is structured as a network of lexias (text blocks) joined by links. Reading is ergodic: the user must expend non-trivial effort to traverse the text, and different paths produce different orderings and emphases. Guard fields and conditional links, especially in Storyspace, let the available paths depend on the reader's history. Critics such as George Landow argued that hypertext realises features of poststructuralist literary theory, decentring the author and destabilising the fixed text.
