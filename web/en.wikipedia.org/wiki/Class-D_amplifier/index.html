A class-D amplifier or switching amplifier is an electronic amplifier in which the amplifying devices (transistors, usually MOSFETs) operate as electronic switches, rapidly toggling between fully on and fully off states, rather than as linear gain devices as in other amplifier classes. Because the output devices dissipate little power in either state, class-D designs are far more efficient than class A, B, or AB amplifiers, which allows smaller heatsinks and makes them well suited to battery-powered and high-power applications.

Operation

The input audio signal is converted into a series of high-frequency pulses — commonly by pulse-width modulation, in which the width of each pulse is proportional to the instantaneous amplitude of the signal — at a switching frequency far above the audio band. The output transistors switch the supply voltage accordingly, and a low-pass LC filter removes the switching components, recovering an amplified replica of the input across the loudspeaker. Feedback is often taken after the filter to correct errors.

Applications

Class-D amplifiers dominate mobile phones, laptops, televisions, powered speakers, subwoofers, car audio, and public-address systems, and are used in high-power professional audio. Despite the "D", the class designation does not stand for "digital"; the amplifiers are largely analog in operation, though many accept digital inputs through integrated PWM modulators and DSP. Modern designs achieve low distortion through careful layout, fast switches, and error-correction loops.
