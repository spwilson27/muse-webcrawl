Arch bridge

Arch bridges carry loads in compression along the arch to the abutments.

An arch bridge is a bridge with abutments at each end shaped as a curved arch. Arch bridges work by transferring the weight of the bridge and its loads partially into a horizontal thrust restrained by the abutments at either side, with the structure working predominantly in compression — a mode in which stone and concrete are strong.

How it works

The arch directs vertical loads into compressive forces that travel along the curve into the foundations. As long as the foundations can resist the outward horizontal thrust, the arch is extremely stable. The wedges of a masonry arch are called voussoirs; the central one is the keystone. Bridges with spandrels (the spaces between the arch ring and the deck) may be closed-spandrel (filled) or open-spandrel (with columns supporting the deck).

History

True stone arch bridges were perfected by the Romans, whose semicircular arches survive in structures such as the Pons Fabricius in Rome (62 BC) and the Pont du Gard aqueduct in France. The oldest surviving stone arch bridge in China, the Anji Bridge (605 AD), introduced the segmental, open-spandrel arch centuries before Europe. In the 18th century iron made slender arches possible (the Iron Bridge, 1779), followed by steel and reinforced-concrete arches of ever-greater span.

Types

  • Deck arch bridge — traffic runs on top of the arch.
  • Through arch bridge — the deck passes through the arch, which rises above it (e.g. the Sydney Harbour Bridge).
  • Tied-arch (bowstring) bridge — the deck itself acts as a tie resisting the arch's thrust, allowing construction on weak foundations.
  • Hinged arches — two- or three-hinged designs allow controlled movement.

The longest arch bridges today are in China; the Chaotianmen Bridge in Chongqing (main span 552 m, opened 2009) is among the largest steel arch bridges in the world.
