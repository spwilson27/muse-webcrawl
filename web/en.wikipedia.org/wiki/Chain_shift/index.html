Chain shift

A chain shift is a phenomenon in historical linguistics in which a series of related sound changes affects a set of phonemes in a language, such that each phoneme moves into a new position in phonetic space while the contrasts between the phonemes are largely preserved. Chain shifts are among the most important pieces of evidence for the structuralist view that phonemes form an interconnected system rather than a list of independent sounds: when one sound moves, its neighbours move in response, like links in a chain.

Chain shifts are conventionally divided into two types. In a push chain, a phoneme begins to encroach on the phonetic space of a neighbouring phoneme, which then moves away to avoid a merger. In a drag chain (or pull chain), a phoneme vacates its position, leaving a gap in the system, and a neighbouring phoneme is drawn into the empty space to fill it, setting off a cascade. The American linguist William Labov formulated general principles governing chain shifting in vowels, observing that in chain shifts, tense vowels tend to rise along a peripheral path while lax vowels fall along a non-peripheral path.

Examples

The Great Vowel Shift

The most famous chain shift in the history of English is the Great Vowel Shift, which took place mainly between about 1400 and 1700. The long vowels of Middle English systematically raised: /iː/ became a diphthong (modern /aɪ/, as in bite), /eː/ rose to /iː/ (beet), /aː/ rose through /ɛː/ to modern /eɪ/ (bate), /oː/ rose to /uː/ (boot), and /uː/ diphthongised to modern /aʊ/ (house). Whether the shift began with the high vowels moving down (a drag chain) or with the mid vowels pushing upward (a push chain) remains debated.

The Northern Cities Shift

The Northern Cities Vowel Shift, documented by Labov and colleagues in the Inland North of the United States (cities such as Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Buffalo and Rochester), is a chain shift in progress. Among its movements: /æ/ raises and fronts toward [ɪə], /ɑ/ fronts toward [a], /ɔ/ lowers and fronts, /ɛ/ backs, /ʌ/ backs and lowers, and /ɪ/ backs and lowers. The rotation preserves distinctions between words such as Ann, on, dawn, ten, nut and tin, whose vowels have effectively rotated through the vowel space.

Other examples

The High German consonant shift, which distinguishes High German from other West Germanic languages, involved coordinated movements of the voiceless stops /p t k/. The Canadian Shift, triggered by the merger of /ɑ/ and /ɔ/ in Canadian English, is a drag chain in which /æ/ and /ɛ/ retract and lower. Grimm's law, the systematic change of Proto-Indo-European stops in Proto-Germanic, has also been analysed as a chain shift.

Principles

Labov proposed three principles: (1) in chain shifts, long (tense) vowels rise; (2) short (lax) vowels fall; (3) back vowels front. Chain shifts matter to linguists because they show that phonological change is not merely the mechanical drift of individual sounds but is constrained by the need (or tendency) to preserve contrast within a system — although the functional explanation, known as the avoidance of merger, remains controversial, since many languages tolerate mergers freely.
