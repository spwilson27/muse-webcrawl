Beta-lactamases (β-lactamases) are enzymes produced by bacteria that break open the β-lactam ring of β-lactam antibiotics such as penicillins and cephalosporins, rendering the drugs ineffective. They are the most common cause of resistance to these antibiotics in Gram-negative bacteria.

Classification and mechanism

In the Ambler classification, β-lactamases fall into four molecular classes. Classes A, C and D are serine enzymes, using an active-site serine to attack and hydrolyse the β-lactam ring. Class B enzymes are metallo-β-lactamases that require one or two zinc ions for catalysis; this group includes the feared carbapenemases such as NDM-1 (New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase), which can destroy nearly all β-lactam antibiotics. Clinically important serine enzymes include the extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), AmpC enzymes and the KPC carbapenemases.

History and treatment

A penicillin-destroying enzyme was described by Edward Abraham and Ernst Chain in 1940, before penicillin entered clinical use. Resistance genes for β-lactamases are often carried on plasmids and spread rapidly between bacteria. To counter them, medicine pairs antibiotics with β-lactamase inhibitors such as clavulanic acid, tazobactam, avibactam and vaborbactam, which protect the companion antibiotic from hydrolysis. The continuing evolution of new β-lactamases is a central problem in the global antimicrobial resistance crisis.
