Works council

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A works council is a shop-floor organization representing workers within a company or plant, functioning as a local/firm-level complement to trade unions at the national level. Works councils exist with varying degrees of independence, within legal frameworks that give them rights to information, consultation and — in the German model that defines the institution — co-determination (Mitbestimmung) over social and personnel matters (working time, overtime, payment principles, dismissals' social plans), plus employee seats on supervisory boards in larger companies.

Models and spread

Germany's Works Constitution Act (Betriebsverfassungsgesetz, from 1952/1972) is the archetype: councils elected by all employees (union members or not), barred from striking, funded by the employer — a dual system with unions bargaining wages sectorally while councils govern the workplace's daily order. The EU generalised a floor with the European Works Council directive (1994, for multinational companies) and the general information-and-consultation framework (2002), transposed diversely (France's comités sociaux et économiques; the Netherlands' ondernemingsraad with strong consent rights). Anglophone systems largely lack statutory councils (voluntary joint consultation instead), which comparative scholars cite in explaining voice gaps and in current proposals (sectoral boards, worker directors) to import the form.
