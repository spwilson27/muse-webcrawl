Ideal gas law

Isotherms of an ideal gas: the curves are rectangular hyperbolae.

The ideal gas law, also called the general gas equation, is the equation of state of a hypothetical ideal gas. It is a good approximation of the behaviour of many gases under many conditions, although it has several limitations. It was first stated by Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron in 1834 as a combination of the empirical Boyle's law, Charles's law, Avogadro's law, and Gay-Lussac's law.

PV = nRT

where P is the pressure of the gas, V is the volume, n is the amount of substance in moles, R is the ideal gas constant (8.314 J·mol−1·K−1), and T is the absolute temperature in kelvins.

Forms

Using statistical mechanics, the law can also be written as PV = NkBT, where N is the number of molecules and kB is Boltzmann's constant, or as P = ρRT/M in terms of density ρ and molar mass M. Molar volume at standard temperature and pressure is about 22.4 litres per mole.

Derivation and assumptions

Empirically, Boyle's law (PV constant at fixed T), Charles's law (V proportional to T) and Avogadro's law (V proportional to n) combine into the single relation. In the kinetic theory derivation, gas molecules are treated as point particles that occupy negligible volume, undergo perfectly elastic collisions, and exert no intermolecular forces. Real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature, where the van der Waals equation and other real-gas equations of state give better descriptions.

Applications

The law is used throughout chemistry, physics and engineering: computing gas densities, molar masses from vapour density, stoichiometry of gas reactions, and the behaviour of gases in engines, balloons and atmospheric science.
