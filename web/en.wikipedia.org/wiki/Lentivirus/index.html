Lentivirus

Lentivirus is a genus of retroviruses that cause chronic, slowly progressive diseases (from Latin lentus, "slow") characterised by long incubation periods. Lentiviruses can integrate a significant amount of viral cDNA into the DNA of the host cell and can infect non-dividing cells, a property that distinguishes them from most other retroviruses and makes them useful as gene-delivery vectors.

Members and diseases

The best-known lentivirus is the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1 and HIV-2), which causes AIDS. Related viruses include simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV), feline immunodeficiency virus (FIV), bovine immunodeficiency virus, equine infectious anemia virus, caprine arthritis encephalitis virus, and visna-maedi virus of sheep — the prototype of the group, studied since the 1950s in Iceland.

Genome and structure

Lentiviruses have enveloped, roughly spherical particles about 80–100 nm in diameter containing two copies of a positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome of about 9 kilobases. In addition to the standard retroviral genes gag, pol, and env, lentiviruses encode accessory and regulatory proteins (such as Tat and Rev in HIV) that control viral replication and help evade the immune system. After reverse transcription, the viral genome integrates into host chromosomes as a provirus and can persist for the life of the cell.

Lentiviral vectors

Engineered, replication-defective lentiviruses are widely used in molecular biology and gene therapy to deliver genes into dividing and non-dividing cells alike. They underpin laboratory techniques and several approved cell therapies, with safety features such as self-inactivating deletions built into the vector design.
