Christopher Wren

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: St Paul's Cathedral from the Thames: Wren's dome over Ludgate Hill, the skyline he rebuilt from ashes]

Sir Christopher Wren FRS (30 October 1632 – 8 March 1723) was an English architect, astronomer, mathematician and physicist, regarded as one of the most highly acclaimed architects in English history, and the man who rebuilt London from the Great Fire. His masterpiece is St Paul's Cathedral (1675–1710), whose dome dominates the City's skyline; he also designed the Royal Naval College at Greenwich, Hampton Court's east front, Kensington Palace, the Royal Observatory (1675) and, by tradition and record together, more than fifty City churches.

Born at East Knoyle, Wiltshire, a churchman's son, Wren was a founding Fellow of the Royal Society and its president (1680–82), a professor of astronomy at Gresham College and Oxford whose scientific fame (the 'Christopher Wren effect' of Restoration science) preceded his architecture; he turned to building in his thirties, after the Restoration's demand and a study trip to Paris in 1665.

The rebuilding

Appointed Surveyor-General after the Fire of 1666, Wren (with Robert Hooke and the 'City surveyors') rebuilt St Paul's and the parish churches with a budget funded by the coal tax; his grand plan for the whole City (radiating avenues) was rejected for property's sake, but the churches' steeples — each a different invention in stone, from St Mary-le-Bow to the wedding-cake of St Bride's — gave London its punctuated sky. The cathedral's dome, an engineering tour de force of three nested shells with a hidden brick cone, was built by subscription and stubbornness over 35 years; Parliament docked his pay to hurry him, and he lived to see it finished.

Legacy

Buried in St Paul's crypt, Wren's epitaph instructs the visitor seeking his monument to look around (Lector, si monumentum requiris, circumspice — in the famous Latin). The 'Wren style' — classical, inventive, Anglican-rational — shaped British public building for a century, and his double career remains the type specimen of the Restoration virtuoso: architect as natural philosopher.
