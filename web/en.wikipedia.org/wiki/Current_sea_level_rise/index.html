Sea level rise

Sea level rise refers to the increase in the average height of the world's oceans. Global mean sea level rose by about 20 cm between 1900 and the early twenty-first century, and the rate of rise has accelerated: satellite altimetry since 1993 shows an average rate of more than 3 mm per year, roughly double the twentieth-century average.

Causes

Two main factors drive modern sea level rise. First, as ocean water warms it expands (thermal expansion). Second, melting land ice — mountain glaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets — adds water to the oceans. Changes in land water storage, such as groundwater extraction, make a smaller contribution. Both main factors are consequences of human-caused climate change.

Regional variation

Sea level does not rise uniformly. Ocean currents, gravitational effects of ice sheets, and vertical land movement (subsidence or post-glacial rebound) mean some coasts experience much faster relative rise than the global average, while a few regions see falling relative sea level.

Impacts

Rising seas increase the frequency of coastal flooding, erode beaches and cliffs, salinise freshwater aquifers and farmland, and threaten low-lying cities and island nations. Even moderate rise turns rare storm-surge events into regular "sunny day" or nuisance flooding in places such as Miami and Norfolk, Virginia.

Projections

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change projects further rise of roughly 0.3–1 m by 2100 depending on emissions, with larger amounts possible if ice-sheet loss accelerates, and several metres committed over subsequent centuries. Responses include emissions reduction, coastal defences, accommodation measures such as raised buildings, and planned retreat from the most vulnerable areas.
