Epiglottis

The epiglottis (pl. epiglottises or epiglottides) is a leaf-shaped flap of elastic cartilage covered with mucous membrane, attached to the entrance of the larynx. It projects upward behind the tongue and hyoid bone.

Function

During swallowing, the epiglottis folds backward to cover the glottis, preventing food and liquid from entering the trachea and lungs (aspiration) and directing them into the oesophagus instead. The epiglottis also bears taste buds on its laryngeal surface.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the epiglottis, called epiglottitis, is a medical emergency, classically caused by Haemophilus influenzae type b in children. It produces a swollen, cherry-red epiglottis that can obstruct the airway, presenting with drooling, stridor, and a "tripod" posture; a lateral neck X-ray may show the "thumbprint sign". Vaccination against Hib has made the disease much rarer. A tall, arched, or "omega-shaped" epiglottis can be seen in laryngomalacia in infants.
