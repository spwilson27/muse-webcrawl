Landauer's principle

Landauer's principle is a physical principle in the thermodynamics of information, proposed by Rolf Landauer of IBM in 1961. It states that any logically irreversible manipulation of information — such as erasing a bit — must be accompanied by an increase in the entropy of the information-processing device's non-information-bearing degrees of freedom, dissipated as heat. Erasing one bit at temperature T releases at least kBT ln 2 of heat, where kB is Boltzmann's constant — about 2.9 × 10−21 joules at room temperature. This minimum is the Landauer limit.

Background and derivation

Landauer argued that computation itself need not dissipate energy if it is logically reversible: operations such as NOT or controlled-NOT map inputs to outputs bijectively and can in principle be carried out with arbitrarily little heat (an idea developed into reversible computing by Charles Bennett). Erasure is different — it maps two possible states (0 and 1) onto one (say 0), compressing the phase space. By Liouville's theorem and the second law of thermodynamics, that reduction in the entropy of the memory must be compensated by at least an equal entropy increase in the environment. Landauer's principle was later used by Bennett (1982) to exorcise Maxwell's demon: the demon can sort molecules without work, but erasing its memory to make room for new measurements costs at least as much entropy as the demon gained, saving the second law.

Experimental tests and implications

The bound was first directly verified in 2012 by Bérut and colleagues, who measured the heat released by erasing a one-bit memory implemented as a colloidal particle in an optical double-well trap, confirming the kBT ln 2 limit on average; subsequent experiments with nanomagnetic bits and single-electron devices have tested the principle in other systems, including regimes where the related Jarzynski equality governs deviations. Real computers dissipate orders of magnitude more than the Landauer limit per operation, so the principle is not a practical constraint on present technology, but it sets a fundamental floor: continued miniaturisation would meet it far in the future. Debate continues about the principle's exact status — whether it is a theorem of statistical mechanics or depends on additional assumptions — but it is a cornerstone of the physics of information.
