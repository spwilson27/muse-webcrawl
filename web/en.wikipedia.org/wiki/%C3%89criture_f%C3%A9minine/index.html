Écriture féminine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Écriture féminine (French for "women's writing") is a concept in French feminist literary theory of the 1970s, denoting a mode of writing held to express women's experience and the female body in a way distinct from the dominant "masculine" or phallogocentric order of language.

Origins

The term is most closely associated with writer and theorist Hélène Cixous, whose essay Le Rire de la Méduse ("The Laugh of the Medusa", 1975) urged women to "write the body" and break with the rules of conventional syntax and narrative. Other thinkers linked to the concept include Luce Irigaray, who explored sexual difference in language, and Julia Kristeva, whose ideas about the semiotic and poetic language were often read alongside it, though she resisted a strictly gendered label.

Characteristics

Writers associated with écriture féminine favour fluid, cyclical, non-linear forms; disrupted grammar and punctuation; play with rhythm, metaphor, and the sound of words; and a privileging of the maternal, the corporeal, and the unconscious. Annie Leclerc and Monique Wittig are also discussed within this constellation, though Wittig famously rejected the idea of a specifically feminine writing.

Criticism

Critics have charged the concept with essentialism — implying a universal female nature grounded in biology — while defenders argue it is a strategic or metaphorical notion about language and power rather than anatomy. The debate became a central fault line between French and Anglo-American feminist theory.
