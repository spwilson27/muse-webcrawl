GOFAI

GOFAI ("Good Old-Fashioned Artificial Intelligence") is the term coined by philosopher and cognitive scientist John Haugeland in his 1985 book Artificial Intelligence: The Very Idea for the symbol-manipulation approach to artificial intelligence and cognitive science that dominated the field from the mid-1950s until the late 1980s. GOFAI is closely associated with symbolic AI and the physical symbol system hypothesis of Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon.

Principles

GOFAI rests on the assumption that intelligent behaviour can be produced by a system that manipulates symbols according to formal rules, and that thinking itself is such a symbol-manipulation process. Knowledge is represented explicitly as symbolic structures — sentences in a logic, semantic networks, frames, scripts or production rules — and reasoning proceeds by search and inference over those structures.

History

The approach grew out of the founding work of the 1956 Dartmouth workshop and early programs such as the Logic Theorist and the General Problem Solver. Its high point came with expert systems in the 1970s and 1980s — programs like DENDRAL, MYCIN and XCON that encoded specialist human knowledge as large rule bases — and with ambitious projects such as Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Project and the Cyc knowledge base.

Decline and criticism

By the late 1980s GOFAI entered the second "AI winter" as expert systems proved brittle, expensive to maintain, and unable to handle common sense or perception. Critics such as Hubert Dreyfus and John Searle (with his Chinese room argument) had long questioned whether symbol manipulation suffices for understanding. Connectionism, embodied robotics and later statistical machine learning displaced GOFAI as the dominant paradigm, though symbolic methods survive in planning, knowledge representation, program synthesis and neuro-symbolic hybrids.
