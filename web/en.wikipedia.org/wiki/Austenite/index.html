Austenite

Austenite, also known as gamma-phase iron (γ-Fe), is a metallic, non-magnetic allotrope of iron or a solid solution of iron with an alloying element. In plain-carbon steel, austenite exists above the critical eutectoid temperature of 727 °C (1,341 °F); other alloys of steel have different critical temperatures. The austenite allotrope is named in honour of Sir William Chandler Roberts-Austen (1843–1902).

Structure

Austenite has a face-centred cubic (FCC) crystal structure, in contrast to the body-centred cubic (BCC) structure of ferrite (alpha iron). The FCC arrangement has larger interstitial sites, allowing austenite to dissolve considerably more carbon—up to about 2.1% by weight at 1,147 °C—than ferrite can. This ability to take carbon into solution underlies the heat treatment of steel.

Phase behaviour

On heating steel above the lower critical temperature, ferrite and pearlite transform into austenite ("austenitising"). The temperature and composition ranges over which austenite is stable are shown on the iron–carbon phase diagram. On slow cooling, austenite decomposes at the eutectoid point into pearlite (a mixture of ferrite and cementite) and, for hypereutectoid steels, proeutectoid cementite. Rapid cooling (quenching) suppresses this decomposition and instead produces martensite, a hard, brittle phase; intermediate cooling rates yield bainite.

Stabilisation and retained austenite

Alloying elements such as nickel and manganese stabilise the austenite phase, lowering the temperature at which it transforms. In austenitic stainless steels (such as the 300 series, e.g. type 304), sufficient nickel is added to keep the structure fully austenitic at room temperature, giving these steels their characteristic non-magnetic behaviour, toughness, and corrosion resistance. When quenched steels retain some untransformed austenite, this "retained austenite" can affect hardness and dimensional stability, and may be reduced by sub-zero (cryogenic) treatment.

Properties

Austenite is ductile and relatively soft compared with martensite, is non-magnetic, and has a higher electrical resistivity and a different thermal expansion behaviour than ferrite. Its FCC structure also allows good formability and weldability in austenitic alloys, though its low yield strength and susceptibility to sensitisation during welding (chromium carbide precipitation) require care in practice.
