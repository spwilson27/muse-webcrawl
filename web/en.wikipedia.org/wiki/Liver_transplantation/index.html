Liver transplantation

Liver transplantation replaces a diseased liver with a healthy donor organ.

Liver transplantation or hepatic transplantation is the replacement of a diseased liver with a healthy liver from a donor (allograft). It is the definitive treatment for end-stage liver disease and acute liver failure, and is also used for some liver cancers and metabolic disorders. Most transplants use livers from deceased donors; living-donor transplantation, in which a portion of a healthy person's liver is transplanted and both segments regenerate, is common in Asia and for paediatric recipients.

History

The first human liver transplant was performed by Thomas Starzl in Denver in 1963; the first successful long-term survivor followed in 1967. Results improved dramatically with ciclosporin-based immunosuppression in the 1980s, and Starzl's Pittsburgh programme and Roy Calne's Cambridge/King's College programme established the procedure. Living-donor and split-liver techniques were developed in the late 1980s and 1990s.

Indications and allocation

Indications include decompensated cirrhosis from viral hepatitis, alcohol-related liver disease, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease; primary biliary cholangitis and sclerosing cholangitis; acute liver failure; hepatocellular carcinoma within defined (Milan) criteria; and inborn errors of metabolism. Because donor organs are scarce, allocation systems prioritise by severity of illness, commonly using the MELD (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease) score.

Outcomes and complications

One-year survival after modern liver transplantation is around 90%, and five-year survival roughly 70–80%, varying by indication and recipient condition. Complications include surgical problems (bleeding, bile leaks, hepatic artery thrombosis), organ rejection — usually manageable with immunosuppressants such as tacrolimus — infection, and recurrence of the original disease. Lifelong immunosuppression and follow-up are required.
