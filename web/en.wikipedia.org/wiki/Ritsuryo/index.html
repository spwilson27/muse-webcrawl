The ritsuryō (Japanese: 律令) system was the historical legal and governmental system of ancient Japan, based on the Chinese legal system of the Tang dynasty, which was in turn influenced by Confucian philosophy and Legalism. It is named after the two bodies of law that composed it: the ritsu (律), a penal code, and the ryō (令), an administrative code. Under the ritsuryō system, Japan was organised as a centralised, bureaucratic imperial state, with the emperor at its apex — a dramatic transformation of the earlier clan (uji) order.

History

The process began with the Taika Reforms of 645, in which the imperial court proclaimed sweeping changes modelled on Tang China. The first full ritsuryō codes are lost, but the Taihō Code of 701, compiled under Prince Osakabe and Fujiwara no Fuhito, survives through later editions and established the classic structure. The Yōrō Code (compiled 718, implemented 757) revised and largely replaced it and became the standard for the remainder of the system's life. Commentaries such as the Ryō no Gige (833) and Ryō no Shūge (c. 880) preserved and interpreted the codes.

Government structure

The codes divided central government into the Department of Divinities (Jingi-kan), overseeing Shinto observances, and the Great Council of State (Daijō-kan), which directed eight ministries (including Central Affairs, Ceremonial, Civil Affairs, Popular Affairs, War, Justice, the Treasury, and the Imperial Household). The country was divided into provinces (kuni) governed by appointed officials (kokushi), subdivided into districts and villages. A census and land-allotment system (handen-shūju) registered households every six years and redistributed paddy land, funded by taxes in rice, produce, and labour (the so-yō-chō system). Rank at court was formalised in a hierarchy of court ranks, and the state maintained post stations, a law-code trained bureaucracy, and conscript militias (later replaced by provincial warrior forces).

Decline

From the 9th century the system eroded in practice: tax-exempt private estates (shōen) drained the fiscal base, hereditary office-holding by families such as the Fujiwara regents displaced the code's meritocratic ideals, and practical governance shifted to extra-code institutions (ryōge no kan). Yet the ritsuryō framework remained the formal constitution of the imperial court throughout the Heian period and beyond, its offices and titles persisting as court dignities long after real power passed to the shogunates; some ceremonial elements survived until the Meiji Restoration.
