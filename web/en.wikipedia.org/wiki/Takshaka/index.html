Takshaka

Takshaka (Sanskrit: तक्षक, Takṣaka) is a king of the nāgas (serpent beings) in Hindu mythology, appearing prominently in the epic Mahabharata and in the Puranas. He is described as a descendant of Kadru and a ruler dwelling in the Khandava forest, and later in Takshashila (Taxila), which tradition associates with his name.

In the Mahabharata

When Arjuna and Krishna burned the Khandava forest to feed the fire god Agni, Takshaka was absent, but his wife was killed and his son Ashvasena narrowly escaped; the serpent king thereafter bore a grudge against the Pandava line. Years later, Takshaka brought about the death of King Parikshit, the grandson of Arjuna and ruler of Hastinapura. Cursed by the young sage Shringin to die of a snakebite within seven days, Parikshit took elaborate precautions, but Takshaka succeeded — in the usual telling, by hiding in a fruit offered to the king — and bit him fatally. The frame story of the Mahabharata follows from this: Parikshit's son Janamejaya, seeking vengeance, performed a great snake sacrifice (sarpa satra) to exterminate the nagas, during which the epic was recited to his court by Vaishampayana. The sacrifice was halted by the intervention of the sage Astika, born of a naga mother.

Other traditions

Takshaka also appears in Jain and Buddhist literature. In Buddhist texts, the nagas are among the beings who honour the Buddha, and Takshaka is listed among the great naga kings. In Chinese and Japanese Buddhist tradition derived from these sources he appears as one of the Eight Great Naga Kings (Hachidai Ryūō), named Duōluó (Takshaka) in Chinese sources.
