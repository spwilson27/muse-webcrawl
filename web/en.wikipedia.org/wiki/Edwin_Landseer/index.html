Sir Edwin Henry Landseer (7 March 1802 – 1 October 1873) was an English painter and sculptor, best known for his paintings of animals — particularly horses, dogs and stags — and for the bronze lions at the base of Nelson's Column in Trafalgar Square.

Career

A prodigy who exhibited at the Royal Academy at thirteen, Landseer became a favourite of Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, painting their pets and teaching the Queen etching. He was elected a Royal Academician in 1831 and knighted in 1850; he declined the Academy's presidency in 1866 amid declining mental health.

Works

His paintings, widely disseminated through engravings made by his brother Thomas and others, were hugely popular in Victorian Britain. Sentimental and anthropomorphic scenes such as The Old Shepherd's Chief Mourner (1837), showing a collie grieving at its master's coffin, defined the Victorian image of animal devotion. The Monarch of the Glen (c. 1851), a majestic red deer stag in the Highlands, became one of Britain's most recognisable images, used for over a century in advertising. His four bronze lions for Trafalgar Square, modelled with some controversy from a dead lion from London Zoo, were unveiled in 1867.

Legacy

The Landseer Newfoundland dog variety is named after him. Though his reputation fell with Victorian sentimentality, his technical mastery of animal anatomy and texture remains admired.
