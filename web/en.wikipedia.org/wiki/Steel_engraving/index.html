Steel engraving

Steel engravings allowed extremely fine lines and very large print runs.

Steel engraving is a technique in printmaking, developed in the early nineteenth century, in which an image is incised into a steel plate with a burin (graver) or by etching. Because steel is much harder than the copper traditionally used for engraving, a steel plate could withstand far longer print runs without the lines wearing down, and it permitted finer and more delicate detail.

Development and use

The American inventor Jacob Perkins pioneered the practical use of softened steel plates for printing banknotes around 1810, since steel engraving's fine detail was difficult to counterfeit; the technique was taken up in Britain by firms founded by artists such as Charles Heath. Through the 1820s to 1860s, steel engravings became the standard means of illustrating books, annuals such as The Keepsake, topographical prints, portraits, and reproductions of famous paintings, and they were used on banknotes and postage stamps.

Technique

The engraver cut lines into a softened steel plate with a burin, often combining the work with etching and machine ruling; the plate was then hardened before printing. Prints were produced on a rolling press as intaglio impressions. Steel-facing (electroplating copper plates with iron) later offered similar durability more cheaply, and photographic processes such as photogravure and halftone displaced steel engraving for book illustration by the end of the nineteenth century — though intaglio steel engraving persisted in security printing well into the modern era.
