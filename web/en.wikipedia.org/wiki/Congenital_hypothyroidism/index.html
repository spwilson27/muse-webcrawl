Congenital hypothyroidism (CH) is a deficiency of thyroid hormone present at birth. If untreated, it can lead to severe and irreversible impairment of growth and intellectual development, historically known as cretinism. It is one of the most common preventable causes of intellectual disability worldwide, and is the reason thyroid function is included in newborn screening programmes in most developed countries.

Causes

The most common cause worldwide is iodine deficiency in the mother and infant. In iodine-sufficient regions, most cases result from an abnormally developed thyroid gland (thyroid dysgenesis), including an absent (athyreosis), underdeveloped (hypoplastic), or ectopically located gland. A smaller proportion result from inherited defects in thyroid hormone synthesis (dyshormonogenesis), often associated with a goitre. Transient congenital hypothyroidism can also occur from maternal antithyroid drugs, iodine excess or deficiency, or transplacental antibodies.

Signs and symptoms

Most affected infants appear normal at birth because of residual maternal thyroid hormone. Signs emerge over the first weeks and include poor feeding, lethargy, constipation, a hoarse cry, prolonged jaundice, a large posterior fontanelle, macroglossia, a distended abdomen with umbilical hernia, and dry, mottled skin.

Screening and diagnosis

Newborn screening typically measures thyrotropin (TSH), sometimes with thyroxine (T4), in a dried blood spot taken at 48–72 hours of life. Abnormal results are confirmed with serum thyroid function tests, and treatment should begin within the first two weeks of life.

Treatment

Treatment is daily oral levothyroxine, dosed at roughly 10–15 micrograms per kilogram in the newborn period, with regular monitoring of TSH and free T4 to keep values in the upper half of the reference range during the first years of life. Started early, treatment allows essentially normal growth and cognitive development.
