Andalusi Arabic (also called Andalusian Arabic) was the variety of spoken Arabic that developed in al-Andalus, the Muslim-ruled parts of the Iberian Peninsula, between the Umayyad conquest of 711 and the expulsion of the Moriscos in 1609. While formal writing used Classical Arabic, the vernacular diverged markedly, acquiring a strong Romance substrate from the local population (Mozarabs) and features shared with the Arabic dialects of North Africa.

Features

Andalusi Arabic is documented mainly through poetry (the zajal strophic verse and the kharjas — Romance or mixed-language refrains closing Arabic and Hebrew muwashshah poems), popular proverbs (notably the collection of Ibn Quzmān), legal documents from Toledo written in Arabic by Christian scribes, and the writings of the botanist and agronomists of Seville. Characteristic features include the imāla (raising of long ā toward ī), loss of case and mood endings, restructuring of the verb system, and a large Romance loanword stock; in turn it lent hundreds of words to Spanish and Portuguese, many still in use today (aceite, ojalá, alcalde).

Decline

Arabic receded with the Christian reconquest of Iberia, surviving longest in the Nasrid kingdom of Granada (until 1492) and among the Moriscos, who also wrote Spanish in Arabic script (aljamiado), until their expulsion in 1609–1614 finally extinguished the language in Iberia.
