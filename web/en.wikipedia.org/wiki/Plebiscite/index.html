Plebiscite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A plebiscite is a direct vote by the electorate on a single political question. The term derives from Roman plebiscita, decrees of the plebeian council, and in modern usage is often treated as a synonym of referendum; where a distinction is drawn, “plebiscite” tends to denote votes on sovereignty, territory or a ruler's mandate, and frequently carries the suggestion of a vote staged by an authoritarian regime to legitimise itself — the plebiscites of Napoleon III, of Hitler's Germany (1933–38) and of other dictatorships being the classic cases.

Plebiscites under international supervision decided several European borders after the First World War (Schleswig, Upper Silesia, the Saar in 1920–21 and again in 1955), and decolonisation and secession questions have often been put to plebiscites, sometimes recognised (South Sudan, 2011) and sometimes not (Crimea, 2014, whose vote most states regard as invalid). Chile's 1988 plebiscite, in which voters rejected extending Pinochet's rule, is the celebrated case of such a vote turning against its author.

Distinctions

Constitutional lawyers distinguish plebiscites from referendums (in some systems, votes initiated by authorities versus votes required by law) and from initiatives (citizen-proposed). Whatever the label, the democratic quality of such votes depends on free campaigning, fair media access and a genuine choice — the features staged plebiscites lack.
