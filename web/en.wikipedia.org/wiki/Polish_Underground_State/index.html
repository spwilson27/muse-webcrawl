The Polish Underground State (Polish: Polskie Państwo Podziemne) was a collective term for the underground resistance organisations in Nazi- and Soviet-occupied Poland during World War II. Subordinate to the Polish government-in-exile in London, it was unique in occupied Europe for replicating the full apparatus of a state: an army, courts, administration, education, and a parliament-like political council, all operating in secret.

The Kotwica, symbol of the Polish Underground State and Home Army.

Structure

Civil authority was exercised by the Government Delegation for Poland, which ran departments mirroring pre-war ministries, enforced verdicts of underground special courts against collaborators, and organised secret schooling and universities attended by thousands. The armed wing, the Home Army (Armia Krajowa), grew to some 350,000–400,000 sworn members by 1944, the largest resistance movement in occupied Europe.

Activities and end

The Underground State gathered intelligence for the Allies (including on the Holocaust and the V-2 rocket), ran the Żegota council that aided Jews, and sabotaged German transport. In 1944 it launched Operation Tempest and the Warsaw Uprising. As the Red Army advanced, Soviet NKVD arrested and dismantled its structures; its leaders were tried in Moscow in the Trial of the Sixteen (1945), and the Delegation dissolved itself in July 1945.
