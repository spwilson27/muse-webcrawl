English drama

English drama encompasses plays written in England (and later Britain) from medieval religious drama through the flourishing Elizabethan and Jacobean theatre, Restoration comedy, and the modern stage. Its most famous figure is William Shakespeare.

Medieval drama

Drama emerged from church liturgy, developing into mystery cycles (such as the York and Wakefield cycles) performed by trade guilds, morality plays like Everyman, and interludes.

Elizabethan and Jacobean drama

The building of permanent theatres in London from 1576 (The Theatre; later the Globe, 1599) enabled a golden age. Playwrights included Christopher Marlowe (Doctor Faustus), Shakespeare, Ben Jonson, Thomas Kyd, John Webster and Thomas Middleton. Genres ranged from history plays and tragedies to city comedy.

Closure and Restoration

Parliament closed the theatres in 1642 during the Civil War. After the Restoration of Charles II in 1660, theatres reopened with actresses on stage for the first time and a vogue for witty Restoration comedy (Congreve, Wycherley, Etherege) and heroic drama.

18th–19th centuries

Sentimental comedy, melodrama, and the plays of Sheridan and Goldsmith gave way to Victorian spectacle and the early work of Wilde and Shaw toward the century's end.

Modern drama

The 20th century saw George Bernard Shaw, Noël Coward, Samuel Beckett (Irish-born, writing in English), Harold Pinter, Tom Stoppard, and the Angry Young Men of the Royal Court, notably John Osborne's Look Back in Anger (1956).
