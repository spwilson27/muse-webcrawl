Pneumonia Severity Index

The Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI), also known as the PORT score, is a clinical prediction rule used to estimate the risk of death in adults with community-acquired pneumonia, helping clinicians decide whether a patient can safely be treated as an outpatient or requires hospital admission. It was developed by the Pneumonia Outcomes Research Team (PORT) and published by Michael Fine and colleagues in 1997.

Calculation

The rule works in two steps. First, a small set of criteria (age 50 or younger, no major comorbidities such as cancer, liver disease, heart failure, stroke, or renal disease, and normal mental status and vital signs) identifies the lowest-risk patients as Class I without any arithmetic. Everyone else is scored on 20 variables: age (points approximately equal to age in years for men, age minus 10 for women), nursing-home residence, comorbid illnesses, abnormal vital signs (fever, tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypotension, altered mental status), and laboratory or imaging findings such as low pH, raised blood urea nitrogen, low sodium, high glucose, anaemia, low oxygen, and pleural effusion.

Use and limitations

The PSI is endorsed in joint American Thoracic Society / Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines for community-acquired pneumonia site-of-care decisions, usually alongside clinical judgement and considerations such as the ability to take oral medication and social support. Compared with the simpler CURB-65 score, the PSI uses more variables and requires laboratory results, but it is better validated for identifying low-risk patients safe for outpatient care. It can underestimate severity in young patients without comorbidities who nonetheless have dangerous physiology, and it should not override clinical judgement.
