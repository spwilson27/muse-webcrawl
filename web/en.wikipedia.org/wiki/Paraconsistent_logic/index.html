Paraconsistent logic is a type of non-classical logic that attempts to deal with contradictions in a discriminating way. Unlike classical logic, a paraconsistent logic does not treat a contradiction as a fatal defect from which every proposition follows (the principle of explosion, ex contradictione quodlibet). Paraconsistent systems allow inconsistent premises — as arise in legal codes, databases, belief sets and semantic paradoxes — to yield useful, non-trivial conclusions.

Motivation

Classically, from A and ¬A one can derive any B whatever, via disjunction introduction and disjunctive syllogism. Real reasoning, by contrast, routinely quarantines contradictions: a database containing two conflicting records is not thereby committed to every statement. Paraconsistent logics modify consequence so that at least one step of the classical derivation fails — most commonly disjunctive syllogism, which is invalid when A may be "both true and false".

Approaches

  • Discussive logic (Jaśkowski, 1948) interprets propositions as claims made by participants in a discussion, so contradictions reflect disagreement rather than collapse.
  • Da Costa's C-systems (from 1963) weaken negation, introducing a "consistency" operator so that explosion holds only for propositions certified as consistent.
  • Logic of Paradox (LP; Priest, 1979) is a three-valued logic assigning some propositions both values true and false ("gluts"); it is the formal backbone of dialetheism, the view that some contradictions — such as the liar paradox — are actually true.
  • Relevance logics require premises to be relevantly connected to conclusions, incidentally blocking explosion.
  • Adaptive logics (Batens) reason classically by default and retract inferences when contradictions surface.

Applications and criticism

Paraconsistent techniques have been applied to inconsistent databases and knowledge bases, deontic and legal reasoning, automated theorem proving, and the semantic paradoxes. Critics question whether any formal system can both tolerate contradiction and preserve ordinary mathematical reasoning; dialetheism's stronger claim — that some contradictions are true of the world itself — remains a minority position, though ably defended by Priest and others.
