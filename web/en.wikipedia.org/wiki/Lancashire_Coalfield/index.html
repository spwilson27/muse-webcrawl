The Lancashire Coalfield in North West England was one of the most productive British coalfields. The area of coal measures extends from near St Helens and Wigan in the west to Burnley in the northeast and southeast towards Manchester and Oldham. Coal was mined here from the medieval period, and the coalfield was central to the Industrial Revolution in Lancashire, supplying fuel to the textile mills, chemical works, glassworks, and domestic hearths of a rapidly growing industrial region.

Geology

The coalfield consists of Carboniferous Coal Measures — alternating sandstones, shales, and numerous coal seams — folded and faulted, dipping generally southward from outcrops around the Rossendale anticline and the West Pennine Moors. The productive seams include the Wigan series (including the celebrated cannel coal) and seams such as the Arley, Trencherbone, and Pemberton seams. In the south the measures pass beneath Permian and Triassic cover towards Cheshire.

History

Coal mining in Lancashire is documented from the 13th century. Output grew with the development of canals — including the Leeds and Liverpool Canal, which served the Burnley and Wigan pits — and later railways. Wigan became famous for coal; the "Wigan peer" music-hall joke and George Orwell's The Road to Wigan Pier reflect the town's mining identity. At its peak around 1900–1914, the coalfield employed well over 70,000 miners and produced more than 25 million tons of coal a year from several hundred pits.

The Lancashire coalfield saw many disasters, including the Pretoria Pit disaster of 1910 near Westhoughton, which killed 344 men and boys — the third-worst mining disaster in British history. After nationalization in 1947, pits were progressively closed as seams were worked out and demand fell; large modern collieries such as Parkside (opened 1964, closed 1993) were the last deep mines. The final deep mines closed in the 1990s, ending large-scale deep mining in the coalfield, though small mines and opencast workings continued for a time.

Legacy

The coalfield's heritage is preserved at Astley Green Colliery Museum and in the Lancashire Mining Museum, and in the landscape of spoil heaps ("ruks"), flashes (subsidence lakes, such as those at Pennington Flash), and miners' settlements across south Lancashire.
