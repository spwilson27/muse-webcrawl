Breton (Breton: brezhoneg) is a Southwestern Brittonic Celtic language spoken in Brittany (Breton: Breizh), in western France. It was brought from Great Britain to Armorica by migrating Britons during the Early Middle Ages, making it an Insular Celtic language that evolved on the continent; its closest relatives are Cornish and Welsh. Breton is the only Celtic language still spoken on the European mainland.

History

Old Breton is attested from the 9th century in glosses and personal names; Middle Breton (12th–17th centuries) has a substantial religious and poetic literature. From the 17th century onward the language retreated westward within Brittany under pressure from French, a process accelerated after the French Revolution by centralising language policy and, in the 20th century, by schooling that punished children for speaking Breton. In 1900 Breton was the everyday language of well over a million people in western ("Breizh Izel") Brittany; by the early 21st century the number of speakers had fallen to around 200,000, most of them elderly.

Status and revival

Breton has no official status in France, although it is recognised as a regional language and appears on bilingual signage. Revival efforts include the Diwan immersion schools (founded 1977), bilingual streams in public and Catholic schools, university courses, and Breton-language media. UNESCO classifies Breton as "severely endangered". The language has four traditional dialect areas — Léon, Trégor, Cornouaille and the more divergent Vannetais — and several competing standard orthographies, of which the peurunvan ("fully unified") spelling is the most widely used.

Linguistic features

Breton is a verb–subject–object language with initial consonant mutations (soft, hard, mixed and aspirate), a definite article an/ar/al but no indefinite article, and conjugated prepositions, all features shared with its Brittonic sisters. Its vocabulary is substantially Celtic with a large stratum of French loanwords.
