Wall walk

A wall walk, also called a parapet walk or chemin de ronde, is the protected walkway running along the top of a fortification wall, behind the parapet. It allowed defenders to patrol the defences, observe an approaching enemy, and shoot or drop missiles through the embrasures and machicolations while remaining partly protected by the parapet's merlons.

Design

The walkway was typically built on the inner face of a curtain wall, wide enough for soldiers to pass one another and to move supplies and ammunition during a siege. Access was by stairs within mural towers or from the castle courtyard. In many designs the wall walk was supported on corbels or arches, which sometimes carried a secondary outer gallery — the basis of the machicolation. Wooden hoardings could be added in wartime to extend the fighting platform outwards.

History

Wall walks appear on fortifications from antiquity onward: Roman town walls, Byzantine defences such as the Walls of Constantinople (which had walkways at multiple levels), and the castles and town walls of medieval Europe. Famous surviving examples include the walls of Carcassonne, York's city walls (where the walk survives as a public footpath), and the Great Wall of China, whose broad top served as both wall walk and communications road.
