Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing

Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) is a type of digital transmission used in telecommunications, consisting of many closely spaced modulated carriers. In conventional frequency-division multiplexing, carriers are separated by guard bands; in OFDM the sub-carrier signals are mathematically orthogonal, so their spectra may overlap without interfering, making highly efficient use of bandwidth.

Principles

An OFDM transmitter splits a high-rate data stream across many narrowband subcarriers, each modulated at a low symbol rate (typically with QAM or PSK). Because the symbol duration on each subcarrier is long relative to channel delay spread, OFDM is resilient to multipath fading and intersymbol interference; a cyclic prefix copied at the start of each symbol further protects against multipath. The modulation and demodulation can be implemented efficiently with the inverse fast Fourier transform (FFT) and FFT, an insight that made practical OFDM possible with modern signal processors. A patent on multicarrier modulation was granted to Robert W. Chang of Bell Labs in 1970.

Applications

OFDM underlies a large share of modern communications: Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11a/g/n/ac/ax), LTE and 5G NR mobile networks (in the OFDMA variant), DSL broadband (discrete multitone), digital television (DVB-T/T2) and radio (DAB), and power-line communication. Its drawbacks include a high peak-to-average power ratio and sensitivity to frequency offset and phase noise.
