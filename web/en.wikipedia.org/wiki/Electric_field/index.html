Electric field

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of electric field lines radiating from a positive charge and converging on a negative one]
Field lines of an electric dipole.

An electric field is the physical field that surrounds electrically charged particles. It exerts a force on all other charged particles in the field, either attracting or repelling them, and is defined as the force per unit charge: E = F/q, measured in newtons per coulomb or volts per metre.

The field concept — action mediated by a space-filling quantity rather than at a distance — was developed by Michael Faraday (field lines) and James Clerk Maxwell, whose equations describe how electric fields are produced by charges (Gauss's law) and by changing magnetic fields (Faraday's law), and how together they propagate as light. In Quantum electrodynamics the electric field is an operator whose quanta are photons, and its vacuum fluctuations have measurable effects (Casimir effect, Lamb shift). Electric fields accelerate particles in machines from cathode-ray tubes to the Large Hadron Collider (where radiofrequency cavities' fields do the work), drive currents in circuits, and — at breakdown strengths of about 3 million volts per metre in air — discharge as lightning.
