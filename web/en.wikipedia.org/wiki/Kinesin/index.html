A kinesin is a protein belonging to a class of motor proteins found in eukaryotic cells. Kinesins move along microtubule filaments, powered by the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and are essential for intracellular transport, mitosis, and meiosis.

Structure

Most kinesins consist of two heavy chains and two light chains. Each heavy chain has a globular motor (head) domain at the amino terminus that binds microtubules and ATP, a flexible neck linker, a long coiled-coil stalk, and a tail domain that binds cargo, often via the light chains. The first kinesin discovered, kinesin-1 (conventional kinesin), was isolated from squid axoplasm in 1985.

Movement

Kinesin-1 walks "hand over hand" along a protofilament of a microtubule, each head alternately stepping 16 nm so the molecule's centre of mass advances about 8 nm per ATP hydrolysed. The motor is highly processive, taking hundreds of steps before detaching, which makes it suited to long-distance transport of vesicles, organelles, proteins, and mRNA from the cell centre toward the periphery (anterograde transport), the opposite direction to cytoplasmic dynein.

Superfamily and functions

The kinesin superfamily comprises at least 14 families (kinesin-1 through kinesin-14) with diverse roles. Many members function in the mitotic spindle: kinesin-5 (Eg5) slides antiparallel microtubules apart, kinesin-13 family members depolymerise microtubule ends rather than walk, and kinesin-14 family members move toward the minus end. In neurons, kinesin-1 and kinesin-3 family motors drive axonal transport; defects are implicated in neurodegenerative disease.

Clinical relevance

Because spindle kinesins are required for cell division, they have been investigated as anticancer drug targets, although inhibitors of Eg5 such as ispinesib showed limited clinical success. Mutations in the kinesin-1 heavy chain gene KIF5A are associated with hereditary spastic paraplegia and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.
