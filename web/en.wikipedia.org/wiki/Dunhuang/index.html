Dunhuang

Dunhuang 敦煌
County-level city, Jiuquan, Gansu, China
Coordinates: 40.14° N, 94.66° E

Dunhuang (Chinese: 敦煌; pinyin: Dūnhuáng) is a county-level city in northwestern Gansu Province, in western China. Known as Shazhou in ancient times, it was a major stop on the ancient Silk Road and is best known for the nearby Mogao Caves, a UNESCO World Heritage Site containing nearly five hundred Buddhist temples grottoes carved into cliffs between the 4th and 14th centuries.

History

Chinese control of the Hexi Corridor was established under Emperor Wu of Han, and Dunhuang was founded as one of four frontier commanderies around 111 BCE, guarding the route westward where the Silk Road split into northern and southern routes around the Taklamakan Desert. The city was a vital garrison, trading post, and gateway for Buddhism entering China from Central Asia and India.

Control of Dunhuang passed between Chinese dynasties, Tibetan Empire, local warlords such as the Guiyi Circuit, and the Western Xia. The trove of manuscripts preserved in the sealed Library Cave (Cave 17) at Mogao, discovered by the monk Wang Yuanlu in 1900 and partly acquired by explorers Aurel Stein and Paul Pelliot, transformed scholarship on medieval Central Asia.

Geography

Dunhuang lies in an oasis amid the Gobi Desert, near the singing sand dunes of Mingsha Shan and Crescent Lake (Yueyaquan). The climate is cold desert, with hot summers and cold, dry winters.

Sights

Principal sights include the Mogao Caves, Mingsha Shan and Crescent Lake, the Yumen and Yangguan passes, and stretches of Han-dynasty Great Wall. See Mogao Caves and Dunhuang manuscripts.
