Privy Council of Japan

The Privy Council of Japan (枢密院, Sūmitsuin) was an advisory council to the Emperor of Japan that operated from 1888 to 1947, established under the Meiji Constitution. Modelled partly on the British Privy Council, it deliberated on important matters of state referred to it by the Emperor, including draft legislation, treaties, imperial household matters, and declarations of war, and it played a significant — often conservative and obstructive — role in Japanese politics before the Second World War.

History

The Council was created in 1888, with Itō Hirobumi as its first president, initially to deliberate in secret on the draft of the Meiji Constitution. Its members — a president, vice-president, and councillors appointed for life from among senior statesmen, peers, and officials — were drawn from the elite; the princes of the imperial family could also attend. Under the constitution the Emperor consulted the Council on proposed laws and ordinances, treaties, and matters of the Imperial House Law.

Role and influence

In the 1920s and 1930s the Council repeatedly clashed with party cabinets, scrutinising and sometimes amending government proposals, as in the controversies over the ratification of the London Naval Treaty of 1930, where its objections raised the issue of the "right of supreme command". Its essentially unelected, elder-statesman character made it a bastion of conservatism within the Meiji constitutional order.

Abolition

The Council ceased to function with the coming into force of the post-war Constitution of Japan on 3 May 1947, which contains no provision for such a body; it was formally abolished that year.
