Geology of China

The geology of China is shaped by several ancient continental blocks (cratons) and the mountain belts formed as they collided with one another and with surrounding terranes. The principal blocks are the North China Craton, the South China Block (Yangtze Craton), and the Tarim Craton, separated by major orogenic belts such as the Central China Orogen (including the Qinling and Dabie ranges) and the Central Asian Orogenic Belt to the north.

Tectonic framework

The North China Craton contains some of the oldest rocks on Earth, with Archean basement more than 3.8 billion years old, but its eastern part was extensively reactivated and thinned during the Mesozoic — a process known as decratonization. The South China Block formed by the amalgamation of the Yangtze and Cathaysia blocks in the Neoproterozoic.

Himalayas and Tibet

The ongoing collision of the Indian Plate with the Eurasian Plate, which began in the Cenozoic, has uplifted the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau, the highest and largest plateau on Earth. This collision continues to drive seismicity across western China.

Resources and hazards

China's geology hosts major deposits of coal, rare earth elements, gold and other minerals. The country is also subject to significant geological hazards, including devastating earthquakes along the margins of the Tibetan Plateau and in the North China Plain, landslides in mountainous regions, and subsidence in mining areas.
