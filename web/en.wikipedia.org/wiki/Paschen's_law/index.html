Paschen's law

Paschen's law is an empirical relationship describing the breakdown voltage of a gas as a function of the product of the gas pressure and the electrode separation. It is named for the German physicist Friedrich Paschen, who published it in 1889 after measuring the voltage at which a spark jumps between parallel metal plates in various gases.

Statement

Paschen found that, for a given gas and electrode material, the breakdown voltage Vb depends only on the product pd of pressure p and gap distance d:

Vb = B·pd / ( ln(A·pd) − ln[ ln(1 + 1/γ) ] )

where A and B are constants depending on the gas and γ is the secondary electron emission coefficient of the cathode. Plotted against pd, breakdown voltage traces the characteristic Paschen curve, which falls to a minimum and then rises steeply.

Explanation

Paschen minima (typical)
Air≈ 327 V at pd ≈ 0.57 Torr·cm
Helium≈ 156 V
Hydrogen≈ 273 V

The shape of the curve reflects two competing effects tied to the electron mean free path. At high pd, electrons collide so frequently that they cannot gain enough energy between collisions to ionise the gas, so a higher voltage is needed. At low pd, the gap contains too few gas molecules: electrons cross without enough ionising collisions, and again the voltage must rise. Between these regimes lies the Paschen minimum, the lowest voltage at which breakdown can occur for that gas — important in the design of gas-discharge lamps, surge protectors, plasma devices, and high-voltage insulation, and a practical concern in low-pressure environments such as aircraft and spacecraft electronics.

Relation to other laws

Paschen's law applies where breakdown proceeds by Townsend avalanche ionisation. At very small gaps, field emission and other mechanisms cause departures from the law; at high pressures and long gaps, streamer mechanisms (described by the Raether–Meek criterion) take over.
