The Rashtrakuta dynasty was a royal Indian dynasty ruling large parts of the Indian subcontinent between the sixth and tenth centuries. The imperial line, founded by Dantidurga around 753 CE after overthrowing the Chalukyas of Badami, governed from Manyakheta in present-day Karnataka and at its height controlled territory stretching from the Ganges doab in the north to Kanyakumari in the south, making it one of the great powers of early medieval India.

History

Dantidurga (r. 735–756) defeated the Chalukya king Kirtivarman II and established Rashtrakuta supremacy in the Deccan. His successor Krishna I (r. 756–774) completed the conquest and commissioned the celebrated rock-cut Kailasa temple at Ellora. Under Dhruva Dharavarsha (r. 780–793) and Govinda III (r. 793–814), the Rashtrakutas campaigned deep into northern India, intervening in the tripartite struggle with the Gurjara-Pratiharas and the Palas for control of Kannauj. Amoghavarsha I (r. 814–878), whose long reign was marked by religious tolerance and literary patronage, moved the capital to Manyakheta. Indra III briefly occupied Kannauj in the early tenth century. The dynasty declined after the death of Krishna III (939–967), and the last Rashtrakuta ruler was overthrown in 982 by Tailapa II, founder of the Western Chalukya Empire.

Administration and society

The empire was divided into provinces (rashtras) governed by officials answerable to the emperor, with village assemblies managing local affairs. The Rashtrakutas maintained a large standing army and a powerful cavalry, and Arab travellers such as Sulaiman and Al-Masudi ranked their king among the great monarchs of the world. Trade with the Arab world flourished, and Arab merchants were welcomed on the western coast.

Culture and architecture

Rashtrakuta patronage produced some of India's finest art and literature. The Kailasa temple at Ellora, excavated downward from a single rock, is considered a marvel of Indian architecture. Kannada literature flourished: the court of Amoghavarsha produced the Kavirajamarga, the earliest surviving work on Kannada poetics, and poets such as Pampa, Ponna and Ranna — the "three jewels" of Kannada literature — were patronised by Rashtrakuta kings and their feudatories. Jainism received strong royal support, and the Jain monk-scholars Jinasena and Gunabhadra composed important works at court.

Religion

Although many Rashtrakuta kings were devoted Jains, the dynasty was notably tolerant, supporting Shaivism, Vaishnavism and Buddhism alongside Jain institutions. This pluralism is reflected in the cave complexes at Ellora, where Hindu, Buddhist and Jain monuments stand side by side.
