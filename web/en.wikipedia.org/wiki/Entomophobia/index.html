Entomophobia (from Greek ἔντομον, entomon, "insect", and φόβος, phobos, "fear") is a specific phobia characterized by an excessive or unrealistic fear of one or more classes of insects. It is classified as a phobia by the DSM-5 among the animal phobias, a group that also includes arachnophobia (fear of spiders), ophidiophobia (fear of snakes), and cynophobia (fear of dogs).

Despite its name, in common usage "entomophobia" is often used loosely to refer to a fear of arthropods generally, including arachnids such as spiders and scorpions, even though arachnophobia is technically a separate diagnosis.

Close encounters with insects can provoke intense anxiety in people with entomophobia.

Signs and symptoms

People with entomophobia may experience anxiety ranging from mild unease to a full panic attack when they see, touch, or even think about insects. Symptoms can include rapid heartbeat, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, nausea, and an overwhelming urge to flee. Many sufferers go to great lengths to avoid places where insects might be present, which can restrict outdoor activities, travel, and even the use of certain rooms in their own homes.

Because insects are ubiquitous, the condition can be more impairing than phobias of rarely encountered animals. Sufferers may repeatedly inspect clothing, bedding, and food, and may react strongly to the sound of buzzing or the sensation of something crawling on the skin.

Causes

Like other specific phobias, entomophobia is thought to arise from a combination of genetic predisposition, learned behavior, and experience. A traumatic encounter in childhood — such as being swarmed by wasps or waking to find insects in the bed — can condition a lasting fear response. Children may also acquire the fear by observing fearful reactions in parents or peers. Some researchers have suggested an evolutionary component: a heightened vigilance toward small, fast-moving, many-legged creatures may once have conferred protection against venomous bites and disease-carrying pests.

Treatment

The most effective treatments are forms of exposure therapy, in which the patient is gradually and systematically exposed to the feared stimulus — beginning, for example, with photographs of insects, progressing to dead specimens, and eventually to live insects — while learning to manage the anxiety response. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is often combined with exposure to challenge catastrophic beliefs about insects. In some cases, short-term medication such as beta blockers or anxiolytics may be used to manage acute symptoms, though medication alone is generally not considered a long-term solution.
