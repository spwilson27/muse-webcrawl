The Williamite War in Ireland (1689–1691), also known in Irish as Cogadh an Dá Rí ("War of the Two Kings"), was a conflict between the supporters of the deposed Catholic king James II (Jacobites) and those of the Protestant William III (Williamites). It was the Irish theatre of the wider Nine Years' War and the decisive episode in the Protestant Ascendancy that followed.

Background

After the Glorious Revolution of 1688 drove James II from England, he landed at Kinsale in March 1689 with French troops and money to use Catholic Ireland — still largely Jacobite under the Earl of Tyrconnell — as a base for recovering his thrones. Protestant resistance held Derry (besieged April–July 1689) and Enniskillen.

Campaigns

The Williamite commander Schomberg landed in August 1689 but campaigned cautiously. William III himself arrived in June 1690 and defeated James at the Battle of the Boyne on 1 July 1690 (Old Style) — still commemorated on 12 July by the Orange Order — after which James fled to France. Jacobite resistance continued under Patrick Sarsfield: the Williamite siege of Limerick failed in 1690, but Godert de Ginkell stormed Athlone and won the decisive, bloody Battle of Aughrim on 12 July 1691, where the French Jacobite commander Saint-Ruhe was killed.

Treaty of Limerick and legacy

Limerick capitulated by treaty on 3 October 1691: its articles promised Catholics a measure of religious tolerance and allowed some 14,000 Jacobite soldiers — the "Wild Geese" — to sail for French service. The promises were soon broken by the Irish Parliament's Penal Laws, completing the dispossession of Catholic landowners and shaping Irish politics for a century.
