The Blue Mountains are a rugged region west of Sydney in New South Wales, Australia. The region consists of a sandstone plateau dissected by deep gorges up to 760 metres deep. It takes its name from the characteristic blue haze created by volatile oils released from the vast eucalyptus forests, which scatter short-wavelength light. The Greater Blue Mountains Area, comprising eight protected areas including Blue Mountains National Park, was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2000.

Geography

The mountains rise from the Cumberland Plain near Penrith to a plateau of around 1,100 m. Principal features include the Jamison, Grose and Megalong valleys, the Three Sisters sandstone formation at Katoomba, Wentworth Falls, and Mount Victoria. Towns along the Great Western Highway and railway include Katoomba, Leura, Blackheath, Springwood and Wentworth Falls, collectively forming the City of Blue Mountains.

History

Aboriginal peoples, including the Gundungurra and Darug, have lived in the region for tens of thousands of years; rock art and occupation sites survive, including the Red Hands Cave. Europeans failed repeatedly to cross the range until the 1813 expedition of Blaxland, Lawson and Wentworth, which opened the way to the western plains. A road followed in 1814–15, and the railway reached the mountains in the 1860s, after which the area became a popular health and holiday resort.

Nature

The region protects remarkable plant diversity, including over 90 eucalypt taxa and the Wollemi pine, discovered in 1994 in a remote canyon. Fauna include the spotted-tail quoll, koala, yellow-bellied glider and the Blue Mountains water skink. Severe bushfires, notably in 2019–20, periodically burn large areas.
