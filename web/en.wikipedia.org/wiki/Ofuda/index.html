Ofuda

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ofuda (御札 / お札) is a talisman made of paper, wood, cloth or metal issued by a Shinto shrine or Buddhist temple in Japan, inscribed with the name of a kami or Buddhist deity, the name of the shrine or temple, and often sacred characters or images. Ofuda are believed to embody the power or blessing of the deity and to protect the household or person who keeps them.

Use

Household ofuda — most famously the Jingū taima, the ofuda of the Grand Shrine of Ise distributed nationwide through local shrines — are enshrined in a household altar (kamidana), or placed high on a pillar or above a lintel facing south or east. Specific ofuda are obtained for purposes such as protection from fire (an important role of the ofuda of Akiba shrines), safe childbirth, road safety and academic success; paper ofuda may be pasted at entrances or carried on the person. Ofuda are traditionally renewed each year, with old ones returned to a shrine for ritual burning (otakiage).

Relation to other talismans

Ofuda overlap with omamori (small brocade amulets) but are larger, are associated principally with households and places rather than with individuals, and serve as an object of worship in themselves — a temporary vessel (shintai-like) of the kami's divided spirit (kanjō). Particular types include the protective slips distributed by temples (in Buddhism, gofu) influenced by Chinese Taoist talisman traditions.

In popular culture

In manga, anime and games, ofuda feature as the paper seals used by shrine maidens, exorcists and onmyōji to seal or ward off spirits — a depiction derived from their real apotropaic use, though usually exaggerated for dramatic effect.
