Neuromodulation (medicine)

Neuromodulation is defined by the International Neuromodulation Society as "the alteration of nerve activity through targeted delivery of a stimulus, such as electrical stimulation or chemical agents, to specific neurological sites in the body." In medicine it refers to a family of therapies in which implanted or external devices deliver electrical pulses — or, in the case of intrathecal pumps, drugs — to nerves, the spinal cord, or the brain in order to treat chronic pain, movement disorders, epilepsy, and other conditions. Unlike ablative surgery, neuromodulation is generally reversible and adjustable.

Methods

Spinal cord stimulation

In spinal cord stimulation (SCS), electrodes are placed in the epidural space over the dorsal columns and connected to an implanted pulse generator. First reported in 1967 by Norman Shealy, SCS is used for failed back surgery syndrome, complex regional pain syndrome, and other neuropathic pain states. Modern systems can deliver tonic, burst, or high-frequency waveforms, some without the paraesthesia of traditional stimulation.

Deep brain stimulation

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) delivers continuous high-frequency pulses to targets such as the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus internus. Developed for Parkinson's disease in the 1990s by Alim-Louis Benabid and colleagues, it is also used for essential tremor, dystonia, and — under humanitarian or approved indications in some regions — epilepsy and obsessive–compulsive disorder.

Other techniques

  • Sacral nerve stimulation — for overactive bladder and faecal incontinence.
  • Vagus nerve stimulation — implanted or transcutaneous stimulation for epilepsy and depression.
  • Peripheral nerve and dorsal root ganglion stimulation — for focal neuropathic pain.
  • Responsive neurostimulation — closed-loop cortical stimulation that detects and responds to abnormal activity in epilepsy.
  • Intrathecal drug delivery — implanted pumps delivering morphine, ziconotide, or baclofen directly to the spinal fluid.
  • Non-invasive methods — transcranial magnetic stimulation and transcranial direct-current stimulation act from outside the body and are sometimes classed with neuromodulation.

History

Medical use of electricity dates to antiquity, but modern neuromodulation began with the gate control theory of pain (Melzack and Wall, 1965), which motivated dorsal column stimulation in 1967 and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation. The field expanded rapidly from the 1990s as device technology, battery life, and imaging improved.

Risks

Risks include infection, lead migration or fracture, hardware malfunction, battery replacement surgery, and stimulation side effects. Careful patient selection and trial stimulation (for SCS) are standard practice.
