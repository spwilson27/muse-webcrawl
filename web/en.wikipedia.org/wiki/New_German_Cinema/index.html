New German Cinema (German: Neuer Deutscher Film) is a period in West German cinema that lasted from the late 1960s to around 1982. A group of young directors, working with low budgets and state subsidy, produced artistically ambitious films that broke with the commercial entertainment cinema of the postwar decades and won major prizes at international festivals, restoring German film's international reputation for the first time since the Weimar era.

Origins

At the Oberhausen Short Film Festival in February 1962, twenty-six young filmmakers issued the Oberhausen Manifesto, famously declaring "Der alte Film ist tot. Wir glauben an den neuen" — "The old film is dead. We believe in the new one." Signatories included Alexander Kluge and Edgar Reitz. In 1965 the federal government created the Kuratorium Junger Deutscher Film to fund first films, and the Film Subsidy Law of 1967 established the Filmförderungsanstalt (FFA). Regional television stations, especially WDR, became crucial co-producers. Alexander Kluge's Yesterday Girl (1966) and Volker Schlöndorff's Young Törless (1966) were among the movement's early successes.

Principal figures and films

  • Rainer Werner Fassbinder — extraordinarily prolific (over 40 films in 14 years), including The Merchant of Four Seasons (1971), Ali: Fear Eats the Soul (1974), The Marriage of Maria Braun (1979) and the television series Berlin Alexanderplatz (1980).
  • Werner Herzog — Aguirre, the Wrath of God (1972), The Enigma of Kaspar Hauser (1974), Nosferatu the Vampyre (1979) and Fitzcarraldo (1982).
  • Wim Wenders — the "road movie trilogy" including Alice in the Cities (1974) and Kings of the Road (1976), and The American Friend (1977).
  • Margarethe von Trotta — The Lost Honour of Katharina Blum (1975, co-directed with Schlöndorff) and Marianne and Juliane (1981).

The collective film Germany in Autumn (1978) responded to the events of the German Autumn of 1977. The movement is generally considered to have ended around 1982, the year of Fassbinder's death, as subsidy structures changed and filmmakers moved into international production.

Themes

Recurring concerns include coming to terms with the Nazi past, criticism of the West German economic miracle and consumer society, the situation of outsiders and guest workers, terrorism and the state's response, and a self-conscious engagement with Hollywood genre cinema.
