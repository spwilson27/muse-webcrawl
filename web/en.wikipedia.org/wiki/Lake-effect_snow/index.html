Lake-effect snow

Lake-effect snow
SeasonLate autumn to early spring (peak November–January in the Great Lakes)
Primary regionsGreat Lakes (North America); also Japan's Sea of Japan coast, Baltic, Caspian and other large lakes
Key requirementCold air passing over warmer open water; temperature difference of ~13 °C (23 °F) between lake surface and 850 hPa level
EffectsIntense narrow snow bands, locally extreme snowfall totals, thundersnow

Lake-effect snow is produced when cold air, typically from Arctic or polar air masses, passes over the warmer waters of a lake. As the cold air moves across the comparatively warm water, the lower layer of the atmosphere picks up heat and water vapour, rises by convection, and forms narrow bands of precipitating clouds that can deposit very heavy snow downwind of the lake shores.

Lake-effect snow is characteristic of the Great Lakes region of North America, where "snowbelts" lie along the southern and eastern shores of the lakes. Cities such as Buffalo, Rochester and Syracuse in New York, Erie in Pennsylvania, and Marquette in Michigan's Upper Peninsula receive some of the heaviest annual snowfalls in the eastern United States from this mechanism.

Formation

Key ingredients are a large temperature contrast between the lake surface and the air aloft (a difference of about 13 °C between the water and the 850-hPa level is the usual threshold for instability), a long fetch of open water over which the air can gather moisture, and weak directional wind shear so that convective bands remain organised. Snowfall rates within a mature band can exceed 5 cm (2 in) per hour, and individual storms can deposit over a metre of snow in a narrow corridor while areas a few kilometres away receive almost none.

Notable events

Buffalo's Christmas blizzard of 2022 and the November 2014 "Snowvember" storm — which buried parts of the Buffalo suburbs under more than 2 m (7 ft) of snow — are prominent recent examples. The phenomenon is not unique to North America: Japan's yukiguni ("snow country") along the Sea of Japan receives some of the world's deepest snowfalls by the analogous "sea-effect" process, and lake-effect snow occurs around the Baltic Sea, the Caspian Sea, the Black Sea, and lakes such as the Great Salt Lake in Utah.

Seasonal cycle

Lake-effect snow is most frequent in late autumn and early winter, when lakes are still largely ice-free and retain summer warmth while Arctic air masses begin to arrive. As lakes freeze over in late winter, the moisture supply is cut off and the effect diminishes.
