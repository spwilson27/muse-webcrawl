Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe

Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe
Period: 2007–2009 (peak Nov 2008)
Peak monthly inflation: est. 79.6 billion %
Currency: Zimbabwean dollar (abandoned 2009)

Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe was a period of currency instability that began in the late 1990s and peaked in November 2008. Zimbabwe's peak month of inflation is estimated at 79.6 billion percent month-on-month, 89.7 sextillion percent year-on-year, with prices doubling roughly every 24–25 hours — the second most severe episode in recorded history after Hungary in 1946.

Causes

Contributing factors included land reform from 2000, which disrupted agricultural production; the costs of involvement in the Second Congo War; foreign-currency shortages; corruption; and government printing of money to finance deficits. The Reserve Bank under Governor Gideon Gono expanded quasi-fiscal activities as confidence collapsed.

Course

The government repeatedly redenominated, lopping zeros: in August 2006 three zeros were removed, and in February 2009 twelve more. Notes reached $100 trillion. Official statistics ceased to reflect reality; ordinary people adopted US dollars, South African rand and barter, despite exchange controls. See Hyperinflation and Zimbabwean dollar.

End and aftermath

In January 2009 foreign currencies were legalised for transactions; in April 2009 the Zimbabwe dollar was suspended, effectively ending hyperinflation, and formally demonetised in 2015. A multi-currency system prevailed until the RTGS dollar / Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG) experiments of later years. The episode destroyed savings, pensions and the formal retail economy, and remains a textbook case alongside Weimar hyperinflation.
