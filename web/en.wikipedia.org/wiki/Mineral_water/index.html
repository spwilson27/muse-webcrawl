Mineral water is water from a mineral spring containing various minerals, such as salts and sulfur compounds. It is usually still, but may be sparkling (carbonated) either naturally or artificially. Mineral water has traditionally been used or consumed at spa towns and wells, and is now widely bottled and sold commercially. Well-known sources include Evian and Volvic in France, San Pellegrino and Acqua Panna in Italy, Perrier in France, and Gerolsteiner in Germany.

Mineral water is bottled at source from protected underground springs.

Definitions and regulation

Definitions vary by jurisdiction. In the European Union, "natural mineral water" must originate in an underground water table or deposit, be microbiologically wholesome, be bottled at source, and may not be disinfected; its mineral composition must be stable and declared on the label, classified by total dissolved solids (TDS) from "very low" (under 50 mg/L) to "rich in mineral salts" (over 1,500 mg/L). In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration defines mineral water as water from a geologically and physically protected underground source containing at least 250 parts per million total dissolved solids, with no minerals added.

Mineral content

The minerals dissolved during the water's passage through rock commonly include calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium, bicarbonate, sulfate and silica. Mineral waters are sometimes classified by their predominant ions — bicarbonate, sulfate, chloride or sulfurous waters — and spa traditions attributed therapeutic effects to particular compositions, although health claims are now tightly regulated in many jurisdictions.

History

Taking the waters at mineral springs dates to Roman times, with spa towns such as Bath in England and Spa in Belgium (from which the word "spa" derives). Commercial bottling expanded in the nineteenth century with rail transport and growing belief in the health-giving properties of particular springs. Today bottled mineral water is a global industry, and environmental concerns over plastic packaging have prompted debate about its footprint compared with tap water.
