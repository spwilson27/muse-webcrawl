A standardized test is a test that is administered and scored in a consistent, standard manner. The standardisation covers the questions, the conditions of administration (such as time limits), and the scoring rules, so that results can be compared across test-takers, schools or years. Any test in which the same test is given in the same manner to all test takers, and graded in the same manner for everyone, is a standardised test; standardised tests need not be multiple-choice or high-stakes.

History

Large-scale standardised examination has roots in imperial China's civil service examinations. In the West, standardised testing expanded with the intelligence testing movement of the early 20th century (the Binet–Simon scale; the U.S. Army Alpha and Beta tests of the First World War) and with the founding of testing organisations such as the Educational Testing Service (1947), which administers the SAT. Machine-scorable multiple-choice formats, popularised by Frederick J. Kelly in 1914, made mass testing practical.

Uses and types

Standardised tests are used for school accountability and placement, university admissions (SAT, ACT, A-levels, the Chinese gaokao), professional licensure, language proficiency (TOEFL, IELTS) and programme evaluation. Tests may be norm-referenced (comparing a candidate to a population) or criterion-referenced (measuring against a fixed standard).

Criticism

Critics argue that high-stakes standardised tests narrow the curriculum ("teaching to the test"), disadvantage candidates with less access to coaching, and may embed cultural bias. Defenders respond that standardised tests are generally more reliable and less biased than subjective alternatives such as interviews or school grades, and provide a common yardstick across schools. Debates over test-optional university admissions intensified after the COVID-19 pandemic, with some institutions reinstating testing requirements.
