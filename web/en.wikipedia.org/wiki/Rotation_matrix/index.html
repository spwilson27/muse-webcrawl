Rotation matrix

In linear algebra, a rotation matrix is a transformation matrix that is used to perform a rotation in Euclidean space. Such matrices preserve lengths and angles, and are characterised among all matrices by two properties: they are orthogonal (their transpose equals their inverse) and their determinant is +1 (which excludes reflections, which have determinant −1).

In two dimensions

The most common rotation matrix rotates points in the xy-plane counter-clockwise through an angle θ about the origin:

R(θ) = [ cos θ  −sin θ ;   sin θ   cos θ ]

Multiplying this matrix by a column vector rotates the vector by θ. To rotate a vector clockwise, or to rotate the coordinate axes instead of the vector, one uses the transpose, which is also the inverse.

In three dimensions

In three dimensions a rotation is specified by an axis and an angle. The basic rotation matrices rotate about a single coordinate axis; for example, rotation about the x-axis leaves the first coordinate unchanged and rotates the y–z plane as in two dimensions. Any rotation in 3D can be written as a product of such basic matrices (for example in an Euler-angle convention), and a general rotation about an arbitrary unit axis u by angle θ is given by Rodrigues' rotation formula.

Properties

  • The set of all n × n rotation matrices forms a group, the special orthogonal group SO(n); see rotation group SO(3) for the three-dimensional case.
  • The columns (and rows) of a rotation matrix form an orthonormal basis — geometrically, they are the images of the standard basis vectors.
  • Every rotation matrix in odd dimensions has eigenvalue +1, corresponding to the rotation axis; in 3D the trace equals 1 + 2 cos θ.
  • Composition of rotations corresponds to matrix multiplication, and the order matters: rotations in 3D do not commute.

Ambiguities and alternatives

Numerical use of rotation matrices can suffer from drift away from orthogonality, and orientation is often represented instead by Euler angles, axis–angle vectors, or unit quaternions, which avoid gimbal lock and are cheaper to normalise. Rotation matrices remain fundamental in computer graphics, robotics, crystallography, and physics.
