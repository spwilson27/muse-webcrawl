The Hale Telescope is a 200-inch (5.1 m) reflecting telescope at Palomar Observatory in San Diego County, California. Named in honour of astronomer George Ellery Hale, who conceived and championed the project, it was the largest telescope in the world from its completion in 1948 until 1976.

History

Hale secured a $6 million grant from the Rockefeller Foundation in 1928 to build a 200-inch telescope for the California Institute of Technology. The primary mirror was cast by Corning Glass Works from Pyrex; a first blank cracked, and the second took nearly a year to cool. World War II delayed the project, and Hale died in 1938, a decade before the telescope's dedication. First light was achieved on 26 January 1949, when Edwin Hubble took the inaugural exposure.

Design

The telescope uses an equatorial horseshoe mount, whose open design allows the instrument to point at the north celestial pole. Its 14.5-ton primary mirror is supported by a sophisticated system of mechanical counterweights to prevent flexure. The mirror's figure was polished to within a few millionths of an inch.

Scientific legacy

The Hale Telescope contributed to the measurement of the expansion of the universe, the discovery of quasars' cosmological distances, and studies of stellar populations in other galaxies. It remains in active use, equipped with modern instruments and adaptive optics, and was also the telescope whose missing decades of photographic plates later allowed the recovery of early observations of the Kuiper Belt object Haumea.
