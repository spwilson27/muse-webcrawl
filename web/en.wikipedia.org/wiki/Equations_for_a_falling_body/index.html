Equations for a falling body

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A set of equations describes the motion of a body falling under the influence of Earth's gravity, neglecting air resistance. Under this idealization, all objects accelerate downward at the same constant rate, g ≈ 9.81 m/s² near the Earth's surface, regardless of their mass — a result associated with Galileo Galilei's experiments and famously demonstrated by Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott, who dropped a hammer and a feather together on the airless Moon in 1971 and watched them strike the surface simultaneously.

A falling ball photographed at equal time intervals: the spacing grows quadratically.

The equations

For a body falling from rest through a distance h, in time t, reaching speed v:

QuantityEquation
Distance fallenh = ½ g t²
Time to fallt = √(2h / g)
Speed on impactv = g t = √(2 g h)
Average speed during fallv̄ = ½ g t = v / 2

These are special cases of the general equations of motion for constant acceleration, v = u + at and s = ut + ½at², with initial velocity u = 0 and a = g. Useful rules of thumb at Earth's surface: an object falls about 4.9 m in the first second, 19.6 m in two seconds, and 44.1 m in three seconds; its speed increases by about 9.81 m/s (roughly 35 km/h) with each second of fall.

Energy formulation

By conservation of energy, gravitational potential energy lost equals kinetic energy gained: mgh = ½mv², which immediately gives v = √(2gh) — the impact speed depends only on the height of the fall, not on the path or the mass.

Effects of air resistance

In reality, drag opposes the motion and grows with speed, so a real object approaches a terminal velocity at which drag balances weight and acceleration ceases. For a human skydiver in a belly-to-earth position this is about 53 m/s (190 km/h). With quadratic drag the equations become:

v(t) = vt tanh(g t / vt),    h(t) = (vt² / g) ln cosh(g t / vt)

where vt is the terminal velocity. For falls of a few metres and dense objects, air resistance is negligible and the simple equations above are accurate.
