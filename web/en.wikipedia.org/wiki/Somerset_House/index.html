Somerset House is a large neoclassical complex in the Strand in central London, overlooking the River Thames. It is one of Britain's major public buildings and cultural centres. The present building was designed by Sir William Chambers and built from 1776 onwards on the site of a Tudor palace of the same name, originally constructed for Edward Seymour, Duke of Somerset, Lord Protector of England, from 1547.

History

The Tudor palace was a royal residence used by princesses and queens consort, including Anne of Denmark and Henrietta Maria. After the old palace was demolished, Chambers's new building housed learned societies and government offices: the Royal Academy (until 1837), the Royal Society, the Society of Antiquaries, and later the General Register Office — whose records of births, marriages, and deaths made "Somerset House" a byword for genealogy — and the Inland Revenue.

Present day

Since the late 20th century Somerset House has been reinvented as an arts centre. Its courtyard hosts open-air concerts, film screenings, and in winter one of London's best-known ice rinks. The complex is home to the Courtauld Gallery (with its celebrated Impressionist collection), the Gilbert Collection, exhibition spaces, creative-industry studios (Somerset House Studios and the Exchange), and parts of King's College London, which occupies the East Wing. The site is managed by the Somerset House Trust.

Architecture

Chambers's design is a masterpiece of British neoclassicism: a grand quadrangle around a large courtyard, with a river terrace originally rising directly from the Thames before the building of the Victoria Embankment in the 1860s–70s. The King's Barge House arch once gave water access to the river. Later wings by James Pennethorne and Robert Smirke extended the complex in the 19th century.
