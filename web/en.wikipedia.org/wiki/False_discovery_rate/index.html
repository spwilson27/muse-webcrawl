False discovery rate

In statistical hypothesis testing, the false discovery rate (FDR) is the expected proportion of "discoveries" (rejected null hypotheses) that are false (incorrect rejections). Controlling the FDR is a way to address the problem of multiple comparisons: procedures that control the FDR at a given level are generally more powerful (less conservative) than those controlling the family-wise error rate, such as the Bonferroni correction.

Benjamini–Hochberg procedure

Yoav Benjamini and Yosef Hochberg introduced the concept in 1995 with a simple step-up procedure: given m p-values sorted from smallest to largest, find the largest k such that p(k) ≤ (k/m)·q, and reject the k corresponding hypotheses. This controls the FDR at level q when the test statistics are independent or positively dependent. The method is widely used in genomics, neuroimaging, and other fields where thousands of hypotheses are tested simultaneously.

Related concepts

The FDR is connected to the positive false discovery rate of John Storey, which underlies q-values, and to the false discovery proportion, the realized share of false rejections in a given study. FDR-controlling procedures differ from per-comparison error control, which only fixes the significance level of each test individually.
