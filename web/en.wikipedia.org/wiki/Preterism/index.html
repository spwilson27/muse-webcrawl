Preterism

Preterism is a Christian eschatological view or system of biblical interpretation that interprets some or all prophecies of the Bible as events which have already happened — most centrally, the destruction of Jerusalem and the Second Temple by the Romans in AD 70. The term comes from the Latin praeter, meaning "past". Preterism stands in contrast to futurism, which reads the same prophecies as yet unfulfilled, and with historicism and idealism as one of the main schools of interpretation of Daniel and Revelation.

Varieties

Partial preterism

Partial preterism holds that most eschatological prophecies — the Olivet Discourse, the Great Tribulation, the Antichrist, and most of Revelation — were fulfilled in the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, while still affirming the creedal doctrines of a future bodily Second Coming of Christ, a future resurrection of the dead, and a final judgement. Partial preterism has been the more widely held form within historic orthodoxy and is common among Reformed and many Catholic and Orthodox interpreters, although its adherents do not always use the label.

Full preterism

Full preterism, defended in modern form by J. Stuart Russell in The Parousia (1878) and by Max King in the 1970s, holds that all prophecy was fulfilled by AD 70, including the Second Coming, the resurrection, and the judgement, understood as covenantal rather than physical events. Full preterists regard the AD 70 destruction as the end of the old covenant age and the full establishment of the new. Most denominations regard full preterism as heterodox because it denies a future bodily return of Christ and resurrection of the dead affirmed in the ecumenical creeds.

Key texts

Preterists emphasize the time statements of the New Testament — "this generation will not pass away until all these things take place" (Matthew 24:34), "the time is near" (Revelation 1:3; 22:10) — arguing that the original audience was promised fulfilment within its own lifetime, and that the "coming of the Son of Man" language of the Olivet Discourse echoes Old Testament "coming in judgment" imagery (e.g. Isaiah 19) applied to Jerusalem's fall, corroborated by the historian Josephus's account of the Jewish War.

History

Antecedents appear in writers such as Eusebius, who read Matthew 24 as fulfilled in AD 70. The system was developed in the Counter-Reformation era by the Spanish Jesuit Luis de Alcázar (1614), in part to deflect Protestant historicist identifications of the papacy with Antichrist. Preterist readings were later taken up by Hugo Grotius and, in various forms, by many modern scholars who date Revelation and the synoptic apocalypses against the background of the first Jewish–Roman war.
