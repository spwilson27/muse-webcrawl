FM broadcasting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FM broadcasting is a method of radio broadcasting that uses frequency modulation (FM) to provide high-fidelity sound over broadcast radio. It was invented by American engineer Edwin Howard Armstrong in 1933, and FM radio was first introduced commercially in the United States in the early 1940s. FM is used worldwide to provide high-fidelity sound for music and speech broadcasts.

Technology

In FM broadcasting, the frequency of the carrier wave is varied in proportion to the instantaneous amplitude of the modulating audio signal, while the amplitude of the carrier remains constant. This makes FM far less susceptible than amplitude modulation (AM) to noise and interference, because most natural and man-made noise manifests as amplitude variations, which an FM receiver can reject with a limiter stage.

A typical FM broadcast channel has a bandwidth of about 200 kHz. The pre-emphasis and de-emphasis networks boost high frequencies before transmission and cut them on reception, improving the signal-to-noise ratio. Most FM broadcasts are in stereo, using a multiplex system standardized in 1961 in which a 38 kHz subcarrier carries the stereo difference (L−R) signal. Many stations also carry subsidiary services such as the Radio Data System (RDS) on a 57 kHz subcarrier.

Broadcast bands

The FM broadcast band falls within the VHF part of the electromagnetic spectrum, usually 87.5 to 108.0 MHz, though Japan uses 76 to 95 MHz and some former Eastern Bloc countries used the lower OIRT band of 65.8 to 74 MHz. Because FM signals travel mainly by line of sight, their range is typically limited to roughly 50–100 km from the transmitter, unlike AM signals which can reflect off the ionosphere at night.

History

Armstrong demonstrated FM to RCA in 1934, showing that it could deliver static-free reception. The first commercial FM station, W1XOJ/WGTR in Massachusetts, began broadcasting in 1939. The Federal Communications Commission allocated the FM band and, in a controversial 1945 decision, moved it from 42–50 MHz to its present 88–108 MHz position, rendering early receivers obsolete. FM overtook AM in popularity for music broadcasting during the 1970s as hi-fi equipment spread, and today it remains the dominant analog broadcast medium for music in most countries.
