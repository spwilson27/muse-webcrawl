Minor-planet moon

A minor-planet moon is an astronomical object that orbits a minor planet as its natural satellite. Several hundred minor planets — asteroids, trans-Neptunian objects, and others — are known or suspected to possess moons, forming binary or multiple systems. Such systems are thought to arise mainly from collisions, rotational fission of "rubble pile" bodies spun up by the YORP effect, and gravitational capture.

History of discovery

The first confirmed minor-planet moon was Dactyl, discovered by the Galileo spacecraft in images of 243 Ida taken during its 1993 flyby. Ground-based discoveries followed using radar, stellar occultations, lightcurves, and adaptive-optics imaging on large telescopes. Notable systems include 87 Sylvia, the first known triple asteroid, with moons Romulus and Remus; 90 Antiope, a double system of two nearly equal components; 243 Ida and Dactyl; 433 Eros is not a binary, but the near-Earth asteroid 162173 Ryugu and others have reshaped understanding of rubble piles. The dwarf planet Pluto has five known moons, the largest being Charon, and Haumea, Eris, and Makemake also have moons.

Characteristics

Moons range from tiny satellites a few hundred metres across to bodies comparable in size to their primaries. Binary systems allow astronomers to determine the mass and density of the components from the satellite's orbit, providing key evidence that many asteroids are loosely bound rubble piles with high porosity. Near-Earth and main-belt binaries are common; in the trans-Neptunian region, binaries are especially frequent among cold classical Kuiper belt objects and may record conditions of the early Solar System.

Notable examples

  • 243 Ida and Dactyl — first confirmed asteroid moon (1993).
  • 87 Sylvia — first known triple asteroid system.
  • 90 Antiope — near-equal-mass double asteroid.
  • 66391 Moshup and Squannit — near-Earth binary visited by radar many times.
  • 65803 Didymos and Dimorphos — target of NASA's DART impactor mission in 2022, which changed Dimorphos's orbital period.
