The Great Basin bristlecone pine (Pinus longaeva) is a long-living species of pine found in the high mountains of the Great Basin in the western United States. It is one of three closely related bristlecone pines and is famous for its extreme longevity: individual trees have been dated at more than 5,000 years old, making them among the oldest known non-clonal organisms on Earth.

Distribution and habitat

The species grows in isolated groves at elevations of about 2,600–3,600 metres in eastern California (the White Mountains and Inyo Mountains), Nevada, and Utah. It typically occupies exposed, rocky dolomitic slopes where competition is low, soils are poor, precipitation is minimal, and the growing season is very short. Trees are often mostly dead, with only a narrow strip of living bark sustaining a small crown — a form imposed by millennia of wind, ice abrasion, and drought.

Longevity

Dendrochronologist Edmund Schulman identified the species' great age in the 1950s. The tree known as Methuselah, in the Ancient Bristlecone Pine Forest of the White Mountains, was dated by tree-ring counting to about 4,850 years old; its location is kept secret. An even older tree, at more than 5,000 years, was reported in 2012. The long, continuous tree-ring record from living trees and preserved deadwood — extending back more than 9,000 years — has been used to calibrate the radiocarbon dating timescale.

Conservation

Although historically secure, the species faces threats from the introduced fungal disease white pine blister rust and from mountain pine beetles, with warming climates allowing both to reach higher elevations. The species is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN but is protected within Inyo National Forest and Great Basin National Park.
