I²C (Inter-Integrated Circuit), pronounced "I-squared-C" or "I-two-C", is a synchronous, multi-controller/multi-target, single-ended, serial communication bus invented in 1982 by Philips Semiconductors (now NXP Semiconductors). It is widely used for attaching lower-speed peripheral integrated circuits to processors and microcontrollers in short-distance, intra-board communication.

Design

I²C uses only two wires: a serial data line (SDA) and a serial clock line (SCL), both pulled up to a supply voltage. Devices are addressed with 7-bit (or extended 10-bit) addresses. Standard-mode transfers run at 100 kbit/s, fast mode at 400 kbit/s, and later revisions reach 1 Mbit/s, 3.4 Mbit/s, and 5 Mbit/s.

Applications

The bus is ubiquitous in embedded systems for connecting sensors, EEPROMs, real-time clocks, and display controllers. Related protocols such as SMBus and PMBus are derived from it.
