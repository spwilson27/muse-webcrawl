DNA methylation

DNA methylation
ProcessAddition of a methyl group to DNA
Typical siteCytosine at CpG sites (5-methylcytosine)
EnzymesDNA methyltransferases (DNMT1, DNMT3A, DNMT3B)
FieldEpigenetics, molecular biology

DNA methylation is a biological process by which methyl groups are added to the DNA molecule. In mammals it occurs mainly at cytosine residues in CpG dinucleotides, producing 5-methylcytosine, and is a central mechanism of epigenetics — heritable changes in gene activity without changes to the DNA sequence itself.

Function

Methylation in gene promoter regions, especially CpG islands, generally represses transcription, by blocking transcription factors and recruiting repressive protein complexes. It is essential to normal development: it underlies genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation in females, suppression of transposable elements and stable cell-type-specific gene expression. Patterns are established by de novo methyltransferases DNMT3A and DNMT3B and copied through cell division by the maintenance enzyme DNMT1; active demethylation involves the TET enzymes.

Disease and ageing

Aberrant methylation is a hallmark of cancer: tumours show global hypomethylation alongside hypermethylation and silencing of tumour-suppressor genes. Changes in methylation patterns with age form the basis of "epigenetic clocks" that estimate biological age. Environmental factors, diet and development can influence methylation, though the extent to which acquired methylation marks are inherited across generations in humans remains debated.

Measurement

Methods include bisulfite sequencing — which converts unmethylated cytosine to uracil — methylation-specific PCR, and microarray platforms widely used in epigenome-wide association studies.
