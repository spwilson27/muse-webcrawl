School integration in the United States

School integration in the United States is the process of ending race-based segregation within American public and private schools. Although segregation was long practiced, the modern movement is associated with the civil rights era. The landmark Supreme Court decision in Brown v. Board of Education (1954) declared state laws establishing separate public schools for Black and white students to be unconstitutional.

Background

Following Reconstruction, Jim Crow laws in the South mandated segregated schooling, upheld by the "separate but equal" doctrine of Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). In practice, schools for Black children were chronically underfunded.

Implementation

Implementation of Brown met massive resistance. In 1957, the Little Rock Nine required federal troops to attend Central High School in Arkansas. Later cases, including Green v. County School Board (1968) and Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg (1971), pushed districts to dismantle dual systems and approved busing as a remedy.

Later developments

From the 1970s onward, court orders were gradually lifted, and scholars have documented rising resegregation driven by residential patterns and district boundaries. Debates continue over affirmative action, magnet schools, and assignment policies, reshaped by decisions such as Parents Involved v. Seattle (2007).
