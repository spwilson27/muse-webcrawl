Need for cognition

People high in need for cognition tend to enjoy effortful thinking tasks such as puzzles.

Need for cognition (NFC) is a personality trait reflecting an individual's tendency to engage in and enjoy effortful cognitive activity. People high in need for cognition seek out, acquire, think about, and reflect back on information to make sense of stimuli, relationships, and events in their world; people low in the trait prefer simpler cognitive tasks and rely more on heuristics and the opinions of others.

Measurement

The construct was developed by social psychologists John Cacioppo and Richard Petty in the early 1980s. The original Need for Cognition Scale (1982) contained 34 items; a widely used 18-item short form was published in 1984. Items include statements such as "I would prefer complex to simple problems" and "I like to have the responsibility of handling a situation that requires a lot of thinking", rated on Likert scales.

Research findings

Need for cognition plays a central role in the elaboration likelihood model of persuasion: high-NFC individuals are more likely to process persuasive messages through the "central route", scrutinizing argument quality, whereas low-NFC individuals are more influenced by peripheral cues such as source attractiveness or credibility. High-NFC people report enjoying abstract thinking, problem solving, and debate; they show better recall of message content and more resistance to weak arguments.

The trait correlates positively with openness to experience, curiosity, and measures of cognitive ability, and it has been studied in education, consumer psychology, health communication, and media use, where it predicts preferences for complex news content and greater information seeking.
