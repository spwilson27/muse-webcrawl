Capacity (law)

Capacity, in law, is the ability of a natural person or legal person to exercise rights, perform legal acts and incur duties that the law will recognise and enforce. People or entities that lack capacity — for example young children, or adults who cannot understand their decisions because of mental impairment — may have their transactions treated as void or voidable, and decisions may be made for them by guardians or courts.

Natural persons

Full legal capacity is generally attained at the age of majority (typically 18). Minors have limited capacity: their contracts are often voidable, except for necessaries. Adults may lose decision-making capacity through mental illness, intellectual disability, intoxication or brain injury. Modern statutes, such as the Mental Capacity Act 2005 in England and Wales, presume capacity and assess it decision-by-decision, asking whether the person can understand, retain and weigh relevant information and communicate a choice. Where capacity is absent, mechanisms include powers of attorney, guardianship and court-appointed deputies.

Specific capacities

  • Contractual capacity — the ability to enter binding agreements.
  • Testamentary capacity — the understanding required to make a valid will, classically defined in Banks v Goodfellow (1870).
  • Criminal capacity — the mental state, including intent and fitness to stand trial, required for criminal responsibility.
  • Capacity to consent — relevant in medicine and in laws on sexual activity.

Legal persons

Corporations and other organisations have capacity defined by their constitutions and governing law; acts beyond a company's objects were historically ultra vires, though modern company law (for example the UK Companies Act 2006) largely protects third parties dealing with a company in good faith.
