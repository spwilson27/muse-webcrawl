Eduard Hanslick

BornSeptember 11, 1825, Prague, Austrian Empire
DiedAugust 6, 1904 (aged 78), Baden, Austria-Hungary
OccupationMusic critic, aesthetician, professor

Eduard Hanslick (September 11, 1825 – August 6, 1904) was an Austrian music critic, aesthetician and historian, based in Vienna, who was among the most influential arbiters of musical taste in the second half of the nineteenth century. He championed the "absolute music" of Johannes Brahms and the classical tradition against the programme music and music dramas of Richard Wagner and the New German School of Franz Liszt.

Life and career

Hanslick was born in Prague, studied piano and composition (taking lessons from Václav Tomášek) and read law at the universities of Prague and Vienna, earning a doctorate in 1849. After work in the Austrian civil service, he became music critic of the Wiener Zeitung and then, from 1864, of the Neue freie Presse, Vienna's leading liberal newspaper, a post he held for some four decades. He was appointed lecturer (1861) and then professor of aesthetics and the history of music at the University of Vienna — among the first such chairs in Europe — and served on state committees for music education and awards.

On the Musically Beautiful

His treatise Vom Musikalisch-Schönen (On the Musically Beautiful, 1854) argued that the beauty of music lies in specifically musical content — "tonally moving forms" (tönend bewegte Formen) — rather than in the expression of definite feelings. The essay founded modern musical formalism and went through many editions, though Hanslick softened some formulations over time.

The "War of the Romantics"

Hanslick's reviews made and damaged reputations. A close friend of Brahms, he praised Brahms's symphonies as the continuation of Beethoven, while his attacks on Wagner's operas and on Tchaikovsky's Violin Concerto — which he famously described as music whose stink one could hear — became legendary. Wagner originally modelled the pedantic critic Beckmesser in Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg on Hanslick (in an early draft the character was named "Hans Lick"), caricaturing him as the enemy of artistic genius. Hanslick's influence waned after his death, but his writings remain central to debates in musical aesthetics.
