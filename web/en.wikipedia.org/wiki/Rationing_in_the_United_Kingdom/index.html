Rationing in the United Kingdom

Rationing was introduced in the United Kingdom during both world wars to ensure fair distribution of scarce food, fuel, clothing and other goods. In the Second World War, rationing began on 8 January 1940 with bacon, butter and sugar, administered by the Ministry of Food under Lord Woolton.

Second World War

Every citizen was issued a ration book containing coupons that had to be surrendered when buying rationed goods. Meat, cheese, tea, eggs, milk, fats and jam were all rationed at various points; bread was rationed only after the war, from 1946 to 1948. Petrol rationing began even earlier, in September 1939. The "Dig for Victory" campaign encouraged householders to grow their own vegetables, and public information campaigns taught cooks to make the most of the rations.

Post-war austerity

Unlike in the First World War, rationing continued long after 1945 because of Britain's balance-of-payments crisis and worldwide shortages. Sweets were derationed in 1953, and the last items, meat and bacon, came off ration on 4 July 1954 — fourteen years after rationing began. Paradoxically, surveys suggested public health and nutrition improved during rationing, as the diet was more evenly shared across social classes.
