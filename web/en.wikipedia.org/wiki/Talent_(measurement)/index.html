Talent (measurement)

The talent (Ancient Greek: τάλαντον, talanton, Latin: talentum) was a unit of weight — and by extension of money, as a weight of gold or silver — used in the ancient world, from Mesopotamia through Greece and Rome and in biblical times. Its mass varied by place and period: the Attic talent was about 26 kilograms (57 lb), while the talent referenced in Near Eastern systems could be around 30–34 kg. A talent was divided into 60 minas, each of 60 shekels in Mesopotamian usage (or 100 drachmae per mina in the Attic system, giving 6,000 drachmae to the talent).

Value

An Attic talent of silver (6,000 drachmae) was an enormous sum — roughly the pay of a skilled worker for many years, or the cost of equipping and operating a trireme for a period. In the New Testament parable of the talents (Matthew 25), a master entrusts servants with one, two, and five talents; the English word "talent" meaning natural aptitude derives from figurative readings of this parable — a semantic shift from a weight of money to a gift of ability that became established in the later Middle Ages. Figures quoted in ancient sources, such as the tribute of 10,000 talents, indicate state-scale wealth.

Regional variants

The Babylonian talent (~30.3 kg) was subdivided sexagesimally into 60 minas of 60 shekels. The Hebrew talent (kikkār) appears in the Old Testament, e.g., in accounts of the gold used for the Tabernacle. The Alexandrian and Roman talents differed again; the Roman talent was often reckoned at 100 librae (~32.3 kg). Because values differed, ancient authors — and modern historians quoting them — must specify which talent is meant.
