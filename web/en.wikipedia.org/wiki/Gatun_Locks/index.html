Gatun Locks

LocationGatún, Panama
WaterwayPanama Canal
Opened1913 (first transit); canal opened 1914
Total lift25.9 m (85 ft) in three steps

The Gatun Locks (Spanish: Esclusas de Gatún) are a three-step flight of ship locks on the Atlantic side of the Panama Canal, near the town of Gatún, Panama. They raise ships 25.9 metres (85 feet) from sea level to the level of Gatun Lake, and lower them again on the return passage.

Description

The locks consist of three paired chambers — twin lanes allowing simultaneous transits in opposite directions. Each original chamber is 33.5 m (110 ft) wide and has a usable length of 320 m (1,050 ft), defining the original "Panamax" ship size. The massive steel miter gates are moved by electric motors, and ships are guided through the chambers by electric towing locomotives known as mulas ("mules") running on tracks along the lock walls. The chambers are filled and emptied by gravity through culverts, using water from Gatun Lake; filling a chamber takes roughly eight to fifteen minutes.

History

The locks were built by the United States between 1907 and 1913 as part of the canal project, requiring enormous quantities of concrete — at the time among the largest concrete structures ever built. The first complete transit through the locks was made by the tug Gatun in September 1913, ahead of the canal's official opening in August 1914. A separate, larger set of locks ("Neopanamax" locks, including the Agua Clara Locks nearby) opened in 2016 for bigger vessels, while the Gatun Locks continue in daily use.
