Sibylline Oracles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sibylline Oracles (also called the pseudosibyllines) are a collection of oracular utterances written in Greek hexameters, attributed to the Sibyls, prophetesses who uttered divine revelations in a frenzied state. The surviving collection consists of fourteen books composed between roughly the 2nd century BC and the 6th–7th centuries AD by Jewish and Christian authors.

These texts must be distinguished from the Sibylline Books kept by the Roman state, a collection of genuinely pagan oracles consulted by the Senate in times of crisis; those originals are lost, having been destroyed around AD 405. The extant Oracles were instead written under the Sibyls' names to give authority and antiquity to Jewish and later Christian religious and political ideas.

Content

The Oracles review world history in prophetic form, denounce idolatry and immorality, announce disasters and the fall of kingdoms, and look forward to divine judgment and a coming messianic age. Book 3, largely Jewish and dating to the Hellenistic period (with material connected to Ptolemaic Egypt), is among the oldest strata. Christian redactors added prophecies of Christ, sometimes in acrostic form; a famous acrostic in Book 8 spells out "Jesus Christ, Son of God, Saviour, Cross" in Greek.

Transmission

The collection was quoted by Church Fathers including Lactantius and Augustine, and survived through Byzantine manuscripts. Its prophecies influenced medieval eschatology and Renaissance depictions of the Sibyls, such as those on Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling.
