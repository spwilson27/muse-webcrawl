The Dirlewanger Brigade, also known as SS-Sonderkommando Dirlewanger, was a Waffen-SS penal unit of Nazi Germany composed originally of convicted poachers and later of criminals, concentration-camp prisoners and other penal recruits. Commanded by Oskar Dirlewanger, a convicted offender and SS officer, it became notorious as one of the most brutal formations of the Second World War.

Formation

The unit was formed in 1940 at the instigation of Heinrich Himmler and Gottlob Berger, initially from poachers whose marksmanship and fieldcraft were thought useful for anti-partisan duties. It was deployed in occupied Poland, where its members guarded forced-labour camps in the Lublin district and committed numerous atrocities against the Jewish population.

Operations

Transferred to Belarus, the brigade conducted so-called anti-partisan operations that in practice involved mass murder of civilians; entire villages were destroyed and their inhabitants killed. In August 1944 the unit was sent to suppress the Warsaw Uprising, where it participated in the Wola massacre and other mass killings of Polish civilians, suffering very heavy casualties in the fighting. Rebuilt and expanded, it was redesignated the 36th Waffen Grenadier Division of the SS in February 1945 and fought on the Eastern Front before surrendering to American forces in May 1945.

Legacy

Historians regard the Dirlewanger Brigade as an extreme example of the criminal character of the Waffen-SS. Dirlewanger himself died in French custody in June 1945, reportedly beaten by Polish guards. Few of the unit's members were ever prosecuted, though some survivors were later identified and tried.
