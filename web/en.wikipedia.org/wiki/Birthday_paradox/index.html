In probability theory, the birthday paradox is the counterintuitive result that in a group of just 23 randomly chosen people, the probability that two of them share a birthday exceeds 50 percent. With 70 people, the probability rises above 99.9 percent.

Explanation

The result feels paradoxical because people instinctively compute the chance that someone shares their own birthday, which would require 253 people for even odds. In fact, any pair counts: 23 people form 253 pairs, and each pair has a 1-in-365 chance of matching. The complementary probability that all birthdays differ is 365/365 × 364/365 × … × 343/365 ≈ 0.493, so the chance of at least one match is about 50.7 percent.

Applications

The same mathematics governs the birthday attack in cryptography, which finds collisions in a hash function much faster than brute force would suggest — roughly after the square root of the number of possible outputs — and informs the required output lengths of secure hash functions.
