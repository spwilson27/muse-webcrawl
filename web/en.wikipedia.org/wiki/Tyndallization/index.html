Tyndallization

Image placeholder: Tyndallization

Tyndallization is a method of sterilisation devised by the physicist John Tyndall in the nineteenth century for destroying heat-resistant bacterial endospores in substances that cannot withstand the high temperatures of an autoclave. It is also known as fractional sterilisation or intermittent steaming.

Method

The substance is steamed at atmospheric pressure (about 100 °C) for some 20–30 minutes on three successive days. The first steaming kills vegetative bacteria and fungi but leaves endospores alive. During the intervals, kept in a warm place, the surviving spores germinate into vegetative cells, which are then killed by the second steaming; the third treatment catches any spores that germinated late. After the process the material should be sterile.

Uses and limitations

Tyndallization was historically used for culture media containing sugars, gelatin, milk or other ingredients damaged by autoclaving, and for some pharmaceutical preparations. It has largely been superseded by autoclaving, membrane filtration and other modern methods, because it is slow (requiring at least three days), unreliable against the most heat-resistant spores and prions, and leaves the material exposed to possible recontamination between heatings. It nevertheless remains a classic demonstration of the biology of endospores and of Tyndall's experimental refutation of spontaneous generation.
