Oganesson is a synthetic chemical element with the symbol Og and atomic number 118. It was first synthesized in 2002 at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, near Moscow, Russia, by a joint team of Russian and American scientists. In December 2015 it was recognised as one of four new elements by the Joint Working Party of IUPAC and IUPAP, and it was formally named on 28 November 2016. The name honours the nuclear physicist Yuri Oganessian, a pioneer in the synthesis of superheavy elements — only the second time an element was named after a living person, after seaborgium.

Discovery

The earliest claimed synthesis, at Berkeley in 1999, was retracted after the data were found to have been fabricated. The accepted discovery came from bombardment of californium-249 targets with calcium-48 ions at Dubna, producing a few atoms of oganesson-294 in experiments reported in 2002 and 2006. Only about five atoms of 294Og have ever been detected, each decaying by alpha emission within a millisecond.

Predicted properties

Oganesson completes the seventh period of the periodic table and sits in group 18, the noble gases. Unlike its lighter congeners, however, calculations predict that strong relativistic effects on its electrons make it surprisingly reactive and possibly a solid semiconductor at room temperature rather than a gas. Its electron cloud is unusually diffuse and polarisable, and it may even have a positive electron affinity — unique among noble gases. Because only single atoms have been produced, none of these properties have been measured experimentally.
