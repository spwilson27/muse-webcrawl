The Shrine of the Báb is a structure in Haifa, Israel, where the remains of the Báb, founder of the Bábí Faith and forerunner of Baháʼu'lláh in the Baháʼí Faith, have been laid to rest on the slopes of Mount Carmel. It is considered the second holiest place for Baháʼís, after the Shrine of Baháʼu'lláh near Acre.

History

The Báb was executed in Tabriz, Persia, in 1850; his remains were secretly transferred and eventually brought to the Holy Land. In 1891 Baháʼu'lláh designated a spot on Mount Carmel for the shrine. The central structure was built by ʻAbdu'l-Bahá, who interred the Báb's remains there in 1909. The superstructure — a golden-domed edifice of rose Baveno granite, Chiampo stone, and a dome of Dutch golden tiles — was designed by Canadian architect William Sutherland Maxwell and completed in 1953 under Shoghi Effendi. The surrounding Baháʼí Terraces, nineteen terraced gardens extending up and down Mount Carmel, were completed in 2001.

Design

The shrine combines Eastern and Western architectural proportions and styles. Its dome rises 40 metres and is covered with some 12,000 fish-scale golden tiles. In 2008 the shrine was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the "Baháʼí Holy Places in Haifa and the Western Galilee".
