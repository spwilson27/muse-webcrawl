The Khanate of Kokand was a Central Asian state in the Fergana Valley that existed from about 1709 to 1876. Ruled by the Ming dynasty of Uzbeks—unrelated to the Chinese Ming—it grew from a small principality around the city of Kokand into a regional power encompassing much of present-day Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and southern Kazakhstan, before being annexed by the Russian Empire.

Rise

Around 1709 the Ming clan chief Shahrukh Biy broke away from Bukhara's control and built a citadel at Kokand. His successors expanded steadily; under Alim Khan (1799–1811), who first took the title of Khan, the state conquered Tashkent and much of the valley. Umar Khan and Madali Khan extended Kokand's frontiers to the steppe fortresses of the Kazakh khans and into the Pamir fringe, building a chain of forts along the Syr Darya—including Aq Masjid (later Kyzylorda)—that brought the khanate into collision with Russia.

Decline and civil strife

From the 1840s the khanate was destabilised by conflict between sedentary Uzbeks and Tajiks and the Kipchak and Kyrgyz military elite, who repeatedly installed and murdered khans. The powerful regent Alimqul briefly held the state together before dying in 1865 defending Tashkent against the Russians. The popular but capricious Khudayar Khan, deposed and restored several times, exhausted the treasury on palaces—including his great palace at Kokand—while ceding territory to Russia.

Annexation

Russian forces took Tashkent in 1865 and Samarkand in 1868. A punitive expedition followed a revolt in 1875–76 under Pulat Khan; in February 1876 General Mikhail Skobelev occupied Kokand, Khudayar fled, and the khanate was abolished outright, its lands forming the Fergana Oblast of Russian Turkestan.

Economy

Kokand prospered on Fergana's irrigated agriculture—cotton, silk, grain, and fruit—and on caravan trade with Kashgar, Russia, and Bukhara; thousands of its merchants traded in Chinese Turkestan, protected by treaty arrangements with Qing China.
