Combinatorics on words

Combinatorics on words is a fairly new field of mathematics, branching from combinatorics, which focuses on the study of words and formal languages. The subject looks at letters or symbols, and the sequences they form. It has applications in algebra, computer science, and the study of aperiodic structures.

History

The field traces back to Axel Thue's pioneering work in the early 1900s on repetition-free words; Thue showed that there exist arbitrarily long words over a three-letter alphabet with no repeated block (squares), and over a two-letter alphabet with no triple repetition (cubes). Marston Morse independently studied related sequences. The subject was revived and named by a group of mathematicians around M. Lothaire in the 1980s and 1990s.

Core topics

  • Avoidability: Which patterns can be avoided in infinite words over a given alphabet?
  • Sturmian words: Aperiodic infinite words of minimal complexity, connected to rotations of the circle and continued fractions.
  • Word equations: Equations over free monoids, including the classic result that a word commuting with another must be a power of a common word.
  • Periodicity: The Fine–Wilf theorem on when two periods of a word force a common period.
  • Subword complexity: Counting distinct factors of an infinite word.

Applications

Combinatorics on words informs the theory of formal languages and automata, data compression, bioinformatics (analysis of DNA sequences), and the mathematics of quasicrystals, where Sturmian words model one-dimensional aperiodic order.
