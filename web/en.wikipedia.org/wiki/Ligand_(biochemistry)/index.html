Ligand (biochemistry)

In biochemistry and pharmacology, a ligand is a substance that forms a complex with a biomolecule to serve a biological purpose. The term usually refers to a molecule that binds to a specific site on a target protein — most commonly a receptor — and thereby triggers or modulates a signal or function. Ligand binding is typically reversible and is governed by intermolecular forces such as hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and van der Waals forces.

Binding and affinity

The strength of ligand binding is described by its affinity, often expressed as the dissociation constant Kd: a lower Kd indicates tighter binding. Binding is also characterised by specificity — the ability of a protein to discriminate between ligands — and by efficacy: agonists activate a receptor, antagonists block it without activation, and inverse agonists reduce its basal activity. The relationship between ligand concentration and response is classically described by dose–response curves and the Hill equation.

Examples

Natural ligands include neurotransmitters (such as acetylcholine and dopamine), hormones (such as insulin and adrenaline), growth factors, and substrates of enzymes. In pharmacology, most drugs act as ligands for receptors, enzymes, ion channels, or transporters. Oxygen binding to haemoglobin is a classic example of cooperative ligand binding.

Measurement

Ligand binding is studied with techniques such as radioligand binding assays, surface plasmon resonance, isothermal titration calorimetry, and fluorescence methods, which quantify affinity, kinetics, and thermodynamics of the interaction and underpin much of modern drug discovery.
