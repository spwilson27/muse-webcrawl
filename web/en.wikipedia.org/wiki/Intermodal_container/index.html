Intermodal container
Also known asISO container; shipping container
Standard lengths20 ft, 40 ft, 45 ft (high cube)
TEU20-foot equivalent unit
StandardsISO 668, ISO 6346

An intermodal container is a large standardised shipping container, designed and built for intermodal freight transport — the movement of goods between ship, rail, and truck without unloading and reloading the cargo itself. Containers can be stacked, locked together with twistlocks, and transferred between modes mechanically. Since their standardisation, they have become the foundation of globalised trade, cutting cargo-handling costs dramatically.

History

Containerisation was pioneered by the American trucking entrepreneur Malcom McLean. In April 1956 his company loaded 58 trailer vans onto the converted tanker Ideal X at Port Newark for Houston, demonstrating the concept. McLean's Sea-Land Service standardised the design; in the 1960s ISO standards fixed the dimensions (8 ft wide, 8.5 ft high, 20 or 40 ft long) and corner fittings, and the industry adopted the 20-foot equivalent unit (TEU) as its measure. By the 1970s, break-bulk cargo liners were being replaced by cellular container ships.

Design and types

General-purpose (dry van) containers have corrugated steel walls; variants include open-top, flat rack, refrigerated (reefer), tank, and ventilated containers. The 40-foot high-cube box (9.5 ft tall) and the 45-foot container are common on high-volume routes. Corner castings and ISO markings (per ISO 6346, including the BIC owner code) allow identification and securing anywhere in the world.

Impact

Containerisation slashed freight costs — one often-cited estimate put the saving at over 90% for general cargo — and enabled just-in-time global supply chains. The largest container ships now carry more than 24,000 TEU. Containers have also found second lives as modular housing, pop-up shops, offices, and disaster-relief clinics.
