Cervical vertebrae

The cervical spine, shown in red, between the skull and the thoracic vertebrae.

In tetrapods, the cervical vertebrae (singular: cervical vertebra) are the vertebrae of the neck, immediately below the skull. Most mammals, including humans, have seven cervical vertebrae, designated C1 through C7. They form the cervical spine, which supports the head, protects the spinal cord, and permits the wide range of head motion characteristic of the neck.

Structure

Cervical vertebrae
Number in humans: 7 (C1–C7)
Part of: Vertebral column
Articulations: Skull, thoracic vertebrae
Latin: vertebrae cervicales

Cervical vertebrae are generally the smallest of the true vertebrae. Their distinguishing features include a foramen transversarium in each transverse process, through which the vertebral artery and vein pass (except in C7, whose foramen transmits only accessory veins), bifid spinous processes in the middle members of the series, and a relatively large vertebral foramen relative to the small body.

Atlas and axis

The first two cervical vertebrae are highly modified. The atlas (C1) lacks a body and a spinous process; its lateral masses bear the occipital condyles of the skull, enabling the nodding "yes" motion. The axis (C2) bears the dens (odontoid process), a peg-like projection around which the atlas rotates, enabling the shaking "no" motion of the head. The seventh vertebra, C7, is called the vertebra prominens because of its long, easily palpable spinous process.

Clinical significance

The cervical spine is vulnerable to injury in trauma, including whiplash, fractures and dislocations that can damage the spinal cord. Degenerative conditions such as cervical spondylosis and disc herniation can cause neck pain, radiculopathy and myelopathy. Cervical vertebrae are also the usual reference points in counting spinal levels during clinical examination and imaging.
