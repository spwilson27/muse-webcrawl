Roosevelt Corollary

The Roosevelt Corollary was an extension of the Monroe Doctrine announced by United States president Theodore Roosevelt in his annual message to Congress on December 6, 1904. It asserted that the United States had the right to intervene in Latin American countries guilty of "chronic wrongdoing" in order to forestall intervention by European powers.

Background

The corollary arose from the Venezuelan crisis of 1902–03, when Britain, Germany and Italy blockaded Venezuela to force payment of debts. Roosevelt feared that European debt collection could lead to territorial occupation in the Western Hemisphere, violating the Monroe Doctrine. His addition held that the United States, as an "international police power", would itself intervene to stabilise the finances and governments of hemisphere nations.

Application

The corollary was invoked to justify US interventions and customs receiverships in the Dominican Republic (1905), Nicaragua, Haiti and Cuba, and it underpinned the "Big Stick" diplomacy of Roosevelt and his successors. Latin American jurists, notably Argentina's Luis María Drago, contested the doctrine. In 1928 the Clark Memorandum repudiated the corollary, and Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy formally abandoned interventionism in the 1930s.
