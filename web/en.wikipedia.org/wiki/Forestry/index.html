Forestry
ScopeCreation, management and conservation of forests
Key disciplinesSilviculture, forest ecology, mensuration, forest economics
ProductsTimber, pulpwood, fuelwood, non-timber forest products

Forestry is the science and craft of creating, managing, planting, using, conserving and repairing forests and woodlands for associated resources and benefits, including timber production, wildlife habitat, water quality, recreation, landscape and community protection, employment, and carbon sequestration. Forestry is practised in plantations and natural stands alike, and foresters work for public agencies, conservation organisations and the forest products industry.

History

Systematic forest management developed in the 18th and 19th centuries, notably in Germany — where the concept of sustained yield (Nachhaltigkeit) was articulated — and in the French and British imperial forest services. Scientific forestry spread worldwide through colonial forest departments and, in the United States, through figures such as Gifford Pinchot, the first chief of the US Forest Service.

Practice

The core applied discipline of forestry is silviculture: controlling the establishment, growth, composition and quality of forests to meet diverse needs. Foresters conduct inventories and mensuration, plan harvesting and regeneration, manage fire, pests and invasive species, and apply systems from clearcutting to continuous-cover forestry. Modern forestry increasingly integrates ecology, conservation biology and climate-change mitigation, and certification schemes such as FSC and PEFC audit management standards in many countries.

Forest resources

Forests cover about 31 per cent of the world's land area. Russia, Brazil, Canada, the United States and China hold the largest forest areas. Forests provide timber and non-timber products, regulate water cycles, store large quantities of carbon, and harbour most of the world's terrestrial biodiversity; deforestation and degradation remain major global environmental concerns.
