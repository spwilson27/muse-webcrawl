Modernist literature
Periodc. 1890s – 1940s (high modernism c. 1910–1930)
Key figuresJames Joyce, Virginia Woolf, T. S. Eliot, Marcel Proust, Franz Kafka, William Faulkner, Ezra Pound
TechniquesStream of consciousness, fragmentation, unreliable narration, mythic method

Modernist literature is a body of experimental writing produced mainly in the late 19th and early-to-mid 20th centuries, originating in Europe and North America, that departed self-consciously from traditional forms of literature and poetry. Characterised by a deliberate break with nineteenth-century realism and Victorian convention — epitomised by Ezra Pound's 1934 injunction to "Make it new" — modernist literature explored new forms of expression in response to industrialisation, urbanisation, scientific and philosophical upheaval, and the trauma of the First World War.

Origins and precursors

Modernism's roots reach back to nineteenth-century figures such as Baudelaire, Flaubert, Dostoevsky, Ibsen and the symbolist poets, and to thinkers who undermined inherited certainties: Darwin, Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud, whose theories of the unconscious transformed ideas of character and mind. Early modernists included Joseph Conrad, Henry James, Ford Madox Ford and the imagist poets.

Characteristics

Modernist works frequently employ stream of consciousness narration, interior monologue, fragmented or non-linear chronology, shifting and unreliable points of view, free indirect discourse, and dense allusiveness. T. S. Eliot praised Joyce's "mythical method" — the use of ancient myth (the Odyssey in Ulysses, the Grail legend in The Waste Land) as a scaffolding for modern disorder. Themes include alienation, the collapse of shared values, the instability of identity, and the difficulty of communication.

High modernism and major works

The year 1922 is often treated as modernism's annus mirabilis: it saw the publication of James Joyce's Ulysses and T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land, with Marcel Proust's À la recherche du temps perdu (1913–1927) already appearing in French. Virginia Woolf's Mrs Dalloway (1925) and To the Lighthouse (1927) reimagined the novel around consciousness and perception; Franz Kafka's The Trial (1925) and The Castle (1926) gave the movement its nightmarish bureaucratic dimension; William Faulkner, Robert Musil, Thomas Mann, Samuel Beckett and Djuna Barnes extended its reach. Important venues included little magazines such as Poetry, The Little Review and transition, and publishers like Sylvia Beach's Shakespeare and Company in Paris.

Late modernism and legacy

Writers of the 1930s–1950s — including Beckett, whose work bridges into the absurd — are sometimes classed as "late modernists". Modernism's legacy is pervasive: its techniques became part of the standard repertoire of literary fiction, and it provoked both the postmodernist experiments that followed and periodic realist reactions against it. Its literature is a central subject of academic "modernist studies", which has expanded to examine global modernisms beyond Europe and North America.
