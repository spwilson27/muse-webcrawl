Rhenish Massif

The Rhenish Massif (German: Rheinisches Schiefergebirge, literally “Rhenish Slate Mountains”) is an ancient upland region in western Germany extending into Belgium, Luxembourg and France. It consists mainly of low mountains and plateaus formed from Paleozoic sedimentary rocks folded during the Variscan orogeny.

Major parts
Eifel, Hunsrück, Westerwald, Bergisches Land, Sauerland, Siegerland and the Ardennes
Highest point
Großer Feldberg area is adjacent in the Taunus; within the massif, Großer Ahrberg and other Sauerland summits rise above 800 metres.

Divisions

The Rhine divides the massif into western and eastern sections. West of the river are the Eifel and Hunsrück; east are the Westerwald, Taunus fringe, Bergisches Land and Sauerland. The Moselle, Lahn, Sieg, Ruhr and other rivers cut deep valleys through the plateau. In Belgium and France, the geologically related uplands are generally called the Ardennes.

Geology

Devonian slates, greywackes, sandstones and limestones dominate. These marine sediments were compressed and folded about 300 million years ago as ancient continents collided. Later uplift and river erosion produced the present dissected plateau. Volcanism affected the Eifel much more recently, leaving maars, lava flows and mineral springs.

Landscape and economy

Forests cover many slopes, while plateau settlements depend on agriculture, forestry, tourism and commuting to Rhine cities. Historically, slate quarrying, iron mining, charcoal burning and small-scale industry were important. Steep river valleys support vineyards, notably along the Moselle, Ahr and Middle Rhine.

Distinction

The Rhenish Massif is a physiographic and geological region, not a dialect area. Rhine Franconian refers to West Central German dialects spoken in a related but different geography.
