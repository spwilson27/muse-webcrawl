Polish Round Table Agreement

The Polish Round Table Talks were negotiations held in Warsaw, Poland, from 6 February to 5 April 1989 between representatives of the communist government of the Polish People's Republic and leaders of the banned Solidarity trade union and other opposition groups. The resulting Round Table Agreement paved the way for partially free elections in June 1989 and the peaceful end of communist rule in Poland, setting a pattern for negotiated transitions across Eastern Europe.

Date6 February – 5 April 1989
LocationWarsaw, Poland
PartiesGovernment of the Polish People's Republic; Solidarity and opposition
OutcomeSemi-free elections of 4 June 1989; legalisation of Solidarity

Background

By 1988 the Polish government faced economic crisis and renewed strikes. Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms in the Soviet Union signalled that Moscow would not intervene militarily. Preliminary talks between Interior Minister Czesław Kiszczak and Lech Wałęsa at Magdalenka led to the full round table, in which some 452 participants worked in main tables and sub-committees; the Roman Catholic Church acted as observer and guarantor.

Terms

The agreement re-legalised Solidarity, created a new upper chamber, the Senate, to be freely elected, restored the office of President, and provided that only 35 percent of seats in the Sejm (the lower house) would be freely contested, with the rest reserved for the communist party and its allies. Opposition access to media was also conceded.

Consequences

In the elections of 4 and 18 June 1989, Solidarity won all 161 contested Sejm seats and 99 of 100 Senate seats. The result shattered the government's legitimacy; in August 1989, Tadeusz Mazowiecki became the first non-communist prime minister in the Soviet bloc, accelerating the Revolutions of 1989. The round table model of negotiated regime change was subsequently imitated in Hungary and elsewhere.
