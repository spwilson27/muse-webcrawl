Annie Besant (née Wood; 1 October 1847 – 20 September 1933) was a British socialist, theosophist, freemason, women's rights and home rule activist, educationist and campaigner for Indian self-rule. She was a leading speaker for the Fabian Society and the Social Democratic Federation, and was involved in the London matchgirls strike of 1888.

Early activism

Besant married the clergyman Frank Besant in 1867 but separated from him after losing her Christian faith, becoming a prominent atheist campaigner with the National Secular Society alongside Charles Bradlaugh. In 1877 the two were prosecuted for republishing a birth control pamphlet, The Fruits of Philosophy; the case brought the Malthusian cause wide publicity. She studied science at University College London and campaigned for trade unionism, supporting the Bryant and May matchgirls and the London dock strike of 1889.

Theosophy

In 1889 Besant reviewed Helena Blavatsky's The Secret Doctrine and converted to Theosophy. She became a leading member of the Theosophical Society and, after Blavatsky's death, its most energetic organiser. In 1907 she became international president of the Society, based at Adyar in Madras (Chennai). She promoted the young Jiddu Krishnamurti as the coming "World Teacher," a role he renounced in 1929.

India and home rule

Besant settled in India in 1893, founded the Central Hindu College at Banaras (later part of Banaras Hindu University), and in 1916 launched the Home Rule League demanding self-government for India within the British Empire. Interned by the British in 1917, she was released amid protests and elected president of the Indian National Congress for that year — the first woman to hold the post. She later opposed Gandhi's civil disobedience campaigns, favouring constitutional methods. She died at Adyar in 1933.
