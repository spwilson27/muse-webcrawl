Pigment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pigment is a substance that absorbs some wavelengths of light and reflects or transmits others, producing colour. Pigments differ from structural colours (such as the iridescence of butterfly wings) and from dyes in being generally insoluble particles suspended in a medium.

Biological pigments

Chlorophyll, green in plants and algae, drives photosynthesis in the chloroplast; carotenoids add yellows and oranges (and autumn colours); melanin browns and blacks animal skin, hair and eyes, protecting against ultraviolet radiation; haemoglobin makes blood red. Animals use pigments for camouflage, warning coloration and display (see adaptation), and some — cephalopods above all — change them in real time.

Pigments in art and industry

Humanity's palette began with ochres and charcoal in cave art and grew through mineral pigments (ultramarine from lapis lazuli, vermilion, lead white), organic lakes, and the synthetic colours of the chemical age (mauveine, 1856, the first synthetic dye; modern phthalocyanine blues and greens). Pigment chemistry decides a painting's survival: some pigments fade or blacken, complicating the conservation of works from Van Gogh — whose chrome yellows are darkening — to medieval manuscripts.
