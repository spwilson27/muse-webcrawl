Accentual verse

Accentual verse
BasisNumber of stressed syllables per line
ExamplesOld English alliterative verse; nursery rhymes; much children's verse
ContrastSyllabic verse; accentual-syllabic verse; quantitative verse

Accentual verse has a fixed number of stresses per line regardless of the total number of syllables within that line. It is the traditional metre of Germanic-language poetry, including Old English and Old Norse verse, and survives in English nursery rhymes, children's verse and much popular song, where the count of unstressed syllables varies freely between beats.

Forms

In Old English alliterative verse, each line typically has four stresses, divided by a caesura into two half-lines, with the stresses bound together by alliteration. Pure accentual verse is distinguished from accentual-syllabic verse (the dominant system of English literary poetry from Chaucer onward), which regulates both stresses and syllable counts, and from syllabic verse and classical quantitative metre.

Modern usage

Poets such as Gerard Manley Hopkins, with his "sprung rhythm", deliberately revived accentual principles. W. H. Auden and others wrote accentual poems in the 20th century, and the metre underlies most rap and spoken-word performance, where flow is organised by beats rather than syllable counts.
