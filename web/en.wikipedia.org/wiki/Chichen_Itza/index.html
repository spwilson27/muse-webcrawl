Chichen Itza (often spelled Chichén Itzá in Spanish) was a large pre-Columbian city built by the Maya people of the Terminal Classic period. The archaeological site is located in Tinum Municipality, Yucatán State, Mexico. Chichen Itza was a major focal point in the northern Maya lowlands from the Late Classic (c. AD 600–900) through the Terminal Classic (c. AD 800–900) and into the early portion of the Postclassic period (c. AD 900–1200); it was one of the largest Maya cities and a centre of political and economic power in the region.

The name means "at the mouth of the well of the Itza", referring to the Sacred Cenote, a natural sinkhole that served as a focus of pilgrimage and offerings. Today the site is Mexico's second-most-visited archaeological zone, receiving well over two million visitors a year.

Principal structures

El Castillo

The Temple of Kukulcán, known as El Castillo ("the castle"), is a step pyramid about 30 metres (98 ft) high — including the temple on top — that dominates the centre of the site. Its four stairways have 91 steps each; with the platform at the top the total is 365, the number of days in the solar year. During the spring and autumn equinoxes, the afternoon sun casts shadows that form a series of triangles along the northern balustrade, creating the illusion of a feathered serpent descending the pyramid — an effect that draws enormous crowds.

Great Ball Court

Chichen Itza preserves the largest ball court in Mesoamerica, 168 metres (551 ft) long and 70 metres (230 ft) wide, flanked by carved panels depicting ballplayers and, famously, scenes interpreted as the decapitation of a player. Stone rings set high on the walls served as goals. The court's acoustics are celebrated: a whisper at one end can be heard at the other.

Other buildings

Notable structures include the Warriors' Temple with its hall of thousand columns and reclining Chacmool figures; the Caracol, a round tower interpreted as an astronomical observatory; the Temple of the Jaguars; the Platform of the Skulls (Tzompantli); and the Sacred Cenote itself, from which dredging recovered gold, jade, pottery, and human remains offered to the rain god Chaac.

History and decline

Settlement began by the 6th century AD near two cenotes that guaranteed water in the dry northern lowlands. In the Terminal Classic the city flourished, building in a distinctive "Puuc" Maya style and, in its later phase, in styles showing strong central-Mexican (Toltec) influence — the origin of this influence (invasion, migration, or trade and emulation) remains debated. Chichen's power waned in the 12th–13th centuries as the rival city of Mayapan rose, and by the time of the Spanish conquest the site was a largely abandoned pilgrimage centre. American explorer John Lloyd Stephens and artist Frederick Catherwood publicised the ruins in the 1840s, and major excavation and restoration were undertaken by the Carnegie Institution and the Mexican government in the 20th century.
