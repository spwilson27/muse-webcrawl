Hell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In religion and folklore, hell is a location or state in the afterlife in which souls are subjected to punitive suffering, most often through torture, as a consequence of their sinful actions in life. Religions with a linear divine history often depict hells as eternal destinations, the biggest examples of which are Christianity and Islam, whereas religions with reincarnation usually depict a hell as an intermediary period between incarnations, as is the case in the dharmic religions.

Religions typically locate hell in another dimension or under Earth's surface. Other afterlife destinations include heaven, paradise, purgatory, limbo, and the underworld.

Overview

Other religions, which do not conceive of the afterlife as a place of punishment or reward, merely describe an abode of the dead, the grave, a neutral place that is located under the surface of Earth (for example, see Kur, Hades, and Sheol). Such places are sometimes equated with the English word hell, though a more correct translation would be "underworld" or "world of the dead".

The modern English word hell is derived from Old English hel, helle, first attested around 725 AD to refer to the nether world of the dead, and ultimately from Proto-Germanic *haljō, "the underworld".

By religion

Christianity

Christian theology traditionally describes hell as the state or place of eternal separation from God, the fate of those who die in mortal sin without repentance. Imagery includes fire, darkness, and "weeping and gnashing of teeth". Theologians debate its nature: eternal conscious torment, annihilationism (conditional immortality), and universalism (eventual reconciliation) are the main positions. See Hell in Christianity.

Islam

In Islam, hell is Jahannam, a place of fiery punishment with seven levels, guarded by angels under Mālik. It is described vividly in the Quran as a place of boiling water, scorching wind, and black smoke; most Muslims hold that punishment may be temporary for believers, eternal for unbelievers.

Judaism

Early Judaism conceived Sheol, a shadowy abode of all the dead. Later tradition developed Gehenna, a place of purification typically lasting no more than twelve months, and Olam Ha-Ba (the world to come).

Dharmic religions

Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism describe multiple hells (Naraka, Diyu-like realms) where beings are punished until their negative karma is exhausted, after which they are reborn. These hells, though dreadful, are impermanent.

Other traditions

Ancient Egypt judged the dead in the Duat; Greek religion placed the wicked in Tartarus; Norse mythology had Hel, a cold realm of the dead ruled by the goddess of the same name. Chinese folk religion blends Buddhist and Taoist ideas in Diyu, the underground maze of courts and punishments.
