Ghost lineage

A ghost lineage is a phylogenetic lineage that is inferred to exist but has no fossil record. The term was coined by the palaeontologist Mark Norell in 1992. Ghost lineages arise when a phylogenetic tree (cladogram) implies that a group must have existed during a certain interval — because its sister group is known from fossils of that age — yet no fossils of the lineage itself have been found for that period. The span of time for which a lineage is inferred but undocumented is sometimes called a ghost range.

A cladogram showing a ghost lineage as a dashed extension.

Concept

If two sister taxa first appear in the fossil record at different times, the earlier appearance pushes the inferred divergence of the pair back to at least that age; the younger-appearing lineage therefore has an implied existence — a ghost lineage — for the interval between the divergence and its first fossil. Ghost lineages thus quantify gaps in the fossil record imposed by phylogenetic hypotheses and can be used to measure how well the fossil record matches a tree (for example with the stratigraphic consistency index).

Examples

Classic examples include the coelacanths, whose fossil record appeared to end in the Cretaceous, yet living specimens were discovered in 1938 — an extreme case of a lineage surviving undocumented (a Lazarus taxon). Long ghost lineages have been inferred for many groups, including early birds, placental mammal orders in the Cretaceous, and numerous marine invertebrate groups. Some ghost lineages are later shortened or eliminated by new fossil discoveries; others persist and may indicate genuine rarity, small body size, or habitats unfavourable to fossilisation.

Interpretation

Ghost lineages do not by themselves show that a phylogeny is wrong or that fossils are missing through sampling failure; they are hypotheses about unobserved history implied by the tree. However, very long ghost lineages concentrated in particular intervals can signal problems — for example, that divergence dates estimated by molecular clocks conflict with the fossil record, or that a group's early members lived in environments such as rainforests where fossilisation is rare.
