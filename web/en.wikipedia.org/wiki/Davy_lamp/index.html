Davy lamp

InventorSir Humphry Davy
Year1815
PurposeSafety lamp for use in mines with firedamp

The Davy lamp is a safety lamp used in flammable atmospheres, invented in 1815 by Sir Humphry Davy. It consists of a wick lamp with the flame enclosed inside a mesh screen; the mesh acts as a flame arrestor, preventing the flame from igniting flammable gases such as methane (firedamp) in coal mines.

Principle

The fine wire gauze surrounding the flame conducts heat away so effectively that the temperature of the gas outside the mesh remains below its ignition point. Changes in the appearance of the flame — a blue "cap" or elongation — also warned miners of the presence of methane, while a flame that dimmed or went out signalled a dangerous lack of oxygen.

History and impact

Davy announced his invention to the Royal Society in November 1815, at about the same time George Stephenson independently developed the similar "Geordie lamp". The Davy lamp allowed mines to be worked deeper and in gassier seams; paradoxically, some historians argue it increased accidents by encouraging work in more dangerous conditions.

Legacy

Modified forms of the lamp remained in use in British mines into the twentieth century, and the principle of the flame arrestor is still used in industrial safety equipment.
