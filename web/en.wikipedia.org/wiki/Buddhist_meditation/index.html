Buddhist meditation is the practice of meditation in association with the teachings of Buddhism. The Buddhist term for meditation and mental cultivation generally is bhāvanā. The closest words for "meditation" in the classical languages are dhyāna/jhāna (absorbed concentration). Core techniques, preserved since the time of the Buddha, are traditionally divided into samatha (calming the mind, developing concentration through objects such as mindfulness of breathing, ānāpānasati) and vipassanā (insight into the true nature of reality — impermanence, suffering and no-self), which together form the basis of the way to awakening.

Range of practices

Around these poles the Buddhist traditions developed a vast repertoire: recollection practices (anussati) such as Buddhānusmṛti (recollection of the Buddha); the four divine abidings (brahmavihāra) — loving-kindness (mettā), compassion, sympathetic joy and equanimity; contemplation of the body's foulness and of death; mantra recitation and visualisation in Vajrayana; the kōan practice and "just sitting" (shikantaza) of Zen; and walking meditation. Meditation is embedded in the Noble Eightfold Path as right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration, and is supported by ethical conduct (sīla).

Modern developments

In the 19th and 20th centuries, lay meditation movements in Burma, Thailand and Sri Lanka spread vipassanā worldwide, and Buddhist meditation techniques — especially mindfulness (sati) — were adapted into secular clinical programmes, generating extensive scientific research on their psychological effects.
