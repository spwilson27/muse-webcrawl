April Fools' Day

April Fools' Day or All Fools' Day is an annual custom on 1 April consisting of practical jokes and hoaxes. Jokesters often expose their actions by shouting "April Fool!" at the recipient. Mass media have participated since the 19th century, publishing false stories that are revealed the next day or are signalled by absurdity.

Origins

The origins of the custom are uncertain. Proposed antecedents include the Roman festival of Hilaria, the medieval Feast of Fools, and the shift of New Year celebrations from late March to 1 January under the Gregorian calendar, which allegedly left some people celebrating the old date and being mocked as "April fools" — an explanation often repeated but not well documented. References to 1 April fooling appear in Chaucer scholarship disputes, in a 1561 poem by the Flemish writer Eduard de Dene, and in John Aubrey's writings in 1686. In Britain, an April fool joke traditionally stops at midday; in many other countries it lasts all day.

Famous hoaxes

Celebrated media hoaxes include the BBC's 1957 Panorama report on Swiss spaghetti harvesting, the 1996 Taco Bell announcement that it had bought the Liberty Bell, and the 1962 Swedish television claim that stockings drawn over a set could convert black-and-white broadcasts to colour. Newspapers, broadcasters, and technology companies continue the tradition with elaborate fabricated announcements each year.

Similar customs

Comparable days of pranking exist worldwide, including the Spanish Día de los Santos Inocentes (28 December) and Iran's Sizdah Bedar, sometimes described as a much older analogue. See also hoax and Feast of Fools.
