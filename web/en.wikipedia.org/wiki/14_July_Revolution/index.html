14 July Revolution

The 14 July Revolution, also known as the 1958 Iraqi coup d'état, took place on 14 July 1958 in Iraq. It resulted in the overthrow of the Hashemite Kingdom of Iraq and the establishment of the Iraqi Republic. King Faisal II, Crown Prince 'Abd al-Ilah and Prime Minister Nuri al-Said were killed, ending the monarchy that had ruled Iraq since 1921.

Background

Discontent with the monarchy had grown through the 1950s. The royal government was widely seen as subordinate to Britain, and Iraq's membership in the Baghdad Pact aligned it with the West amid rising Arab nationalism inspired by Gamal Abdel Nasser's Egypt. Corruption, inequality and the unpopularity of the veteran premier Nuri al-Said fuelled opposition among army officers, who formed a clandestine Free Officers movement modelled on its Egyptian namesake.

The coup

In the early hours of 14 July 1958, army units under Brigadier Abd al-Karim Qasim and Colonel Abdul Salam Arif seized Baghdad, capturing the radio station and the royal palace. King Faisal II and members of the royal family were shot at the Rihab Palace. Nuri al-Said attempted to flee disguised as a woman but was captured and killed the following day. A Republic was proclaimed, with Qasim as Prime Minister.

Aftermath

The revolution ended Iraq's Hashemite monarchy and the short-lived Arab Federation with Jordan. Iraq withdrew from the Baghdad Pact, recognised the People's Republic of China and pursued closer ties with the Soviet Union. Qasim's rule lasted until his overthrow and execution in the Ramadan Revolution of February 1963. The monarchy's fall remains one of the most consequential events in modern Iraqi history.
