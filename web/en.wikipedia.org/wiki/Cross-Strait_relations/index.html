Cross-Strait relations (sometimes called Mainland–Taiwan relations) are the relations between China (the People's Republic of China, PRC) and Taiwan (the Republic of China, ROC) across the Taiwan Strait. Since 1949, when the ROC government retreated to Taiwan at the end of the Chinese Civil War, both governments have claimed to be the legitimate government of all China, and the political status of Taiwan remains unresolved.

History

During the 1950s the two sides fought artillery duels over the offshore islands of Kinmen and Matsu in the Taiwan Strait Crises of 1954–55 and 1958. Martial law in Taiwan lasted until 1987, after which contacts expanded: indirect trade, family visits, and semi-official talks through Taiwan's Straits Exchange Foundation and the mainland's ARATS began in the early 1990s. The 1992 Consensus — an understanding that both sides belong to "one China" while differing on its meaning — underpinned later agreements.

The Third Taiwan Strait Crisis (1995–96), triggered by President Lee Teng-hui's visit to the United States, saw PLA missile exercises and US carrier deployments. Direct transport, postal, and trade links (the "three links") opened in 2008 under President Ma Ying-jeou, and in November 2015 Ma met Xi Jinping in Singapore — the first meeting between the leaders of the two sides since 1949. The Democratic Progressive Party governments of Tsai Ing-wen (from 2016) and Lai Ching-te (from 2024) declined to endorse the 1992 Consensus, and official dialogue was suspended, while military activity around Taiwan increased.

Issues

  • Sovereignty and status: the PRC's One-China principle versus Taiwan's evolving positions on independence and the status quo.
  • Trade and investment: deep economic integration, including the 2010 Economic Cooperation Framework Agreement (ECFA).
  • Military balance: PLA modernisation, US arms sales to Taiwan under the Taiwan Relations Act, and deterrence.
  • International space: Taiwan's participation in international organisations remains contested.
