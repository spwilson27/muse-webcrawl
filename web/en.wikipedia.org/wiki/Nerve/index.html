Nerve

A nerve is an enclosed, cable-like bundle of axons (nerve fibres) in the peripheral nervous system. Nerves carry electrical impulses — action potentials — between the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and the rest of the body: sensory (afferent) fibres bring information from receptors to the CNS, while motor (efferent) fibres carry commands to muscles and glands. Most nerves are mixed, containing both types of fibre as well as autonomic fibres.

Structure

Each axon is wrapped by Schwann cells (myelinated in larger fibres) and by a connective tissue layer, the endoneurium. Axons are grouped into bundles called fascicles, each wrapped in perineurium, and the whole nerve is enclosed in a tough outer epineurium that also carries the nerve's blood vessels (vasa nervorum). Myelination allows fast conduction by saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier; fibre classes (A, B, C) differ in diameter, myelination, and speed.

Classification

Nerves are categorised by their origin: twelve pairs of cranial nerves emerge from the brain and brainstem, and 31 pairs of spinal nerves emerge from the spinal cord, named for their vertebral level (cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral, coccygeal). Spinal nerves typically join into plexuses — cervical, brachial, lumbar, and sacral — from which named peripheral nerves such as the median, ulnar, radial, femoral, and sciatic nerves arise. The sciatic nerve is the largest in the human body.

Function and clinical aspects

Nerves mediate sensation, movement, and autonomic control of organs. Damage — from trauma, compression (e.g. carpal tunnel syndrome), diabetes, or infection — causes pain, weakness, numbness, or paralysis in the nerve's distribution. Peripheral axons can regenerate slowly (roughly 1 mm per day) if the connective tissue scaffolding survives; nerve conduction studies and electromyography are used to diagnose nerve disorders.
