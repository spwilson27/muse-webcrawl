Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC – c. AD 50), also called Philo Judaeus, was a Hellenistic Jewish philosopher who lived in Alexandria in Roman Egypt. He sought to harmonise Jewish scripture with Greek philosophy, especially Platonism and Stoicism, and is the most important representative of Hellenistic Judaism.

Life and writings

Philo belonged to one of the wealthiest and most prominent Jewish families of Alexandria; his brother Alexander was the alabarch (customs chief) and one of the richest men of the city. In AD 38–40, after anti-Jewish riots in Alexandria, Philo led an embassy to the emperor Caligula in Rome to plead the community's cause, an experience he recounts in the Legatio ad Gaium. His surviving works, written in Greek, include extensive commentaries on the Pentateuch, treatises on the virtues and on figures such as Abraham and Moses, and apologetic works.

Thought

Philo interpreted the Torah allegorically, reading its narratives as symbols of the soul's journey toward God, while also upholding the literal observance of Jewish law. Central to his system is the Logos, the divine Word or Reason that mediates between the utterly transcendent God and the material world. His synthesis profoundly influenced the Church Fathers, including Clement and Origen, and through them early Christian theology, although he had little direct influence on later rabbinic Judaism.
