Gale–Shapley algorithm


The Gale–Shapley algorithm finds a stable matching in the stable marriage problem.

In mathematics, economics, and computer science, the Gale–Shapley algorithm (also known as the deferred acceptance algorithm) is an algorithm for finding a solution to the stable marriage problem. It was proposed in 1962 by David Gale and Lloyd Shapley, who proved that for any equal number of participants of two types, the algorithm always terminates with a stable matching — one with no two people who would both prefer each other to their assigned partners.

The algorithm

In the algorithm, one side (conventionally the men) proposes to the other side in rounds. Each free man proposes to the highest-ranked woman on his list who has not yet rejected him. Each woman who receives proposals holds the best one "on a string" (tentatively engaged) and rejects the rest; a woman already tentatively engaged may trade up if a new proposal is better, freeing her previous partner. The process ends when every man is tentatively engaged, at which point the engagements become final.

while some man m is free:
  w := highest-ranked woman on m's list not yet proposed to
  m proposes to w
  if w is free: w tentatively accepts m
  elif w prefers m to her current partner m':
      w tentatively accepts m; m' becomes free
  else: w rejects m

Properties

The algorithm runs in O(n²) time. The resulting matching is stable and is optimal for the proposing side: every proposer receives the best partner achievable in any stable matching, while the other side receives its worst stable outcome. The algorithm is also strategy-proof for the proposing side. Shapley shared the 2012 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this work on stable allocations and market design.

Applications

Deferred acceptance underlies real matching markets, including the National Resident Matching Program for medical residents, school-choice systems such as those in New York City and Boston, and kidney exchange programs.
