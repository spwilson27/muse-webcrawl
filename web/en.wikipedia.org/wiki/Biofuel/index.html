Biofuel

Biofuel is a fuel produced from biomass — recently living plant or animal material — rather than from fossilised organic matter. Common biofuels include ethanol, made by fermenting sugars from corn, sugarcane, or wheat, and biodiesel, made from vegetable oils and animal fats. Biofuels are used mainly in transport, often blended with petrol or diesel, and are promoted as a renewable way to cut greenhouse gas emissions.

Generations

First-generation biofuels are made from food crops. Second-generation fuels use non-food biomass such as straw, wood chips, and agricultural residues, typically requiring more advanced processing to break down cellulose. Third-generation fuels derive from algae, which can yield high amounts of oil per hectare. Advanced or drop-in fuels, such as hydrotreated vegetable oil (HVO), are chemically similar to fossil fuels and compatible with existing engines and pipelines.

Production and use

The largest producers include the United States (corn ethanol), Brazil (sugarcane ethanol), and the European Union and Indonesia (biodiesel). Mandates such as the US Renewable Fuel Standard and the EU Renewable Energy Directive require set proportions of renewable fuel in transport fuel supplies. Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) is a growing segment aimed at a sector that is hard to electrify.

Criticism

Biofuels are contested. Indirect land-use change — food production displaced onto newly cleared land — can release large amounts of carbon, and fertiliser use adds nitrous oxide emissions. Critics also cite food-price effects and biodiversity loss from monoculture plantations. Supporters argue that waste-based and advanced biofuels avoid most of these problems and provide energy security and rural income.
