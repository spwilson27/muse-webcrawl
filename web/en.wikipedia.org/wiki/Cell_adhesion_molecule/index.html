Cell adhesion molecule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


[Image: diagram of adhesion molecules linking two adjacent cells]
TypeMembrane protein class
Major familiesIntegrins, cadherins, selectins, immunoglobulin superfamily (IgSF)
FunctionCell–cell and cell–matrix adhesion

Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) are proteins located on the cell surface that mediate binding between cells, or between cells and the extracellular matrix. They are essential to the organisation of tissues, embryonic development, wound healing, immune responses and the maintenance of tissue architecture, and they also transmit signals across the membrane that influence cell survival, proliferation and migration.

Major families

Integrins are heterodimeric receptors that mainly bind extracellular matrix components such as fibronectin and collagen, anchoring cells at focal adhesions and hemidesmosomes. Cadherins mediate calcium-dependent homophilic cell–cell adhesion and are the core of adherens junctions; E-cadherin is characteristic of epithelial tissues. Selectins bind carbohydrate ligands and initiate the rolling of leukocytes on endothelium during inflammation. Members of the immunoglobulin superfamily, such as NCAM, ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, mediate calcium-independent adhesion in the nervous and immune systems.

Clinical significance

Loss of E-cadherin function is associated with tumour invasion and metastasis. Defects in the integrin β2 subunit cause leukocyte adhesion deficiency, a severe immunodeficiency. CAMs on endothelium are targets of inflammatory signalling and of several therapeutic antibodies, and integrin antagonists have been developed as anti-thrombotic and anti-inflammatory drugs.
