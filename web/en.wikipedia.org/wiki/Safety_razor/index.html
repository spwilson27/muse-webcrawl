A safety razor is a shaving implement with a protective device positioned between the edge of the razor blade and the skin. The initial purpose of these protective devices was to make shaving safer and easier for men to perform themselves at home, without the training required to use a straight razor or the expense of visiting a barber. Safety razors progressed from single-edge designs to the double-edge razor, and later to cartridge razors with multiple pivoting blades.

History

The first device described as a safety razor was patented in 1880 by the Kampfe brothers of New York, whose Star razor mounted a small blade behind a protective comb-like guard. William Samuel Henson had earlier, in 1847, patented a comb tooth guard for a hoe-shaped razor handle. The decisive innovation came from King Camp Gillette, who conceived a razor with a thin, disposable, double-edged blade. Gillette filed his patent in 1901, founded the American Safety Razor Company (soon the Gillette Safety Razor Company), and began selling razors in 1903. The disposable-blade system, in which the handle was sold cheaply and profits came from blade sales, became a famous example of the "razors and blades" business model.

During the First World War, Gillette supplied razors to the United States armed forces, putting millions of safety razors into the hands of servicemen and cementing their dominance over the straight razor. In the following decades the design was refined with twist-to-open mechanisms, slant-bar heads, and adjustable razors whose blade exposure could be varied. From the 1960s and 1970s, cartridge systems (such as the Wilkinson Sword Bonded and Gillette Trac II) and fully disposable razors progressively displaced the classic double-edge razor in most markets, though double-edge shaving has experienced a revival among enthusiasts and in many countries worldwide it remains common.

Design

A double-edge safety razor consists of a handle and a head that sandwiches a thin flexible blade between a top cap and a base plate. The safety bar or open comb beneath the blade edge limits the blade's contact with the skin. Common configurations include:

  • Three-piece razors, in which cap, base plate, and handle unscrew for blade changes
  • Twist-to-open ("butterfly") razors, whose head opens by turning the handle
  • Adjustable razors, with a dial that changes blade gap and exposure
  • Closed comb (safety bar) and open comb guards, the latter generally more aggressive
  • Slant-bar razors, whose head twists the blade to slice at an angle

Single-edge safety razors, such as the GEM and Schick Injector designs, use thicker rigid blades. Cartridge razors carry proprietary multi-blade heads and are generally considered safety razors in ordinary usage.
