Continental Divide of the Americas

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A hiker standing on a rocky ridge of the Continental Divide in Colorado, watersheds falling away on both sides]
A hiker standing on a rocky ridge of the Continental Divide in Colorado, watersheds falling away on both sides

The Continental Divide of the Americas (the Great Divide) is the principal hydrological divide of the Americas, separating watersheds that drain into the Pacific Ocean from those that drain into the Atlantic and Arctic Oceans. It follows the crests of the Andes in South America and the Rocky Mountains through Central and North America to Alaska. The Continental Divide Trail (some 5,000 km from Mexico to Canada) is one of the world's great long-distance footpaths.

Course

From Cape Horn the divide climbs the Andes — skirting the endorheic Altiplano, whose waters reach no ocean — crosses Central America at low elevations, then in Mexico follows the Sierra Madre Occidental before meeting the Rockies at New Mexico. Through the United States it threads Colorado's high parks (crossing the road over Independence Pass), Wyoming's Wind River Range and the Yellowstone plateau (where one lake, Isa, drains to both oceans by repute), Montana's Bob Marshall Wilderness, and into Canada along the Alberta–British Columbia border, ending in the far northwest.

Triple divides and exceptions

The Divide meets other great divides at triple points: at Triple Divide Peak in Montana (Pacific/Atlantic/Arctic) and at the Snow Dome in Canada (Pacific/Arctic/Atlantic via Hudson Bay). Closed basins — the Great Divide Basin of Wyoming, Mexico's Bolsón de Mapimí, the Altiplano — break its logic, reminders that continental drainage is messier than the clean line on schoolroom maps.
