John Quincy Adams (July 11, 1767 – February 23, 1848) was an American statesman, politician, diplomat, lawyer, and diarist who served as the sixth president of the United States from 1825 to 1829. The eldest son of President John Adams and First Lady Abigail Adams, he had an extraordinary career spanning six decades, serving as a diplomat, senator, and Secretary of State before his presidency and for seventeen years in the House of Representatives afterward.

Early life and diplomatic career

Adams spent much of his youth in Europe accompanying his father on diplomatic missions, acquiring fluency in French and Dutch and a lifelong habit of meticulous diary-keeping. He graduated from Harvard in 1787 and practiced law in Boston. Appointed minister to the Netherlands by Washington in 1794, he later served as minister to Prussia. As Secretary of State under James Monroe, Adams was the principal architect of the Monroe Doctrine (1823), negotiated the Adams–Onís Treaty (1819) acquiring Florida and defining the western boundary with Spanish America, and settled outstanding disputes with Britain.

Presidency

In the election of 1824, no candidate won an Electoral College majority and the House of Representatives chose Adams over Andrew Jackson — an outcome Jackson's supporters denounced as a "corrupt bargain" after Adams named Henry Clay Secretary of State. Adams proposed an ambitious program of internal improvements, a national university, and scientific expeditions, but Congress blocked most of it. He lost the bitter election of 1828 to Jackson.

Congressional career and abolitionism

Uniquely among former presidents (until William Howard Taft became Chief Justice), Adams returned to public office, serving in the House of Representatives from 1831 until his death. There he became a leading opponent of slavery: he fought the "gag rule" that tabled anti-slavery petitions until its repeal in 1844, and in 1841 he successfully argued before the Supreme Court for the freedom of the African captives in the Amistad case. He also championed science, helping establish the Smithsonian Institution.

Death and legacy

Adams collapsed on the floor of the House on February 21, 1848, and died two days later in the Speaker's Room of the Capitol. His marathon diary, kept from age twelve, is one of the great records of early American public life. Historians frequently rank him among the greatest American diplomats and secretaries of state.
