Pastoral power

Pastoral power is a concept developed by the French philosopher Michel Foucault to describe a form of power modelled on the relationship between a shepherd and his flock. Unlike sovereign power, which commands and punishes, pastoral power is exercised through care and guidance: the shepherd watches over each member of the flock individually and the flock as a whole, and is prepared to sacrifice himself for its welfare.

Development in Foucault's work

Foucault developed the idea in his 1977–78 lectures at the Collège de France, Security, Territory, Population, and in essays such as "Omnes et Singulatim" ("each and all"). He traced the shepherd–flock metaphor from ancient Near Eastern and early Christian thought, arguing that the Christian pastorate transformed it into a detailed technology of government: the pastor must know each soul, guide its conscience, and elicit the truth from it through confession and self-examination.

Characteristics

Foucault described pastoral power as salvation-oriented, oblative (devoted to those it serves), individualizing, and co-extensive with the whole life of the subject. It produces knowledge of individual conscience and operates through obedience and truth-telling rather than through force alone.

Influence

The concept became central to the study of governmentality: Foucault argued that the modern state combines this older pastoral, individualizing power with newer forms of population management, producing a state that governs "each and all" simultaneously. The idea has been applied in sociology, political science, and studies of welfare, medicine, and education.
