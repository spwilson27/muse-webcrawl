The Dawes Rolls, officially the Final Rolls of Citizens and Freedmen of the Five Civilized Tribes in Indian Territory, are the lists of individuals approved for tribal enrolment and land allotment by the Dawes Commission between 1898 and 1907. They constitute the definitive record of citizenship in the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee (Creek) and Seminole nations at the time their communal lands were allotted in severalty.

Compilation

To build the Rolls, commission field parties travelled Indian Territory taking testimony and comparing applicants against earlier tribal censuses and rolls, such as the Cherokee rolls of 1880 and 1896 and the Choctaw and Chickasaw rolls. Each approved person was recorded on a census card — the "enrolment card" or "Dawes card" — noting name, age, sex, degree of Indian blood as determined by the commission, family relationships, and the category of enrolment: citizen by blood, citizen by marriage, newborn or minor citizen, or freedman (descendants of people enslaved by the tribes, admitted to citizenship under the treaties of 1866).

The rolls closed on 4 March 1907, months before Oklahoma statehood; a small number of names were added by acts of Congress up to 1914. Altogether, roughly 101,000 people were enrolled, of whom about 36,000 were Cherokee — reflecting the size of that nation and its extensive adoption of freedmen and intermarried whites.

Use today

The five modern successor nations use the Dawes Rolls as their base rolls. A person seeking citizenship in, for example, the Cherokee Nation or the Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma must document lineal descent from someone listed on the relevant roll; the US Bureau of Indian Affairs likewise uses the Rolls in issuing Certificates of Degree of Indian Blood. The cards and rolls, held by the National Archives (Record Group 75, archival series T529 and related microfilm), are among the most heavily used genealogical records in the United States.

Controversies

The Rolls' authority is also their burden. Blood degrees recorded by commissioners were often guessed, misrecorded or deliberately reduced, yet they now govern descendants' legal status. Enrolment of freedmen on separate rolls without recorded blood quantum underlies continuing disputes over the citizenship rights of Freedmen descendants, litigated and legislated into the 21st century — including the Cherokee Nation's 2021 court ruling removing "by blood" language and affirming Freedmen citizenship. Conversely, many Native people whose ancestors refused enrolment, were missed, or belonged to tribes outside the commission's remit have no path to recognition through the Rolls at all.
