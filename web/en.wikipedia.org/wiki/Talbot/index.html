Talbot

IndustryAutomotive
Founded1903 (as Clément-Talbot, London)

SuccessorsRootes Group; Chrysler Europe; PSA Peugeot Citroën

Talbot was a British-French automobile marque with a complicated dual history. The name derives from Charles Chetwynd-Talbot, 20th Earl of Shrewsbury, who in 1903 backed the importation of French Clément cars into Britain under the name Clément-Talbot, building a factory in London. After the First World War the British company combined with Darracq and Sunbeam to form STD Motors, while the French Darracq operations in Suresnes also adopted the Talbot name for their products, so that for a time British and French Talbots were related but distinct.

French Talbot and Talbot-Lago

In the 1930s the French company passed to the Venetian-born engineer Anthony Lago, becoming Talbot-Lago. Its elegant grand tourers — above all the T150C with "teardrop" (goutte d'eau) coachwork by Figoni et Falaschi — are among the most celebrated French cars of the pre-war era, and Talbot-Lago also competed successfully in Grand Prix racing and won the 24 Hours of Le Mans in 1950. The company was bought by Simca in 1959 and the name disappeared.

Revival under Chrysler and Peugeot

The British Talbot had passed through the Rootes Group to Chrysler in the 1960s. In 1978 Chrysler sold its European operations to PSA Peugeot Citroën, which revived the Talbot name in 1979 for the former Chrysler Europe range — models such as the Talbot Horizon, Alpine, Solara, Tagora and the little Samba. In Britain the Talbot Express van survived longest, as a sibling of the Fiat Ducato family. Falling sales led PSA to phase out the marque on passenger cars by 1986, and the name ended entirely in 1994.
