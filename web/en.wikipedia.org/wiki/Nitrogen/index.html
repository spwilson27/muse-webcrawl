Nitrogen is the chemical element with the symbol N and atomic number 7. It is a colourless, odourless diatomic gas (N₂) that makes up about 78% of Earth's atmosphere by volume, and it is an essential constituent of amino acids, proteins and nucleic acids in all living organisms.

Although nitrogen gas is largely inert, nitrogen compounds are vital fertilisers and explosives. Industrially, nitrogen is fixed from the air by the Haber process to make ammonia, and liquid nitrogen (boiling at −196 °C) is a common cryogenic coolant.

History

Nitrogen was recognised as a separate gas in the 1770s by Daniel Rutherford, Carl Wilhelm Scheele and others; Lavoisier called it azote ('no life') because animals cannot survive in it. The name nitrogen, from Greek roots meaning 'nitre-former', was proposed by Jean-Antoine Chaptal in 1790.

Properties and compounds

Nitrogen atoms form exceptionally strong triple bonds in N₂, which is why the gas is so unreactive. Its compounds span oxidation states from −3 (ammonia) to +5 (nitrates). Important compounds include ammonia, nitric acid, nitrogen oxides and nitrogen dioxide, a polluting brown gas. The nitrogen cycle moves the element between air, soil, water and living things.
