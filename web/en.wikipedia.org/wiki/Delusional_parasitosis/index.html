Delusional parasitosis

Delusional parasitosis, also known as delusional infestation or Ekbom syndrome, is a mental disorder in which individuals have a persistent belief that they are infested with living or non-living pathogens, such as parasites, insects, or bugs, when in reality no such infestation exists.

Signs and symptoms

Sufferers commonly report sensations of crawling, biting or stinging on or under the skin (formication). Many present to dermatologists with specimens — the classic "matchbox sign" — consisting of skin flakes, fibres or debris offered as evidence of the organisms. Self-treatment with pesticides, excessive washing and picking can cause significant skin damage and chemical injury.

Classification

Primary delusional parasitosis is classified as a somatic type of delusional disorder. Secondary forms occur in schizophrenia, dementia, and medical conditions, or as a side effect of substances such as cocaine and amphetamines. The closely related term "Morgellons" is used by some patients who believe fibres emerge from their skin; mainstream medicine regards Morgellons as a presentation of delusional infestation.

Treatment

Patients typically reject psychiatric referral, so care is often delivered through dermatology. Antipsychotic medication, historically pimozide and now more commonly risperidone or olanzapine, can reduce or resolve the belief; supportive, non-confrontational engagement is essential.
