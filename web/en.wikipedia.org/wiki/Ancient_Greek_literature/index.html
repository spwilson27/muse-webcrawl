Ancient Greek literature is literature written in the Ancient Greek language from the earliest texts, around the 8th century BC, through to the Byzantine period. It is one of the foundational literatures of Western civilisation: the epic, lyric poetry, drama, history, and the philosophical dialogue all took their classic forms in Greek, and Greek works were transmitted through Roman, Byzantine, and Arabic scholarship to shape European literature.

Epic and early poetry

The tradition begins with Homer, to whom the Iliad and Odyssey are attributed, products of a long oral-formulaic tradition written down in the 8th century BC. Hesiod's Theogony systematised the Greek myths of the gods, and his Works and Days combined farming instruction with moralising and the myth of the Ages of Man. Short Homeric Hymns and the lost poems of the Epic Cycle filled out the mythological inheritance.

Lyric poetry

The Archaic period (7th–6th centuries BC) produced lyric poetry sung to the lyre: Sappho and Alcaeus on Lesbos, the elegies of Solon and Tyrtaeus, Archilochus's iambic verse, and the victory odes of Pindar in the 5th century BC, celebrating athletes at the Olympic and Pythian games with dense mythological allusion.

Drama

In 5th-century Athens, tragedy and comedy took shape at the City Dionysia festival. Of the tragedians, Aeschylus (Oresteia), Sophocles (Oedipus Rex, Antigone), and Euripides (Medea, The Bacchae) survive in complete plays; Aristophanes is the sole complete representative of Old Comedy, and Menander of the later New Comedy. Aristotle's Poetics, analysing tragedy, remains a foundational work of literary criticism.

History, philosophy, and oratory

Herodotus, the "father of history", narrated the Persian Wars; Thucydides wrote a rigorously analytical history of the Peloponnesian War; Xenophon continued it and left memoirs of Socrates. Socrates' pupil Plato cast philosophy as dialogue, and his student Aristotle ranged over logic, ethics, politics, rhetoric, and natural science. Athenian courts and assemblies produced the great orators, above all Demosthenes, whose speeches against Philip of Macedon defined political eloquence.

Hellenistic and later literature

After Alexander, the centre of scholarship shifted to Alexandria and its Library. Callimachus, Theocritus (inventor of pastoral), and Apollonius of Rhodes (the epic Argonautica) exemplify the learned, allusive style of the age. Later centuries produced Polybius and Plutarch (whose Parallel Lives shaped Renaissance biography), the satires of Lucian, the Greek novel (Longus's Daphnis and Chloe), and the meditations of Marcus Aurelius, before Greek letters continued within the Byzantine tradition.
