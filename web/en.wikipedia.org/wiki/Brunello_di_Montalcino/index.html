Brunello di Montalcino is a red Italian wine produced in the vineyards surrounding the town of Montalcino, in the province of Siena, Tuscany. It is made exclusively from Sangiovese grapes — locally a clone of the variety is called "Brunello", meaning "little dark one" — and is among Italy's best-known and most expensive wines.

History

Wine had been made around Montalcino for centuries, but the modern Brunello was created in the late 19th century by Ferruccio Biondi-Santi of the Biondi-Santi estate, who isolated a distinct Sangiovese clone and vinified it alone with long ageing, producing the celebrated 1888 vintage. In 1966 the wine received DOC status, and in 1980 it was among the very first Italian wines elevated to DOCG, the country's highest classification.

Production rules

Regulations require 100% Sangiovese grown within the Montalcino commune, yields limited to 8 tonnes per hectare, and lengthy ageing: Brunello may not be sold until 1 January of the fifth year after the harvest, having spent at least two years in oak and four months in bottle. A Riserva must age a year longer. The resulting wines are full-bodied, tannic and long-lived, with flavours of cherry, dried herbs, leather and tobacco. The lighter, earlier-drinking Rosso di Montalcino is made in the same zone.
