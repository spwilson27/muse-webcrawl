Courtyard house

[Image: the courtyard of a traditional Chinese siheyuan with rooms on four sides]
Building typeDwelling organised around an open courtyard
ExamplesRoman domus, Chinese siheyuan, Islamic riad, Spanish hacienda
BenefitsLight, ventilation, privacy, security, microclimate

A courtyard house is a type of house in which the main living areas are arranged around a central open-air or partially covered courtyard. One of the oldest and most widespread forms of domestic architecture, it appears independently in many cultures and climates, from ancient Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley to the present day.

History and variants

In the Roman world the domus centred on an atrium and later a peristyle garden. The traditional Chinese siheyuan arranges halls on four sides of a rectangular court, oriented to feng shui and Confucian family hierarchy; larger compounds add successive courtyards. Islamic architecture developed inward-looking courtyard houses — the Moroccan riad with its sunken garden, or the courtyard homes of Damascus and Baghdad — providing privacy for family life, shade, and cooling through fountains and air circulation. In Spain and Latin America the patio-centred hacienda and colonial house follow the same logic, as do the courtyard dwellings of India, such as the haveli and the Kerala nalukettu.

Climatic and social function

The courtyard admits light and air into deep urban plots while shielding occupants from street noise, dust and heat; walls shade the court, and evaporative cooling from water features moderates the internal climate. Socially it offers a private outdoor room at the heart of the home, secure for children and adaptable to work, livestock, cooking or ceremony.

Modern use

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century architects — from Alvar Aalto and Geoffrey Bawa to contemporary designers in hot climates — have revived the courtyard house as a sustainable form for dense cities, and it remains standard in much new housing across the Middle East, China and Latin America.
