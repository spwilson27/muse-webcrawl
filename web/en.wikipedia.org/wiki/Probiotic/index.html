Probiotic

Probiotics
DefinitionLive microorganisms which, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host (FAO/WHO)
Common generaLactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Saccharomyces
SourcesYogurt, kefir, fermented foods, dietary supplements

Probiotics are live microorganisms promoted with claims that they provide health benefits when consumed, generally by improving or restoring the gut microbiota. The term derives from the Greek pro bios, "for life", in contrast to antibiotics. Commonly consumed probiotics include lactic acid bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species, and the yeast Saccharomyces boulardii.

History

The concept is associated with Élie Metchnikoff, who in the early 1900s proposed that the longevity of Bulgarian peasants resulted from consumption of fermented milk, and suggested deliberately replacing harmful gut microbes with beneficial ones. Ferdinand Vergin coined the term "probiotika" in 1954, and the modern FAO/WHO definition was adopted in 2001.

Health claims and evidence

Evidence is strongest for certain specific uses, such as reducing the risk of antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and shortening some infectious diarrhoea. For many popular claims — general digestive health, immune support, allergy or mood — scientific evidence remains preliminary or strain-specific. Health authorities such as EFSA have rejected most generic probiotic health claims for food labelling, and regulators caution that benefits must be demonstrated for each individual strain.

Safety

Probiotics are generally regarded as safe for healthy people, but rare cases of bacteraemia or fungaemia have occurred in the severely immunocompromised. Product quality varies, and independent testing sometimes finds that contents differ from label claims.
