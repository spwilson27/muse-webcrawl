History of the Jews in the Czech Republic

The history of the Jews in the Czech Republic (the Czech lands of Bohemia, Moravia and Czech Silesia) stretches back more than a thousand years. Jewish communities were established in Prague by the 10th century, and the Czech lands became one of the major centres of Ashkenazi Jewish life in Europe.

Middle Ages

Jewish merchants and moneylenders settled in Prague by the 900s. The community suffered during the Crusades and in periodic pogroms, including the Easter pogrom of 1389. Nevertheless Prague's Jewish Town grew into one of Europe's largest Jewish quarters, centred on synagogues such as the Old New Synagogue (built around 1270), among the oldest surviving synagogues in Europe.

Golden age in Prague

Under Rudolf II in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, Prague Jewry flourished. Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel, the Maharal of Prague, became the community's most famous figure and is associated in legend with the Golem of Prague. The financier Mordechai Maisel funded synagogues and public works. Jewish printers in Prague produced Hebrew books from 1512 onwards.

Emancipation

The Edicts of Tolerance of Joseph II (1781–82) eased restrictions, though the Familiants Laws limited Jewish families in Bohemia to 8,541. Full emancipation came with the revolutions of 1848 and the constitution of 1867 in the Austrian Empire. Jews contributed prominently to Czech and German culture, industry and scholarship; the Czech lands produced figures such as Franz Kafka, Max Brod and Sigmund Freud (born in Příbor, Moravia).

Interwar period and the Holocaust

In independent Czechoslovakia (from 1918) Jews enjoyed full civic equality, and Zionism and Jewish political parties were active. After the Munich Agreement of 1938 and the German occupation of 1939, the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia subjected Jews to Nazi persecution. Some 78,000 Czech Jews were murdered in the Holocaust, many deported via the Theresienstadt (Terezín) ghetto to Auschwitz and other camps.

Postwar and today

A small community survived and re-established itself after 1945, but many emigrated after the communist coup of 1948 and the antisemitic Slánský trial of 1952. After the Velvet Revolution of 1989, Jewish communal life revived; today roughly 3,000–4,000 registered Jews live in the Czech Republic, mainly in Prague, whose Jewish Museum and preserved quarter are major heritage sites.
