Hypoxemia

Hypoxemia
Specialty: Pulmonology, critical care medicine
Definition: Abnormally low oxygen level in arterial blood
Normal PaO2: 80–100 mmHg
Measurement: Arterial blood gas, pulse oximetry

Hypoxemia is an abnormally low level of oxygen in the blood — specifically, a reduced partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO2 below about 80 mmHg on room air). It is distinguished from hypoxia, which refers to inadequate oxygen at the tissue level; hypoxemia is one cause of hypoxia, but tissues can also be hypoxic with normal blood oxygen (as in anemia or ischemia). Conversely, hypoxemia does not always produce tissue hypoxia if compensatory mechanisms are adequate.

Causes

The five classic mechanisms of hypoxemia are: (1) hypoventilation, as in opioid overdose or neuromuscular weakness; (2) ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, the most common mechanism, seen in pneumonia, asthma, COPD, and pulmonary embolism; (3) right-to-left shunt, as in congenital heart disease or ARDS, which responds poorly to supplemental oxygen; (4) diffusion impairment, as in pulmonary fibrosis; and (5) low inspired oxygen, as at high altitude. The alveolar–arterial (A–a) gradient helps distinguish these: it is normal in hypoventilation and low inspired oxygen, and elevated in V/Q mismatch, shunt, and diffusion defects.

Signs and symptoms

Manifestations include shortness of breath, rapid breathing and heart rate, restlessness, confusion, and cyanosis (bluish discoloration of lips and fingertips, typically when deoxygenated hemoglobin exceeds 5 g/dL). Chronic hypoxemia can cause clubbing, polycythemia, and pulmonary hypertension. Notably, some patients — famously described in COVID-19 pneumonia — present with severe hypoxemia but little breathlessness ("happy hypoxia").

Diagnosis and treatment

Hypoxemia is measured directly by arterial blood gas analysis and estimated noninvasively by pulse oximetry (SpO2). Treatment addresses the underlying cause and provides supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula, mask, high-flow therapy, or mechanical ventilation. Oxygen targets are typically SpO2 94–98% for most acutely ill patients, and 88–92% for those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure.
