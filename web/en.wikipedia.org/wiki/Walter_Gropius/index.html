Walter Gropius

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Bauhaus building in Dessau: the glass curtain wall of the workshop wing turning the corner, modernism's most famous facade]

Walter Adolph Georg Gropius (18 May 1883 – 5 July 1969) was a German-American architect and founder of the Bauhaus school, widely regarded as one of the pioneering masters of modern architecture alongside Le Corbusier, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe and Frank Lloyd Wright. He founded the Bauhaus in Weimar in 1919, merging the fine-art academy and the arts-and-crafts school into an institution whose manifesto declared the building the ultimate aim of all visual arts.

Born in Berlin to a family of architects and officials, Gropius built before the First World War the Fagus Factory (1911, with Adolf Meyer) — a glass-cornered shoe-last factory that announced industrial modernism. After the war he built the Bauhaus's Dessau building (1925–26), the school's manifesto in steel, glass and asymmetric balance.

Exile and Harvard

Driven from Germany by the Nazis (the Bauhaus closed in 1933), Gropius worked in London with Maxwell Fry, then emigrated in 1937 to Harvard, where as chair of architecture he remade American architectural education (his own Gropius House in Lincoln, Massachusetts, 1938, was the school's demonstration). Through TAC (The Architects Collaborative, founded 1945) he built the Harvard Graduate Center and the Pan Am Building in New York — the last, looming over Grand Central, his most argued-about work.

Legacy

Gropius's deepest achievement was institutional: the Bauhaus idea — art, craft and technology unified, design as social mission — conquered design education worldwide through its diaspora. His buildings are respected; his school was a century.
