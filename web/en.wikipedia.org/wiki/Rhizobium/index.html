Rhizobium is a genus of Gram-negative, soil-dwelling bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen in symbiosis with leguminous plants. The name derives from the Greek rhiza (root) and bios (life). Rhizobia colonize the roots of legumes such as peas, beans, clover and soybeans, forming specialized organs called root nodules, inside which the bacteria — differentiated into bacteroids — convert inert nitrogen gas (N2) into ammonia usable by the plant.

Symbiosis

The partnership begins with an exchange of chemical signals: plant roots release flavonoids that activate bacterial nod genes, and the bacteria in return secrete lipo-chitooligosaccharide Nod factors that trigger nodule development and curling of root hairs. The plant supplies the bacteria with carbon compounds and a low-oxygen environment (maintained by the oxygen-carrying protein leghemoglobin, which gives active nodules their pink colour), while the nitrogenase enzyme complex carries out fixation — an energetically costly process requiring about 16 ATP per N2 molecule.

Agricultural importance

Biological nitrogen fixation by rhizobia is a cornerstone of sustainable agriculture: legume rotations and inoculants of selected Rhizobium strains reduce dependence on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers. Related nitrogen-fixing genera include Bradyrhizobium, Sinorhizobium (now Ensifer) and Mesorhizobium, collectively termed rhizobia.
