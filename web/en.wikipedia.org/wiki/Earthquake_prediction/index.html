Earthquake prediction

FieldSeismology
StatusNo reliable short-term method established
RelatedEarthquake forecasting, earthquake early warning

Earthquake prediction is the attempt to specify, in advance, the time, place and magnitude of a future earthquake within narrow limits, so that precautions such as evacuation could be taken. It is distinguished from earthquake forecasting, which gives probabilistic estimates of seismic hazard over years or decades, and from earthquake early warning, which detects an earthquake already in progress and alerts more distant locations seconds before strong shaking arrives.

Scientific status

Despite more than a century of research, no method of short-term prediction has been shown to work reliably. The consensus of seismologists is that earthquakes are a chaotic, self-organised process in which the state of stress on a fault cannot be measured directly at the required detail, so deterministic prediction may be impossible in principle. Many claimed precursors — anomalous animal behaviour, radon emissions, groundwater changes, electromagnetic signals, foreshocks, and patterns such as seismic quiescence — have repeatedly failed prospective testing.

Notable cases

The 1975 Haicheng earthquake in China was evacuated partly on the basis of foreshocks and other anomalies and is often cited as a successful prediction, but the far deadlier 1976 Tangshan earthquake the following year came without recognised warning. The Parkfield experiment in California instrumented a fault segment where a magnitude-6 earthquake was forecast with 95% confidence by 1993; the earthquake did not occur until 2004 and showed none of the expected precursors. Controversial claims include the Greek VAN method based on electrical signals and the predictions of the Italian technician Giampaolo Giuliani before the 2009 L'Aquila earthquake, after which several Italian scientists were controversially prosecuted (and ultimately acquitted) over risk communication.

Forecasting and early warning

In practice, societies rely on probabilistic hazard maps for building codes, operational aftershock forecasts, and early-warning systems such as Japan's Earthquake Early Warning and the US ShakeAlert, which exploit the speed difference between fast, weak P-waves and slower destructive S-waves.
