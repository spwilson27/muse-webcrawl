Cannon

A cannon is a large-calibre gun classified as a type of artillery, which usually launches a projectile using explosive chemical propellant. Gunpowder ("black powder") was the primary propellant before the invention of smokeless powder during the late 19th century. Cannons were developed in China during the 12th and 13th centuries and spread westward, transforming siege warfare, naval combat and battlefield tactics for more than six centuries.

History

The earliest known cannon appeared in Song-dynasty China; a bronze hand cannon dated to 1288 (the Heilongjiang hand cannon) is among the oldest surviving examples. Cannon reached Europe by the 1320s — a Florentine order of 1326 for metal arrows and guns is one of the earliest European records — and were used at battles such as Crécy (1346). Cast-bronze and later cast-iron smoothbore muzzle-loaders fired solid shot, exploding shells, canister and grapeshot. The Ottoman bombard used at the fall of Constantinople in 1453 exemplified the great siege guns of the age.

During the 19th century, rifled barrels, breech-loading mechanisms and steel construction greatly increased range and accuracy, and by the First World War the cannon had evolved into the modern field gun and howitzer. In the 20th century, "cannon" came to denote especially aircraft and vehicle armament of 20 mm calibre and above, such as autocannon.

Design

A cannon traditionally consists of a barrel (tube), a breech, and a mounting or carriage permitting elevation and traverse. Early pieces were loaded from the muzzle with powder, wad and shot, and fired through a touch-hole; later designs use a separate or fixed cartridge and a mechanical firing mechanism.
