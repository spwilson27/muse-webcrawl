An anechoic chamber (from Greek for "without echo") is a room designed to absorb as completely as possible the reflections of sound or electromagnetic waves, and to insulate the interior from outside noise. Inside, a person hears sounds with essentially no reverberation, approximating a free field.

Acoustic chambers

Acoustic anechoic chambers are lined on all surfaces, including the floor (usually covered by a tensioned wire mesh to walk on), with long wedges of sound-absorbing material such as glass fibre or foam. The wedge shape gradually matches acoustic impedance so that very little energy is reflected; performance degrades below a cut-off frequency set by the wedge length, typically a quarter-wavelength.

Such rooms are used to measure loudspeakers, microphones and hearing aids, to characterise noise from products, and for psychoacoustic research. The quietest chambers reach background levels below the threshold of human hearing, around −20 dBA or lower; visitors often find the absolute silence, in which bodily sounds become prominent, disorienting.

Radio-frequency chambers

RF anechoic chambers absorb electromagnetic radiation using pyramidal foam loaded with carbon and iron compounds, often combined with ferrite tiles. They are used for antenna measurements and electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) testing of electronic equipment.

History

The first modern anechoic chamber was built at Harvard University during the Second World War under Leo Beranek, for research on electroacoustic devices for the military.
