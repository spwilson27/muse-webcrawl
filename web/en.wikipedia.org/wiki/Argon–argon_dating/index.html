Argon–argon dating

[Image: Argon–argon dating — A mass spectrometer used for argon isotope analysis in a geochronology laboratory]

Argon–argon (or 40Ar/39Ar) dating is a radiometric dating method invented to supersede potassium–argon (K/Ar) dating in accuracy. The older method required splitting samples into two for separate potassium and argon measurements, while the newer technique requires only one rock fragment or mineral grain and uses a single measurement of argon isotopes.

Method

The sample is irradiated in a nuclear reactor, converting part of its stable 39K to radioactive 39Ar. Because the amount of 39K is proportional to 39Ar produced, measuring the ratio of 40Ar (produced by decay of 40K over geological time) to 39Ar allows the age to be calculated from a single argon measurement, using a co-irradiated standard of known age to calibrate the conversion.

Step heating

A major advantage of the method is that the sample can be heated in a series of steps (by laser or furnace), releasing argon from different sites in the crystal. If the ages computed from successive steps agree — forming a "plateau" — the result is considered robust; disturbed samples reveal their thermal history in the pattern of step ages. Laser spot analysis can even date individual crystals or zones within crystals.

Applications

Argon–argon dating is routinely used to date volcanic rocks from a few thousand years to billions of years old. It has dated the volcanic ash layers bracketing hominin fossils in East Africa, calibrated the geomagnetic polarity time scale, and dated impacts and mass extinctions, including refinements to the age of the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary.
