In algebraic topology, simplicial homology is a method for computing the homology groups of a topological space by first approximating the space with a simplicial complex — a structure built from vertices, edges, triangles, tetrahedra, and their higher-dimensional analogues. The homology groups count "holes" of each dimension and are fundamental invariants in topology; for spaces that admit triangulations, simplicial homology agrees with singular homology.

A triangulated torus: its first homology group has rank two, reflecting its two independent cycles.

Construction

Given an oriented simplicial complex, one forms for each dimension k the free abelian group Ck generated by the oriented k-simplices, called the group of k-chains. A boundary operator ∂k : Ck → Ck−1 sends each simplex to the alternating sum of its faces; the key identity ∂∂ = 0 makes the chains into a chain complex. The k-th homology group is the quotient Hk = ker ∂k / im ∂k+1 — cycles modulo boundaries.

Interpretation

H0 counts connected components; H1 counts independent loops that do not bound a surface; H2 counts enclosed voids, and so on. The ranks of the free parts of the homology groups are the Betti numbers, and the alternating sum of the chain-group ranks recovers the Euler characteristic. Torsion in the homology groups captures subtler twisting phenomena, as in the real projective plane.

History and computation

The ideas descend from Poincaré's foundational work on analysis situs (1895–1904), and the rigorous simplicial framework was developed in the early twentieth century by Alexander, Veblen, and others. Because simplicial homology is defined combinatorially, it can be computed algorithmically — via Smith normal form of the boundary matrices — and it underlies modern computational topology and topological data analysis.
