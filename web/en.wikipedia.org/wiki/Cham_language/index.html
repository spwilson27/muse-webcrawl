Cham is an Austronesian language spoken by the Cham people of Vietnam and Cambodia, the descendants of the kingdom of Champa. It is the principal language of the Chamic branch and notable among Austronesian languages for having been reshaped by centuries of contact with the tonal, monosyllabic languages of mainland Southeast Asia.

Varieties

Two main varieties are recognised. Eastern Cham is spoken in Vietnam's Ninh Thuận and Bình Thuận provinces, written in the Cham script, a descendant of the Brahmi script carried to Southeast Asia from India — among the earliest writing systems attested in the region, with inscriptions from around the 4th century CE. Western Cham is spoken mainly in Cambodia (and in diaspora communities), where most Cham are Muslim and the language is written in an Arabic-based script (Jawi); it has diverged considerably from the eastern variety.

Features

Cham has undergone "monosyllabicization" and developed register (voice-quality) contrasts under the influence of neighbouring Mon-Khmer and Vietic languages, making it typologically unusual for an Austronesian language. Cham literature includes the epic poem Akayet Dewa Mano and a rich corpus of ritual and historical texts.
