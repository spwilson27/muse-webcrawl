Thunk

In computer programming, a thunk is a subroutine used to inject a calculation into another subroutine. Thunks are primarily used to delay a calculation until its result is needed, or to insert operations at the beginning or end of the other subroutine. They are used in a variety of contexts, including compilers, lazy evaluation, and interoperability layers between programming languages and calling conventions.

Background

The term originated in the 1960s during the development of compilers for the language ALGOL. In implementations of ALGOL 60's call-by-name parameter passing, the compiler generated a small parameterless subroutine — a thunk — for each actual parameter. Evaluating the parameter in an expression meant jumping to the thunk, which computed the parameter's value in the caller's environment and returned it.

Uses

Thunks appear in lazy evaluation, where an expression is wrapped in a thunk that is forced only when its value is demanded, as in Haskell. In the Windows and OS/2 worlds, thunking refers to a mapping of system functions between 16-bit and 32-bit address spaces. In object-oriented programming, a thunk may adjust a this pointer before dispatching to a method in multiple inheritance.
