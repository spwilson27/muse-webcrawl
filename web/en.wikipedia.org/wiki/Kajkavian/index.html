Kajkavian

Kajkavian (Croatian: kajkavski, kajkavština) is one of the three main dialect groups of the Croatian language, spoken in northern and northwestern Croatia — including the capital Zagreb, Zagorje, Međimurje (in part), Podravina, and parts of Gorski Kotar — by roughly a third of Croats. It takes its name from the interrogative pronoun kaj ("what"), contrasting with Chakavian ča and Shtokavian što. In the west and north it gradually shades into Slovene dialects, with which it shares several features.

Characteristics

Kajkavian preserves several archaic features, such as keeping distinct reflexes of the Proto-Slavic nasal vowel and the yat vowel, a distinctive prosody of pitch accents differing from standard Croatian, and vocabulary with notable German and Hungarian loanwords reflecting centuries under Habsburg rule. Subdialects are conventionally divided by their reflexes of two Proto-Slavic vowel phonemes into groups named by forms such as križevačko-podravski and zagrebačko-žumberački. Zagreb's urban speech mixes Kajkavian with the standard Shtokavian-based language.

Literary history

Kajkavian has a substantial literary tradition: Zagreb was a centre of printing from the late 16th century, and writers such as Antun Vramec produced Kajkavian works while it served as the written language of civil Croatia. In the 1830s, Ljudevit Gaj — himself a Kajkavian speaker from Krapina — led the Illyrian movement to adopt Shtokavian as the common literary standard, after which Kajkavian literature continued mainly in regional and dialectal poetry, notably by Dragutin Domjanić and in the 20th century by Miroslav Krleža in his Ballads of Petrica Kerempuh.
