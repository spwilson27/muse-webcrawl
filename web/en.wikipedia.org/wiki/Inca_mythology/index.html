Inca mythology

Inca mythology is the body of myths and religious beliefs of the Inca Empire of the Andes, recorded largely through Spanish chronicles written after the conquest of Peru in the 16th century. Because the Inca had no writing system, their stories were preserved orally and in ritual, and the surviving accounts blend indigenous traditions with the interpretations of colonial authors.

Creation myths

The central creator god was Viracocha, who was said to have emerged from Lake Titicaca, created the sun, moon, stars, and the first human beings, and then travelled across the land teaching civilisation before departing over the Pacific. Another foundational myth tells of Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo, children of the sun god Inti, who were sent to earth to found the city of Cuzco and civilise the peoples of the Andes.

Principal deities

The Inca pantheon included Inti, the sun god and ancestor of the emperors; Mama Quilla, the moon goddess; Illapa, the god of thunder and weather; and Pachamama, the earth mother, who remains widely venerated in the Andes today. The emperor, the Sapa Inca, was regarded as the living descendant and representative of Inti.

Sacred landscape

Inca religion was closely tied to the landscape. Sacred places and objects called huacas — mountains, springs, stones, and mummies — were worshipped throughout the empire, and a system of ritual lines (ceques) radiated from the Coricancha temple in Cuzco, linking hundreds of shrines. Human sacrifice, including the capacocha ceremonies in which children were offered on high mountain peaks, formed part of the state religion.

Legacy

After the Spanish conquest, Catholic evangelisation suppressed Inca ritual, but many beliefs survived in syncretic form and remain influential in Quechua and Aymara communities. Festivals such as Inti Raymi, the festival of the sun, have been revived in modern times.
