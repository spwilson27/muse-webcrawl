António de Oliveira Salazar (28 April 1889 – 27 July 1970) was a Portuguese statesman, academic and economist who served as Prime Minister of Portugal from 1932 to 1968. Having come to power as finance minister during the military dictatorship that followed the 1926 coup, he founded and led the Estado Novo ("New State"), a corporatist authoritarian regime that governed Portugal until the Carnation Revolution of 1974.

Rise to power

Salazar, a professor of political economy at the University of Coimbra, was invited to become finance minister in 1928 on condition that he be given control over all government spending. He balanced the budget, stabilised the escudo and accumulated gold reserves. Appointed prime minister in 1932, he promulgated a new constitution in 1933 that institutionalised the Estado Novo, with the single party National Union, censorship, and the political police PIDE.

Rule

Salazar kept Portugal neutral in the Second World War while allowing the Allies to use bases in the Azores from 1943. Portugal was a founding member of NATO in 1949. From 1961, his refusal to decolonise led to colonial wars in Angola, Mozambique and Portuguese Guinea, which consumed much of the budget and military manpower. His regime rested on the pillars of "God, Fatherland and Family," on the Catholic Church (strengthened by the 1940 Concordat), and on fiscal conservatism. Political opponents were imprisoned, exiled or held in camps such as Tarrafal in Cape Verde.

Fall and death

In 1968 Salazar suffered a cerebral haemorrhage after a fall and was replaced by Marcelo Caetano; famously, he was never told he had been removed and continued to sign papers as though still in office. He died in 1970. The Estado Novo survived him by four years before collapsing in 1974. His legacy remains contested: credited with financial stabilisation and keeping Portugal out of the Spanish Civil War and the Second World War, he is condemned for dictatorship, political repression and the colonial wars.
