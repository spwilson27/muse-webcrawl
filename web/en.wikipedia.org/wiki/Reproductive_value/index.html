Reproductive value

Reproductive value is a concept in demography and evolutionary biology introduced by Ronald Fisher in The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection (1930). It measures the expected future reproductive contribution of an individual of a given age, relative to a newborn, taking into account both its probability of surviving to later ages and the fecundity it can expect at each.

Definition

For an individual aged x, Fisher's reproductive value vx weights offspring expected at each future age by survival to that age and discounts them by the population's growth rate, so that in a growing population early offspring count for more than late ones. A newborn's value is conventionally set by its chance of surviving to maturity; reproductive value rises through the juvenile period as mortality is survived, peaks near the onset of breeding, and declines thereafter as remaining fecundity shrinks.

Uses

Reproductive value is central to life-history theory: natural selection is strongest where reproductive value is high, which explains why senescence evolves — selection is weak against deleterious effects expressed after reproduction has largely ceased. In conservation biology and fisheries, weighting individuals by reproductive value rather than by head count improves estimates of a population's growth potential; in social insect biology it helps explain patterns of altruism toward colony members of different ages and castes.
