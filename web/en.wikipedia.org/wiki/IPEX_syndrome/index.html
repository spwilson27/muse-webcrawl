IPEX syndrome

IPEX syndrome
SpecialtyImmunology, endocrinology
SymptomsSevere diarrhoea (enteropathy), eczema, type 1 diabetes in infancy

Immunodysregulation polyendocrinopathy enteropathy X-linked (or IPEX) syndrome is a rare autoimmune disease caused by mutations in the FOXP3 gene, which encodes the master transcription factor of regulatory T cells. The disorder overwhelmingly affects boys and usually presents in the first months of life.

Loss of functional FOXP3 prevents development of regulatory T cells, unleashing multi-organ autoimmunity.

Signs and symptoms

The classic triad consists of intractable watery diarrhoea from autoimmune enteropathy, eczematous dermatitis, and endocrinopathy — most characteristically neonatal-onset type 1 diabetes mellitus. Other features include autoimmune thyroiditis, haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, hepatitis, nephritis, and food allergies with markedly elevated IgE. Without treatment the disease is usually fatal in the first years of life.

Genetics and mechanism

IPEX is inherited in an X-linked recessive manner. FOXP3, located at Xp11.23, is essential for the development and function of CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells (Tregs). Loss-of-function mutations cripple peripheral immune tolerance, so autoreactive T cells attack the gut, skin, pancreas, and other organs. The analogous mouse mutant, scurfy, shows a strikingly similar phenotype.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is suspected from the clinical picture in a male infant and confirmed by genetic testing for FOXP3 mutations, together with absent or reduced FOXP3 expression in Tregs. IPEX-like disorders can arise from mutations in other tolerance genes such as IL2RA (CD25), STAT5b, CTLA4, and LRBA.

Treatment

Management includes supportive care (parenteral nutrition, insulin), immunosuppressive drugs such as sirolimus and calcineurin inhibitors, and — as the only curative option — allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, ideally performed early before organ damage accumulates.
