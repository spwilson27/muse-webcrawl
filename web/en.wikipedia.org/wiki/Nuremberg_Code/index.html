Nuremberg Code

Nuremberg Code
OriginJudgment in United States v. Karl Brandt et al. (the Doctors' Trial)
Date1947 (verdict delivered 19–20 August 1947)
PlaceNuremberg, Germany
SubjectEthics of human experimentation; permissible medical experiments
Key principleVoluntary, informed consent of the human subject is "absolutely essential"

The Nuremberg Code is a set of ten principles for human experimentation set out in the judgment of the American military tribunal in the Doctors' Trial (United States v. Karl Brandt, et al.), one of the subsequent Nuremberg trials, delivered in August 1947. The trial prosecuted Nazi physicians and administrators for conducting brutal, often lethal experiments on concentration-camp prisoners. The code became a foundational document of modern research ethics and helped shape later frameworks such as the Declaration of Helsinki.

The ten points

  1. The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential — free, informed and given without coercion.
  2. The experiment should yield fruitful results for the good of society, unprocurable by other means, and not be random or unnecessary.
  3. It should be based on prior animal experimentation and knowledge of the disease or problem studied.
  4. It should be conducted so as to avoid all unnecessary physical and mental suffering and injury.
  5. No experiment should be conducted where there is reason to believe death or disabling injury will occur (except, perhaps, where the experimenters also serve as subjects).
  6. The degree of risk should never exceed the humanitarian importance of the problem to be solved.
  7. Proper preparations and adequate facilities must protect the subject against even remote possibilities of injury, disability or death.
  8. The experiment must be conducted only by scientifically qualified persons.
  9. The subject must be free to end the experiment at any time.
  10. The scientist in charge must be prepared to terminate the experiment at any stage if continuation is likely to result in injury, disability or death to the subject.

Legacy

Initially little noticed in the United States, the Code gained influence from the 1960s as research abuses (such as Tuskegee) came to light. Its emphasis on voluntary informed consent underpins the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964), the US federal regulations for protection of human subjects (the "Common Rule"), and the work of institutional review boards, although debates continue over how strictly its absolute requirements — such as the prohibition of experiments likely to kill — apply in practice.
