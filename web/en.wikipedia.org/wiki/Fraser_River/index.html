Fraser River

Length1,375 km (854 mi)
SourceFraser Pass, Rocky Mountains, British Columbia
MouthStrait of Georgia, near Vancouver
Basin size220,000 km²

The Fraser River is the longest river within British Columbia, Canada, rising at Fraser Pass near Blackrock Mountain in the Rocky Mountains and flowing for 1,375 kilometres (854 mi) into the Strait of Georgia just south of the City of Vancouver. The river's annual discharge at its mouth is 3,475 cubic metres per second, and it discharges some 20 million tons of sediment into the ocean each year. It is named for explorer Simon Fraser, who led an expedition in 1808 down the river.

Course

The Fraser drains a 220,000-square-kilometre basin. From its source it flows northwest through the Rocky Mountain Trench, then south past Prince George, cutting through the Fraser Canyon — including the notorious Hell's Gate — before emerging into the fertile Fraser Valley and reaching the sea between Vancouver and Richmond. Major tributaries include the Nechako, Thompson, and Harrison rivers.

Ecology and economy

The Fraser is one of the world's most productive salmon rivers, supporting all five species of Pacific salmon as well as white sturgeon. Its estuary is a key stopover on the Pacific Flyway for migratory birds. The river was central to the Fraser Canyon Gold Rush of 1858, which brought tens of thousands of prospectors to the colony of British Columbia. Today its valley is a major agricultural region, and the river supports commercial and recreational fisheries, hydroelectric tributaries, and transport corridors.

History

Indigenous peoples, including the Stó:lō and Nlaka'pamux, have lived along the river for thousands of years. Simon Fraser's 1808 descent by canoe, undertaken for the North West Company, was a harrowing journey through the canyon; Fraser concluded the river was too dangerous to serve as a trade route. The lower river was surveyed for the boundary between British and American territory, settled by the Oregon Treaty of 1846.
