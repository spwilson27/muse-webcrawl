Treaty of Campo-Formio

Signed17 October 1797
LocationCampoformido, Republic of Venice
SignatoriesFrench Republic, Habsburg Monarchy

The Treaty of Campo-Formio (today Campoformido) was signed on 17 October 1797 (26 Vendémiaire VI) by Napoleon Bonaparte and Count Philipp von Cobenzl as representatives of the French Republic and the Austrian Habsburg Monarchy, respectively. The treaty followed the armistice of Leoben (18 April 1797) and the preliminary peace preliminaries, and ended the War of the First Coalition.

Terms

The treaty saw Austria cede the Austrian Netherlands (modern Belgium) to France and recognize the Cisalpine Republic, a French client state in northern Italy formed from various Italian territories. In return, Austria received the territories of the Republic of Venice, which France and Austria partitioned between themselves: Venice itself, Istria, and Dalmatia went to Austria, while the Venetian Ionian Islands went to France. The 1,100-year-old Venetian republic thus ceased to exist as an independent state.

Secret articles promised Austria additional German territory, including the Archbishopric of Salzburg and part of Bavaria, in return for further French gains on the left bank of the Rhine.

Significance

The treaty dramatically redrew the map of Italy and central Europe, marked the rise of Napoleon as a diplomatic as well as military figure, and effectively ended the First Coalition against revolutionary France, leaving Britain alone at war with France. The settlement of Italy it imposed lasted, with interruptions, until the Congress of Vienna in 1815. The fate of Venice — handed over to Austria without the consent of its people — became a lasting symbol of cynical great-power diplomacy.
