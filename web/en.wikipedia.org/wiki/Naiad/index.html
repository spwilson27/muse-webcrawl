Naiad

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Greek mythology, the naiads (Greek: ναϊάδες, naïádes) are a type of female spirit, or nymph, that preside over fountains, wells, springs, streams, brooks and other bodies of fresh water. They are distinct from the river gods (Potamoi) and the very ancient spirits of waters that inhabited the stagnant waters of marshes, ponds and lagoon-type of waters of the pre-Olympic forms of Greek religion.

The naiads were associated with fresh water, as the Oceanids were with salt water and the Nereids specifically with the Mediterranean Sea, but because in Greek thought the waters of the world were all one system flowing from the subterranean Sea of Oceanus, a nymph might be common to both classes.

Names and families

Naiads were variously classified by the waters they inhabited: Pegaeae (springs), Potameides (rivers), Limnades or Limnatides (lakes), Crinaeae (fountains), and Eleionomae (marshes). Individual naiads were often described as the daughters of river gods, of Zeus, or of Oceanus. Famous naiads include Arethusa, transformed into a spring to escape the river god Alpheus; Salmacis, who merged with Hermaphroditus; and the nymphs of the river Spercheios.

Worship

Naiads were the object of local cults, worshipped as essential to human and animal fertility; boys and girls at coming-of-age dedicated locks of their hair to their local spring naiads, and sick people sought healing at their springs. Sacrifices of lambs, kids, oil, and honey were made at their waters. Their worship was essentially local, attached to particular springs and fountains rather than to pan-Hellenic sanctuaries.

Dangers

Though gentle by nature, naiads could be dangerous: a beautiful spring or fountain might lure the unwary. Hylas, companion of Heracles, was dragged into a spring by naiads who admired his beauty. Naiads were also thought to strike the jealous or the impious with madness, and the sight of a naiad bathing might be fatal.
