Sérsic profile

The Sérsic profile (or Sérsic model) is a mathematical function that describes how the intensity I of a galaxy varies with distance R from its center. It is a generalization of de Vaucouleurs' law and was introduced by the Argentine astronomer José Luis Sérsic in the 1960s. In its common form:

I(R) = Ie exp{ −bn [ (R/Re)1/n − 1 ] }

where Ie is the intensity at the effective radius Re (the radius enclosing half of the total light), n is the Sérsic index, and bn is a constant that depends on n, approximately bn ≈ 2n − 1/3 for the range of indices typical of galaxies.

Sérsic index

The index n measures the concentration of the light profile. A profile with n = 1 is a pure exponential, describing most spiral galaxy discs and dwarf elliptical galaxies. The case n = 4 reproduces the de Vaucouleurs r1/4 law, which fits giant elliptical galaxies and the bulges of spirals. Values between about 0.5 and 10 are found in practice, and the index correlates with galaxy mass and luminosity: more massive galaxies tend to have larger n. Fitting Sérsic profiles — often with software such as GALFIT — is a standard technique in extragalactic astronomy for decomposing galaxies into bulge and disc components and for studying galaxy evolution.

History

Sérsic published the general law in 1963 in the Boletín de la Asociación Argentina de Astronomía and applied it in his Atlas de Galaxias Australes (1968). It attracted little attention in the English-speaking literature for decades before being widely adopted in the 1990s, when CCD imaging and automated fitting made general-index profiles practical.
