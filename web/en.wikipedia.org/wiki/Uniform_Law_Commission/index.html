The Uniform Law Commission (ULC), formerly the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws, is a non-profit association of lawyers appointed by the governments of the US states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico and the US Virgin Islands. Founded in 1892, it drafts model and uniform legislation — such as the Uniform Commercial Code — and promotes its enactment to bring consistency to state law in areas where uniformity is useful, while respecting the federal structure of the United States.

Organisation

Each jurisdiction appoints commissioners, who must be members of the bar and serve without compensation from the Commission. Drafting committees, advised by reporters (usually law professors) and observers from interested organisations, prepare acts that must be read and debated at two annual meetings before approval. A "uniform act" is one the Commission urges be enacted exactly as written in all states; a "model act" may be adapted to local conditions.

Notable acts

The ULC's most successful product is the Uniform Commercial Code (1952), produced jointly with the American Law Institute and enacted, in whole or part, by every state. Other widely adopted acts include the Uniform Probate Code, the Uniform Partnership Act, the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act, the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, and the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act.

Criticism

Critics have occasionally questioned the democratic accountability of a private body shaping state law, and some acts have been controversial or widely amended on adoption; supporters reply that the process is open, expert and advisory only, with legislatures free to accept, modify or reject each act.
