North Atlantic right whale

The North Atlantic right whale (Eubalaena glacialis) is a baleen whale and one of three right whale species belonging to the genus Eubalaena. It is among the most endangered large whales in the world, with a population of only a few hundred individuals—roughly 370 in the early 2020s—inhabiting the coastal waters of the western North Atlantic.

Description

Right whales are stocky, broad-backed whales with no dorsal fin. They have a strongly arched mouth, long baleen plates for filter-feeding on copepods and other zooplankton, and distinctive patches of roughened skin called callosities on the head, whose patterns allow researchers to identify individuals. The species takes its name from whalers, who considered it the "right" whale to hunt: it swims slowly, floats when killed, and yielded large quantities of oil and baleen.

Distribution and migration

The whales calve in winter in shallow waters off the southeastern United States (Florida, Georgia, and the Carolinas) and migrate north in spring to feeding grounds in New England waters, the Bay of Fundy, and the Scotian Shelf. Critical habitat areas include Cape Cod Bay and Roseway Basin.

Threats and conservation

Commercial whaling devastated the species by the early 20th century; it has been protected internationally since 1935. Today the chief causes of death are entanglement in fishing gear and collisions with ships. Because females produce a calf only every three or more years under good conditions, the death of even a few breeding females can push the population into decline. Conservation measures include vessel speed restrictions, fishing-gear modifications, and aerial and acoustic monitoring. See also right whale, baleen whale, and whaling.
