Nizari Isma'ilism (Arabic: النزارية, romanized: an-Nizāriyya) is the largest branch of Isma'ilism, itself the second-largest branch of Shia Islam after Twelver Shi'ism. Nizaris emphasise social justice, pluralism, and the ongoing guidance of a living, hereditary Imam — the Aga Khan — whom they believe to be the direct descendant of the Prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatimah and her husband Ali.

The Nizari tradition emerged from a succession dispute in 1094 following the death of the Fatimid caliph-imam al-Mustansir Billah. Those who held that his elder son Nizar was the rightful successor became known as Nizaris, while supporters of his younger son al-Musta'li formed the Musta'li branch.

History

Origins and the Alamut period

After the schism of 1094, the Persian missionary Hassan-i Sabbah, who had seized the mountain fortress of Alamut in 1090, championed Nizar's cause and established an independent Nizari state in Persia and Syria. The Nizari state, famous in Western legend for its fedayeen (the so-called "Assassins"), withstood Seljuk pressure for over a century and a half. In 1164, the fourth lord of Alamut, Hassan II, proclaimed the qiyama (resurrection), a spiritual reinterpretation of the faith.

The Alamut state was destroyed by the Mongols under Hulagu in 1256, when the last lord, Rukn al-Din Khurshah, surrendered the fortress. The Imamate nonetheless survived in concealment (satr), with the Imams living quietly in Persia for centuries.

Anjudan revival and the Aga Khans

A revival of Nizari missionary activity centred on Anjudan in central Iran during the 15th century. In the 19th century the 46th Imam, Hassan Ali Shah, received the title Aga Khan from the Qajar shah and later settled in India, where the community — known as the Khojas — flourished. His successors, notably Aga Khan III (Sir Sultan Muhammad Shah, Imam 1885–1957) and Aga Khan IV (Prince Karim, Imam 1957–2025), modernised the community's institutions and founded the Aga Khan Development Network (AKDN). Upon Aga Khan IV's death in February 2025, his son Prince Rahim succeeded as the 50th hereditary Imam, Aga Khan V.

Beliefs

Nizari teaching holds that the Quran has both an exoteric meaning (zahir) and an esoteric, inner meaning (batin), which the Imam alone can authoritatively interpret (ta'wil). The Imam, believed divinely guided and infallible, provides authoritative guidance (farman) to his followers in each age. Core practices include the daily du'a prayers recited three times a day, observance of Ramadan, and a strong ethic of voluntary service (khidma), charity, and the tithe-like contribution known as dasond.

Community

Nizaris today live in more than 35 countries, with significant populations in Central and South Asia, East Africa, the Middle East, and, through migration, Europe and North America. Congregational life centres on the jamatkhana. The community is notable for its women's participation, emphasis on education, and the development work of the AKDN in health, education, and economic development.
