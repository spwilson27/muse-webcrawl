Chelation

Chelation is a type of bonding of ions and molecules to metal ions. It involves the formation of two or more separate coordinate covalent bonds between a polydentate (multiple bonded) ligand and a single central metal atom. These ligands are called chelants, chelators, chelating agents, or sequestering agents. They are usually organic compounds, but this is not a requirement, as with zinc and its use as a maintenance therapy to prevent the absorption of copper in people with Wilson's disease.

Etymology

The word chelation derives from the Greek χηλή (chēlē), meaning "claw", and Latin chela; the ligands lie around the central atom like the two pincer-like claws of a lobster. The term was coined in 1920 by Sir Gilbert T. Morgan and H. D. K. Drew, who described compounds in which multidentate groups function as claws gripping the metal.

Chelate effect

Chelate complexes are generally much more stable than comparable complexes with monodentate ligands — a phenomenon known as the chelate effect, explained largely by entropy: displacing several bound solvent molecules with one multidentate ligand increases the number of free particles in solution. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), a hexadentate ligand, forms exceptionally stable complexes and is used to sequester metal ions in detergents, food preservation, and analytical chemistry.

Applications

Chelating agents are used industrially to soften water, prevent metal-catalysed oxidation in food, and control metal ions in chemical processes. In biology, chelation is fundamental to the function of metalloproteins such as haemoglobin and chlorophyll. In medicine, chelation therapy with agents such as EDTA, dimercaprol (BAL), penicillamine and deferoxamine is an approved treatment for heavy-metal poisoning, including lead poisoning and iron overload; its use for other conditions, such as cardiovascular disease or autism, is controversial and not supported by mainstream evidence.
