Daniel Kahneman

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Daniel Kahneman in his later years, speaking at a lecture]
Daniel Kahneman in his later years, speaking at a lecture

Daniel Kahneman (5 March 1934 – 27 March 2024) was an Israeli-American psychologist and Nobel laureate, widely regarded as the founder of the psychology of judgment and decision-making and a father of Behavioural economics. With Amos Tversky he developed the heuristics and biases programme and Prospect theory; he shared the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. His bestselling book Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) popularised the 'System 1 / System 2' model of cognition.

Life and research

Born in Tel Aviv and raised in Paris — where as a Jewish child he survived the Nazi occupation — Kahneman emigrated to Israel, served as an army psychologist and taught at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, later at Berkeley and Princeton University. His collaboration with Tversky (1969–1996) produced the landmark papers on representativeness, availability and anchoring (1973–74) and prospect theory (1979), transforming economics' picture of rationality.

Later work

Kahneman turned to the psychology of well-being: the experiencing versus remembering self, the peak–end rule and the focusing illusion, and co-founded research on decision 'noise' — Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment (2021, with Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein). He also championed adversarial collaboration and, late in life, voiced public doubts about priming research he had promoted, an act of scientific self-correction widely admired.

Influence

Kahneman's ideas reshaped economics, medicine, law, military training and public policy (including 'nudge' units modelled partly on behavioural findings). Thinking, Fast and Slow sold millions of copies and made 'System 1' and 'System 2' household terms; Michael Lewis's The Undoing Project (2016) chronicled his partnership with Tversky. He died in 2024 aged 90.
