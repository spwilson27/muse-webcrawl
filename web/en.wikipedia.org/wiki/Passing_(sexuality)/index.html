Passing (sexuality)

In the context of sexuality and gender, passing refers to the ability of a person to be regarded as a member of a social group other than their own, generally with the aim of gaining social acceptance or avoiding stigma and discrimination. In LGBT usage, the term most often describes:

Presentation and perception are central to the concept of passing.

  • A gay, lesbian, or bisexual person being perceived as heterosexual ("passing as straight"), a common aspect of remaining in the closet.
  • A transgender or gender-nonconforming person being perceived as cisgender and as the gender with which they identify; this usage developed from the earlier transsexual and cross-dressing communities.

History of the term

The concept derives from the broader sociological idea of "passing", notably racial passing in the United States, studied by Erving Goffman in his work on stigma (1963). Within queer communities, "passing" was long bound up with safety, employment, and access to housing. For transgender people, the achievement of passing has historically been emphasised by clinicians as a goal of transition, though many in the community now critique the notion for implying that the alternative is deception or failure, and prefer terms such as "being read as" one's gender.

Debates

Passing can offer practical protection from harassment and violence, but it may also be accompanied by anxiety about being "clocked" (recognised as transgender or as gay). Critics argue that an emphasis on passing reinforces binary gender norms and can divide communities between those who pass and those who cannot or choose not to. Others note that for many people passing is simply the experience of being seen as oneself, without any intention to deceive.

Related concepts

The inverse phenomenon, in which a person's identity is assumed to be something it is not by default (for example, assuming all people are heterosexual and cisgender), is sometimes discussed in terms of heteronormativity and "assumed passing". A person who is not open about their sexuality is described as closeted; disclosure is called coming out.
