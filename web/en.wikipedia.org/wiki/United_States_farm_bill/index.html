United States farm bill

The United States farm bill is the primary agricultural and food policy legislation of the United States federal government. Farm bills are omnibus, multiyear laws renewed roughly every five years, governing farm commodity support, crop insurance, conservation, trade, rural development, research, forestry, energy, and nutrition programs such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP).

History

The modern farm bill tradition began with the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933, a New Deal measure responding to the agricultural collapse of the Great Depression. Successive bills in 1938, 1948, and later decades established the framework of price supports, acreage controls, and later direct payments.

Nutrition programs became a major part of the legislation beginning with the food stamp program in the 1960s and 1970s, joining agricultural and urban interests in a durable coalition. In recent decades, nutrition spending has accounted for the majority of farm bill expenditures, followed by crop insurance and commodity programs.

Recent bills

The Agricultural Act of 2014 and the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 continued the pattern of five-year authorizations. The 2018 bill legalized industrial hemp production and was projected to cost roughly $428 billion over five years, with about 76 percent for nutrition programs.
