Micrographia

Micrographia is a historically significant book by Robert Hooke, published in January 1665 by the Royal Society. Its full title is Micrographia: or Some Physiological Descriptions of Minute Bodies Made by Magnifying Glasses. With Observations and Inquiries Thereupon. It was the first major work devoted to observations made through a microscope, and its large, detailed copperplate engravings of the microscopic world — a flea, a louse, the eye of a fly, and the pores of cork — caused a sensation.

Contents

The book presents sixty observations, most made with Hooke's compound microscope. Examining a thin slice of cork, Hooke saw a honeycomb of tiny compartments which he named "cells" — the first use of the word in biology, though what he saw were the empty cell walls of dead plant tissue. Other plates show the sting of a bee, mould growths (the first depiction of a microorganism), snowflakes, and the point of a needle and edge of a razor contrasted with natural structures. Hooke also discussed the colours of thin films, combustion, and the nature of light and gravity.

Impact

Micrographia was a bestseller — Samuel Pepys called it "the most ingenious book that ever I read in my life" — and it demonstrated the power of the new scientific instruments to reveal an unseen world. It is credited with popularizing the microscope, establishing the book as a model of scientific illustration, and coining the biological term "cell". Not all of its reasoning was Hooke's alone, and its author's disputes with contemporaries such as Isaac Newton later overshadowed parts of its reputation, but it remains a landmark of the Scientific Revolution.
