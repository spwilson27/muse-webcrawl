Brevetoxin

Brevetoxins are a set of at least ten lipid-soluble neurotoxic compounds produced by the marine dinoflagellate Karenia brevis, the organism responsible for the harmful algal blooms known as Florida red tides. Brevetoxins are cyclic polyethers — large "ladder-frame" molecules — that bind to voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve and muscle cells, holding them open and causing uncontrolled depolarization.

Effects

Humans are exposed to brevetoxins by eating contaminated shellfish, which causes neurotoxic shellfish poisoning (NSP) — gastrointestinal and neurological symptoms such as nausea, tingling, temperature reversal, and vertigo, lasting hours to days and rarely fatal — or by inhaling sea-spray aerosols during red tides, which causes coughing, sneezing, and eye and throat irritation, and can aggravate asthma. The toxins also kill fish in enormous numbers during blooms and poison marine mammals and birds, including manatees and dolphins.

Chemistry

Brevetoxins are classified into A-type (backbone of ten fused rings) and B-type (eleven rings) families, with brevetoxin B (PbTx-2) being the most abundant. Their total chemical synthesis, achieved by K. C. Nicolaou's group in the 1990s, was a landmark of organic chemistry because of the molecules' size and complexity. Brevetoxin binds at site 5 of the voltage-sensitive sodium channel, shifting activation to more negative potentials and inhibiting inactivation.

Monitoring

Because the toxins are tasteless, odourless, and heat-stable, shellfish beds in affected areas are monitored for K. brevis cell counts and closed when thresholds are exceeded. Related compounds, the brevenals, produced by the same organism, antagonize brevetoxin binding and have been studied as potential treatments for respiratory effects.
