Vacuum tube


Vacuum tubes dominated electronics from the 1900s to the 1960s.

A vacuum tube, electron tube, valve (in British English), or tube is a device that controls electric current flow in a high vacuum between electrodes to which an electric potential difference has been applied. Vacuum tubes rely on thermionic emission of electrons from a heated cathode and formed the foundation of electronics throughout the first half of the 20th century, enabling radio, television, telephony, radar, and the first electronic computers.

History

The phenomenon behind the tube was observed by Thomas Edison in 1883 (the "Edison effect"), but the first practical device was John Ambrose Fleming's diode (1904), used as a radio detector. Lee de Forest's Audion (1906) added a control grid, creating the triode, the first amplifying device, and made transcontinental telephony and broadcast radio possible. Further electrodes produced the tetrode and pentode, reducing distortion and increasing gain. Tubes remained the core of electronics until the transistor (1947) and integrated circuits displaced them from most applications in the 1960s and 1970s.

Operation

A heated cathode emits electrons into the evacuated envelope; a positively charged anode (plate) attracts them, producing current flow. One or more grids between cathode and anode modulate that flow with a small voltage, allowing rectification, amplification, and oscillation. The cathode is usually coated with alkaline earth oxides to lower its work function and is heated by a filament, either directly or via a separate heater.

Applications and legacy

Tubes powered early computers such as ENIAC, which used some 18,000 of them. Today they survive in niches: high-power radio transmitters and microwave devices (magnetrons in ovens, klystrons and traveling-wave tubes in radar and satellites), cathode-ray displays in legacy equipment, and — famously — guitar amplifiers and high-end audio, where their distortion characteristics are prized. In the 2010s–2020s, shortages of Russian- and Chinese-made audio tubes renewed interest in their manufacture.
