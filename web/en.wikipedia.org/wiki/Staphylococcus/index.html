Staphylococcus

Staphylococcus is a genus of Gram-positive bacteria in the family Staphylococcaceae. The name, from the Greek staphylē (a bunch of grapes) and kokkos (berry), describes their appearance under the microscope: round cells about 0.5–1.5 μm in diameter that divide in multiple planes to form irregular, grape-like clusters. They are facultative anaerobes, catalase-positive, and are divided into coagulase-positive and coagulase-negative groups.

Species and disease

The genus contains around 50 recognised species and subspecies, many of them ordinary commensals of the skin and mucous membranes of humans and animals. The most medically important is Staphylococcus aureus, a coagulase-positive species that colonises the nose of roughly a third of the population and can cause boils, abscesses, wound infections, pneumonia, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, toxic shock syndrome and staphylococcal food poisoning through its array of toxins and enzymes.

Coagulase-negative species such as S. epidermidis are normally harmless skin residents but are frequent contaminants of blood cultures and important causes of infections of prosthetic joints, heart valves and indwelling catheters. S. saprophyticus is a notable cause of urinary tract infections in young women.

Antibiotic resistance

Staphylococci have repeatedly evolved resistance to antibiotics. Penicillin-resistant S. aureus appeared within a few years of penicillin's introduction; methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) was identified in 1961 and became a major hospital pathogen before spreading into the community. Vancomycin has remained a mainstay against MRSA, though strains with intermediate (VISA) and, rarely, full resistance have been reported.
