Hebbian theory
Proposed byDonald O. Hebb, 1949
SourceThe Organization of Behavior
Popular summary"Neurons that fire together, wire together"

Hebbian theory is a neuropsychological theory that proposes an explanation for the adaptation of neurons in the brain during the learning process. It describes a basic mechanism for synaptic plasticity, in which an increase in synaptic efficacy arises from a presynaptic cell's repeated and persistent stimulation of a postsynaptic cell. The theory was introduced by Donald O. Hebb in his 1949 book The Organization of Behavior.

Hebb's postulate

Hebb stated: "When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite a cell B and repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing it, some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells such that A's efficiency, as one of the cells firing B, is increased." The idea is often summarised as "cells that fire together wire together" — a phrase coined later by Carla Shatz. Hebb also proposed that repeatedly co-active neurons form "cell assemblies" underlying memories and concepts.

Mechanisms and evidence

The discovery of long-term potentiation (LTP) in the hippocampus by Bliss and Lømo (1973) provided a concrete mechanism: coincident pre- and postsynaptic activity activates NMDA receptors, admitting calcium that strengthens the synapse. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) refines the rule — if the presynaptic neuron fires just before the postsynaptic one, the synapse strengthens (LTP); the reverse order weakens it (LTD). Pure Hebbian rules are unstable (strengthening feeds on itself), so biological and computational models add normalisation, competition, or sliding thresholds (as in the BCM theory).

Influence

Hebbian learning is foundational in computational neuroscience and machine learning: it underlies unsupervised learning in artificial neural networks, principal-component extraction rules such as Oja's rule, and associative memory models such as Hopfield networks.
