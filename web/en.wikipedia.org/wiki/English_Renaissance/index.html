English Renaissance

The English Renaissance was a cultural and artistic movement in England from the late 15th century to the early 17th century. It is associated with the pan-European Renaissance that originated in Italy, but arrived in England later and took distinctive forms, especially in literature and theatre — the age of William Shakespeare, Christopher Marlowe and Ben Jonson.

Origins

The movement is often dated from the accession of Henry VII in 1485, the introduction of printing by William Caxton, and growing humanist scholarship influenced by Erasmus and Thomas More, whose Utopia (1516) exemplified Renaissance humanism. The English Reformation under Henry VIII reshaped religion and learning, dissolving monasteries but also spurring vernacular Bible translation.

Literature and theatre

Under Elizabeth I, English drama flourished in public playhouses such as the Globe. Poets including Edmund Spenser (The Faerie Queene), Philip Sidney and John Donne developed sonnets, epic and metaphysical poetry. Prose advanced with Francis Bacon's essays and the King James Bible (1611).

Music, art and architecture

Composers Thomas Tallis, William Byrd and John Dowland led a golden age of English polyphony and lute song. Architecture moved slowly from Tudor Gothic toward classical symmetry in great houses such as Hardwick Hall, while portraiture was dominated by miniaturists like Nicholas Hilliard and foreign artists at court.

Decline

By the 1620s–1640s, political and religious tensions culminating in the English Civil War closed the theatres (1642) and ended the Renaissance flowering, giving way to Restoration culture.
