Diesel cycle

p–V diagram of the idealised Diesel cycle
Named afterRudolf Diesel

The Diesel cycle is the thermodynamic cycle that models the combustion process of the Diesel engine, the compression-ignition engine invented by Rudolf Diesel. In contrast to the Otto cycle of spark-ignition petrol engines, heat is added at (approximately) constant pressure rather than constant volume, reflecting the gradual injection and burning of fuel as the piston descends.

The ideal cycle

The idealised Diesel cycle consists of four processes:

  1. Isentropic compression — air alone is compressed to a high ratio (typically 15:1 to 22:1), heating it to above the fuel's ignition temperature.
  2. Constant-pressure heat addition — fuel is injected and burns as the piston begins to move down, so pressure stays roughly constant while volume increases.
  3. Isentropic expansion — the hot gases expand and do work on the piston.
  4. Constant-volume heat rejection — at the end of the stroke, exhaust is released and pressure drops at constant volume.

Efficiency

The thermal efficiency of the ideal Diesel cycle depends on the compression ratio and the cut-off ratio (the ratio of volumes at the end and start of combustion). For the same compression ratio, the ideal Diesel cycle is less efficient than the ideal Otto cycle; however, real diesel engines use much higher compression ratios than petrol engines can tolerate, and so achieve higher practical efficiency, commonly 35–45% in large engines.

Applications

Diesel-cycle engines power most trucks, buses, trains, ships, agricultural and construction machinery, and many passenger cars, valued for their efficiency, torque and durability.
