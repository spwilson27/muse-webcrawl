Sand Creek massacre

The Sand Creek massacre was a massacre of Cheyenne and Arapaho people by the U.S. Army in the American Indian Wars that occurred on November 29, 1864, when a 700-man force of Colorado Territory militia attacked and destroyed a village of friendly Cheyenne and Arapaho in southeastern Colorado Territory, killing and mutilating an estimated 69 to more than 160 Native Americans, about two-thirds of them women and children. The village, led by the peace chiefs Black Kettle, White Antelope and Left Hand, had camped near Fort Lyon under assurances of safety and flew a U.S. flag and a white flag.

Background

The Colorado Gold Rush from 1858 brought tens of thousands of settlers onto Cheyenne and Arapaho land. The 1861 Treaty of Fort Wise, signed by few chiefs, ceded most tribal territory; violence escalated in 1864 amid the Civil War, and Territorial Governor John Evans issued proclamations authorising citizens to kill hostile Indians and summoning friendly bands to military posts for protection. Black Kettle's band, having reported to Fort Lyon, was directed to camp on Sand Creek.

The attack

At dawn on November 29, Colonel John Chivington's Third Colorado Cavalry — 100-day volunteers — together with elements of the First Colorado, attacked the sleeping village, ignoring its flags and the fact that many warriors were away hunting. Soldiers killed indiscriminately and desecrated bodies, taking scalps and body parts later displayed in Denver. A few officers, notably Captain Silas Soule and Lt. Joseph Cramer, refused to fire; Soule later testified against Chivington and was murdered in 1865.

Aftermath

Initial press reports celebrated a "battle", but investigations by Congress and the Army condemned the massacre. The Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War denounced Chivington, who resigned and was never punished. Outrage in the East contrasted with support in Colorado. The massacre drove the Cheyenne, Arapaho and their Lakota allies to wider war on the Plains. The Treaty of the Little Arkansas (1865) promised reparations that were never paid. The site is now preserved as the Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site, established in 2007, and the event remains a touchstone in relations between the United States and the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes.
