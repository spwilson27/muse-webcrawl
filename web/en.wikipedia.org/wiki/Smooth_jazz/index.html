Smooth jazz

Smooth jazz
Stylistic origins: Jazz fusion, R&B, funk, pop
Cultural origins: 1970s–1980s, United States
Key artists: Grover Washington Jr., Kenny G, George Benson, David Sanborn

Smooth jazz is a commercially oriented, radio-friendly subgenre of jazz that emerged from jazz fusion in the late 1970s and 1980s. It emphasises melodic, polished production, mellow grooves drawn from R&B and funk, and restrained improvisation, typically featuring saxophone, guitar, or piano over programmed or lightly funky rhythm sections.

History

Precursors include Grover Washington Jr.'s albums of the 1970s and his hit "Just the Two of Us" (1981), George Benson's crossover success with Breezin' (1976), and the work of Bob James, David Sanborn, and Spyro Gyra. In the late 1980s, American radio consultants codified "smooth jazz" as a format; stations such as KTWV "The Wave" in Los Angeles built large audiences, and saxophonist Kenny G became one of the best-selling instrumental artists of all time.

Characteristics

Smooth jazz recordings favour mid-tempo grooves, lush synthesiser pads, clean-toned solos, and instrumental covers of pop and soul songs. Improvisation is present but generally concise and consonant, prioritising mood and accessibility over the harmonic risk-taking of bebop or avant-garde jazz.

Criticism and decline

Many jazz critics and musicians dismiss smooth jazz as background music lacking jazz's improvisational essence — Kenny G in particular has been a lightning rod, famously criticised by guitarist Pat Metheny. The radio format declined sharply in the United States after the late 2000s, with most stations switching formats, though the genre persists through festivals, cruises, streaming, and international audiences.
