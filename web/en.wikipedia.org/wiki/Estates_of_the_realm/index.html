The estates of the realm, or three estates, were the broad social orders into which medieval and early modern European society was divided: the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and the commoners (Third Estate). The scheme was often expressed in the formula of those who pray (oratores), those who fight (bellatores), and those who work (laboratores). Membership was largely hereditary, and each estate had distinct legal privileges, obligations, and tax status.

France

The system is most famously associated with the French Ancien Régime. The Estates General, an assembly of representatives of the three estates, met only rarely after 1614 until Louis XVI summoned it in 1789 to address the fiscal crisis. Deadlock over whether to vote by estate or by head led the Third Estate to declare itself the National Assembly, a pivotal step in the French Revolution. The clergy and nobility enjoyed exemption from the principal direct tax, the taille, burdens that fell overwhelmingly on the Third Estate despite it comprising the vast majority of the population.

Elsewhere

Similar estate systems existed across Europe. Sweden's Riksdag of the Estates uniquely included a fourth estate of peasants alongside clergy, nobility, and burghers, lasting until 1866. In the Holy Roman Empire, the Imperial Diet included electors, princes, and imperial cities. Estate assemblies generally declined as representative parliaments based on population replaced them in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Later usage

The term "Fourth Estate" came to be applied to the press, and sometimes to other influential groups, in allusion to the original three.
