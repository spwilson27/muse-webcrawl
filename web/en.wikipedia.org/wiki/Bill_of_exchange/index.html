Bill of exchange

A bill of exchange is a written order used primarily in international trade that binds one party to pay a fixed sum of money to another party on demand or at a predetermined date. It is a negotiable instrument: the drawer orders the drawee to pay the payee; when the drawee signs it ("accepts" it), the drawee becomes liable for payment. Bills payable on demand are called sight drafts; those payable after a period are time drafts or usance bills.

History

Bills of exchange developed in medieval Italy and were refined by merchant bankers in centres such as Genoa, Venice and the Champagne fairs, allowing merchants to settle debts across distance without transporting coin. By the 17th and 18th centuries the London bill market was a cornerstone of world trade, and legal frameworks such as the British Bills of Exchange Act 1882 and the Geneva Uniform Law codified their use. A cheque is a specialised form of bill of exchange drawn on a bank and payable on demand.

Use

In trade finance, an exporter may draw a bill on the importer or its bank; a bank-accepted bill ("banker's acceptance") can be sold at a discount in the money market, giving the exporter immediate funds. If the payee endorses the bill, it transfers to a new holder. Dishonour by non-acceptance or non-payment triggers rights of recourse against prior parties.
