Henry Louis Mencken (September 12, 1880 – January 29, 1956) was an American journalist, essayist, satirist, cultural critic and scholar of American English. Known as the "Sage of Baltimore", he is regarded as one of the most influential American writers and prose stylists of the first half of the twentieth century.

Career

Mencken began his career at the Baltimore Morning Herald in 1899 and soon moved to the Baltimore Sun, with which he remained associated for most of his life. He co-edited the literary magazine The Smart Set from 1914 to 1923 and founded and edited The American Mercury from 1924 to 1933, making both influential platforms for new American writing and for his own caustic commentary.

Writings

His multi-volume study The American Language (1919, with later editions and supplements) documented how English spoken in the United States diverged from British English and remains a landmark of American lexicography. Mencken's books include A Book of Prefaces (1917), Prejudices (1919–1927), Notes on Democracy (1926) and Treatise on the Gods (1930). His coverage of the Scopes "Monkey Trial" of 1925, in which he coined the term "Bible Belt" in print, is among the most famous episodes in American journalism.

Style and views

Mencken wrote in a vigorous, slangy, epigrammatic style, lampooning Puritanism, organised religion, politicians and what he called the "boobus americanus". Many of his aphorisms remain widely quoted, including his definition of Puritanism as "the haunting fear that someone, somewhere, may be happy".

Later life

Mencken suffered a stroke in 1948 that left him unable to read or write, though he recovered some speech before his death in Baltimore in 1956.
