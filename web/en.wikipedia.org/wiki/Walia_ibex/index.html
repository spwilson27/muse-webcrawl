Walia ibex

The walia ibex (Capra walie) is a vulnerable species of ibex endemic to the mountains of northern Ethiopia. It is found principally in the Simien Mountains, at altitudes between about 2,500 and 4,500 metres (8,200–14,800 feet), where it inhabits steep cliff terrain, alpine grasslands and juniper forest edges.

Description

Adult males are larger than females and carry massive, ridged horns that curve backwards in a semicircle; both sexes are a brownish colour with a paler belly, and mature males develop a dark beard and chest. The species feeds mainly on grasses, herbs and shrubs, and often grazes alongside gelada monkeys, which provide an early-warning system against predators such as leopards and hyenas.

Conservation

Hunted heavily and threatened by habitat loss and competition with livestock, the walia ibex population fell to perhaps 150 animals in the 1960s. Protection within the Simien Mountains National Park — established in 1969 and now a UNESCO World Heritage Site — has allowed numbers to recover to several hundred individuals. The species remains classified as Vulnerable, and the walia is a national symbol of Ethiopia, appearing on banknotes and as the nickname emblem of the Ethiopian national football team.
