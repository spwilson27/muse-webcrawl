Anthropocentrism

Anthropocentrism treats human beings as the central or most significant entities in the world.

Anthropocentrism (from Greek anthropos, "human", and kentron, "centre") is the belief that human beings are the most important entity in the universe, and interprets or regards the world in terms of human values and experiences. It is a major concept in environmental ethics and philosophy, where it is often contrasted with biocentrism and ecocentrism.

Forms

In environmental philosophy, anthropocentrism usually refers to a value system in which nature is valued only instrumentally, for its usefulness to humans, and in which only humans possess intrinsic value or full moral standing. Philosophers such as William Baxter defended anthropocentric environmental policies on the grounds that goals such as clean air and water are themselves human-centred. A distinction is sometimes drawn between "strong" anthropocentrism, which recognises only felt human preferences, and "weak" or "enlightened" anthropocentrism, in which human interests are considered in an informed, long-term way that may support conservation.

Origins and criticism

Anthropocentrism is often traced to the account in Genesis of humanity's dominion over the earth, and to Aristotle's view that nature exists for the sake of human beings. The historian Lynn White Jr., in his influential 1967 essay "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis," blamed Western Christianity's anthropocentrism for environmental degradation. Critics including deep ecologists such as Arne Næss argue that anthropocentrism underlies the ecological crisis by licensing the exploitation of nature. Defenders reply that stewardship traditions within the same religions, and pragmatic human self-interest in a stable climate and functioning ecosystems, can motivate environmental protection without abandoning a human-centred frame.

Cognitive and scientific senses

The term is also used for the human tendency to interpret animal behaviour, intelligence and even artificial systems in human terms, a bias related to anthropomorphism. In cosmology, the weak anthropic principle observes that the universe's apparent fine-tuning for life is conditioned by the fact that observers must exist to observe it. Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection is often described as having displaced humanity from the centre of nature, following Copernicus's displacement of Earth from the centre of the cosmos.
