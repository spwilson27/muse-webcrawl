Bolita

Bolita (Spanish for "little ball") is a type of lottery game popular in Cuba and among Cuban exiles in Florida, especially in Tampa and Miami. It was brought to the United States by Cuban immigrants in the late 19th century and became one of the most widespread illegal gambling operations in Florida's history, closely intertwined with organised crime.

How the game works

Traditionally, one hundred small numbered balls were placed in a bag. Bettors wagered on a number from 1 to 100 (often 00–99), frequently choosing numbers by charada — a dream-and-symbol code in which images such as a horse, a butterfly or a dead man correspond to specific numbers. A single ball was thrown or drawn, and those who had picked its number won, typically at odds of around 70 or 80 to 1, giving the operator a substantial house edge.

When legal lotteries were established, bolita operators often simply pegged the winning number to the last digits of an official draw — for example, the Cuban national lottery or, later, the Florida Lottery — removing the need to run their own drawings and the risk of rigged results.

History

Bolita flourished in Tampa's Ybor City from the 1880s. In the 20th century it generated enormous revenue and corruption, funding bribery of police and politicians. Santo Trafficante Sr. and his son Santo Trafficante Jr., heads of the Tampa crime family, built much of their power on bolita. The Kefauver Committee hearings of 1950–51 exposed the game's links to organised crime and political corruption in Florida. Crackdowns and the arrival of the legal Florida Lottery in 1988 shrank bolita's market, but illegal games persist in some communities.

Cultural legacy

The game's number symbolism, the charada china, remains a living folk tradition in Cuba and the diaspora, and bolita appears in novels, films and histories of Tampa as the economic engine of its underworld.
