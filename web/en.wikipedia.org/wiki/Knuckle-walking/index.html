Knuckle-walking

Knuckle-walking is a form of quadruped walking in which the forelimbs hold the fingers in a partially flexed posture that allows body weight to press down on the ground through the knuckles. It is described in primates, and in anteaters. It is a form of locomotion used by African apes — the common chimpanzee, the bonobo, and gorillas — and it is one of the characteristics thought to have been present in the last common ancestor of humans and other apes.

Knuckle-walking in apes

Chimpanzees and gorillas knuckle-walk when travelling on the ground, supporting their weight on the dorsal surface of the middle phalanges of the fingers, which are curled under. The palms do not contact the ground. This posture lets the animals retain long, curved fingers adapted for climbing while still moving efficiently over land. Gorillas may travel several hundred metres a day in this fashion between feeding sites, and chimpanzees use it as their principal mode of terrestrial locomotion, interspersed with climbing and occasional short bouts of bipedal walking.

When knuckle-walking, the hand is held stiffly, with the wrist locked and the fingers flexed so that the knuckles — specifically the backs of the middle segments of the fingers — bear the weight. Because the fingers are kept curled, the long phalanges used to grip branches are kept clear of the ground.

Anatomy

African apes show several skeletal features associated with knuckle-walking, including a broad distal radius, dorsal ridges on the metacarpal heads that stabilise the fingers in extension, and a wrist morphology that resists hyperextension under load. Chimpanzees bear weight on the middle phalanges of digits III and IV in particular. The degree to which similar features appear in early hominin fossils has been debated, because it bears on whether the human lineage passed through a knuckle-walking phase.

Other knuckle-walkers

The giant anteater (Myrmecophaga tridactyla) walks on its knuckles to protect its enormous digging claws, tucking them under so that weight rests on the knuckles rather than the claws themselves. Ground pangolins may also walk with their foreclaws folded beneath them. These are cases of convergent evolution, since neither anteaters nor pangolins are closely related to the great apes.

Evolutionary significance

Knuckle-walking is relevant to the question of how human bipedalism evolved. One hypothesis holds that the last common ancestor of humans, chimpanzees and gorillas was a knuckle-walker, and that humans became bipedal directly from that posture; another holds that knuckle-walking evolved independently in the gorilla and chimpanzee lineages from a more generalised arboreal ancestor, and that the human lineage was never knuckle-walking. Fossil evidence from taxa such as Ardipithecus ramidus has been used to argue both positions.
