Neurospora crassa is a species of red bread mould in the phylum Ascomycota and one of the most important model organisms in genetics and molecular biology. Its name means "nerve spore," referring to the characteristic striations on its ascospores that resemble axons. Neurospora grows rapidly on simple media, is haploid for most of its life cycle, and produces bright orange asexual spores (conidia).

Research history

Neurospora was first described from bakery infestations in France in the 1840s and was developed as a genetic system by Bernard Dodge and Carl Lindegren. It became famous through George Beadle and Edward Tatum's 1941 experiments, in which X-ray-induced mutants revealed that genes act by controlling specific enzymes — the "one gene–one enzyme" hypothesis — earning them a share of the 1958 Nobel Prize. The fungus has also been central to research on circadian rhythms (the frequency clock gene), meiotic silencing, DNA methylation, and repeat-induced point mutation (RIP). Its genome of about 43 megabases and ~10,000 genes was sequenced and published in 2003.

Life cycle

The life cycle includes an asexual phase of filamentous hyphae producing conidia, and a sexual phase in which protoperithecia are fertilized by the opposite mating type, forming fruiting bodies (perithecia) whose ordered asci make tetrad analysis straightforward — a key advantage for genetics.
