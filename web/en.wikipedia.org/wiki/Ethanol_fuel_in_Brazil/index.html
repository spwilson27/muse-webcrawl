Brazil is the world's second-largest producer of ethanol fuel and long its largest exporter. Brazil and the United States together account for the great majority of world ethanol production. The Brazilian sugarcane ethanol industry is often cited as the most successful large-scale alternative-fuel program, owing to the high productivity of Brazilian sugarcane, a favorable energy balance, and five decades of government support.

History

The Brazilian government launched the National Alcohol Program, Proálcool, in 1975, following the 1973 oil crisis, to reduce dependence on imported petroleum. The program subsidized sugarcane distillation and guaranteed purchases; by the mid-1980s most new cars sold in Brazil ran on neat hydrous ethanol (E100). Falling oil prices and sugar-market shifts caused a supply crisis and decline in the 1990s.

The industry was revived after 2003 with the introduction of flex-fuel vehicles, which can run on any mixture of gasoline and ethanol. Flex-fuel cars quickly took the overwhelming share of new light-vehicle sales, letting drivers choose fuel by price — ethanol is typically economical when its pump price is below about 70% of the gasoline price, reflecting its lower energy content.

Production and sustainability

Brazilian ethanol is distilled from sugarcane grown mainly in the Center-South, especially São Paulo state. Mills cogenerate electricity from bagasse. Studies generally find sugarcane ethanol achieves large net greenhouse-gas reductions relative to gasoline, though land-use change, mechanization of harvesting (replacing manual cane cutting), and the social conditions of cane workers remain debated. Since 2019 the RenovaBio policy has issued decarbonization credits (CBIOs) to incentivize lower-carbon production.
