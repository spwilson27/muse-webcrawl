Camelidae

Camelidae
Order: Artiodactyla
Extant genera: Camelus, Lama, Vicugna
Includes: camels, llamas, alpacas, guanacos, vicuñas

Camelidae is a family of even-toed ungulates within the order Artiodactyla. Its living members are the dromedary and Bactrian camels of the Old World and the llamas, alpacas, guanacos, and vicuñas of South America. Camelids are distinguished by their padded two-toed feet, long curved necks, split upper lips, and oval red blood cells — unique among mammals.

Evolution

The family originated in North America about 45 million years ago; early camelids such as Protylopus were small forest animals. Ancestors crossed into Asia over the Bering land bridge and into South America over the Isthmus of Panama, while North American camelids — including the giant Titanotylopus and the western camel — went extinct at the end of the Pleistocene.

Characteristics

Camelids lack true hooves, walking instead on broad padded toes with toenails. They are foregut fermenters with three-chambered stomachs, able to subsist on coarse dry vegetation, and Old World camels famously tolerate long periods without water. South American camelids are prized for fine wool, especially the vicuña.
