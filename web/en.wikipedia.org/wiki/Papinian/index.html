Papinian

Aemilius Papinianus (142–212 AD), known as Papinian, was a celebrated Roman jurist and one of the most authoritative writers of Roman law. Of Syrian origin, possibly from Emesa, he rose to become praetorian prefect under the emperor Septimius Severus. His writings were so highly regarded that later law gave his opinions special weight, and he is often considered the greatest of the classical Roman jurists.

Career and death

Papinian may have studied law alongside Septimius Severus under the jurist Quintus Cervidius Scaevola. He served as imperial legal secretary (magister libellorum) and in 203 AD succeeded to the praetorian prefecture, one of the highest offices in the empire. Severus on his deathbed entrusted his sons Caracalla and Geta to Papinian's care. After Caracalla murdered Geta in 211, Papinian refused — according to tradition — to compose a public justification of the fratricide, remarking that it was easier to commit a murder than to excuse one. He was dismissed and executed in 212 during Caracalla's purge.

Works and influence

Papinian's principal works were the Quaestiones (37 books), the Responsa (19 books), and the Definitiones. Extracts make up a significant portion of Justinian's Digest. The Law of Citations of 426 AD, which ranked the five leading jurists, directed judges that where opinions were equally divided, Papinian's view should prevail. His reputation for acuity, independence, and principled reasoning endured through the medieval and early modern study of Roman law.
