Faun

The faun (Latin: faunus) is a half-human, half-goat creature of Roman mythology, associated with forests, plains and fields. Fauns derive from the god Faunus, a horned deity of nature and prophecy, and were equated by the Romans with the Greek satyrs and the god Pan, though the Roman faun was generally gentler in character than the lustful Greek satyr.

Characteristics

Fauns are typically depicted with the body of a man but the legs, hooves, tail and sometimes horns and ears of a goat. They were rustic music-makers — the panpipe (syrinx) is their characteristic instrument — and companions of nymphs and of the wine god Liber (Dionysus). In later European art and literature, from Renaissance painting to Stéphane Mallarmé's poem L'Après-midi d'un faune (and Debussy's orchestral prelude on it), the faun became a symbol of untamed nature and sensual reverie. Fauns also appear in modern fantasy, notably Mr. Tumnus in C. S. Lewis's The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe.
