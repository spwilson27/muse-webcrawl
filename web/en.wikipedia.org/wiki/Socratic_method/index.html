Socratic method

The Socratic method (also known as maieutics) is a form of argumentative dialogue between individuals, based on asking and answering questions to stimulate critical thinking and to draw out ideas and underlying presuppositions. It is named after the classical Greek philosopher Socrates, as portrayed in Plato's dialogues, and is a method of hypothesis elimination: better hypotheses are found by steadily identifying and eliminating those that lead to contradictions.

The elenchus

The characteristic technique of the early Platonic dialogues is the elenchus (Greek for "cross-examination" or "refutation"). Socrates asks an interlocutor for a definition of a virtue — courage, piety, justice — and then, through a series of questions, shows that the proposed definition contradicts other beliefs the interlocutor accepts. The dialogue typically ends in aporia, a state of puzzlement, which Socrates presents as progress: the interlocutor now knows that they do not know.

Maieutics

In Plato's Theaetetus, Socrates compares his role to that of a midwife (Greek maia): he does not himself give birth to wisdom but helps others bring their ideas to birth and tests whether those ideas are sound or mere "wind-eggs." This metaphor gives the method its alternative name, maieutics.

Socratic irony

Socrates characteristically professes ignorance — "I know that I know nothing" — and presents himself as a learner while questioning reputed experts. This stance, known as Socratic irony, exposes the pretensions of those who claim knowledge they cannot defend.

Modern uses

The Socratic method is widely used in law school teaching, where professors question students about cases to expose the reasoning behind legal rules. Variants are used in psychotherapy and cognitive behavioural therapy ("Socratic questioning"), in science education, and in structured formats such as Socratic seminars and Harkness discussions in schools.

Criticism

Critics have observed that the method can shade into humiliation of the interlocutor, and that Socrates' questions often steer answers in a predetermined direction, making the dialogues less open-ended than they appear. Some scholars also debate how faithfully Plato's portrayal reflects the historical Socrates.
