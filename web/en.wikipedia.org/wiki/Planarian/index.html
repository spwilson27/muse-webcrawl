Planarian

Planarians are free-living flatworms of the class Turbellaria, traditionally grouped in the order Tricladida, found in freshwater, marine, and moist terrestrial habitats worldwide. They are best known for their extraordinary powers of regeneration: a planarian cut into pieces can regrow a complete animal from each fragment.

Anatomy

Planarians have soft, unsegmented, bilaterally symmetrical bodies, typically a few millimetres to a few centimetres long. The head bears two eyespots (ocelli) that detect light intensity but form no image, and chemosensory "ears" (auricles). The gut has three main branches — the origin of the name Tricladida — and a single opening: the animal feeds through a muscular, eversible pharynx protruding from a mid-body mouth, and expels waste through the same opening. Planarians are hermaphrodites, though many species also reproduce asexually by fission, tearing themselves in two behind the pharynx.

Regeneration

Regeneration is driven by neoblasts, the only dividing somatic cells in the adult, which make up roughly a fifth to a third of all the animal's cells and can produce every cell type. Even a fragment containing a few thousand cells can rebuild a complete worm, restoring head, brain, and polarity in about two weeks. Thomas Hunt Morgan studied planarian regeneration in the 1890s and 1900s, and species such as Schmidtea mediterranea and Dugesia japonica remain important model organisms. Studies have shown that positional information is encoded by signalling pathways such as Wnt, and that planarians can retain learned behaviour through regeneration of the head, suggesting memory is stored outside the brain as well.

Ecology

Freshwater planarians glide over stones and vegetation on a bed of mucus, propelled by cilia, and prey on small invertebrates and carrion. They avoid light and are widely used in school and university laboratories as classic examples of regeneration, simple nervous systems, and stem-cell biology.
