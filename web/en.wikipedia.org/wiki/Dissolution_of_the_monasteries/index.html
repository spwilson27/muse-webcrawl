The dissolution of the monasteries was the process between 1536 and 1541 by which Henry VIII disbanded Catholic monasteries, priories, convents, and friaries in England, Wales, and Ireland, confiscating their income, lands, and treasures. Administered by Thomas Cromwell and the Court of Augmentations, it was the largest transfer of property in England since the Norman Conquest, roughly doubling the crown's revenue and reshaping English landownership, society, and landscape. It formed a central strand of the English Reformation.

Background

Following the break with Rome, the crown surveyed church wealth in the Valor Ecclesiasticus (1535), a detailed valuation of every benefice and monastery, and sent commissioners to report on monastic discipline; their reports emphasised corruption and laxity, though their evidence was selective and often exaggerated. Monasticism itself had critics within the church, and smaller houses had occasionally been suppressed before — Cardinal Wolsey closed some thirty houses in the 1520s to fund colleges — but nothing on the coming scale had been attempted.

The suppressions

Parliament's first Suppression Act (1536) dissolved houses with incomes under £200 a year — some 300 institutions — on grounds of alleged vice. The closures provoked the Pilgrimage of Grace (1536), a great northern rising in defence of the monasteries and the old religion, whose leaders Henry pardoned and then executed after a second scare. A second Act in 1539 authorised the surrender of the greater houses, and the remaining abbeys fell one by one: Glastonbury, whose abbot Richard Whiting was hanged in 1539; Walsingham; Reading; and finally Waltham Abbey in March 1540, the last to surrender. In all, around 800 houses were suppressed and some 8,000–10,000 monks, nuns, and friars displaced, most receiving pensions. Henry himself was excommunicated in part over the policy, and relics and shrines — above all the shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury — were destroyed and their gold taken for the crown.

Disposal of the lands

The Court of Augmentations managed the ex-monastic estates, initially worth on some estimates as much as the crown's entire ordinary revenue. Within a decade the crown had sold or granted away the majority, largely to courtiers, gentry, and speculators, to finance Henry's wars against France and Scotland. Many abbeys were stripped of lead and stone and fell into the picturesque ruins that survive today; others became great houses (as at Lacock and Mottisfont) or parish churches purchased by their congregations. A few were refounded as cathedrals of new dioceses, such as Gloucester and Peterborough. Monastic libraries were dispersed and countless manuscripts destroyed — the antiquary John Bale lamented the loss — while the disappearance of monastic hospitality, schooling, and poor relief left gaps that parishes and the state only slowly filled.

Consequences

By binding hundreds of landowning families to the Reformation settlement through their purchases, the dissolution made a Catholic restoration politically perilous: Mary I, though she reconciled England with Rome, could not compel the return of monastic lands. The process remains debated — older Protestant narratives of corrupt monasteries have been balanced by scholarship stressing the vitality of late medieval religion and the crown's fiscal motives — but its scale as a social and economic revolution is undisputed.
