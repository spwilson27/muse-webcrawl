Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions

The Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions were political statements drafted in 1798 and 1799 in which the Kentucky and Virginia legislatures took the position that the federal Alien and Sedition Acts were unconstitutional. The resolutions argued that the Constitution was a compact among the states, that the federal government had only the powers delegated to it, and that the states had the right to judge the constitutionality of federal laws.

Authorship and content

The Kentucky Resolutions were secretly authored by Vice President Thomas Jefferson, and the Virginia Resolutions by James Madison. The Virginia Resolutions (December 1798) asserted that states are "duty bound to interpose" against federal overreach, giving rise to the doctrine of interposition. The Kentucky Resolutions of 1798 and especially 1799 went further: the 1799 Kentucky Resolutions declared that "nullification" of unconstitutional federal laws was the "rightful remedy" of the states — the first articulation of the doctrine of nullification.

Reaction and legacy

No other state legislature endorsed the resolutions, and several formally rejected them, arguing that constitutional interpretation belonged to the federal courts. Nevertheless, the resolutions became foundational texts for the states' rights tradition in American constitutional thought. They were invoked by John C. Calhoun and South Carolina during the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33, and the compact theory they expressed influenced arguments leading up to the American Civil War. Madison, later in life, insisted that the resolutions had never sanctioned nullification or secession by a single state.
