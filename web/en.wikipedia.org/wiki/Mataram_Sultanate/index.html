Mataram Sultanate

The Mataram Sultanate (Javanese: ꦏꦱꦸꦭ꧀ꦠꦤꦤ꧀ꦩꦠꦫꦩ꧀, Kasultanan Mataram) was a Javanese Islamic state centred in south-central Java, Indonesia, between Yogyakarta and Surakarta. Founded in the late 16th century, it rose to become the dominant power in Java, and at its height under Sultan Agung (r. 1613–1645) controlled most of the island. In 1755 the sultanate was partitioned by the Treaty of Giyanti into the Surakarta Sunanate and the Yogyakarta Sultanate, ending Mataram as a unified state.

Origins

Mataram began as a small domain granted to Ki Ageng Pamanahan by the Sultan of Pajang in the 1570s. His son, Panembahan Senopati (r. c. 1586–1601), broke free of Pajang's authority, made Kota Gede his capital, and expanded at the expense of neighbouring powers, founding the dynasty that Javanese tradition links to the earlier Hindu-Buddhist Mataram Kingdom of the 8th–10th centuries.

Sultan Agung

Senopati's grandson, Sultan Agung Adi Prabu Anyakrakusuma, brought Mataram to its zenith. Through campaigns in 1613–1645 he conquered Surabaya and the principalities of eastern Java, Madura, and much of the north coast (Pasisir), and subdued Sukadana in Borneo. He twice besieged the Dutch East India Company fortress of Batavia (1628 and 1629) but failed to expel the Dutch. Agung reformed the calendar (creating the Anno Javanico), patronised a syncretic court culture blending Islam with older Javanese traditions, and moved the capital to Karta and then Plered. His royal cemetery at Imogiri remains a pilgrimage site.

Decline and partition

After Agung's death, succession disputes and rebellions — notably the Trunajaya rebellion of the 1670s — drew the sultanate into dependence on the Dutch East India Company (VOC), to which it ceded territory and trade rights in successive treaties. Court intrigue and the Javanese War of Succession weakened the state further; Chinese and Javanese revolts in the 1740s forced the capital's move to Surakarta. Exhausted by the Third Javanese War of Succession, the sultanate was divided by the Treaty of Giyanti (13 February 1755) between Pakubuwono III, who kept Surakarta, and his uncle Mangkubumi, who became Sultan Hamengkubuwono I of Yogyakarta. The Salatiga agreement of 1757 carved out the further principality of Mangkunegaran.

Legacy

The modern royal houses of Surakarta and Yogyakarta, still recognised within the Republic of Indonesia (with Yogyakarta a special region), descend directly from Mataram, and its court culture — gamelan, wayang, batik, and Javanese etiquette — shaped the identity of central Java.
