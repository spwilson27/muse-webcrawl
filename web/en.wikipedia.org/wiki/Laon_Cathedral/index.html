Laon Cathedral (French: Cathédrale Notre-Dame de Laon) is a Roman Catholic church located in Laon, in the Aisne department of northern France. Built in the second half of the twelfth century, it is one of the most important and stylistically unified examples of Early Gothic architecture, notable for its five completed towers (of seven planned), its four-storey nave elevation, and the celebrated life-size oxen that appear to peer from its west towers.

History

The present cathedral replaced a Romanesque predecessor that was burned during the communal revolt of Easter 1112, in which Bishop Gaudry was murdered. Construction of the Gothic church began around 1155–1160 under Bishop Gautier de Mortagne, starting with the choir; the nave and the great west front followed, and the building was substantially complete by about 1235. The flat eastern chevet — unusual for a French Gothic cathedral — replaced an original rounded apse. The cathedral ceased to be a bishop's seat after the Revolution, when the diocese of Laon was suppressed, but remains a parish church.

Architecture

Laon was a laboratory of early Gothic design. The nave elevation has four levels — arcade, gallery, triforium and clerestory — an arrangement it shares with Noyon and that was later simplified at Chartres and Reims. The deep, openwork west front, with its three great portals, gablets and rose window, set a pattern copied widely. Of the seven planned towers (two on the west front, two on each transept, and a crossing lantern), five were built; the lantern tower lights the crossing in a manner reminiscent of Norman practice. The sixteen oxen carved on the west towers commemorate, by legend, the ox that miraculously appeared to haul stone up Laon's steep hill, and symbolise the beasts that genuinely carried the building stone.

The cathedral also preserves important medieval stained glass, including its three lancets and rose of the east end, and the sketchbook of the thirteenth-century master mason Villard de Honnecourt contains drawings of Laon's towers and windows, evidence of the building's fame among contemporaries.
