Sand mandala

A sand mandala (Tibetan: རྡུལ་ཚོན.dkyil.'khor, dul tson kyil khor, "mandala of coloured powder") is a Tibetan Buddhist tradition involving the creation and ritual destruction of a mandala made from coloured sand. The intricate geometric diagram represents the palace of a deity and, symbolically, the structure of the universe and the enlightened mind.

Creation

After an opening ceremony, monks lay out the mandala's geometry with rulers and compasses, then apply millions of grains of coloured sand using narrow metal funnels called chak-pur, which are vibrated with a rod to release a controlled stream of sand. Traditional pigments come from crushed stone, including yellow ochre, red coral, turquoise, and lapis lazuli. A large mandala may take a team of monks several weeks to complete, working from the centre outward.

Destruction

When finished, the mandala is blessed, exhibited briefly, and then deliberately dismantled in a closing ceremony: the sand is swept up, placed in an urn, and carried in procession to a body of water, where it is poured out. The ritual teaches the central Buddhist doctrine of impermanence (anicca), and the dispersed sand is believed to bless the environment and all beings. Public creations by visiting Tibetan monks, including those associated with the Dalai Lama and Namgyal Monastery, have become well-known exhibitions in museums worldwide.
