Cohen v. California

Cohen v. California, 403 U.S. 15 (1971), is a landmark free-speech decision of the U.S. Supreme Court.

Cohen v. California, 403 U.S. 15 (1971), was a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court holding that the First Amendment prevents a state from making the public display of a profane word a criminal offence, because the display was political speech and the word itself was not obscene, fighting words, or directed at inciting violence.

Background

In April 1968, 19-year-old Paul Robert Cohen wore a jacket bearing the words "Fuck the Draft" in a Los Angeles County courthouse corridor to protest the Vietnam War draft. He was convicted under a California statute prohibiting "offensive conduct" and sentenced to 30 days in jail; the conviction was upheld on appeal within California.

Decision

Writing for a 5–4 majority, Justice John Marshall Harlan II reversed the conviction. The Court held that the expletive was not obscene, was not addressed to any specific person as "fighting words", and that the state could not suppress it merely to protect unwilling viewers who could avert their eyes. Harlan's opinion famously observed that "one man's vulgarity is another's lyric", and stressed that the emotive content of speech is itself protected because words convey not only ideas but feelings.

Significance

The case remains a foundational authority for the protection of profane and offensive political speech, frequently cited in later disputes over protest messages, clothing, and online expression.
