Broch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type: Drystone round tower
Region: Northern and western Scotland
Period: Iron Age, c. 400 BC – AD 200

In archaeology, a broch is an Iron Age drystone hollow-walled structure found in Scotland, a type of complex Atlantic roundhouse. Brochs were built primarily in the far north and west—Caithness, Sutherland, Orkney, Shetland and the Western Isles—with some examples in the Scottish Borders and Dumfries and Galloway.

Brochs are characterized by their massive circular double-skinned walls, which enclose galleries and stairs rising within the thickness of the wall, and a single low entrance passage. The tallest still standing, the Broch of Mousa on Shetland, survives to a height of about 13 metres.

Distribution and dating

More than 570 broch sites have been identified across Scotland. Radiocarbon dating suggests most were constructed between roughly 400 BC and AD 200, during the middle Iron Age, though the tradition of Atlantic roundhouse building is older.

Function

The purpose of brochs has long been debated. Early antiquarians regarded them as defensive refuges; later interpretations emphasized their role as high-status farmsteads and symbols of power and prestige for local elites. Most archaeologists now see them as primarily domestic—impressive defended homesteads of prosperous farming families—rather than purely military structures.

Legacy

The broch tradition influenced later Norse and medieval building in the Northern Isles, and brochs remain iconic monuments of Scotland's Iron Age, forming part of the proposed "Heart of Neolithic Orkney" adjoining World Heritage landscapes and several scheduled monuments in Shetland.
