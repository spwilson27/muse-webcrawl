High-pressure area

A high-pressure area, high, or anticyclone is an area near the surface of a planet where the atmospheric pressure at sea level is greater than the surrounding environment. High-pressure systems are associated with descending air, light winds, and generally fair weather; they rotate clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, the opposite of cyclones, because of the Coriolis effect.

A high-pressure system (marked H) on a surface weather map

Formation and behaviour

High-pressure areas form where air aloft converges and sinks toward the surface, warming as it descends and drying, which suppresses cloud formation and precipitation. At the surface the air diverges outward, and the Coriolis effect deflects the outflow, producing anticyclonic rotation. Highs may be cold-core systems, like the Siberian High that builds over the cold Eurasian landmass in winter, or warm-core systems such as the subtropical highs that sit over the oceans in the descending branches of the Hadley cells.

Weather associated with highs

Weather under a high is typically settled and dry, with clear skies and light winds. In summer this can bring heat waves and drought; in winter, clear nights allow strong radiational cooling, producing frost, fog, and temperature inversions that trap pollution. Slow-moving "blocking" highs can stall the normal progression of weather systems for days or weeks, with significant consequences for agriculture and water supplies.

Examples

Permanent or semi-permanent high-pressure regions include the Azores High and Pacific High over the subtropical oceans, the Siberian High over northern Asia in winter, and the polar highs over the Arctic and Antarctic. The highest barometric pressure ever recorded at a station, adjusted to sea level, was 1083.8 hPa at Agata in Siberia in December 1968, beneath an intense Siberian High.
