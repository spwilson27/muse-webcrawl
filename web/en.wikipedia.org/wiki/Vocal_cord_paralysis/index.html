Vocal cord paralysis


Endoscopic view of the vocal cords

Vocal cord paralysis is a condition in which the nerves controlling the vocal cords (vocal folds) are damaged so that one or both folds cannot move normally. Because the vocal cords control voice production and protect the airway during swallowing, paralysis can cause a weak, breathy voice, hoarseness, difficulty swallowing and, in bilateral cases, airway obstruction.

Causes

The recurrent laryngeal nerve, a branch of the vagus nerve, supplies nearly all the intrinsic muscles of the larynx. Paralysis may follow thyroid or neck surgery, tumours of the neck, chest or skull base, intubation injury, viral illness, or neurological disease; in many cases no cause is identified.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by laryngoscopy, which shows the immobile fold, often supplemented by laryngeal electromyography and imaging along the course of the vagus and recurrent laryngeal nerves to exclude a structural lesion.

Treatment

Management includes voice therapy, injection laryngoplasty to medialise the paralysed fold, thyroplasty, and reinnervation surgery. Bilateral paralysis may require tracheotomy or procedures to widen the airway.
