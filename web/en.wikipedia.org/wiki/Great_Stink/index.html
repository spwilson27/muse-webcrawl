Great Stink
DateJune – August 1858
LocationRiver Thames, London
CauseUntreated human waste and industrial effluent discharged into the Thames, worsened by hot weather
ConsequenceParliament funded Joseph Bazalgette's London sewerage system

The Great Stink was an event in central London during July and August 1858 in which the hot weather exacerbated the smell of untreated human waste and industrial effluent that was present in and around the River Thames. The problem had been mounting for some years, with an ageing and inadequate sewer system that emptied directly into the Thames. The miasma from the effluent was thought to transmit contagious diseases such as cholera, and three outbreaks of the disease in London in the 1850s killed tens of thousands.

The crisis of 1858

In the hot summer of 1858 the Thames became so foul that the sittings of Parliament at Westminster were disrupted — curtains soaked in chloride of lime were hung at the windows — and there were serious proposals to move the courts and government out of London. Michael Faraday's famous 1855 letter to The Times, describing the river as an opaque brown fluid, had already dramatised the problem. Under the pressure of the stink, Parliament quickly passed legislation empowering the Metropolitan Board of Works to build a comprehensive main-drainage system for London.

Bazalgette's sewers

The Board's chief engineer, Joseph Bazalgette, designed a network of intercepting sewers — eventually over 130 km (82 miles) of main sewers and some 1,770 km of street sewers — that carried London's waste eastwards to outfalls at Beckton and Crossness, discharging into the Thames at ebb tide far downstream of the city centre. Construction ran from 1859 to 1875, accompanied by the building of the Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments. The system, much of it still in use, is credited with dramatically reducing cholera and typhoid in London; the last cholera outbreak (1866) was confined largely to an area not yet connected to the new sewers.
