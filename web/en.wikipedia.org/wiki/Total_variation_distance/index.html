Total variation distance of probability measures

In probability theory, the total variation distance between two probability measures P and Q on a measurable space is the largest possible difference between the probabilities they assign to the same event: δ(P, Q) = supA |P(A) − Q(A)|.

Equivalent forms

For discrete distributions with masses p and q, the distance equals half the L1 distance: δ = ½ Σ |p(x) − q(x)|. For distributions with densities it is half the integral of the absolute difference of densities. It also equals the minimum, over all couplings of P and Q, of the probability that the coupled variables differ — the coupling characterisation that makes it central to Markov chain mixing-time theory.

Properties and uses

Total variation is a metric bounded between 0 and 1; it is the strongest common notion of convergence for distributions — convergence in total variation implies convergence in distribution and convergence of probabilities of every event. It is used to define mixing times (via the mixing-time threshold at distance ¼), in statistics for bounding testing error, and in information theory where it relates to the optimal success probability for distinguishing two distributions; Pinsker's inequality bounds it by the square root of half the Kullback–Leibler divergence.
