The Grand Junction Canal is a canal in England, running from the River Thames at Brentford, west London, to Braunston in Northamptonshire, where it joined the Oxford Canal. Authorised in 1793 and opened throughout in 1805, it provided the first direct inland waterway link between London and the Midlands industrial districts, and formed the principal section of the waterway created in 1929 as the Grand Union Canal, of which it remains the busiest and best-known part.

Construction

Promoted to shorten the long Oxford Canal route from London to Birmingham, the canal was surveyed under William Jessop with James Barnes as resident engineer. Construction began in 1793 from both ends. The southern section from Brentford to Uxbridge opened in 1794 and the line reached the great summit works by 1800, but completion of the whole route was delayed by the two great tunnels at Braunston and Blisworth. At Blisworth (2,813 yards), quicksand and faulty work forced the abandonment of an earlier bore; boats were hauled over the hill by a temporary tramroad until the tunnel finally opened on 25 March 1805, completing the through route.

Route and branches

Leaving the Thames at Brentford, the canal climbs the Brent valley through Hanwell and the Hanwell flight of locks, passes through Uxbridge, Rickmansworth, Watford, Hemel Hempstead and Berkhamsted, and crosses the Chilterns at Tring summit. It descends past Leighton Buzzard, crosses the Ouse valley on the great iron aqueduct at Cosgrove (the "Iron Trunk"), passes Stoke Bruerne and Blisworth Tunnel, and descends the Braunston flight to Braunston. Early branches included the Paddington Arm (opened 1801, giving access to the London docks basin at Paddington, later linked to the Regent's Canal), and arms to Buckingham, Aylesbury, Wendover, Northampton and Slough.

Trade and decline

The Grand Junction was among the most successful canals of the late canal age, paying strong dividends on coal, building materials, grain and manufactured goods traffic. From 1894 its carrying was largely in the hands of Fellows Morton & Clayton. Railway competition eroded profits from the 1840s, but the canal remained a significant freight artery into the twentieth century; in 1929 its owners merged it with the Leicester and Warwickshire lines to form the Grand Union Canal Company, which promoted a modernisation scheme (the "English Channel-to-Midlands" improvement) in the 1930s. Commercial carrying ended in the 1960s–70s; today the canal is heavily used for leisure boating, and Braunston and Stoke Bruerne — home of the Canal Museum — are centres of canal heritage.
