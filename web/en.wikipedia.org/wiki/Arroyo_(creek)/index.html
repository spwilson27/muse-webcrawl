Arroyo (creek)

An arroyo (from Spanish arroyo, "brook") is a dry creek, stream bed or gulch that temporarily or seasonally fills and flows after sufficient rain. Arroyos are characteristic of arid and semi-arid regions, especially the southwestern United States, Mexico and other Spanish-speaking drylands, and are equivalent to the wadi of North Africa and Arabia and the nullah of India.


A dry arroyo in the American Southwest

Formation

Arroyos form where sparse vegetation lets intense, infrequent rainfall run off rapidly, cutting steep-sided, flat-floored channels into alluvium. Many arroyos in the American Southwest incised dramatically in the late 19th and early 20th centuries — a phase of "arroyo cutting" attributed to a combination of climate shifts, grazing pressure and other land-use changes, which lowered water tables and stranded former floodplain farmland above the new channel.

Flash flooding

Because a storm some distance away can send a wall of water down an otherwise dry channel, arroyos are notorious flash-flood hazards. Hikers and campers are advised never to camp in dry washes, and roads crossing arroyos are often built as dips rather than bridges, with warning signs about flooding.

Ecology

Despite being dry most of the time, arroyos concentrate moisture and support ribbons of vegetation — cottonwoods, mesquite and other phreatophytes — that serve as important wildlife corridors in desert landscapes.
