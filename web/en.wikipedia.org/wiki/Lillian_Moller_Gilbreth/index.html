Lillian Evelyn Moller Gilbreth (May 24, 1878 – January 2, 1972) was an American psychologist, engineer, consultant, and educator who was an early pioneer in applying psychology to time-and-motion studies. Her work with her husband, Frank Bunker Gilbreth, in the fields of industrial engineering and management laid the groundwork for modern ergonomics and human factors. She was described in the 1940s as "a genius in the art of living", and was the first woman elected to the National Academy of Engineering.

Early life and education

Born in Oakland, California, Gilbreth earned bachelor's and master's degrees from the University of California, Berkeley, and completed a doctorate in psychology at Berkeley in 1911 — though the degree was initially withheld because she had not met residency requirements — receiving her PhD from Brown University in 1915. She married Frank Gilbreth in 1904, and together they raised twelve children.

Career

The Gilbreths' firm pioneered motion study, using film to analyze workers' movements and eliminate wasted effort (their elementary motions were called "therbligs"). Lillian brought a psychologist's attention to fatigue, worker satisfaction and the human element in management, publishing The Psychology of Management in 1914. After Frank's death in 1924, she continued the consultancy alone, advising companies and government, and applied motion study to the home — designing the practical kitchen with its continuous countertops and the foot-pedal trash can — and to work for people with disabilities.

She taught at Purdue University, where she became the first female professor of engineering there, and at other institutions. Her life with her husband and children was chronicled by two of their children in the bestselling memoir Cheaper by the Dozen (1948), later adapted into films. In 1966 she received the Hoover Medal, and she appeared on a U.S. postage stamp in 1984.
