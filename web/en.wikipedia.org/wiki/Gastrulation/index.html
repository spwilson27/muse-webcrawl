Gastrulation

Gastrulation is the stage in early embryonic development of most animals during which the blastula (or blastocyst) is reorganised into a multilayered structure known as the gastrula. During gastrulation the three germ layers — ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm — are formed, establishing the basic body plan of the embryo. Developmental biologist Lewis Wolpert famously remarked that "it is not birth, marriage, or death, but gastrulation which is truly the most important time in your life."

Gastrulation converts a hollow ball of cells into a layered embryo with a primitive gut.

Mechanism

Gastrulation involves dramatic, coordinated cell movements: invagination (inward folding), involution, ingression (individual cells migrating inward), delamination and epiboly (spreading of cell sheets). The precise movements vary among animal groups. In sea urchins, cells at the vegetal pole invaginate to form the archenteron, the primitive gut. In amphibians, cells move inward through the blastopore, whose dorsal lip acts as the "organiser" — the region shown by Spemann and Mangold to induce a secondary body axis.

In amniotes and humans

In birds, reptiles and mammals, gastrulation proceeds through the primitive streak, a groove in the epiblast through which cells ingress to form the endoderm and mesoderm, leaving the remaining epiblast as ectoderm. The primitive node at the streak's anterior end organises the axis. In humans, gastrulation begins around day 14–15 after fertilisation; the appearance of the primitive streak is the basis of the widely adopted "14-day rule" limiting embryo research.

Germ layer derivatives

  • Ectoderm: nervous system, epidermis, sensory organs.
  • Mesoderm: muscle, bone, blood, heart, kidneys, gonads.
  • Endoderm: lining of the gut and respiratory tract, liver, pancreas.
