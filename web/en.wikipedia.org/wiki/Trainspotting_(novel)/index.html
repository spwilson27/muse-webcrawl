Trainspotting is the first novel by Scottish writer Irvine Welsh, first published in 1993. It takes the form of a series of loosely connected short episodes, told in alternating first- and third-person voices, following a group of heroin addicts in an economically depressed area of Edinburgh, centred on Leith, in the late 1980s. Much of the dialogue is written in the working-class Scots dialect of Edinburgh, which initially posed a challenge for readers but became one of the book's defining features.

Plot summary

The novel's principal narrator is Mark Renton ("Rent Boy"), an intelligent but disaffected young man who cycles in and out of heroin addiction. Around him are his friends: the cheerful and hapless Spud, the handsome but increasingly predatory Sick Boy, the violent alcoholic psychopath Francis Begbie — who pointedly does not use heroin — and the clean-cut athlete Tommy, whose descent into addiction and death from HIV-related illness forms one of the book's darkest arcs.

The episodes are episodic rather than a continuous plot: botched attempts to quit using opium suppositories, petty theft to fund habits, encounters with dealers such as Mother Superior, and trips to London. The novel ends with Renton betraying his friends by absconding with the proceeds of a heroin deal in London, resolving to start a new life in Amsterdam — though the narration undercuts any simple redemption.

Style and themes

Welsh writes extensively in phonetic Scots dialect for dialogue, switching to standard English for some narration. The effect places the reader inside the characters' world and resists the voice of an outside observer. Major themes include addiction and the monotony of life on the dole in Thatcher-era Scotland, friendship and betrayal, masculinity, and the attractions and costs of refusing mainstream life. Renton's famous "Choose life" speech — a rant against consumer respectability — opens the later film adaptation and has become a widely quoted cultural reference.

Reception and legacy

On publication the novel divided critics: some praised its energy and authenticity, while others found its subject matter sordid. It was reportedly passed over for the Booker Prize shortlist after offending judges. It became a bestseller and a defining novel of 1990s British fiction.

The book was adapted into a highly successful 1996 film directed by Danny Boyle, starring Ewan McGregor as Renton, which brought the story to a mass audience. Welsh published a prequel, Skagboys (2012), and a sequel, The Blade Artist (2016), returning to the characters; the earlier novel Porno (2002) also continued their story and was partly adapted as T2 Trainspotting (2017).
