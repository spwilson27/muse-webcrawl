Pell's equation, also called the Pell–Fermat equation, is any Diophantine equation of the form

x2 − n·y2 = 1,

where n is a given positive nonsquare integer, and integer solutions are sought for x and y. In Cartesian coordinates, the equation is represented by a hyperbola, and solutions correspond to integer points on the curve. The theory of the equation is closely connected with continued fractions and the arithmetic of quadratic number fields: all solutions are generated from a single fundamental solution, the solution with smallest positive values.

The equation is named after the seventeenth-century English mathematician John Pell, although Pell's own involvement was minimal. Leonhard Euler appears to have attached Pell's name to the equation, apparently confusing the work of William, Lord Brouncker — who developed an early solution method — with Pell's. The problem had in fact been studied extensively in India many centuries earlier.

History

The earliest work on the equation appeared in India. Brahmagupta, in the seventh century CE, developed a composition method (bhāvanā) for combining solutions and found solutions for several values of n. In the twelfth century, Bhāskara II gave the cakravāla ("cyclic") algorithm, which solves the equation in general; applied to n = 61 it produces the celebrated minimal solution x = 1,766,319,049 and y = 226,153,980.

The equation re-entered European mathematics in 1657, when Pierre de Fermat issued a challenge to English mathematicians, asking in particular for solutions when n = 61 and n = 109. Brouncker and John Wallis responded with recursive methods, published in Wallis's Commercium Epistolicum (1658) and later in his treatise on algebra. In 1766–1769 Joseph-Louis Lagrange supplied the first complete rigorous proof that a solution always exists for every nonsquare n, using the theory of continued fractions.

Fundamental solution

The trivial solution x = 1, y = 0 always exists. The theory guarantees that for every positive nonsquare n there is exactly one solution with x > 0 and y > 0 of smallest size, called the fundamental solution. Every other solution in positive integers is generated from it: if (x1, y1) is the fundamental solution, then all solutions are given by

xk + yk√n = (x1 + y1√n)k,   k = 1, 2, 3, …

The fundamental solution is found from the continued fraction expansion of √n: it appears among the convergents of the expansion, at the end of the first period if the period length is even, and at the end of the second period if the period length is odd. The size of the fundamental solution varies wildly with n: it is small for n = 2 but astronomically large for some values below 100, such as n = 61.

Examples

For n = 2, the fundamental solution is (x, y) = (3, 2), since 9 − 8 = 1 and no smaller pair with y > 0 works. The solutions of x2 − 2y2 = 1 in positive integers continue (17, 12), (99, 70), (577, 408), generated by successive powers of the fundamental unit 3 + 2√2 (itself the square of 1 + √2, which solves the negative equation x2 − 2y2 = −1).

For n = 3, the fundamental solution is (x, y) = (2, 1), since 4 − 3 = 1, generating (7, 4), (26, 15), and so on. For n = 61, as Bhāskara II found, the fundamental solution is the enormous pair x = 1,766,319,049, y = 226,153,980.

Recurrence and algebraic structure

Once the fundamental solution (x1, y1) is known, all solutions can be generated recursively by xk+1 = x1xk + n·y1yk and yk+1 = x1yk + y1xk. The quantities x + y√n range over the elements of norm 1 in the ring Z[√n], which explains this multiplicative structure; the fundamental solution corresponds to the fundamental unit of the real quadratic field Q(√n) whenever that unit has norm 1.

Generalised Pell equation

The equation x2 − ny2 = N, for a fixed nonzero integer N, is called the generalised (or general) Pell equation. Its solutions, when they exist, fall into finitely many families, each generated from a seed solution by multiplication with powers of the fundamental unit. The negative Pell equation, x2 − ny2 = −1, is solvable exactly when the period of the continued fraction of √n has odd length; a solution of the negative equation, squared, yields the fundamental solution of the positive equation.

Applications

Pell's equation underlies Archimedes' cattle problem, whose smallest solution involves numbers with more than two hundred thousand digits. It also appears in rational approximation: the convergents of √n yield best-possible approximations x/y to √n and satisfy Pell-type equations. Through its connection with units in quadratic fields, the equation is intertwined with algebraic number theory, the study of class numbers, and the theory of binary quadratic forms.
