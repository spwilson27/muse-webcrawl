United States House of Representatives

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United States House of Representatives is the lower chamber of the United States Congress, with the Senate as the upper chamber. Together they compose the national legislature of the United States. The House is composed of representatives who sit in congressional districts allocated to each state on the basis of population as measured by the census; the number of voting representatives is fixed by law at 435, each serving a two-year term, plus non-voting members representing the District of Columbia and the territories.

Powers and procedure

The House holds exclusive powers to originate revenue bills, to impeach federal officials (the Senate tries the case), and to elect the President if no candidate wins an Electoral College majority. Its size dictates majoritarian machinery: the Speaker (the chamber's presiding and party leader), a powerful Rules Committee controlling floor access, strict debate limits and germaneness rules — the opposite pole from the Senate's individualism. Two-year terms and district electorates make the House the chamber most sensitive to electoral tides (midterm swings), while single-member plurality districts and redistricting politics (gerrymandering) shape its representativeness. Apportionment after each census reallocates seats among the states; the permanent 435 cap dates from 1929, a standing debate for representation scholars.
