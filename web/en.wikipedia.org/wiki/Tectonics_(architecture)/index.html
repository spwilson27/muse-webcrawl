Tectonics (architecture)

In architecture, tectonics refers to the theory and poetics of construction: how a building's structure, materials, and joints are expressed and given artistic form. Derived from the Greek tektōn (builder, carpenter), the concept was developed in 19th-century German architectural theory — notably by Karl Bötticher and Gottfried Semper — and revived in the late 20th century by historians such as Kenneth Frampton, who argued that the expressive articulation of construction is central to architectural culture as a counterweight to purely visual ("scenographic") design.

Expressed joinery and structure are central concerns of tectonic theory.

Development of the concept

Karl Bötticher, in Die Tektonik der Hellenen (1844–52), analysed Greek temples by distinguishing the structural core-form (Kernform) from its artistic dressing (Kunstform), arguing that ornament should symbolically express the structural action of each member. Gottfried Semper's The Four Elements of Architecture (1851) grounded architecture in craft techniques — the hearth, earthwork/stereotomy, the tectonic frame, and the woven enclosure — famously illustrated by the "primitive hut". For Semper, tectonics specifically denoted the art of assembling rigid, linear members into a frame, contrasted with stereotomy, the shaping of heavy, compressive masses such as masonry and earth.

Frampton and the modern revival

Kenneth Frampton's essay "Towards a Critical Regionalism" (1983) and his book Studies in Tectonic Culture (1995) placed tectonics at the centre of a resistance to placeless, image-driven architecture, emphasising the tactile, the joint, and the expressive potential of construction from Greek temples through Carlo Scarpa and Jørn Utzon. Frampton famously contrasted the tectonic with the "scenographic" — design reduced to visual effect.

Contemporary discussion

The concept informs debates about "honesty" in construction, digital fabrication, and mass-timber building. Critics note that in an era of layered wall assemblies and concealed services, literal structural expression can itself become a representation; theorists respond that tectonics concerns the poetics of making rather than literal transparency. The term is also used more loosely in design discourse to describe the visual articulation of assemblies and details.
