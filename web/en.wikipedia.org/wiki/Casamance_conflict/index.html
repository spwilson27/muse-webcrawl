The Casamance conflict is a low-level separatist insurgency in the Casamance region of southern Senegal, waged since 1982 by the Movement of Democratic Forces of Casamance (MFDC) and its armed wing, Atika ("warrior" in Diola). It is one of Africa's longest-running conflicts, though casualties have been far lower than in many other insurgencies.

Background

Casamance is geographically separated from the rest of Senegal by The Gambia. Its largest ethnic group, the Diola (Jola), differs from northern Senegalese groups in language, religion (many are Christian or animist in a Muslim-majority country) and social organisation. Separatist sentiment grew after independence amid complaints of economic marginalisation, land expropriation for tourism, and the imposition of northern officials.

Course of the conflict

A protest march in Ziguinchor in December 1982 was violently suppressed, prompting the MFDC to take up arms. Fighting through the 1980s and 1990s included ambushes, landmine attacks and army operations; thousands of civilians were displaced, and landmines rendered farmland unusable. Ceasefires were signed in 1991, 1993 and 2001, and a general peace agreement was reached in December 2004, but the MFDC had splintered into rival factions, some of which rejected the deal. Sporadic violence — including the 2018 Boffa-Bayotte massacre of woodcutters — has continued, alongside periodic army offensives and renewed peace initiatives.

Impact

The conflict devastated Casamance's tourism and agriculture, scattered refugees into The Gambia and Guinea-Bissau, and left a legacy of landmine contamination. Overall deaths are commonly estimated in the low thousands over four decades.
