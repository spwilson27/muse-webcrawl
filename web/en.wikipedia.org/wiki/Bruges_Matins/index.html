The Bruges Matins (Dutch: Brugse Metten) was the massacre of the French garrison of Bruges and their local supporters by members of the local Flemish militia on the night of 17–18 May 1302. Named, ironically, after the early-morning prayer service of matins, the uprising ignited the Franco-Flemish War's decisive phase and led within two months to the Flemish victory at the Battle of the Golden Spurs (11 July 1302), a landmark in the history of infantry warfare and of Flemish identity.

Background

Flanders, wealthy from the cloth trade with England, was contested between the French crown and the Count of Flanders, Guy of Dampierre. After the Franco-Flemish War of 1297–1300, Philip IV of France annexed the county and installed Jacques de Châtillon as governor. His taxation and the arrogance of the French garrison and the francophile patrician faction (the Leliaards, "lily-men") alienated the guilds and commoners (the Klauwaards, "claw-men", after the lion's claw of Flanders). A first revolt in 1301 was suppressed, and leaders including the weaver Pieter de Coninck were exiled or imprisoned, then released under popular pressure.

The massacre

On the night of 17–18 May 1302, after Châtillon entered the city with troops, de Coninck and the butcher Jan Breydel led armed bands through Bruges killing French soldiers and Leliaard collaborators in their lodgings. Tradition holds that suspects were identified by requiring them to pronounce the Dutch shibboleth "schild en vriend" ("shield and friend"), which French speakers could not pronounce correctly. Casualty figures are uncertain; contemporary estimates range from several hundred to about two thousand dead.

Aftermath

The revolt spread across Flanders. Guy of Dampierre's sons rallied an army of urban militias that annihilated the French chivalry at the Battle of the Golden Spurs near Kortrijk on 11 July 1302 — a date now celebrated as the official holiday of the Flemish Community. France eventually recovered ground, and the Treaty of Athis-sur-Orge (1305) imposed heavy reparations, but the Matins and the Golden Spurs remain central symbols of Flemish resistance and the political power of the medieval guilds.
