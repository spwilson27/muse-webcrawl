Seraphim

A six-winged seraph in medieval art.

The seraphim (singular seraph) are celestial beings in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, traditionally ranked among the highest orders of angels. The word derives from the Hebrew saraph, "burning one," and in the Hebrew Bible the plural seraphim appears most famously in the prophet Isaiah's vision of the heavenly throne room. In later Christian angelology, systematized by Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, the seraphim constitute the first and highest of the nine choirs of angels, closest to God, burning with divine love and light.

In the Hebrew Bible

The term saraph appears in several contexts in the Hebrew Bible, including references to fiery serpents sent against the Israelites in the wilderness (Numbers 21) and to flying serpents in Isaiah's oracles. In Isaiah 6, the seraphim stand above the throne of God in the Temple vision: each has six wings — with two they cover their faces, with two their feet, and with two they fly — and they cry antiphonally, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory," a passage that became the source of the Sanctus in Christian liturgy and the Kedushah in Jewish prayer. One seraph touches Isaiah's lips with a burning coal from the altar, purifying him for his prophetic mission.

In Judaism

In rabbinic literature and medieval Jewish philosophy, the seraphim occupy a high rank in the angelic hierarchy; Maimonides placed them fifth among the ten ranks of angels in his Mishneh Torah. They feature in Merkabah mysticism and in liturgical poetry (piyyut) as the beings who perpetually proclaim God's holiness.

In Christianity

Pseudo-Dionysius's Celestial Hierarchy (c. 5th–6th century) placed the seraphim in the first triad of angels, together with the cherubim and thrones, contemplating God directly. Medieval theologians such as Thomas Aquinas held that the seraphim excel in charity — the burning of love — while the cherubim excel in knowledge. In Christian iconography seraphim are shown with six wings, often in red to signify fire and love. Francis of Assisi's vision of a seraph on Mount La Verna in 1224, after which he was said to bear the stigmata, made the seraph an important Franciscan motif.

In Islam

Islamic tradition knows seraph-like angels around the divine throne, including the bearers of the Throne (hamalat al-'arsh), and the angel Israfil is sometimes described with seraphic attributes. The Hebrew term was also borrowed into Islamic angelology in discussions of the ranks of angels.
