Anglo-Sikh wars

Date1845–1846 and 1848–1849
LocationPunjab, South Asia
CombatantsBritish East India Company vs the Sikh Empire
ResultBritish victory; annexation of the Punjab (1849)
Major battlesMudki, Ferozeshah, Sobraon; Ramnagar, Chillianwala, Gujrat

The Anglo-Sikh wars were two conflicts fought between the British East India Company and the Sikh Empire of the Punjab: the First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–1846) and the Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–1849). They were among the hardest-fought wars of British expansion in India, and they ended with the annexation of the Punjab, the last major independent kingdom of the subcontinent, in 1849.

The Sikh Khalsa army was among the most formidable opponents the Company faced in India.

Background

Maharaja Ranjit Singh (d. 1839) had built a powerful, European-drilled army — the Sikh Khalsa — and maintained a wary friendship with the British under the Treaty of Amritsar (1809), which fixed the Sutlej River as the frontier. After his death, court intrigue, army interference in politics, and a succession of weak rulers destabilised the empire, while the Company massed troops on the border.

First war (1845–1846)

In December 1845 the Khalsa crossed the Sutlej. Fierce battles at Mudki, Ferozeshah — where the British position came close to disaster — Aliwal, and Sobraon ended in Sikh defeat. The Treaties of Lahore and Bhairowal imposed indemnities, ceded the Jalandhar Doab and Kashmir (sold to Gulab Singh), placed a British Resident at Lahore, and reduced the Sikh army, leaving the boy Maharaja Duleep Singh on the throne under British tutelage.

Second war (1848–1849)

A revolt at Multan in April 1848, joined by Sikh forces under Chattar Singh and his son Sher Singh, ignited a general rising. After the bloody drawn battle of Chillianwala (January 1849), Lord Gough's forces won a decisive victory at Gujrat (21 February 1849), the "battle of the guns". On 29 March 1849 the Punjab was annexed; Duleep Singh was pensioned off, and the Koh-i-Noor diamond was surrendered to Queen Victoria. The Punjab thereafter became a major recruiting ground for the British Indian Army.
