Cockapoo

A cockapoo, a Cocker Spaniel–Poodle cross

Other names: Cockerpoo; Spoodle (Australia)

Origin: United States

Type: Crossbreed / companion dog

Height: 25–38 cm (10–15 in), varying by poodle parent

Coat: Wavy or curly, low-shedding

A cockapoo, also spelled cockerpoo, is a dog crossbreed bred from a Cocker Spaniel (American or English) and a Poodle, usually a Miniature or Toy Poodle. One of the oldest of the popular "designer dog" crosses, the cockapoo has been bred in the United States since the 1960s and became especially popular from the 1990s onward as interest grew in poodle crosses perceived to combine a friendly temperament with a low-shedding coat.

Characteristics

Cockapoos vary considerably depending on the size of the poodle parent: toy cockapoos may weigh under 5 kg, while those bred from standard-spaniel and miniature-poodle parents commonly weigh 7–11 kg. The coat is usually wavy to curly, comes in a wide range of colours including black, cream, apricot, red, chocolate and parti-colour, and requires regular grooming to prevent matting. Breeders often describe the cockapoo as cheerful, sociable, intelligent and people-oriented, inheriting the spaniel's affectionate nature and the poodle's trainability.

Health

As a crossbreed, the cockapoo may be prone to health conditions found in either parent breed, including ear infections (owing to long, drop ears), patellar luxation, hip dysplasia, progressive retinal atrophy and certain skin conditions. Lifespan is typically 13–15 years or more. Because most kennel clubs classify the cockapoo as a crossbreed rather than a breed, there is no single breed standard; dedicated breed clubs in the United States and United Kingdom maintain registries and promote multi-generational breeding programmes.

Popularity

The cockapoo consistently ranks among the most popular crossbreeds in the United Kingdom, the United States and Australia (where it is known as the spoodle). Its popularity has drawn criticism from some pedigree breeders and animal-welfare campaigners concerned about unregulated breeding and inflated prices, while its supporters point to its temperament as an adaptable family companion.
