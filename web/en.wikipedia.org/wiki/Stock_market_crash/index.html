Stock market crash

A stock market crash is a sudden dramatic decline of stock prices across a significant cross-section of a stock market, resulting in a significant loss of paper wealth. Crashes are driven by panic selling and underlying economic factors, and often follow speculative stock market bubbles.

Causes

Crashes typically occur after long periods of rising prices fuelled by speculation and leverage. Contributing mechanisms include margin calls forcing sales, herd behaviour and panic, external shocks (wars, pandemics, interest-rate changes), and, in the modern era, feedback loops in automated and program trading. Charles Kindleberger's model of manias, panics and crashes describes a recurring sequence of displacement, boom, euphoria, distress and revulsion.

Notable crashes

  • Wall Street Crash of 1929 — Black Thursday (24 October) and Black Tuesday (29 October) 1929; the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell some 25% in two days and ultimately nearly 89% by July 1932, ushering in the Great Depression.
  • Black Monday, 1987 — 19 October 1987, the Dow fell 22.6% in one day, still its largest one-day percentage drop; portfolio insurance and program trading were widely blamed.
  • Dot-com bubble collapse, 2000–2002 — the NASDAQ Composite lost about 78% from its March 2000 peak.
  • 2007–2008 financial crisis — global markets fell roughly by half amid the subprime mortgage crisis and the failure of Lehman Brothers.
  • 2020 COVID-19 crash — the fastest fall into bear-market territory on record at the time, followed by a rapid recovery.

Mitigation

Exchanges employ circuit breakers that halt trading after set percentage declines, and central banks may intervene with liquidity and interest-rate cuts to limit contagion.
