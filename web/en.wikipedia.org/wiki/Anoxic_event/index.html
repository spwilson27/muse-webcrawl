Anoxic event

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An anoxic event — in the geological record usually called an oceanic anoxic event (OAE) — describes an interval in Earth's past during which large parts of the oceans became severely depleted of dissolved oxygen, in places becoming anoxic and euxinic (containing free hydrogen sulfide). These events are recorded by the widespread deposition of organic-rich black shales and are frequently associated with major perturbations of the carbon cycle, rapid warming, and biotic crises.

Causes and mechanisms

Oceanic anoxic events are thought to have been triggered by massive volcanism from large igneous provinces, which released carbon dioxide, warmed the climate, accelerated continental weathering and nutrient runoff, and boosted surface productivity; the sinking organic matter then consumed oxygen faster than circulation could replace it. Sluggish circulation in warm, low-latitude "supergreenhouse" oceans amplified the effect.

Major events

Well-studied examples include the Toarcian event (about 183 million years ago), the early Aptian OAE1a (about 120 million years ago), and the Cenomanian–Turonian boundary event, OAE2 (about 94 million years ago), which coincided with the extinction of ichthyosaurs and many marine invertebrates. Because they bury large quantities of organic carbon, anoxic events also generated many of the world's petroleum source rocks.
