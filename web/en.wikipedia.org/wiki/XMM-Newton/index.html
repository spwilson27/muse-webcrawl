XMM-Newton

XMM-Newton, also known as the High Throughput X-ray Spectroscopy Mission and the X-ray Multi-Mirror Mission, is an X-ray space observatory launched by the European Space Agency (ESA) in December 1999 on an Ariane 5 rocket. It is the largest scientific satellite ever built in Europe, named after physicist and astronomer Sir Isaac Newton.

Mission

Funded for an initial period of about two years, the spacecraft proved to be in such good health that its mission has been repeatedly extended, and it remains in operation well beyond a decade, with further extensions approved into the 2020s. The telescope is tasked with investigating interstellar X-ray sources, performing broad and deep spectroscopy, and undertaking the first comprehensive imaging of X-ray sources.

Spacecraft and instruments

XMM-Newton is 10.8 metres long and carries three X-ray telescopes whose focal planes hold the European Photon Imaging Camera (EPIC) instruments: two MOS CCD cameras and one pn CCD camera, together giving the observatory the largest collecting area of any X-ray telescope. Behind two of the telescopes, grating spectrometers (the Reflection Grating Spectrometer, RGS) provide high-resolution spectroscopy. A co-aligned 30-cm optical/ultraviolet telescope, the Optical Monitor (OM), observes the same field simultaneously.

The spacecraft flies a highly elliptical 48-hour orbit, spending most of its time outside Earth's radiation belts and able to make long, uninterrupted observations.

Discoveries

XMM-Newton has surveyed large areas of sky (the XMM-Newton Serendipitous Source Catalogue contains hundreds of thousands of sources), studied the hot gas in galaxy clusters, probed the environments of supermassive black holes, mapped the remnants of supernovae, and discovered new classes of transient X-ray sources. Together with ESA's later missions it forms a cornerstone of European high-energy astrophysics, complementary to NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory.
