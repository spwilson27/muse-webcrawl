The transputer is a series of pioneering microprocessors from the 1980s, featuring integrated memory and serial communication links, intended to make it easy to build massively parallel computers. The name is a portmanteau of transistor and computer. The transputer was designed by the British company Inmos, led architecturally by David May, and was the first general-purpose microprocessor designed specifically to be used in parallel computing systems.

Design

The design philosophy was that many transputers could be linked together to form a large multicomputer without shared memory, communicating by message passing over point-to-point serial links — four links per chip, each bidirectional. Each processor had a small amount of fast on-chip RAM, an interface to external memory, and hardware support for multitasking: a built-in scheduler allowed a single transputer to run many processes concurrently, switching between them at negligible cost when a process waited on communication.

The instruction set was a compact stack architecture. Programs were written in occam, a language based on Tony Hoare's CSP (communicating sequential processes) formalism, in which parallel processes interact only through channels — exactly matching the hardware. This tight coupling of language, architecture, and concurrency model was the transputer's most distinctive feature.

Models and use

The first products, announced in 1985, included the 16-bit T212 and the 32-bit T414. The T800 (1987) added an on-chip floating-point unit and more RAM, and became the workhorse of systems such as the Meiko Computing Surface and Parsytec machines, used in scientific computing, image processing, and research into parallel architectures. Transputers flew in space and were used in applications from industrial control to early HDTV research. The ambitious T9000, with a superscalar pipeline and faster links, was delayed and suffered problems; by the early 1990s general-purpose microprocessors and standard interconnects overtook the design.

Legacy

Inmos was acquired by SGS-Thomson (now STMicroelectronics); transputer descendants lived on in embedded and set-top-box chips. The architecture's ideas — message passing, channels, hardware multitasking — influenced later designs, notably the XMOS XCore processors founded by David May, and the CSP style resonates in modern languages such as Go, whose goroutines and channels echo occam.
