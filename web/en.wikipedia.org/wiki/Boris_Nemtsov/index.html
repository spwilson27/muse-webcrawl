Boris Yefimovich Nemtsov (9 October 1959 – 27 February 2015) was a Russian physicist and liberal politician. One of the leading reformers of the 1990s, he served as governor of Nizhny Novgorod Oblast and as First Deputy Prime Minister under Boris Yeltsin, and was widely tipped as a possible successor. In the 2000s he became one of the most prominent critics of Vladimir Putin. He was assassinated in February 2015, shot dead on a bridge beside the Kremlin.

Early life and career

Nemtsov was born in Sochi and studied radiophysics at Gorky State University, earning a candidate of sciences degree in 1985. He entered politics through the environmental movement that blocked a nuclear heating plant near Gorky, and was elected to the Congress of People's Deputies in 1990. In 1991 Yeltsin appointed him governor of Nizhny Novgorod, where his market reforms made the region a showcase of Russian liberalisation.

National politics

Brought to Moscow in 1997 as minister of fuel and energy and then First Deputy Prime Minister, Nemtsov fought the influence of the oligarchs and was seen as a reformist counterweight in government. The 1998 financial crisis swept away his cabinet. He co-founded the Union of Right Forces (SPS), which entered the State Duma in 1999, but lost his seat as the liberal parties declined.

Opposition

From the mid-2000s Nemtsov co-authored a series of reports — Putin. Results, Putin. Corruption and others — documenting alleged corruption and the costs of Putin's rule. He was a leading figure in the 2011–13 protest movement and co-chaired the RPR-PARNAS party. At the time of his death he was preparing a report on Russian military involvement in eastern Ukraine.

Assassination

On the night of 27 February 2015, Nemtsov was shot four times while walking home across the Bolshoy Moskvoretsky Bridge, within sight of the Kremlin. Five Chechen men were later convicted of the murder, but the person who ordered it has never been officially identified. The site of his death has been maintained by volunteers as an informal memorial, and streets and squares near Russian embassies abroad have been named in his honour.
