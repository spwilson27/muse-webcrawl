Monadic second-order logic

In mathematical logic, monadic second-order logic (MSO) is the fragment of second-order logic in which the second-order quantifiers range only over sets of elements of the domain — that is, only monadic (unary) predicates may be quantified — rather than over arbitrary relations. MSO is more expressive than first-order logic, since it can express properties such as connectivity and reachability that first-order logic cannot, but it is far better behaved than full second-order logic: over important classes of structures, notably words and trees, MSO model checking is decidable and MSO corresponds exactly to regular languages.

Expressive power

A classical result of Büchi, Elgot, and Trakhtenbrot states that over finite words (strings), a language is definable in MSO if and only if it is regular, i.e. recognized by a finite automaton. The analogous result for finite trees (Doner, Thatcher, and Wright) equates MSO-definability with recognizability by a finite tree automaton. Over graphs of bounded treewidth, Courcelle's theorem shows that every MSO property can be model-checked in linear time (for fixed formula and treewidth bound), making MSO a central tool in parameterized algorithms and structural graph theory.

Variants

Two variants are commonly distinguished: MSO1, which quantifies over sets of vertices, and MSO2, which can also quantify over sets of edges and is strictly more expressive on general graphs. Counting MSO (CMSO) adds modulo-counting predicates. Over infinite words and trees, MSO matches the automata of Büchi and Rabin, and Rabin's tree theorem gives decidability of the MSO theory of the infinite binary tree, one of the strongest decidability results in logic.

Applications

MSO is used in verification (specifying regular properties of systems), database theory, natural language semantics, constraint satisfaction, and algorithmic meta-theorems. Its limitation is expressive power: MSO cannot express, for example, that a set has even cardinality without counting extensions, or Hamiltonian properties over general graphs.
