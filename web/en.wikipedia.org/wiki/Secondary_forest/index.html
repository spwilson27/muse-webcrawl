Secondary forest

A secondary forest is a forest that has regrown after a major disturbance — such as logging, shifting cultivation, fire, or the abandonment of farmland — has removed most or all of the original (primary) forest cover. Secondary forests develop through ecological succession and differ from primary forests in structure and species composition: they typically have a more even-aged, lower canopy, denser undergrowth, fewer large trees, and a greater proportion of fast-growing, light-demanding pioneer species.

Formation and succession

Regeneration begins from seed banks in the soil, resprouting stumps and roots, and seeds dispersed from nearby forest. In the tropics a recognisable thicket of pioneer trees can close canopy within a few years; recovery of biomass to primary-forest levels may take many decades, while recovery of the original species composition — especially of slow-growing hardwood specialists and the animals that depend on them — may take centuries or may never occur if the site is isolated from seed sources. Repeated disturbance, grazing, or conversion to plantation arrests the process; on badly degraded soils the result may be persistent grassland or scrub instead.

Extent and importance

Secondary forests now account for a large and growing share of the world's forest area — more than half of tropical forests are estimated to be secondary or degraded. They are increasingly valued in conservation and climate policy: young secondary forests sequester carbon rapidly, protect watersheds and soils, and can recover a substantial fraction of primary-forest biodiversity if left to mature, though they are not a substitute for preserving old-growth. Large parts of the eastern United States and of Europe are secondary forest regrown on farmland abandoned in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; in the tropics, swidden-fallow mosaics produce extensive secondary growth. Because secondary forests are often treated as having low value, they are disproportionately targeted for conversion to agriculture and plantations, which forest-policy reforms increasingly seek to prevent.
