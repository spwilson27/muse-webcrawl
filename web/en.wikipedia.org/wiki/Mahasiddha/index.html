Mahasiddha

Mahāsiddha (Sanskrit: "great adept"; Tibetan: grub thob chen po) is a term for someone who embodies and cultivates the view, meditation and conduct of tantric Buddhism in order to attain siddhis (powers) and enlightenment. The mahasiddhas were the eccentric yogic masters of medieval India — many of them laypeople such as kings, craftsmen, hunters and courtesans — who stood at the origins of the Vajrayana traditions later transmitted to Tibet. A conventional list enumerates the eighty-four mahasiddhas.

The eighty-four mahasiddhas

The best-known collection of lives is Abhayadatta's 11th–12th-century Lives of the Eighty-Four Siddhas (Caturaśītisiddhapravṛtti), preserved in Tibetan. Its subjects defy monastic respectability: Luipa ate fish entrails, Virupa stopped the sun to keep drinking, and the poet Saraha — author of famous doha verses — worked as an arrowsmith and consorted with a low-caste consort. The stories celebrate direct realisation over scholastic learning, and many lineages of Tibetan Buddhism trace their teachings through mahasiddhas such as Tilopa and his disciple Naropa, whose "Six Yogas" became central to the Kagyu school.

Siddhis

Siddhis are traditionally divided into ordinary powers — flight, clairvoyance, invisibility, mastery of the elements — and the supreme siddhi of mahamudra, enlightenment itself. In hagiography the miraculous powers function as signs of realisation and as provocative teaching devices rather than ends in themselves. The mahasiddha ideal remains central to how Vajrayana understands the guru and the possibility of awakening within ordinary life.
