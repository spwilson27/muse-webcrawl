Hurdling

Hurdling is the act of jumping over an obstacle at a high speed or in a sprint. In the sport of track and field, hurdling forms a group of events in which athletes run over a series of barriers (hurdles) set at precisely measured heights and distances. Hurdle races are contested at the Olympic Games and World Championships.

Events

The standard outdoor events are the 100 metres hurdles for women, the 110 metres hurdles for men, and the 400 metres hurdles for both sexes. Indoors, the 60 metres hurdles is standard. Hurdle heights range from 0.686 m (27 in) for younger age groups up to 1.067 m (42 in) for the men's sprint hurdles; the barriers are designed to topple over when struck rather than injure the athlete, though hitting them slows the runner.

Technique

Elite sprint hurdlers take three strides between hurdles. The lead leg extends over the hurdle with a slight knee bend and snaps down quickly ("lead leg snap-down"), while the trail leg folds tightly to the side and is pulled through rapidly. Minimising vertical displacement of the centre of mass is essential: a good hurdler "runs over" the hurdle rather than jumping it. In the 400 metres hurdles, athletes must manage stride patterns over ten barriers while fatigued, and alternate lead legs are common.

History

Hurdle racing developed in England in the 19th century; early barriers were heavy sheep hurdles and wooden gates. The 110 metres hurdles appeared at the first modern Olympics in 1896. Technique was revolutionised by the straight lead-leg style and later by pioneers such as Edwin Moses, who dominated the 400 m hurdles for a decade, and Aries Merritt, whose 110 m hurdles world record of 12.80 seconds was set in 2012.
