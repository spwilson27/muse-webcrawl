Levofloxacin, sold under the brand name Levaquin among others, is a broad-spectrum antibiotic of the fluoroquinolone class. It is the levorotatory isomer of ofloxacin and is used to treat a range of bacterial infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, bacterial sinusitis, prostatitis, skin infections, and — as part of combination therapy — tuberculosis and Helicobacter pylori infection. It is also used to prevent and treat anthrax and plague. It appears on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism of action

Like other fluoroquinolones, levofloxacin kills bacteria by inhibiting the enzymes DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, which are needed to separate and supercoil bacterial DNA during replication. It is active against many Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms, including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Escherichia coli, and atypical pathogens such as Mycoplasma pneumoniae and Legionella species. Its oral bioavailability is close to 100 percent, so intravenous and oral doses are essentially interchangeable.

Adverse effects and warnings

Common side effects include nausea, diarrhoea, headache, insomnia, and dizziness. Fluoroquinolones carry regulatory boxed warnings for rare but potentially disabling and irreversible adverse reactions, including tendon inflammation and rupture, peripheral neuropathy, central nervous system effects, and exacerbation of myasthenia gravis. They have also been associated with aortic aneurysm and dissection, disturbances of blood sugar, and prolongation of the QT interval. Because of these risks, regulators in the United States and Europe advise reserving levofloxacin for infections where no alternative treatment exists in conditions such as uncomplicated urinary tract infection, sinusitis, and bronchitis. Growing bacterial resistance has further reduced its empirical use in some settings.
