Improvisational theatre

Improvisational theatre, often called improv, is a form of theatre in which most or all of what is performed is unplanned and created spontaneously by the performers, often using suggestions from the audience. Scenes, characters, and dialogue arise in the moment, making every performance unique. Although improvisation has ancient roots in traditions such as the commedia dell'arte, modern improv developed in the twentieth century as both an art form and a training method for actors.

Improv performers build scenes collaboratively, without a script.

History

In the 1930s and 1940s, the educator Viola Spolin developed "theatre games" in Chicago to help children and actors act naturally and spontaneously; her book Improvisation for the Theater (1963) became a foundational text. Her son Paul Sills founded The Second City in 1959, which turned improv into popular comedy and launched generations of performers. In the 1970s, Keith Johnstone in Britain and Canada developed Theatresports, framing improv as competitive games, and Del Close in Chicago pioneered long-form improvisation, including the "Harold", with troupes such as iO (ImprovOlympic).

Principles

The best-known principle of improv is "yes, and…": performers accept what their partners establish (the "yes") and add new information (the "and"), rather than blocking or denying. Other core skills include listening, establishing who-where-what quickly, playing characters with clear status, and treating mistakes as gifts. Short-form improv consists of brief games with rules and audience suggestions, as popularised by the television show Whose Line Is It Anyway?; long-form improv builds extended, interconnected scenes from a single suggestion.

Applications

Beyond entertainment, improv training is used in actor education, comedy writing, business communication, and therapy, because it exercises spontaneity, collaboration, and tolerance of uncertainty.
