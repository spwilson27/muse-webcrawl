Elizabeth Gurley Flynn (August 7, 1890 – September 5, 1964) was an American labor leader, activist, and feminist who was a leader of the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW). She played a leading role in a number of early-20th-century strikes, including the 1912 Lawrence textile strike ("Bread and Roses"), and helped found the American Civil Liberties Union. In her later years she became a prominent member and, in 1961, national chairwoman of the Communist Party USA.

Early activism

Born in New York City to Irish immigrant parents active in socialist politics, Flynn gave her first public speech at age fifteen and was arrested for the first time at sixteen. She joined the IWW in 1906 and became one of its most famous travelling organizers — the songwriter Joe Hill immortalized her as "The Rebel Girl" in his 1915 song of the same name.

Career

Flynn organized among textile workers, miners, and garment workers, and defended immigrants and radicals in free-speech fights across the country. Breaking with the IWW during the 1920s, she worked for the release of Sacco and Vanzetti and for imprisoned trade unionists. She joined the Communist Party in 1936, was expelled from the ACLU's board in 1940 because of her party membership, and in the 1950s was convicted under the Smith Act and served two years in prison. Her autobiography, I Speak My Own Piece (1955), was followed by a second volume published after her death.

Death and legacy

Flynn died while visiting Moscow in 1964 and was given a state funeral by the Soviet Union; her remains were buried in Chicago near the Haymarket martyrs' monument, as she had wished.
