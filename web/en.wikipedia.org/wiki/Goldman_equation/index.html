Goldman equation

The Goldman equation, also known as the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation, is used in cell membrane physiology to calculate the resting membrane potential of a cell taking into account all of the ions that permeate its membrane. It was developed by David E. Goldman in 1943 and popularised by Alan Lloyd Hodgkin and Bernard Katz, and it generalises the simpler Nernst equation, which applies to a single ion species.

Equation

For the three principal physiological ions, potassium, sodium and chloride, the equation states that the membrane potential equals (RT/F) multiplied by the natural logarithm of a ratio: the numerator sums the permeabilities of the cations times their external concentrations together with the permeability of chloride times its internal concentration, while the denominator mirrors this with internal cation and external chloride concentrations. Because chloride is an anion, its concentrations appear on the opposite sides from those of the cations. If only potassium permeated, the equation would reduce to the Nernst equation for potassium; in a typical resting cell, high potassium permeability keeps the resting potential near, but slightly above, the potassium equilibrium potential.

Assumptions and use

The derivation assumes that the electric field within the membrane is constant and that ions move independently of one another — the "constant field" assumptions. Despite their simplifications, the equation successfully explains how changes in external potassium concentration or in sodium permeability, as during an action potential, shift the membrane voltage, and it remains a standard tool for interpreting electrophysiological experiments.
