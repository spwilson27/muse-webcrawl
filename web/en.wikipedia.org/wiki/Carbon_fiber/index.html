Diameter5–10 micrometres

PropertiesVery high strength-to-weight ratio, stiffness, low thermal expansion

Carbon fibers or carbon fibres are fibers about 5 to 10 micrometres in diameter composed mostly of carbon atoms bonded together in crystals aligned parallel to the fiber's long axis. This alignment gives an exceptional strength-to-weight ratio. Thousands of individual filaments are bundled into a "tow", which may be woven into fabric and combined with epoxy or other resins to form carbon-fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) composites.

Manufacture

Most carbon fiber is made from polyacrylonitrile (PAN) precursor fiber, which is stabilised by heating in air, then carbonised at 1,000–2,000 °C in an inert atmosphere, and sometimes graphitised at still higher temperatures for high-modulus grades. Smaller amounts are produced from petroleum pitch or rayon. Surface treatment and sizing improve adhesion to the resin matrix.

Applications

Carbon fiber composites are used wherever low weight and high stiffness are critical: aircraft structures (the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350 have composite airframes by weight), Formula One monocoques, high-end bicycle frames, racing yachts, wind turbine blades, sporting goods, and increasingly in automotive structures. Limitations include high cost, brittleness under impact, and the difficulty of recycling thermoset composites.

History

Thomas Edison used carbonised bamboo fibers as filaments in early light bulbs in 1879. Modern high-strength fibers were developed in the late 1950s and 1960s, notably by Roger Bacon at Union Carbide and by Akio Shindo in Japan, with commercial production expanding rapidly from the 1970s.
