Sources of international law

Sources of international law
Authoritative list: ICJ Statute, Article 38(1)

Sources of international law are the materials and processes from which the rules of international law derive. Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice is widely accepted as the authoritative enumeration.

Article 38 sources

The Court applies: (a) international conventions (treaties); (b) international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law; (c) general principles of law recognised by civilised nations; and, as subsidiary means, (d) judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists.

Customary international law

Custom requires two elements: state practice (usus) that is widespread, representative and consistent, and opinio juris — the belief that the practice is legally obligatory. Custom binds all states except persistent objectors.

General principles and jus cogens

General principles fill gaps, drawn from domestic systems (good faith, res judicata, equity). Peremptory norms (jus cogens) — such as prohibitions of genocide, slavery and aggression — accept no derogation and override conflicting treaties.

Soft law

UN General Assembly resolutions, declarations and codes of conduct are not binding per se but may evidence opinio juris or crystallise emerging custom.
