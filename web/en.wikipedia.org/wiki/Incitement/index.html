Incitement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In criminal law, incitement is the encouragement of another person to commit a crime. Depending on the jurisdiction, it may be an inchoate offence — punishable even if the encouraged crime is never carried out — and in many legal systems it overlaps with the related offences of solicitation, conspiracy, and aiding and abetting.

Common law

At English common law, incitement consisted of persuading, encouraging, or coercing another to commit an offence. In England and Wales the common-law offence was abolished by the Serious Crime Act 2007, which replaced it with statutory offences of intentionally encouraging or assisting crime. In the United States, inchoate liability is governed by state penal codes and the Model Penal Code's provisions on solicitation.

Free speech limits

Because incitement is committed through speech, it sits at the boundary of freedom of expression. Under Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), the US Supreme Court held that advocacy of lawbreaking is protected by the First Amendment unless it is "directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action." International law separately criminalizes direct and public incitement to genocide, prosecuted after the Rwandan genocide in the so-called Media Case.
