The Jargon File is a glossary and usage dictionary of slang used by computer programmers and hackers. The original file was begun in 1975 at the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, collecting jargon from technical cultures including the MIT AI Lab, Bolt Beranek and Newman, and early ARPANET communities. It documents not only vocabulary — words such as foo, kludge, wizard and munge — but also the folklore, humour and values of hacker culture.

History

The file was maintained collaboratively across the ARPANET as a plain-text document, passing through editors at Stanford, MIT and elsewhere. A first print edition appeared in 1983 as The Hacker's Dictionary, edited by Guy L. Steele Jr. and others. In 1990 Eric S. Raymond took over maintenance, expanding the file with slang from the Unix and personal-computer communities; his editions were published by MIT Press as The New Hacker's Dictionary (1991; 3rd edition 1996). The file continues to be distributed online for free.

Content and influence

Entries combine definitions with pronunciation guidance, etymology, usage notes and often elaborate jokes, parodies and "sound" examples. Appendices include the famous "Story of Mel" and essays on hacker folklore and the Hacker Koans. The Jargon File is credited with popularising much computing slang in wider culture and is frequently cited by lexicographers; the Oxford English Dictionary drew on it for early citations of computing terms. Critics have charged that later editions reflect the editor's own subculture (Unix and open-source communities) more than the mainframe culture of the original file.
