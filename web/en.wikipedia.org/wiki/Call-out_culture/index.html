Call-out culture

Related conceptsCancel culture, public shaming, accountability culture
ArenaSocial media, activism, workplaces
PractitionersActivists, online communities, journalists

Call-out culture is the practice of publicly drawing attention to — "calling out" — perceived offensive, harmful, or unjust words or behaviour by an individual, organisation, or institution, most visibly on social media. The term is closely related to cancel culture, though the two are used somewhat differently: a "call-out" is the public criticism itself, while "cancellation" usually implies a collective withdrawal of support, platform, or livelihood in response.

Origins and practice

Public accountability campaigns long predate the internet (boycotts, pickets, letters to editors), but call-out culture in its current sense grew with blogs, Tumblr, and Twitter in the late 2000s and 2010s, particularly within activist and marginalised communities, where naming behaviour was framed as a way for people without institutional power to seek accountability from those with it. A call-out may consist of a single post, screenshots of the offending material, or a coordinated campaign; it often aims at an apology, a change of behaviour, or reputational consequences.

Debate

Supporters argue that public criticism is a legitimate and often effective accountability tool against the powerful, and that much of what is labelled "cancellation" is simply criticism with consequences. Critics argue that online call-outs can be disproportionate, error-prone, and resistant to apology or context, punishing minor or misunderstood offences, chilling speech, and sometimes targeting relatively powerless people. Within activist circles, figures such as Loretta Ross have advocated "calling in" — private, compassionate confrontation — as an alternative to public shaming. The phenomenon is a recurring subject of political and cultural debate, and empirical studies of its frequency and effects remain contested terrain.
