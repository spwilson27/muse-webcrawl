Ionic bonding

In ionic bonding, electrons are transferred from a metal atom to a non-metal atom.

Ionic bonding is a type of chemical bonding that involves the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, or between two atoms with sharply different electronegativities. It is the primary interaction occurring in ionic compounds.

Overview

Ionic bonding typically occurs when a metallic atom donates one or more valence electrons to a non-metallic atom, producing a positively charged cation and a negatively charged anion. The archetypal example is sodium chloride: a sodium atom transfers its outer electron to a chlorine atom, and the resulting Na+ and Cl− ions arrange into a regular crystal lattice held together by electrostatic forces.

Properties of ionic compounds

Ionic compounds generally have high melting and boiling points, are hard and brittle, conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water (but not as solids), and are often soluble in water but insoluble in organic solvents. The strength of the bonding is quantified by the lattice energy.

Comparison with covalent bonding

In practice there is a continuum between ionic and covalent bonding: bonds between atoms whose electronegativity difference is large are described as ionic, while those between atoms of similar electronegativity share electrons covalently. Many compounds exhibit partial ionic character.
