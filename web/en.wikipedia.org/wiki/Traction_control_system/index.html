Traction control system
Also known asTCS, ASR, TRAC
Introduced1971 (Buick MaxTrac); widespread from late 1980s
Related systemsAnti-lock braking system, electronic stability control

A traction control system (TCS), also known as ASR (from German Antriebsschlupfregelung), is a secondary function of the anti-lock braking system (ABS) on production motor vehicles, designed to prevent loss of traction (i.e., wheelspin) of the driven wheels. When intervening, it helps the driver maintain control under acceleration, especially on slippery surfaces.

Operation

The system monitors the rotational speed of each wheel using the ABS wheel-speed sensors. If a driven wheel spins significantly faster than the others or faster than the vehicle's estimated road speed, the control unit intervenes by one or more of: applying the brake to the spinning wheel (brake-based traction control, which also simulates a limited-slip differential), reducing engine torque by retarding ignition, cutting fuel, or closing the throttle, or, in electric vehicles, reducing motor torque directly.

History

Buick offered an early electronic system called MaxTrac in 1971. Modern integrated systems appeared in the late 1980s — Mercedes-Benz and BMW introduced ASR/ASC around 1986–87 — and traction control later became a core component of electronic stability control, which has been mandatory on new cars in the EU and US since the early 2010s.

Use in motorsport

Traction control was a controversial driver aid in Formula One, permitted in the early 1990s and again from 2001 before being banned from 2008 with the standardised ECU. It remains common in many production-based racing classes and in motorcycle racing, where sophisticated lean-sensitive systems manage rear-wheel spin.
