Emulsion polymerization

Emulsion polymerization is a type of radical polymerization that usually starts with an emulsion incorporating water, monomer, and surfactant. The most common type of emulsion polymerization is an oil-in-water emulsion, in which droplets of monomer (the oil) are emulsified (with surfactants) in a continuous phase of water. Water-soluble polymers, such as certain polyvinyl alcohols or hydroxyethyl cellulose, can also be used to act as emulsifiers/stabilizers.

Mechanism

Polymerization occurs inside surfactant-stabilised particles rather than in the monomer droplets. A water-soluble initiator generates radicals in the aqueous phase, which enter monomer-swollen micelles, nucleating polymer particles. As described by the Smith–Ewart theory, once particles are formed their number remains roughly constant, and monomer diffuses from the reservoir droplets through the water to the growing particles. The compartmentalisation of radicals within separate particles both speeds up the reaction and produces very high molecular weights.

Applications and characteristics

The products of emulsion polymerization are typically used as water-based dispersions (latexes) without isolation of the polymer: examples include synthetic rubbers such as styrene-butadiene rubber (developed at scale during the Second World War), polyvinyl acetate for adhesives, and acrylic latex paints. Advantages of the process include good heat removal through the aqueous phase, high rates with high molecular weight, and low viscosity of the final product; disadvantages include residual surfactant in the product and difficulty in applications requiring the dry, pure polymer.
