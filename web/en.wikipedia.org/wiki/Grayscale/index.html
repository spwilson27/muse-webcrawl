Grayscale

A grayscale image encodes brightness only, without colour.

In digital photography, computer-generated imagery and colorimetry, a grayscale image is one in which the value of each pixel is a single sample representing only an amount of light — that is, it carries only intensity (luminance) information. Grayscale images, a kind of black-and-white or monochrome image, are composed exclusively of shades of gray, varying from black at the weakest intensity to white at the strongest.

Grayscale images are distinct from one-bit bi-tonal black-and-white images, which in image processing are images with only two colours: black and white (also called bilevel or binary images). Grayscale images have many shades of gray in between.

Numerical representations

The intensity of a pixel is most often stored as an integer between 0 (black) and a maximum value: 255 for the common 8-bit-per-sample representation, giving 256 distinct shades. Medical and astronomical imaging may use 10, 12 or 16 bits per sample, giving thousands of shades and allowing fine gradations to be revealed by windowing. In floating-point formats, intensities may instead be stored as values from 0.0 to 1.0.

Converting colour to grayscale

To convert a colour image to grayscale, the red, green and blue values of each pixel must be combined into a single intensity that represents perceived brightness. Because human vision is most sensitive to green and least to blue, weighted sums are used: the ITU-R BT.601 standard defines luma as Y′ = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B, while the newer BT.709 coefficients used for HDTV are 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722 respectively. Simple unweighted averaging produces a flatter, less natural result.

Uses

Grayscale is used throughout imaging: black-and-white photography and film; the luminance channel of colour television and video compression (where the eye's greater sensitivity to brightness than to colour allows chroma subsampling); medical imaging such as X-ray, CT and MRI; document scanning and fax; printing halftones; and as a preprocessing step in computer vision, where colour is irrelevant to the task. Single-channel grayscale data also requires less storage and bandwidth than full colour.

Colour spaces

Grayscale may be represented as a dedicated colour space, or as the lightness/value channel extracted from a colour space such as HSL, HSV or CIELAB. Converting from grayscale back to colour is not possible in general since hue and saturation information is discarded; assigning colours to gray values artificially is called pseudocolouring or false colour.
