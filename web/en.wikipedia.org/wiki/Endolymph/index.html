Endolymph

Endolymph is the fluid contained in the membranous labyrinth of the inner ear. The major cation in endolymph is potassium, with the values of sodium and potassium concentrations in the endolymph being 0.91 mM and 154 mM, respectively. It is also called Scarpa's fluid, after Antonio Scarpa.

Structure

The inner ear has two parts: the bony labyrinth and the membranous labyrinth. The membranous labyrinth is contained within the bony labyrinth, and within it is a fluid called endolymph. Between the outer wall of the membranous labyrinth and the wall of the bony labyrinth is the location of perilymph.

Composition

Perilymph and endolymph have unique ionic compositions suited to their functions in regulating electrochemical impulses of hair cells. The electric potential of endolymph is about 80–90 mV more positive than perilymph due to a higher concentration of K+ than Na+. Endolymph resembles intracellular fluid in composition, whereas perilymph resembles cerebrospinal fluid and extracellular fluid.

Function

  • Fluid waves in the endolymph of the semicircular canals cause the endolymph to move, stimulating the vestibular hair cells and allowing the vestibular system to sense movement of the body in space.
  • Fluid waves in the cochlear endolymph move the basilar membrane, bending the hair cells of the organ of Corti and producing the electrochemical impulses that the auditory nerve relays to the brain for processing as sound.

Clinical significance

Disruption of endolymph volume or composition is implicated in Ménière's disease, in which an abnormal increase in endolymph volume (endolymphatic hydrops) is associated with attacks of vertigo, tinnitus, aural fullness and fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss. Motion sickness can occur when there is a mismatch between the movement sensed via the endolymph in the semicircular canals and visual input.
