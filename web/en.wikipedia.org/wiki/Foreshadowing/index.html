Foreshadowing

Foreshadowing is a narrative device in which a storyteller gives an advance hint of what is to come later in the story. Foreshadowing often appears at the beginning of a story or chapter, and helps the reader develop expectations about the coming events. It may be subtle, embedded in a character's dialogue, or overt, through a sense of doom or a prophecy.

The term is related to similar concepts such as flashforward, red herrings, and prophecy. Unlike a red herring, which is designed to mislead, foreshadowing is meant to make the reader anticipate a future event that does in fact occur, creating dramatic tension and making the eventual outcome feel inevitable or "earned" rather than arbitrary.

History and usage

Foreshadowing has been used since ancient storytelling. In Greek drama, prologues such as those in the plays of Sophocles and Euripides could preview the action for the audience. In Indian epic poetry, Divine Beings and dreams often foreshadow future events. Shakepeare's works contain numerous examples: in Macbeth, the witches' prophecies foreshadow the title character's rise and fall; in Romeo and Juliet, the prologue explicitly foretells the lovers' deaths.

Types

Foreshadowing may be direct, openly stating what will happen (as in a prologue or prophecy), or indirect, hinting through symbols, recurring imagery, weather, or ominous remarks. A related device is Chekhov's gun, the principle attributed to Anton Chekhov that every element introduced in a story should be necessary — a rifle shown in the first act should fire in a later one. When a foreshadowed element never pays off, it may be criticized as a form of broken promise to the reader; when a detail is planted purely to misdirect, it functions as a red herring instead.
