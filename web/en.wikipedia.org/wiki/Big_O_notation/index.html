Big O notation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A graph comparing growth curves: n log n, n squared, 2 to the n]

Big O notation is a mathematical notation that describes the limiting behaviour of a function when the argument tends towards a particular value or infinity. In computer science it classifies algorithms by how their run time or space requirements grow as the input size grows.

Formally, f(x) = O(g(x)) if |f(x)| is bounded above by a constant multiple of |g(x)| for all sufficiently large x. Introduced by the number theorist Paul Bachmann (1894) and promoted by Edmund Landau, the notation — and its relatives Ω (big omega, a lower bound), Θ (big theta, a tight bound), and little-o — became the standard vocabulary of algorithm analysis through Donald Knuth's work.

Common classes

Constant O(1) (hash-table lookup on average), logarithmic O(log n) (binary search), linear O(n), linearithmic O(n log n) (efficient sorting), quadratic O(n²) (naive sorting), exponential O(2^n) (brute-force combinatorial search), and factorial O(n!) (travelling-salesman brute force). Polynomial versus exponential growth is the great divide of complexity theory (P versus NP).

Big O suppresses constant factors and lower-order terms and describes worst-case growth unless otherwise stated; average-case and amortised analyses complement it. Critics note that constants matter in practice and galactic algorithms can be fast in theory yet useless in practice.
