Social exclusion

Social exclusion or social marginalization is a social disadvantage and relegation to the fringe of society. It is a term used widely in Europe and was first used in France in the late 20th century. In the European Union, it refers to a process by which individuals or groups are wholly or partially excluded from full participation in the society in which they live. It overlaps with, but is broader than, the concept of poverty: a person may be excluded not only materially but also from employment, education, housing, social networks, and political participation.

Definition and dimensions

Social exclusion is usually understood as multidimensional and dynamic: it describes processes rather than just states, and it is relative to the society in question. Dimensions commonly include:

  • Economic exclusion — unemployment, precarious work, low income, lack of access to credit and services.
  • Social exclusion — weak social networks, isolation, lack of recognition and respect.
  • Political exclusion — lack of voting rights, voice, and access to decision-making.
  • Spatial exclusion — concentration in deprived neighbourhoods with poor services and transport.

History of the concept

The term les exclus gained currency in France in the 1970s, notably through René Lenoir's 1974 book Les Exclus, which estimated that a tenth of the French population was shut out of mainstream prosperity. From the 1990s the concept was adopted by the European Union as a policy framework, leading to national action plans for social inclusion and the UK's Social Exclusion Unit (1997). Academics such as Hilary Silver distinguished different paradigms of exclusion — solidarity, specialization, and monopoly — rooted in distinct political philosophies.

Causes and consequences

Exclusion can arise from discrimination (on grounds of race, disability, age, gender, religion, or migration status), institutional rules, labour-market change, family breakdown, illness, and addiction. It often overlaps with poverty, homelessness, and ill health, and can be transmitted across generations. Its opposite, social inclusion, is pursued through policies on employment, education, housing, anti-discrimination law, and community development.
