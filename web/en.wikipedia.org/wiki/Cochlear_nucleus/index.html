Cochlear nucleus

The cochlear nuclei at the junction of the pons and medulla.

The cochlear nucleus (also called the cochlear nuclear complex) is a collection of neurons in the brainstem that forms the first relay station of the central auditory system. Located on the dorsolateral surface of the brainstem at the junction of the pons and medulla, it receives all of the afferent fibres of the cochlear nerve from the spiral ganglion of the cochlea.

Divisions

The complex is divided into two main parts. The dorsal cochlear nucleus (DCN) sits on the surface of the brainstem and has a layered, cerebellum-like structure; it integrates auditory input with somatosensory information about the position of the head and ears, and is involved in analysing spectral cues used for localizing sounds in elevation. The ventral cochlear nucleus (VCN), further divided into anterior and posterior parts, preserves the precise timing and frequency information of the incoming signal. Its bushy cells, with their large secure synapses — the endbulbs of Held — are specialized to transmit timing information with minimal jitter to the nuclei that compare the two ears.

Function

Axons leaving the cochlear nuclei form the principal ascending auditory pathways, projecting through the trapezoid body and lateral lemniscus to the superior olivary complex, inferior colliculus, and ultimately the auditory cortex. Because each cochlear nucleus receives input from only the ipsilateral ear, it is the last point in the auditory pathway at which information is purely monaural. Damage to the dorsal cochlear nucleus is also implicated in some forms of tinnitus, in which the nucleus becomes hyperactive after hearing loss.
