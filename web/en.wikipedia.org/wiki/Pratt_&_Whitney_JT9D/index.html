Pratt & Whitney JT9D

The Pratt & Whitney JT9D is a high-bypass turbofan engine developed by Pratt & Whitney. It was the first high-bypass-ratio engine to power a widebody airliner, entering service on the Boeing 747 in 1970, and went on to power the Boeing 767, Airbus A300 and A310, and McDonnell Douglas DC-10. More than 3,200 JT9Ds were built, and the engine established Pratt & Whitney's position in the widebody market for decades.

Development

The JT9D originated in a U.S. Air Force requirement for a very large transport (won by Lockheed's C-5 Galaxy with the General Electric TF39), but Pratt & Whitney continued the design commercially, and Boeing selected it for the 747. Early service brought serious teething troubles — ovalization of the engine case under thrust caused fan blade rubbing and in-flight shutdowns — which nearly bankrupted both companies before fixes were introduced.

Design and variants

The JT9D is a two-spool axial-flow turbofan with a single-stage fan, three- or four-stage low-pressure compressor, eleven-stage high-pressure compressor, annular combustor, and two- and four-stage turbines. Successively uprated variants (JT9D-7, -59A, -70A, and the -7R4 series for twinjets) raised thrust and reliability, and the engine was a pioneer of electronic engine monitoring. Its core directly evolved into the PW4000 family.

Legacy

Although production has ended and most passenger fleets have retired it, JT9Ds remained in service with cargo and military operators (including the E-4B) well into the 21st century. The engine's troubled debut and subsequent success are a standard case study in aero-engine development risk.
