Anthony Ashley-Cooper, 7th Earl of Shaftesbury (28 April 1801 – 1 October 1885), known as Lord Ashley until 1851, was a British Tory politician and philanthropist, one of the leading social reformers of the Victorian era. He championed the regulation of factory labour, the protection of child workers, the reform of lunacy laws and public health, and the education of poor children.

Born into the aristocracy and educated at Harrow and Christ Church, Oxford, Ashley entered Parliament in 1826. His evangelical Anglican faith drove a lifelong campaign on behalf of the working poor, often against the interests of his own class and party.

Factory and labour reform

Ashley took over leadership of the Ten Hours Movement in Parliament after the illness of Richard Oastler's parliamentary allies, and steered through a series of Factory Acts that restricted the working hours of women and children in textile mills; the Ten Hours Act 1847 limited their working day to ten hours. His Mines Act 1842, passed after a royal commission exposed the underground employment of young children, banned the employment of women and of boys under ten in coal mines. He later campaigned against the use of climbing boys by chimney sweeps, achieving a final effective ban in 1875.

Lunacy and public health

As chairman of the Lunacy Commission for nearly sixty years, Ashley oversaw the inspection of asylums and the humane treatment of people with mental illness. He also supported the Public Health Act 1848 and worked on improving working-class housing through model dwellings associations.

Ragged Schools and philanthropy

Ashley was president of the Ragged School Union, which provided free education to destitute children, and supported the work of Dr Barnardo and other philanthropists. He was involved in numerous religious and charitable societies, including the British and Foreign Bible Society.

Legacy

Shaftesbury died in 1885 and was widely mourned; his funeral drew large crowds of working people. He is commemorated by the Shaftesbury Memorial Fountain at Piccadilly Circus in London (1893), whose winged figure represents Anteros, the god of selfless love — popularly misnamed "Eros". Historians regard him as a central figure of Victorian "Tory paternalism" and humanitarian reform.
