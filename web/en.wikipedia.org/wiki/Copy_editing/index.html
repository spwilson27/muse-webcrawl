Copy editing

Copy editing (also known as copy-editing or manuscript editing) is the process of revising written material ("copy") to improve quality and readability, and to ensure that a text is free of errors, consistent in style, and suitable for its intended audience and medium. A person who performs this task is called a copy editor (or copyeditor). In magazine and book publishing the term is commonly distinguished from proofreading, which is the final check of typeset proofs against the edited manuscript.

Scope of the work

A copy editor typically checks:

  • Mechanics — spelling, grammar, punctuation, capitalization, and usage;
  • Consistency — uniform treatment of numbers, abbreviations, hyphenation, names, and cross-references;
  • Style — conformance to a house style guide such as The Chicago Manual of Style, AP style, or an in-house stylesheet;
  • Clarity and flow — flagging or fixing awkward phrasing, redundancies, jargon, and logical gaps;
  • Factual accuracy — verifying names, dates, quotations, and arithmetic where feasible;
  • Legal and ethical concerns — flagging potential libel, plagiarism, bias, and missing permissions or credits.

Levels of editing

Editors often describe a hierarchy of interventions. Light (or baseline) editing corrects only clear errors. Medium editing additionally smooths awkward constructions and enforces style more aggressively. Heavy editing may reorganize sentences and paragraphs, query the author extensively, and approach substantive or developmental editing, which addresses the structure and content of a work as a whole. Proofreading is the lightest final pass and traditionally does not involve rewriting.

History

Copy editing emerged as a distinct newsroom function in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when large metropolitan newspapers separated the roles of reporter, editor, and "copy reader" seated around a shared desk — the origin of the term rim for the copy desk's outer edge. Wire services and stylebooks standardized practice, and figures such as The New York Times's Theodore Bernstein helped popularize usage guidance. The migration from paper mark-up with a standard set of proofreaders' marks to on-screen editing in word processors and content-management systems transformed the craft from the 1980s onward, and most copy editors now work digitally, using tracked changes and style-checking software.

Professional practice

Copy editors work in newspapers, magazines, book publishing, broadcasting, corporate communications, and as freelancers. Professional organizations include ACES: The Society for Editing (United States), Editors Canada, and the Chartered Institute of Editing and Proofreading (United Kingdom). Many employers test applicants with standardized editing exercises covering grammar, style, headline writing, and judgment about queries to the author.
