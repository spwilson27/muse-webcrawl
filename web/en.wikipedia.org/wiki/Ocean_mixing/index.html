Ocean mixing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ocean mixing is the process by which water properties — heat, salt, nutrients, gases and momentum — are redistributed within the ocean by turbulent and molecular motion. Mixing controls the stratification of the water column, the ventilation of the deep ocean, the supply of nutrients to sunlit surface waters, and the ocean's uptake of heat and carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

Processes

In the surface mixed layer, wind stress and surface cooling generate turbulence that homogenises the upper ocean. Below the mixed layer, the main source of mixing is the breaking of internal waves, generated by tides flowing over rough topography, winds, and currents interacting with the seabed. Where internal waves break, they produce diapycnal (cross-density-surface) mixing that slowly transforms the cold, dense waters of the abyss so that they can return to the surface, closing the overturning circulation. Double diffusion — arising because heat diffuses faster than salt — produces "salt fingering" and diffusive layering in parts of the ocean. Shear instability (Kelvin–Helmholtz billows) also contributes at fronts and in the equatorial undercurrent.

Measurement

Mixing rates are measured with microstructure profilers that resolve centimetre-scale temperature and shear fluctuations, with tracer-release experiments (deliberately injecting a dye-like tracer such as sulfur hexafluoride and following its spread), and inferred from finescale parameterisations of internal-wave energy. Typical background diapycnal diffusivities in the thermocline are around 10−5 m²/s, rising by orders of magnitude over rough topography such as mid-ocean ridges and seamounts.

Importance

Walter Munk's classic "abyssal recipes" analysis (1966) showed that sustaining the deep overturning circulation requires a basin-averaged diffusivity of about 10−4 m²/s, motivating decades of research into where the necessary mixing occurs. Climate models are sensitive to how mixing is parameterised, because it affects heat uptake, sea level, and the strength of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation.
