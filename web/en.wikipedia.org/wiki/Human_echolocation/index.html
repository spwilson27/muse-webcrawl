Human echolocation

Human echolocation
Method: Tongue clicks; reflected sound
Users: Primarily blind people
Brain regions: Calcarine cortex (visual cortex) recruited
Notable practitioner: Daniel Kish

Human echolocation is the ability of humans to detect objects in their environment by sensing echoes from those objects, by actively creating sounds — for example, by tapping a cane, snapping fingers, or making clicking noises with the mouth. It is used by some blind people to navigate and perceive their surroundings.

Mechanism and research

Expert echolocators such as Daniel Kish, founder of World Access for the Blind, produce sharp palatal clicks and interpret returning echoes to judge distance, size, shape, and texture, enabling activities including hiking and cycling. Brain-imaging studies show echoes activate the calcarine (visual) cortex in blind echolocators, an example of cross-modal neuroplasticity. Experiments demonstrate the skill can be learned by sighted and blind people alike with training.

History

Reports of a "facial vision" sense among blind travellers date to the 18th century; controlled experiments in the 1940s–50s established its auditory basis.
