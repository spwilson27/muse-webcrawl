Urban density

High urban density in a European city district.

Urban density is a concept used in urban planning and demography to describe the concentration of people, dwellings, or built floor area within a given urban area. It is most often measured as population per unit of land (inhabitants per square kilometre or per acre), but planners also use dwelling density, employment density, and floor-area ratio, and distinguish gross density (over a whole district) from net density (over residential land only).

Measurement issues

Density figures depend heavily on the area chosen: administrative boundaries can include parks, water, or farmland, distorting comparisons. Weighted or "perceived" density attempts to capture the density at which the average resident actually lives. Very high reported densities occur in districts of cities such as Dhaka, Manila, Mumbai, and Hong Kong, while historic European cores achieve moderate-high densities with mid-rise perimeter blocks.

Effects and debate

Higher density is associated with more efficient public transit, shorter trips, lower per-capita infrastructure costs and energy use, and vibrant street life — arguments advanced by Jane Jacobs and contemporary movements such as New Urbanism and transit-oriented development. Critics point to congestion, noise, high land prices, overcrowding where density outruns services, and the spread of disease. Research generally distinguishes density from overcrowding: well-designed, well-serviced dense districts differ sharply from informal settlements with equivalent raw densities.

Policy

Policies to manage density include zoning codes, minimum-density requirements near transit, "gentle density" measures such as legalized accessory dwellings and missing-middle housing, and upzoning debates in housing-shortage cities.
