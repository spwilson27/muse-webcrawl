Giotto was a European robotic spacecraft mission from the European Space Agency. The spacecraft was designed to fly by and study Halley's Comet and in doing so became the first spacecraft to make close up observations of a comet. It was named after the early Renaissance painter Giotto di Bondone, who depicted Halley's Comet as the Star of Bethlehem in his 1304–1306 fresco Adoration of the Magi.

Halley encounter

Launched on July 2, 1985, Giotto was part of the international "Halley Armada" of probes sent to meet the comet during its 1986 return, alongside the Soviet Vega 1 and Vega 2 and the Japanese Sakigake and Suisei spacecraft. On March 14, 1986, Giotto passed within about 596 km of the comet's nucleus at a relative speed of about 68.7 km/s. Its multicolour camera returned the first close-up images of a comet nucleus, revealing a dark, irregular "dirty snowball" roughly 15 km long and 7–10 km wide, with jets of gas and dust. About 14 seconds before closest approach, an impact with a dust particle knocked the spacecraft's axis off alignment, interrupting transmissions; it recovered and continued returning data, though the camera was destroyed.

Extended mission

After a period of hibernation, Giotto was reactivated and flew by Earth in 1990 for a gravity assist, then encountered a second comet, 26P/Grigg–Skjellerup, on July 10, 1992, passing within about 200 km — the closest comet approach at the time. The spacecraft was switched off on July 23, 1992, and remains in heliocentric orbit.
