Flash Crash

This article is about the event of 6 May 2010. For the general phenomenon, see Flash crash.

Date6 May 2010
Time2:32–3:00 p.m. EDT (approx.)
LocationUnited States financial markets
EffectDow Jones fell ~998 points intraday (~9%), then largely recovered within minutes

The Flash Crash of 6 May 2010 was a trillion-dollar stock market crash in the United States that lasted approximately 36 minutes. Major equity indices — the S&P 500, Nasdaq Composite and Dow Jones Industrial Average — collapsed and then rebounded with extraordinary speed. The Dow fell nearly 1,000 points (about 9 per cent), its largest intraday point drop at the time, before recovering much of the loss within minutes.

Sequence of events

Markets were already unsettled by the European sovereign debt crisis. At about 2:32 p.m. EDT, a large mutual fund firm executed an automated sale of 75,000 E-mini S&P 500 futures contracts worth about $4.1 billion, using an algorithm keyed to trading volume that did not account for price or elapsed time. High-frequency trading firms absorbed the initial sales, then rapidly resold the contracts among themselves in a "hot potato" pattern while withdrawing liquidity. Prices spiralled downward across futures and equities; some major stocks, including Accenture, traded at one cent, while others printed at absurdly high prices, and more than 20,000 trades were later cancelled as clearly erroneous.

Investigations and causes

A joint CFTC–SEC report (September 2010) identified the large automated sell order as a key trigger interacting with the withdrawal of liquidity by high-frequency traders. In 2015, U.S. authorities charged London-based trader Navinder Singh Sarao with contributing to the crash through "spoofing" — placing and cancelling large orders to manipulate E-mini prices; he pleaded guilty in 2016. The precise weighting of causes remains debated among economists.

Aftermath

Regulators introduced single-stock circuit breakers, revised market-wide circuit breaker rules, tightened "limit up–limit down" bands, and banned stub quotes. The event became the canonical example of the fragility of highly automated, fragmented electronic markets.
