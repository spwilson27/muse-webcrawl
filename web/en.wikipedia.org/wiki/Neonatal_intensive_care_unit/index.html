A neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), also known as an intensive care nursery, is an intensive care unit specialising in the care of ill or premature newborn infants. Neonatal refers to the first 28 days of life. NICUs are staffed by neonatologists, neonatal nurses, respiratory therapists, and other specialists, and provide continuous monitoring and life support.

Patients and levels of care

Infants are admitted to a NICU most commonly for prematurity and its complications — respiratory distress syndrome, infection, congenital anomalies, birth asphyxia, and very low birth weight. In the United States, NICUs are classified from Level I (basic newborn care) to Level IV (the most advanced, capable of surgical repair of complex congenital conditions and care for extremely premature infants).

Care and equipment

Care centres on maintaining body temperature (typically in an incubator or radiant warmer), respiratory support ranging from supplemental oxygen to mechanical ventilation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), intravenous or tube feeding, phototherapy for jaundice, and continuous monitoring of heart rate, breathing, and oxygen saturation. Modern practice emphasises family-centred care, including parental presence, skin-to-skin ("kangaroo") care, and developmental care practices that reduce light and noise exposure.

History

Special care for premature infants developed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries with the introduction of incubators, championed by physicians such as Stéphane Tarnier and Martin Couney. Dedicated neonatal units expanded after the 1960s, and surfactant therapy for respiratory distress syndrome, introduced around 1990, dramatically improved survival for very premature infants. Survival at 22–24 weeks of gestation, once almost unheard of, is now possible at specialised centres, though often with significant risk of long-term complications.
