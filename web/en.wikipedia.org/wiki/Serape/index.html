Serape

A Saltillo-style serape with diamond pattern.

A serape (Spanish: sarape) is a long, blanket-like shawl or cloak traditionally worn by men in Mexico. Typically woven of wool or cotton in bright colors with fringes at the ends, the serape serves as an outer garment, a blanket, and a saddle pad. The most celebrated type is the Saltillo serape, produced in and around the city of Saltillo in the northern state of Coahuila, renowned for its fine weave and intricate central diamond medallion designs.

History

The serape developed during the Spanish colonial period from a blending of Indigenous Mesoamerican weaving traditions and Spanish textile techniques and garment forms. Weaving centers in Tlaxcala, Puebla, and later Saltillo supplied the northern frontier, where the serape became standard dress for horsemen, laborers, and travelers in the cool highlands. In the nineteenth century, Saltillo serapes reached the height of their refinement: densely woven, nearly waterproof tapestries whose patterns combined stepped diamonds, zigzags, and fine striped bands in natural and, later, aniline dyes.

With the opening of trade along the Santa Fe Trail and the annexation of Mexico's northern territories by the United States, serapes circulated widely in the American Southwest, influencing Navajo weaving and becoming part of the visual culture of the region. In the twentieth century the serape became an emblem of Mexican identity and a staple tourist craft, while production shifted largely to cotton and synthetic fibers and to centers such as Tlaxcala and Guadalupe, Zacatecas.

Construction and design

Traditional serapes are woven on treadle looms, often in two panels sewn together along the center, with warp-faced stripes and weft-faced tapestry patterning. Classic Saltillo designs feature a large central diamond with serrated edges, surrounded by repeating geometric motifs. Colors in early examples came from natural dyes, including indigo and cochineal. Contemporary serapes are produced in a range of sizes, from full blankets to small decorative pieces, and the garment is still worn in rural areas and by charros and folk dancers; miniature and mass-produced versions are common in markets throughout Mexico.

In popular culture

Serapes feature prominently in imagery of the Mexican Revolution, in charreada costume, and in Western films. In the United States they are associated with the Southwest borderlands and with Mexican-American cultural celebrations such as Cinco de Mayo and Día de los Muertos festivities, and the garment's bright striped pattern has been adapted into fashion and home furnishings.
