Demand characteristics

In social research, particularly in psychology, demand characteristics are cues in an experiment that convey to participants what behaviour is expected of them or what the experimenter hopes to find. Such cues can bias results if participants change their behaviour to conform to (or deliberately defy) the perceived hypothesis. The term was coined by psychologist Martin T. Orne in 1962.

Mechanisms

Demand characteristics may arise from the experimental setting, instructions, the experimenter's manner, rumours about the study, or the structure of the tasks themselves. Orne proposed that participants often act as "good subjects", motivated to confirm the hypothesis, though others may display reactance or try to appear normal. Related phenomena include the Hawthorne effect, expectancy effects, and social desirability bias.

Mitigation

Researchers reduce demand characteristics through double-blind designs (in which neither participants nor administrators know group assignments), deception with careful debriefing, unobtrusive measures, cover stories, and post-experimental interviews (such as Orne's "post-experimental enquiry") to assess what participants actually believed the study was about.
