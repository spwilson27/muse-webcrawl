Huntington's disease (HD) is an incurable neurodegenerative disorder that is mostly inherited. It results in the death of neurons in the basal ganglia, especially the striatum. Early symptoms are often subtle problems with mood or mental/psychiatric abilities, followed by a general lack of coordination and unsteady gait; as the disease advances, uncoordinated, involuntary body movements known as chorea become more apparent, alongside progressive dementia. Symptoms usually begin between 30 and 50 years of age, and life expectancy after onset is roughly 15 to 20 years.

Cause and genetics

Huntington's disease is caused by an expanded CAG trinucleotide repeat in the huntingtin gene (HTT) on chromosome 4, inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern: each child of an affected parent has a 50 percent chance of inheriting the mutation. Alleles with 36–39 repeats may or may not cause disease (reduced penetrance); 40 or more repeats are fully penetrant. Longer repeats are associated with earlier onset, and repeats can expand between generations (anticipation), particularly with paternal inheritance. The gene was mapped and identified in 1993 by an international consortium, building on studies of a large affected population around Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela.

History

The disease is named after the American physician George Huntington, whose 1872 paper "On Chorea" gave a classic description of its hereditary nature, drawing on cases observed by three generations of his family in East Hampton, New York.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis can be confirmed by genetic testing. Predictive testing is available for at-risk adults, with pre-test counselling recommended because of the psychological implications. There is no cure and no treatment that alters the course of the disease; medications such as tetrabenazine and deutetrabenazine can suppress chorea, and antidepressants, physiotherapy, speech therapy and nutritional support address other features.

Epidemiology

Prevalence is around 5–10 per 100,000 people of European ancestry, and considerably lower in East Asian and African populations.
