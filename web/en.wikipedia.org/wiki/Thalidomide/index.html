Thalidomide


Thalidomide — a sedative later found to be a powerful teratogen

Thalidomide, sold under the brand name Thalomid among others, is an immunomodulatory drug and the prototype of the thalidomide class of drugs. First marketed in 1957 in West Germany under the trade name Contergan, it was originally prescribed as a sedative and hypnotic and was promoted for the treatment of morning sickness in pregnant women. Within a few years it had caused one of the largest man-made medical disasters in history: severe birth defects in more than 10,000 children worldwide.

History

Thalidomide was developed by the German pharmaceutical company Chemie Grünenthal. Because it appeared non-toxic in animal tests and no lethal dose could be established, it was marketed as a remarkably safe sedative, even suitable for children. In the United States, FDA reviewer Frances Kelsey withheld approval over safety concerns, largely sparing the US from the disaster.

In 1961 the Australian obstetrician William McBride and the German paediatrician Widukind Lenz independently linked the drug to a surge in cases of phocomelia — severe malformation of the limbs — and other congenital abnormalities in newborns. The drug was withdrawn from most markets in 1961–62.

The thalidomide scandal

The tragedy transformed drug regulation worldwide. It led to stricter requirements for teratogenicity testing, informed consent, and drug approval, including the 1962 Kefauver–Harris Amendments in the United States and the establishment of bodies such as the UK's Committee on Safety of Drugs. Survivors, known as "thalidomiders", campaigned for decades for compensation and recognition.

Later medical uses

In 1964 Jacob Sheskin discovered that thalidomide dramatically relieved erythema nodosum leprosum, a complication of leprosy. It was subsequently approved for that indication and, in 2006, for the treatment of multiple myeloma. Its immunomodulatory analogues lenalidomide and pomalidomide are now widely used in oncology. Because of its teratogenicity, thalidomide is distributed only under strict pregnancy-prevention programmes.

Mechanism

Thalidomide binds to the protein cereblon, altering the activity of an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex; this both underlies its teratogenic effects on embryonic limb development and its anti-myeloma activity.
