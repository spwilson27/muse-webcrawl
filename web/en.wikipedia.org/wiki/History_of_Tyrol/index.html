History of Tyrol

The history of Tyrol, a region in the Eastern Alps of western Austria and northern Italy, spans from prehistoric settlement — famously including Ötzi the Iceman, whose mummified remains date to about 3300 BC — through Roman rule as part of Raetia and Noricum, medieval county formation, more than five centuries under the Habsburgs, and the division of the region between Austria and Italy after the First World War. The name derives from Tyrol Castle near Merano, seat of the Counts of Tyrol.

Tyrol Castle, from which the region takes its name.

Prehistory and Roman era

The Alpine valleys were settled in the Mesolithic and Neolithic periods; Ötzi, discovered in 1991 in the Ötztal Alps on the Austrian–Italian border, is Europe's oldest known natural human mummy. In 15 BC the Roman legions of Drusus and Tiberius conquered the area, incorporating it into the provinces of Raetia and Noricum. The Brenner route became a principal transalpine corridor.

Middle Ages

After the fall of Rome the region was settled by Bavarians, Lombards and Romansh peoples. In the High Middle Ages the Counts of Tyrol, ministeriales of the bishops of Trent and Brixen, consolidated power; Meinhard II (d. 1295) effectively founded the county. In 1363 Countess Margaret, nicknamed Maultasch, ceded Tyrol to Rudolf IV of Habsburg, beginning centuries of Habsburg rule.

Habsburg era

Tyrol's mines — notably the silver of Schwaz — made it one of the richest Habsburg lands in the sixteenth century. The Peasants' War of 1525 under Michael Gaismair was suppressed. During the Napoleonic Wars, Tyrolean sharpshooters under Andreas Hofer rose against Bavarian and French occupation in 1809; Hofer was captured and executed at Mantua in 1810, becoming a Tyrolean national hero.

Twentieth century

The Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919) divided Tyrol: North and East Tyrol remained with Austria, while South Tyrol and Trentino passed to Italy. Under Fascism, South Tyrol was subjected to forced Italianisation; the 1939 Option Agreement allowed German speakers to emigrate to the Reich or stay and assimilate. The 1946 Gruber–De Gasperi Agreement promised autonomy, fully realised only with the second autonomy statute of 1972. In Austria, Tyrol is today a federal state (Bundesland) with its capital at Innsbruck.
