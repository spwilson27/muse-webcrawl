Shruti (Sanskrit: श्रुति, Śruti, "that which is heard") is the category of Hindu sacred texts regarded as divinely revealed and of the highest authority. It stands in contrast to Smriti ("that which is remembered") — the epics, Purāṇas and law books, which are of human composition and subordinate authority. Shruti comprises the four Vedas — Rigveda, Yajurveda, Sāmaveda and Atharvaveda — together with their attached Brāhmaṇas (ritual commentaries), Āraṇyakas ("forest texts") and Upaniṣads (philosophical teachings).

Transmission

For most of their history the shruti texts were not written down but transmitted orally with extraordinary fidelity. Vedic recitation developed elaborate mnemonic techniques — reciting the text forwards, backwards, and in interlocking permutations (jaṭā, ghana) — together with precise rules of pitch accent and phonetics, preserving the Saṃhitās essentially unchanged for well over two thousand years. Traditional schools regard the Veda as apauruṣeya, "not made by any person": eternal sound perceived by the ancient seers (ṛṣis).

Authority

The orthodox (āstika) schools of Hindu philosophy accept the authority of shruti as a valid means of knowledge (śabda-pramāṇa), and where smriti conflicts with shruti, shruti prevails. The Upaniṣads, forming the concluding portion of shruti, are called Vedānta ("the end of the Veda") and are the principal scriptural basis of Hindu philosophy and theology. Schools such as Buddhism and Jainism, by contrast, defined themselves partly by rejecting Vedic authority.
