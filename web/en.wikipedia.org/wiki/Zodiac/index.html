Zodiac

Zodiac
OriginBabylonian astronomy, by c. 5th century BCE
Division12 signs of 30° each along the ecliptic
SignsAries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpio, Sagittarius, Capricorn, Aquarius, Pisces
SystemsTropical (Western) and sidereal (Vedic)

The zodiac is a belt-shaped region of the sky that extends approximately 8° north and south (as measured in celestial latitude) of the ecliptic — the apparent path of the Sun across the celestial sphere over the course of the year. The paths of the Moon and visible planets are within the belt. In Western astrology, and in earlier Babylonian and Greek astronomy, the zodiac is divided into twelve signs of 30° each: Aries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpio (or Scorpius), Sagittarius, Capricorn, Aquarius, and Pisces. The name derives from the Greek zōidiakòs kýklos, "circle of little animals," since most signs represent animals.

History

Babylonian astronomers divided the ecliptic into twelve equal signs by about the 5th century BCE, correlating them with their calendar. The system passed to the Greeks — Ptolemy's 2nd-century Tetrabiblos codified Western astrology — and independently influenced Indian (Jyotiṣa) astrology, which uses a sidereal zodiac aligned to the fixed stars rather than the equinoxes.

Tropical vs. sidereal

Because of the precession of the equinoxes, the Sun's position against the backdrop of stars shifts by about one sign every 2,150 years, so the tropical signs used in the West no longer coincide with the constellations of the same names; the sidereal zodiac used in Hindu astrology applies a correction (ayanāṃśa) to track the stellar background. A thirteenth constellation, Ophiuchus, also crosses the ecliptic but is not a traditional sign.

Use

The zodiac remains the organizing framework of horoscopic astrology, in which the positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets in the signs at a person's birth are interpreted in a natal chart. In astronomy, the signs survive as a historical coordinate convention; zodiacal light, a faint glow along the ecliptic caused by dust in the plane of the Solar System, takes its name from the belt.
