Weather front
TypesCold front, warm front, stationary front, occluded front
Associated withExtratropical cyclones, air masses

A weather front is a boundary separating air masses with contrasting densities, temperature, and humidity. Fronts are the leading edge of one air mass advancing into another and are the principal cause of significant weather in the middle latitudes: the passage of a front typically brings a change in temperature and humidity, a shift in wind, clouds, and often precipitation. The concept was developed by the Bergen School of meteorologists, including Vilhelm Bjerknes and his son Jacob, during and after the First World War; the term "front" evoked the battle fronts of that war.

Types of fronts

A cold front is the leading edge of a colder air mass displacing a warmer one; denser cold air undercuts the warm air, often producing a narrow band of showers or thunderstorms followed by clearing skies and cooler temperatures. A warm front is the leading edge of warm air advancing over retreating cold air; the warm air rises gradually along a broad slope, producing widespread stratiform cloud and steady precipitation ahead of the surface front, followed by warmer, more humid conditions. A stationary front stalls, with air flowing parallel to the boundary, and can bring days of cloud and rain. An occluded front forms when a cold front overtakes a warm front in a maturing cyclone, lifting the warm sector off the ground.

Fronts on weather maps

On surface analysis charts, a cold front is drawn as a blue line with triangles pointing in the direction of movement, a warm front as a red line with semicircles, a stationary front as alternating triangles and semicircles, and an occluded front in purple with both symbols on the same side.

Frontogenesis

Frontogenesis is the formation or strengthening of a front through the convergence and deformation of the wind field acting on an existing temperature gradient; its decay is frontolysis.
