Vagrancy

Vagrancy
Meaning: Condition of homelessness, wandering without settled residence or employment
Legal history: Criminalised in England from the 14th century; Vagrancy Act 1824
Modern status: Largely repealed or struck down as unconstitutionally vague in many jurisdictions

Vagrancy is the condition of a person who wanders from place to place without a permanent home or regular work — a "vagrant" — and, historically, a criminal offence in many legal systems. Vagrancy laws punished not specific harmful acts but a person's status or lifestyle, and they were used for centuries to police the mobile poor.

History in England

English vagrancy law began in earnest after the Black Death, when the Ordinance of Labourers (1349) and the Statute of Labourers (1351) sought to control a newly mobile workforce. Tudor statutes escalated the penalties: under the Vagabonds Acts of the 16th century, "sturdy beggars" could be whipped, branded, enslaved for repeat offences, or even hanged, while the Elizabethan Poor Law of 1601 made each parish responsible for its own settled poor and the removal of outsiders. The comprehensive Vagrancy Act 1824 criminalised rough sleeping, begging and various forms of street life in England and Wales; remarkably, it remained in force for two centuries, and its repeal was legislated only in 2022, with effect in the following years.

United States

Colonial and later American jurisdictions adopted similar laws, and after the Civil War Southern states used vagrancy statutes among the "Black Codes" to coerce freedpeople into labour contracts. In Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville (1972), the US Supreme Court unanimously struck down a typical vagrancy ordinance as void for vagueness, Justice Douglas writing that wandering and strolling are historically "amenities of life". Modern litigation instead targets specific anti-camping and anti-sleeping ordinances, and the Supreme Court's decision in City of Grants Pass v. Johnson (2024) held that enforcing such ordinances against homeless people does not constitute cruel and unusual punishment.

Modern usage

Today "vagrancy" survives mainly in historical, sociological and some statutory contexts. Contemporary policy debates concern how cities regulate public sleeping, begging and encampments, balancing public order against the rights and needs of homeless people, for whom the condition once simply called vagrancy is now understood primarily as a problem of housing and poverty.
