Forced settlements in the Soviet Union

Period1930s–1950s (largely under Stalin)
AdministrationNKVD / MVD "special settlements"
AffectedMillions: kulaks and deported ethnic groups
DestinationsSiberia, Kazakhstan, Central Asia, the Far North

Forced settlements in the Soviet Union were the result of population transfers carried out by the Soviet authorities, in which entire categories of people — "kulaks" deported during collectivisation, and later whole ethnic groups accused of disloyalty — were exiled to remote "special settlements" under police supervision.

History

Beginning in 1930, some 1.8 million peasants labelled kulaks were deported to Siberia, the Urals and Kazakhstan, where many died of hunger, cold and disease. During the 1930s and the Second World War, ethnic deportations followed a similar pattern: Soviet Koreans (1937), Volga Germans (1941), and in 1943–44 the Karachays, Kalmyks, Chechens, Ingush, Balkars, Crimean Tatars and Meskhetian Turks, among others — in total several million people. Settlers were required to register with the local commandant, could not leave their assigned districts, and supplied coerced labour in forestry, mining and agriculture.

After Stalin

Most special-settlement restrictions were lifted in the mid-1950s under Khrushchev, and many deported peoples were allowed to return home and had their autonomous republics restored; the Crimean Tatars and Volga Germans, however, waited until the late Soviet period or after 1991 for full rehabilitation. The deportations are recognised by a number of parliaments and scholars as crimes, and several as genocide, notably that of the Crimean Tatars.
