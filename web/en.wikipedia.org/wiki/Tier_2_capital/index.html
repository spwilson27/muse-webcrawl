Tier 2 capital

ContextBank regulation (Basel Accords)
ComponentsSubordinated debt, hybrid instruments, certain loan-loss reserves, revaluation reserves
RelatedTier 1 capital, Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1), capital adequacy ratio

Tier 2 capital, under the Basel framework of international banking regulation, is a bank's supplementary capital. It consists of instruments and reserves that are less permanent and lower-quality than Tier 1 capital but can still absorb losses in the event of a bank's failure ("gone concern" capital). Together, Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital make up a bank's total regulatory capital, against which the capital adequacy ratio is measured.

Components

Typical components of Tier 2 capital include:

  • Subordinated term debt with an original maturity of at least five years, whose claims rank below depositors and general creditors;
  • Hybrid capital instruments combining debt and equity features, such as certain contingent convertible instruments not qualifying as Additional Tier 1;
  • General loan-loss reserves (provisions for unidentified losses), up to specified limits;
  • Revaluation reserves arising from the revaluation of assets, subject to discounts or haircuts;
  • Historically, undisclosed reserves and certain perpetual preference shares, subject to jurisdiction.

Basel III rules

Under Basel III, Tier 2 instruments must be subordinated, have a minimum original maturity of five years (with regulatory amortisation of capital recognition in the final five years), and may not contain incentives to redeem (such as interest step-ups). Total capital must be at least 8% of risk-weighted assets, of which Tier 2 may contribute at most 2 percentage points; banks must also hold at least 4.5% Common Equity Tier 1 and 6% Tier 1 capital. Tier 2 differs from Tier 1 in that its instruments typically have fixed maturities and mandatory charges, and they absorb losses mainly in resolution or insolvency rather than on a going-concern basis.
