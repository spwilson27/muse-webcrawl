Turbo roundabout

A turbo roundabout is a type of multi-lane roundabout developed in the Netherlands in the late 1990s, principally by L.G.H. Fortuijn of TU Delft. It modifies the conventional roundabout by requiring drivers to choose their lane before entering, according to their intended exit, and then spiralling them through the junction with physically separated lanes so that no lane changes — and therefore almost no weaving conflicts — occur within the circulatory area.

Design

The defining features are spiral-shaped lane markings radiating from the centre, raised or painted lane dividers on the approaches and within the roundabout, and entry geometry that forces each lane to serve specific exits. Because drivers cannot change lanes mid-junction, a key source of collisions and hesitation in conventional two-lane roundabouts is eliminated. The design typically offers the capacity of a two-lane roundabout with safety closer to a single-lane roundabout, whose conflict points are far fewer.

Use

Since the first examples were built in the Netherlands around 2000, several hundred turbo roundabouts have been constructed there, and the concept has spread to Germany, Poland, the Czech Republic, Scandinavia and other countries. Variants include the "rotor", "egg", "knee" and "stretched-knee" layouts adapted to different traffic-flow patterns at three- and four-arm junctions. Critics note that the design can be less forgiving of driver error — a wrong lane choice cannot be corrected — and that careful signing of destinations on the approach is essential.
