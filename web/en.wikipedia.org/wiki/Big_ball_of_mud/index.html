Big ball of mud

A big ball of mud is a software system that lacks a perceivable architecture, with sprawling, tangled structure, significant irregularities, and "spaghetti code." The term was popularized by Brian Foote and Joseph Yoder in their 1997 paper "Big Ball of Mud," presented at the Pattern Languages of Programs conference, where they treated the big ball of mud as the most prevalent of software "anti-patterns" while also exploring the forces that make such systems so common.

Description

Foote and Yoder defined a big ball of mud as "a casually, even haphazardly, structured system. Its structure has been dictated by expediency, and siphoned through a series of expedient, unprincipled (and often emergency) repairs and patches." Such systems typically exhibit high coupling, duplicated code ("copy-and-paste programming"), unclear module boundaries, and information shared promiscuously among components.

Causes

The paper identified forces that lead systems toward this state, including time pressure, cost pressure, inexperienced developers, staff turnover, changing requirements, and the tendency of successful prototypes and piecemeal patches to persist. Rather than treating the pattern purely as a failure, Foote and Yoder argued that big balls of mud can be a rational response to market pressure — "throwaway" code and premature structuring can be more damaging — but that healthy systems need periodic architectural attention ("shearing layers" and "sweeping it under the rug" are among the strategies they described for managing mud).

Related concepts

The term is now used broadly in software engineering to describe legacy systems maintained without architectural discipline. Related anti-patterns include spaghetti code, the god object, and lava flow (untouched, fossilized code). Remedies commonly proposed include refactoring, strangler-fig migration, and incremental re-architecture.
