Guillain-Barré syndrome

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)
Specialty: Neurology
Symptoms: Muscle weakness beginning in the feet and hands, usually ascending
Causes: Usually triggered by infection; autoimmune
Treatment: Supportive care, intravenous immunoglobulin, plasmapheresis

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a rapid-onset muscle weakness caused by the immune system damaging the peripheral nervous system. It is the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis worldwide. Typically, both sides of the body are involved, and the initial symptoms are changes in sensation or pain together with muscle weakness, beginning in the feet and hands and spreading to the arms and upper body.

Cause

GBS is an autoimmune disease in which antibodies and inflammatory cells attack peripheral nerves, damaging myelin or the axon itself. In about two-thirds of cases it follows an infection, most often Campylobacter jejuni, but also influenza, cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr virus and Zika virus, by a process of molecular mimicry.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture, nerve conduction studies, and cerebrospinal fluid showing raised protein with a normal white cell count. There is no cure, but supportive care — including mechanical ventilation when the breathing muscles fail — together with intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange, shortens recovery. Most people recover substantially, though some have residual weakness; the condition is fatal in roughly 3–7% of cases.

History

The syndrome is named after Georges Guillain and Jean Alexandre Barré, who described it in French soldiers in 1916.
