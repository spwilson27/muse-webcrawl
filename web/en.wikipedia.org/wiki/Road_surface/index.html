Road surface

The road surface, or pavement, is the durable material laid down on an area intended to sustain vehicular or foot traffic. The choice of surface affects driving comfort, safety, noise, cost, and the environment. The two dominant modern surfaces are asphalt concrete and Portland cement concrete, supplemented by gravel, brick, cobblestone, and composite pavements.

History

Roman roads were built with deep foundations of layered stone topped with fitted blocks. After centuries of simpler tracks, the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries brought systematic methods: Thomas Telford's pitched-stone foundations and John Loudon McAdam's compacted broken stone ("macadam"), which relied on angular aggregate interlocking under traffic. Tar-bound macadam ("tarmac") and then asphalt concrete followed with the motor age, while the first concrete roads appeared in the late nineteenth century.

Asphalt concrete

Asphalt pavement consists of mineral aggregate bound with bitumen. It is laid hot, compacted, and can be opened to traffic quickly. Engineers class it as a "flexible pavement," distributing loads through successive layers to the subgrade. Asphalt is relatively quiet and easy to repair, but it softens in heat, is susceptible to rutting and oxidation, and typically requires resurfacing every 15–20 years. Recycled asphalt pavement is one of the most recycled materials in the world.

Concrete

Concrete pavement ("rigid pavement") uses a slab of Portland cement concrete that spreads loads by its own stiffness. Properly built concrete roads can last 30–40 years or more with limited maintenance, and they resist rutting and fuel spills, but they cost more initially, take longer to cure, and are noisier unless textured or ground. Joints are cut into the slab to control cracking.

Other surfaces and considerations

Gravel and dirt roads still constitute a large share of the world's road mileage, particularly in rural areas. Historic cobblestone and sett surfaces persist in old city centers. Modern practice considers skid resistance, surface drainage, rolling resistance (and thus fuel use), tire–road noise, and the urban heat island effect; porous asphalt and permeable pavements are used where stormwater management matters. Surface distresses—potholes, cracking, rutting, and raveling—drive maintenance programs measured by indices such as the International Roughness Index.
