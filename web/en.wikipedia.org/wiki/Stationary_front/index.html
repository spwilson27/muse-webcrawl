Stationary front

The surface-analysis symbol for a stationary front.

A stationary front is a weather front — the boundary between two air masses — that is moving very slowly or not at all. On weather maps it is drawn as a line with alternating red semicircles pointing toward the colder air and blue triangles pointing toward the warmer air, on opposite sides of the line, indicating that neither air mass is displacing the other.

Formation and behavior

Stationary fronts form when a cold front or warm front stalls, typically when upper-level winds blow parallel to the front rather than across it, so the surface boundary makes little progress. The two air masses retain their temperature contrast, but the front itself may remain in place for days.

Weather

Weather along a stationary front resembles that of a warm front: widespread stratiform cloud, light to moderate precipitation, and fog on the cold side of the boundary. Because the front does not move, precipitation can train over the same area for many hours, making stationary fronts a common cause of flooding. If waves develop along the front — low-pressure disturbances traveling along the boundary — it may resume motion as a cold or warm front, or a new cyclone may form in a process called frontogenesis.
