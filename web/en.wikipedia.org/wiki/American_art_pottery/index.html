American art pottery
PeriodRoughly 1870s–1950s
Notable makersRookwood, Grueby, Teco, Van Briggle, Roseville, Weller
CentresCincinnati; Zanesville, Ohio; Boston; Colorado Springs

American art pottery refers to the aesthetically driven, often handcrafted ceramic ware produced by American potteries from the 1870s through the mid-20th century. It emerged alongside the Arts and Crafts movement and was displayed to acclaim at the Philadelphia Centennial Exposition of 1876, where American firms sought to rival European and Japanese art ceramics.

Major potteries

The Rookwood Pottery of Cincinnati, founded in 1880 by Maria Longworth Nichols, became the largest and most celebrated American art pottery, famed for its Standard Glaze and iris wares and for employing talented decorators. Other leading firms included Grueby Faience of Boston, known for matte green glazes; the Gates Potteries' Teco ware near Chicago; Van Briggle Pottery in Colorado Springs; and the Ohio firms of Roseville, Weller and McCoy, which brought art pottery to a broader commercial market.

Styles and techniques

American art pottery embraced hand-painted underglaze decoration, carved and modelled ornament, crystalline and matte glazes, and designs inspired by nature, Native American art and Art Nouveau. Many firms employed women artists as decorators, making the industry an important opening for women in the applied arts.

Decline and collecting

The market contracted after the First World War and again during the Great Depression, though some firms survived into the 1950s and 1960s. Today American art pottery is widely collected, and fine pieces by Rookwood, Grueby and other makers command high prices at auction.
