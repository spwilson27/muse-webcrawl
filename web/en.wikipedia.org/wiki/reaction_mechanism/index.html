Reaction mechanism

In chemistry, a reaction mechanism is the step-by-step sequence of elementary reactions by which overall chemical change occurs. A chemical mechanism is a theoretical conjecture that tries to describe in detail what takes place at each stage of an overall chemical reaction. The detailed steps are usually not directly observable; a proposed mechanism is chosen because it is thermodynamically and kinetically feasible and is supported by experimental evidence, such as the isolation of intermediates or kinetic studies.

Elementary steps

Each elementary step describes the collision or rearrangement of a small number of species and is characterised by its molecularity: unimolecular (one molecule reacts or decomposes), bimolecular (two molecules collide), or, rarely, termolecular. The slowest elementary step — the rate-determining step — governs the overall rate of reaction, and the sum of all elementary steps must equal the overall balanced equation.

Intermediates and arrow pushing

Species produced in one step and consumed in a later step are called intermediates; they may be radicals, carbocations, carbanions or other transient structures. In organic chemistry, mechanisms are conventionally drawn using "arrow pushing" (curved arrows), in which a full-headed arrow shows the movement of an electron pair and a half-headed ("fishhook") arrow shows the movement of a single electron in radical reactions.

Common mechanisms

Well-known named mechanisms include the SN1 and SN2 nucleophilic substitutions, the E1 and E2 eliminations, electrophilic aromatic substitution, free-radical chain reactions (with initiation, propagation and termination steps), and catalytic cycles in organometallic and enzymatic chemistry.

Determining a mechanism

Evidence used to propose or refute a mechanism includes the rate law obtained from kinetics experiments, the stereochemical outcome of the reaction, isotopic labelling studies (for example kinetic isotope effects), crossover experiments, spectroscopy of reactive intermediates, and computational modelling of transition states.
