Mudbrick

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

A mudbrick or mud-brick is an air-dried brick made of a mixture of mud — loam, clay, sand and water — usually with a binding material such as chopped straw, rice husks or dung, pressed into a mould and dried in the sun. Mudbrick is one of the oldest building materials in the world, closely related to adobe, cob and rammed earth, and remains in widespread use in arid regions today.

History

Mudbricks were used in the ancient Near East from the Neolithic period; the walls of Jericho and the houses of Çatalhöyük were built of them. In Mesopotamia, where stone and timber were scarce, entire cities, temples and ziggurats were raised in mudbrick, sometimes faced with baked brick set in bitumen. Ancient Egypt built houses, granaries and fortresses of Nile mud brick, reserving stone for temples and tombs. The Indus Valley, pre-Columbian Americas (adobe), and sub-Saharan Africa all developed major mudbrick traditions — the Great Mosque of Djenné in Mali, the largest mud-brick building in the world, is still replastered annually in a community festival.

Properties and use

Mudbrick is cheap, locally available and has excellent thermal mass, keeping interiors cool by day and warm at night in desert climates. Its weakness is water: unprotected walls erode in rain, so buildings require wide eaves, plaster renders and regular maintenance. Typical mixes are roughly clay and sand balanced so bricks neither crack nor crumble, with straw to bind. Modern interest in low-carbon construction has revived mudbrick and adobe for sustainable housing, while in the developing world it remains the default material for rural homes.
