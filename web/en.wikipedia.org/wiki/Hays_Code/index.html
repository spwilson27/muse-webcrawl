The Motion Picture Production Code, commonly called the Hays Code, was a set of industry moral guidelines for the production of most United States motion pictures released by the major studios from 1934 to 1968. It is also often known as the Breen Code, after Joseph Breen, the administrator of the Production Code Administration (PCA) for most of its strict-enforcement period. The code took its popular name from Will H. Hays, president of the Motion Picture Producers and Distributors of America (MPPDA), who presided over its adoption in 1930.

Background and contents

Facing scandals, sensational early sound films ("pre-Code" cinema), and the threat of federal censorship and boycotts — notably by the Catholic Legion of Decency, founded in 1934 — the studios agreed to submit scripts and finished films to the PCA, without whose seal a film could not be shown in major theatres. The Code's general principles required that no picture "lower the moral standards of those who see it", that correct standards of life be presented, and that law, natural or human, not be ridiculed nor sympathy created for its violation. Detailed "Don'ts and Be Carefuls" prohibited, among much else, explicit nudity and suggestive dances, ridicule of the clergy, depiction of illegal drug use, miscegenation, "sex perversion" (a euphemism covering homosexuality), profanity, and unpunished crime; criminals and adulterers had to be punished, and married couples were famously shown sleeping in twin beds.

Effects and erosion

The Code shaped classical Hollywood storytelling for three decades: innuendo replaced explicitness, crime films ended in retribution, and writers became adept at double meanings (as in screwball comedy and the films of Ernst Lubitsch and Billy Wilder). Enforcement weakened in the 1950s under pressure from television, foreign films, independent producers such as Otto Preminger (who released The Moon Is Blue in 1953 without a seal), and court decisions treating film as protected speech (Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson, 1952).

Replacement

By the 1960s the Code was untenable; in November 1968 the new Motion Picture Association of America president Jack Valenti replaced it with the MPAA film rating system (G, M/PG, R, X), which — in theory — advised rather than censored.
