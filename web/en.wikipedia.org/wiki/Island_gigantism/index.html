Island gigantism

Galápagos tortoises are among the best-known examples of island gigantism.

Island gigantism, or insular gigantism, is a biological phenomenon in which the size of an animal species isolated on an island increases dramatically in comparison to its mainland relatives. Island gigantism is one aspect of the more general "island effect" or "Foster's rule," which posits that when mainland animals colonize islands, small species tend to evolve larger bodies, and large species tend to evolve smaller bodies (the latter known as island dwarfism).

Following the arrival of humans and associated introduced predators, many of these giant island species have gone extinct, including the moa of New Zealand, the elephant birds of Madagascar, and numerous giant rodents and tortoises.

Causes

Several factors are thought to drive insular gigantism. Released from mainland predators and competitors, island populations may evolve larger body size with little penalty. Limited territory favors efficient use of resources, and larger size can aid in defending territory, digesting coarse island vegetation, and surviving periods of scarcity by storing energy. In ectotherms such as tortoises and iguanas, large size also helps with thermoregulation.

Examples

  • Giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands and Seychelles.
  • Moa, giant flightless birds of New Zealand, the largest reaching about 3.6 metres (12 ft) in height.
  • Elephant birds of Madagascar, among the largest birds ever known.
  • Komodo dragon of Indonesia, a giant monitor lizard.
  • Giant rats and rodents such as the extinct Canariomys of the Canary Islands.
  • Giant insects, including the wētā of New Zealand and the Lord Howe Island stick insect.
