Apologetics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Apologetics (from Greek ἀπολογία, apología, "speaking in defense") is the religious discipline of defending religious doctrines through systematic argumentation and discourse. Early Christian writers who defended their beliefs against critics and recommended their faith to outsiders were called Christian apologists. In contemporary usage, the term often refers especially to the defence of Christianity, though it is also applied to the defence of other religions and, by extension, any systematic defence of a position.

History

The genre has roots in classical antiquity: Plato's Apology presents Socrates' defence of himself at his trial, and Jewish writers such as Josephus defended Judaism against pagan critics in works like Against Apion. In the second century, Christian apologists including Justin Martyr, Athenagoras, Tatian, and Tertullian addressed apologies to Roman emperors and the educated public, arguing against charges of atheism and immorality, asserting the antiquity and rationality of Christian teaching, and pleading for toleration.

During the Middle Ages, apologetic argument took forms such as the philosophical proofs for the existence of God developed by Anselm of Canterbury and Thomas Aquinas, and in the Islamic world the discipline of kalām defended Islamic doctrine through rational argument. In the modern period, apologists such as Blaise Pascal addressed religious scepticism, while the nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw the rise of historical apologetics concerning the resurrection of Jesus and the reliability of the Bible, alongside counter-apologetics from critics of religion.

Approaches

Several schools of Christian apologetic method are commonly distinguished:

  • Classical apologetics proceeds in two steps: first establishing the existence of God through philosophical arguments (cosmological, teleological, ontological, moral), then presenting historical evidence for Christianity.
  • Evidential apologetics emphasises historical and empirical evidence, such as the alleged evidence for the resurrection of Jesus.
  • Presuppositional apologetics, associated with Cornelius Van Til and Greg Bahnsen, argues that Christian presuppositions are necessary for intelligibility itself and that arguments must proceed on that basis.
  • Cumulative case apologetics builds an informal case from multiple lines of evidence, analogous to a legal brief.
  • Reformed epistemology, associated with Alvin Plantinga, holds that belief in God can be "properly basic" and warranted without argument.

Apologetics in other religions

Analogous defensive disciplines exist in other traditions. Islamic kalām and works such as Al-Ghazali's engage philosophical criticism of Islam. In Hindu thought, schools such as Nyāya developed arguments for the existence of God (Īśvara) against Buddhist and materialist critics. Buddhist, Jewish, Baháʼí, and Mormon thinkers have likewise produced apologetic literature responding to critics and presenting their respective traditions as rational and true.

Criticism

Critics of apologetics argue that it begins from a predetermined conclusion and selects evidence accordingly, functioning as advocacy rather than open inquiry. Some theologians have likewise been suspicious of apologetics, holding that faith should not rest on human argument. Others defend the discipline as a legitimate form of persuasion and intellectual accountability.
