Gland

A gland is a cell, group of cells, or organ of endothelial origin that discharges a substance for use elsewhere in the body or in a body cavity, or that eliminates it from the body. Glands are found throughout the bodies of humans and other animals, and their products include hormones, enzymes, and fluids such as saliva, sweat, and milk.

Types

Glands are divided based on their function into two groups. Endocrine glands are ductless and secrete their products, hormones, directly into the blood that ultimately reaches the target tissue. Examples include the pituitary gland, thyroid gland, and adrenal glands. Exocrine glands secrete their products through a duct onto an epithelial surface, such as the skin or the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract; examples include sweat glands, salivary glands, and mammary glands. Some organs, such as the pancreas, have both endocrine and exocrine functions.

Structure

Exocrine glands typically consist of a secretory unit and a duct; they may be classified by the shape of the secretory portion (tubular, alveolar/acinar, or tubuloalveolar) and by the manner of secretion — merocrine, apocrine, or holocrine. The secretions of endocrine glands enter the interstitial fluid and diffuse into the bloodstream without flowing through a duct.

Clinical significance

Disorders of glands include hypersecretion and hyposecretion of hormones, inflammation (such as sialadenitis of the salivary glands), and benign or malignant tumors such as adenomas. Adenocarcinoma is a cancer arising in glandular tissue.
