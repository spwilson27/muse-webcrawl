Leopold I (16 December 1790 – 10 December 1865) was the first King of the Belgians, reigning from 1831 until his death. A German prince of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, he was the founder of the Belgian monarchy and a central figure in the diplomacy of nineteenth-century Europe — uncle of both Queen Victoria and Prince Albert.

Early life and marriages

Leopold served as a cavalry general in Russian service against Napoleon. In 1816 he married Princess Charlotte of Wales, heir presumptive to the British throne; her death in childbirth in 1817 devastated him. He declined the throne of Greece in 1830, but in 1831 accepted election by the Belgian National Congress as king of the newly independent Belgium, swearing allegiance to the constitution on 21 July 1831 — still Belgium's national day. His second marriage, to Louise of Orléans, daughter of King Louis-Philippe of France, secured French support for the new state.

Reign

Leopold defended Belgium against Dutch attempts at reconquest, promoted its first railways (continental Europe's first line opened in 1835), and used his vast family network — earning the nickname "the uncle of Europe" — to make Brussels a diplomatic crossroads, including arranging the marriage of Victoria and Albert. He was succeeded by his son Leopold II.
