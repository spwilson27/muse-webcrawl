Isotropic radiator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An isotropic radiator is a theoretical point source of electromagnetic (or sound) waves that radiates the same intensity of radiation in all directions. Because it is spherically symmetric, it is used as a reference against which the gain and directivity of real antennas are measured, in units of dBi (decibels relative to isotropic).

Properties

At any distance r from the source, the power density of an isotropic radiator of total radiated power P is P/(4πr2), by conservation of energy. A hypothetical isotropic antenna has a gain of exactly 1 (0 dBi) in every direction and zero directivity. It is not physically realisable: electromagnetic theory forbids a radiator with a truly uniform pattern — every real antenna has some directionality, and there is no known way to produce the required uniform spherical wavefront from a point source.

Use in antenna theory

The isotropic radiator is the reference for the gain of all other antennas: a directional antenna with a gain of 10 dBi, for instance, produces ten times the power density in its main beam as an isotropic radiator fed with the same power. The concept is distinct from the electric dipole (1.64, or 2.15 dBi) and half-wave dipole references sometimes used instead.

Analogues

Approximate physical analogues include the Sun as a near-isotropic source of light at interplanetary distances, and omnidirectional microphones, which are sometimes described as "isotropic" receivers. The term also appears in acoustics for an idealised point source of sound.
