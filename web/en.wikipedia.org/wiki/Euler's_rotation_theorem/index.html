Euler's rotation theorem

In geometry, Euler's rotation theorem states that, in three-dimensional space, any displacement of a rigid body such that a point on the rigid body remains fixed is equivalent to a single rotation about some axis that runs through the fixed point. It also means that the composition of two rotations is a rotation. The theorem is named after Leonhard Euler, who proved it in 1775.

Statement and proof idea

Consider the points of a sphere centred on the fixed point. A rotation of the body moves points of the sphere along its surface; composing two rotations moves a given point along two arcs of great circles. Euler showed geometrically that one can always find two points on the sphere that are unchanged by the motion: the endpoints of the rotation axis. In modern linear-algebra terms, a rotation is represented by a 3×3 orthogonal matrix with determinant +1; its characteristic polynomial is cubic with real coefficients, so it has a real eigenvalue, and orthogonality forces that eigenvalue to be +1, giving an eigenvector that defines the axis.

Consequences

The theorem underlies the use of a single axis–angle pair to describe any three-dimensional orientation, and it connects with Euler's theorem on the motion of rigid bodies: the general motion of a body with one fixed point is a rotation about an instantaneous axis through that point. Chasles' theorem extends the result to arbitrary displacements, decomposing them into a translation along, and a rotation about, a single screw axis. The quaternion representation of rotations is closely related: every unit quaternion encodes exactly one such axis–angle rotation.
