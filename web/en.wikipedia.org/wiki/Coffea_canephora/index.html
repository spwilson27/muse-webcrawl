Coffea canephora

Coffea canephora
Kingdom: Plantae
Order: Gentianales
Family: Rubiaceae
Genus: Coffea
Species: C. canephora
Common name: Robusta coffee

Coffea canephora (especially its variety C. canephora var. robusta) is a species of coffee plant native to central and western sub-Saharan Africa. Together with Coffea arabica, it accounts for nearly all of the world's coffee production; robusta beans make up roughly 30–40% of global output.

Description

The plant is a robust shrub or small tree growing up to 10 metres tall in the wild, though pruned much lower in cultivation. It has larger, thinner leaves than arabica, white fragrant flowers, and round cherries that ripen to red. The beans are smaller and rounder than arabica beans and contain roughly twice as much caffeine, as well as more chlorogenic acids, giving robusta coffee a stronger, harsher, more bitter flavour with earthy and woody notes.

Cultivation

Robusta is easier to cultivate than arabica: it thrives at lower altitudes (sea level to about 800 m), tolerates higher temperatures and heavier rainfall, and is considerably more resistant to pests and diseases, including the coffee leaf rust fungus Hemileia vastatrix — hence its name. It is self-incompatible and requires cross-pollination, which increases genetic diversity but makes cropping less uniform. Major producers include Vietnam (the world's largest), Brazil (where it is called conilon), Indonesia, India, and Uganda.

Uses

Robusta is used mainly in instant coffee, in espresso blends — where its thick crema and body are valued — and as a filler in ground coffee blends. Fine robusta, carefully grown and processed, has begun to attract specialty interest, but the species is still generally traded at a discount to arabica.

History

The species was recognised scientifically in the late nineteenth century, after plants were collected in the Congo Basin. Cultivation spread rapidly in the early twentieth century, especially in colonial plantations in Africa and Southeast Asia, filling the gap left where coffee leaf rust had devastated arabica plantings.
