Missing women

"Missing women" is a term coined by economist Amartya Sen in a widely cited 1990 essay to describe the shortfall of women relative to the number that would be expected if women received similar care in health, medicine and nutrition as men. Sen estimated that more than 100 million women were "missing", principally in Asia and North Africa, because of sex-selective abortion, infanticide, and unequal treatment of girls and women.

Causes

Demographers attribute the phenomenon to a combination of cultural son preference, sex-selective abortion made possible by prenatal sex determination, differential access to food and medical care for girls, and maternal mortality. Skewed sex ratios at birth have been recorded in parts of China, India, the Caucasus and elsewhere.

Debate and estimates

Later research has revised Sen's estimates and examined how much of the deficit reflects mortality differences, under-registration, or sex-selective abortion, and has studied the social consequences of large cohorts of surplus men, including effects on marriage markets and crime.

Policy responses

Governments have responded with bans on prenatal sex determination, incentives for daughters, and public campaigns against son preference, with varying success in normalizing sex ratios.
