Mimetic theory

Girard's triangular model of desire: subject, model (mediator), and object.

Mimetic theory is a theory of human culture and desire developed by the French historian and philosopher René Girard (1923–2015), first presented in Deceit, Desire, and the Novel (1961) and extended in Violence and the Sacred (1972) and Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World (1978).

Mimetic desire

The theory's core claim is that human desire is fundamentally mimetic, or imitative: people desire objects not spontaneously but according to the desire of a model or mediator. Desire is thus triangular — subject, model, and object — and because subject and model converge on the same object, the model becomes a rival, generating conflict. In internal mediation the model is close enough to compete directly; in external mediation (as with a fictional hero or saint) rivalry does not arise.

The scapegoat mechanism

When mimetic rivalry spreads through a community, Girard argued, it produces a sacrificial crisis resolved by the scapegoat mechanism: the community unites against a single victim, whose expulsion or murder restores peace and who is subsequently divinized, becoming the seed of myth, ritual, and prohibition — the elementary forms of religion and culture. Girard held that the Judeo-Christian scriptures progressively expose this mechanism from the victim's perspective, culminating in the Passion.

Influence

Mimetic theory has influenced literary criticism, anthropology, theology, and social science, and has attracted followers such as the investor Peter Thiel, who has cited Girard's account of imitation and competition in business thought.
