Leo Joseph Ryan Jr. (May 5, 1925 – November 18, 1978) was an American politician and a member of the United States House of Representatives from California. He was the first—and remains the only—member of the U.S. Congress killed in the line of duty by assassination in an international incident while on a fact-finding mission: he was murdered at an airstrip in Guyana during his investigation of the Peoples Temple settlement at Jonestown, hours before the mass murder-suicide there in which more than 900 people died.

Early life and career

Ryan was born in Lincoln, Nebraska, and served in the United States Navy during the Second World War, including as a submariner. After the war he became a teacher and school administrator in California. His political career began on the city council of South San Francisco, where he served as mayor, followed by election to the California State Assembly in 1962. Known for immersive fact-finding, Ryan famously taught in a Watts classroom after the 1965 Los Angeles riots and, while investigating prison conditions, spent ten days as an inmate at Folsom Prison. He was elected to Congress in 1972, representing the San Francisco Peninsula.

Jonestown investigation and death

In 1978, concerned by reports from constituents whose relatives had joined Jim Jones's Peoples Temple commune in Guyana—including allegations of abuse, forced labor, and people being held against their will—Ryan organized a congressional delegation to visit Jonestown. After touring the settlement on 17–18 November, Ryan offered passage out to any residents who wished to leave; a number of defectors accepted. As the party prepared to depart from the Port Kaituma airstrip on 18 November 1978, members of the Temple's security unit opened fire, killing Ryan, three journalists, and one defector, and wounding nine others. Later that day, Jones directed the mass poisoning at Jonestown.

Legacy

Ryan was posthumously awarded the Congressional Gold Medal in 1983, and the Leo J. Ryan Memorial Park in Foster City, California, is named in his honour. His daughter's later advocacy and the annual Leo Ryan Award reflect his reputation for hands-on oversight of government and institutions.
