In human anatomy, the wrist (also called the carpus) is the complex joint region connecting the hand to the forearm. It consists of eight small carpal bones arranged in two rows, together with the lower ends of the radius and ulna, numerous ligaments, and the tendons, nerves and blood vessels passing into the hand.

Structure

The carpal bones are, in the proximal row from thumb side to little-finger side, the scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum and pisiform; and in the distal row, the trapezium, trapezoid, capitate and hamate. The radiocarpal joint, between the radius and the proximal carpal row, is the main wrist joint and permits flexion, extension and sideways deviation; rotation of the hand takes place at the forearm joints.

On the palm side, the carpal bones and the flexor retinaculum form the carpal tunnel, through which the median nerve and the flexor tendons pass.

Clinical significance

The wrist is vulnerable to injury and disease. A common injury is fracture of the distal radius (Colles' fracture) or of the scaphoid, typically from a fall onto an outstretched hand. Compression of the median nerve in the carpal tunnel causes carpal tunnel syndrome, with numbness and tingling in the hand. Inflammatory arthritis, ganglion cysts and tendon disorders such as De Quervain's tenosynovitis also affect the wrist. The radial pulse is conventionally felt just above the wrist on the thumb side.
