Hart–Devlin debate

The Hart–Devlin debate is a famous debate in legal philosophy between legal philosopher H. L. A. Hart and judge Patrick Devlin concerning the enforcement of morality through the criminal law. Sparked by the 1957 Wolfenden Report, which recommended the decriminalisation of homosexual acts between consenting adults in private, the debate addressed whether society may use law to enforce its shared moral code even where conduct harms no one else.

Background

The Wolfenden Committee reported in 1957 that there is a realm of private morality and immorality which is "not the law's business". Lord Devlin responded in his 1959 Maccabaean Lecture, "The Enforcement of Morals", arguing that a society is held together by a shared morality, so that suppression of vice is as much the law's function as suppression of subversion; conduct that provokes intolerance, indignation, and disgust in the reasonable person may therefore be criminalised.

Hart's response

Hart, in a series of lectures and his book Law, Liberty, and Morality (1963), defended the Wolfenden position, drawing on John Stuart Mill's harm principle. He distinguished between the fact that a society's morality may change and the claim that any departure from it destroys society, demanding evidence that private immorality causes social disintegration. He argued that Devlin conflated "positive morality" — the morality a society happens to accept — with "critical morality", the principles by which existing practices should be judged. Hart also warned against treating majority disgust alone as a sufficient ground for coercion, comparing moral populism to the rule of the mob.

Legacy

The debate shaped Anglo-American jurisprudence on morals legislation, influencing later discussions of victimless crimes, decriminalisation of homosexuality (achieved in England and Wales by the Sexual Offences Act 1967), and liberal theories of the state. Ronald Dworkin and others continued the exchange, and it remains a standard topic in legal philosophy curricula.
