Loki Patera /ˈloʊki pəˈtɪərə/ is the largest volcanic depression on Jupiter's moon Io, some 202 kilometres (126 mi) across. It is an active lava lake lying within a caldera-like depression (a patera), with a solidified crust that periodically founders and renews itself. Named after the Norse god Loki, it is the most powerful volcano in the solar system, radiating more heat than all of Earth's volcanoes combined.

Observations and behaviour

Loki was first imaged by Voyager 1 in 1979, which revealed its distinctive dark, U-shaped lake with a lighter "island" in the middle that has remained unchanged in position for decades. Ground-based infrared observations since the 1980s have shown that Loki brightens dramatically on a roughly periodic cycle—about every 540 days in modern data—as the lake's crust overturns: the chilled surface crust slowly thickens, becomes unstable, and sinks in a wave that sweeps across the lake, exposing fresh molten lava that then cools in its turn. The lake's total thermal output has been estimated at around 10–25 terawatts during active phases. NASA's Juno spacecraft imaged Loki Patera at high resolution during its close Io flybys in 2023–24, resolving the glassy lake surface and numerous small islands.

Loki's enormous, steady heat flow and regular overturn cycle make it a natural laboratory for studying lava lakes, comparable in process—though vastly greater in scale—to terrestrial lava lakes such as those at Kīlauea and Erta Ale.
