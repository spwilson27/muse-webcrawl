The Galactic Center is the barycenter of the Milky Way galaxy, located in the direction of the constellation Sagittarius about 26,700 light-years from Earth. Its exact location coincides with the compact radio source Sagittarius A*, a supermassive black hole of about 4.3 million solar masses. Interstellar dust obscures the center at visible wavelengths, so it is observed mainly in radio, infrared and X-ray light.

Sagittarius A*

Observations of stars orbiting Sagittarius A* — notably the star S2, which completes an orbit in about 16 years — provide the strongest evidence for a supermassive black hole at the center. In 2020 the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded in part to Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez for this work. In May 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released the first image of Sagittarius A*, showing a glowing ring of hot gas around the black hole's shadow.

Environment

The central parsecs contain a dense nuclear star cluster, young massive stars arranged in disks, and streamers of ionized gas such as the minispiral. A few hundred light-years across lies the central molecular zone, one of the most extreme star-forming environments in the galaxy. Enormous gamma-ray and X-ray structures called the Fermi bubbles extend thousands of light-years above and below the galactic plane, evidence of past energetic outbursts from the center.

Discovery

Harlow Shapley located the galactic center direction in 1918 from the distribution of globular clusters. Karl Jansky detected radio emission from the region in 1933, and Balick and Brown identified the compact source Sagittarius A* in 1974.
