Infantile hemangioma

Infantile hemangioma
Specialty: Dermatology, pediatrics
Symptoms: Raised red or purple skin lesion
Onset: First weeks of life
Prognosis: Usually resolves spontaneously

An infantile hemangioma (IH) is a type of benign vascular tumor that affects babies. It appears as a red or purple raised mark on the skin, often called a "strawberry mark" when superficial. Infantile hemangiomas are the most common tumors of infancy, occurring in roughly 4–5% of infants, and are more frequent in girls, premature infants, and infants of low birth weight.

Signs and symptoms

Lesions typically appear in the first days to weeks of life, grow rapidly during a proliferative phase over the first few months, and then slowly involute over several years. Most are small and harmless, but large or strategically located lesions—near the eye, airway, or lip—can cause complications such as ulceration, bleeding, visual obstruction, or breathing difficulty.

Cause and pathophysiology

The exact cause is unknown. The tumors consist of proliferating endothelial cells that characteristically express the glucose transporter GLUT1, a marker that distinguishes infantile hemangioma from other vascular anomalies. Hypoxia and placental embolization theories have been proposed.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually clinical. Imaging such as ultrasound or MRI may be used for deep or atypical lesions. Multiple cutaneous hemangiomas can be associated with visceral involvement, and large facial hemangiomas may occur in PHACE syndrome.

Treatment

Many lesions require no treatment beyond observation. Since 2008, oral propranolol has become the first-line therapy for hemangiomas needing treatment; topical timolol is used for small superficial lesions. Other options include corticosteroids, laser therapy, and surgery in selected cases.
