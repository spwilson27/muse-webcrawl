Neopterygii

Neopterygii is a subclass of ray-finned fishes (Actinopterygii) that includes the overwhelming majority of living fish species. The name means "new fins," reflecting evolutionary changes in the fins, jaws, and scales that distinguish the group from more primitive ray-finned fishes such as sturgeons and bichirs. Neopterygians comprise the gars and bowfin (Holostei) and the Teleostei, which alone contain about 30,000 species — roughly half of all vertebrates.

Evolution

Neopterygians first appear in the fossil record in the late Permian to Triassic periods and diversified greatly during the Mesozoic. Early neopterygians experimented with deep bodies, durophagous (shell-crushing) dentitions, and varied fin shapes. Teleosts arose in the Triassic–Jurassic and went on to dominate marine and freshwater ecosystems; holosteans, once diverse, survive only as seven gar species and the bowfin.

Characteristics

Key neopterygian features include a more mobile upper jaw permitting protrusion and suction feeding, a simplified fin skeleton with fewer branched fin rays allowing finer control, an interoperculum and other changes to the gill-cover apparatus, and — in teleosts — a homocercal tail and further modifications of the jaw and swim bladder. These innovations underlie the feeding versatility and locomotor diversity of the group.

Classification

Modern classifications divide Neopterygii into:

  • Holostei — gars (Lepisosteiformes) and bowfin (Amiiformes); the monophyly of this grouping, long doubted, is supported by molecular studies.
  • Teleostei — the teleosts, divided into four cohorts (Elopomorpha, Osteoglossomorpha, Clupeocephala, and others).
