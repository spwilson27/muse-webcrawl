Piccolo

The piccolo (Italian for 'small') is a half-size flute and a member of the woodwind family of musical instruments. Sometimes called the ottavino in Italian (on scores: flauto piccolo), it is the smallest and highest-pitched member of the flute family, sounding one octave higher than written. The modern piccolo has most of the same fingerings as the standard transverse flute, but its smaller bore and embouchure require adjusted technique. In the orchestra, the piccolo player is often also a flautist who switches instruments.

Description

The piccolo is pitched in C (some are in D♭), sounding an octave above the concert flute. Traditional bodies are of wood (grenadilla or cocuswood), with metal and composite models used in marching bands for durability. Its conical or cylindrical bore gives a bright, penetrating tone that can be heard over a full orchestra or band, making it ideal for brilliant effects, bird imitations, and storm scenes.

History and repertoire

Small flutes existed in military music for centuries; the piccolo entered the orchestra in the late 18th and early 19th centuries—Beethoven used it in the finales of the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Symphonies for storm and triumphal brilliance. Berlioz, Mahler (who sometimes wrote for two or three piccolos), Tchaikovsky (Fourth Symphony), and Shostakovich exploited its shrill or comic colours. Its most famous solo is the piccolo obbligato in John Philip Sousa's march "The Stars and Stripes Forever." Concertos exist by Lowell Liebermann and others, and Vivaldi's concertos for "flautino" are often played on the piccolo.

Use in ensembles

In wind bands the piccolo is nearly indispensable, typically playing the top line an octave above the flutes. Drum and bugle corps and marching bands value its carrying power outdoors. Despite its size, the instrument demands precise intonation control, as pitch flexibility in the top register is limited.
