Chickenpox

Chickenpox, also known as varicella, is a highly contagious disease caused by first infection with the varicella zoster virus (VZV), a member of the herpesvirus family. The disease results in a characteristic skin rash that forms small, itchy blisters, which eventually scab over. It usually starts on the chest, back and face, then spreads to the rest of the body. Other symptoms may include fever, tiredness and headaches. Symptoms typically last five to seven days.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms in adolescents and adults may include nausea, loss of appetite, aching muscles and headache, followed by the characteristic rash, mouth sores, malaise and low-grade fever. The rash begins as small red dots on the face, scalp, torso and upper arms and legs, progressing over 10–12 hours to small fluid-filled blisters (vesicles) and pustules, which then form scabs. In children the rash is often the first sign. A person is contagious from one to two days before the rash appears until all lesions have crusted over.

Cause and transmission

Chickenpox is an airborne disease which spreads easily from person to person through the coughs and sneezes of an infected person, and by contact with fluid from the blisters. The incubation period is 10–21 days. After the illness resolves, the virus remains dormant in nerve ganglia and may reactivate decades later to cause shingles (herpes zoster), a painful localised rash.

Complications

Complications may include bacterial infection of the skin lesions, pneumonia (more common in adults), and inflammation of the brain (encephalitis). The disease is usually more severe in adults than in children, and is dangerous in pregnancy, in newborns, and in people with weakened immune systems.

Prevention and treatment

The varicella vaccine, developed in Japan in the 1970s by Michiaki Takahashi, was first licensed for general use in Japan and Korea in 1988 and in the United States in 1995; where routine childhood vaccination has been introduced, cases and deaths have fallen dramatically. Treatment is usually symptomatic — calamine lotion and antihistamines for itching, paracetamol for fever (aspirin is avoided because of the risk of Reye syndrome) — while antiviral drugs such as aciclovir are used for severe cases and high-risk patients.

Epidemiology

Before the vaccine, chickenpox was a near-universal childhood infection in temperate climates, with roughly as many cases each year as births. It results in about 6,400 deaths per year globally. The name's origin is uncertain; explanations include the resemblance of the blisters to chickpeas and an old sense of "chicken" meaning childish or mild.
