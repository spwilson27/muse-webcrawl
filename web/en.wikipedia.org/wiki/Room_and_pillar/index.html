Room and pillar

Room and pillar (also called pillar and stall or bord and pillar in Britain) is a mining system in which material is extracted from horizontal openings ("rooms" or "bords") while pillars of unmined material are left in place to support the roof. It is one of the oldest and most widely used methods of underground mining for flat-lying deposits.

Method

Miners drive a regular grid of parallel entries and cross-cuts through the deposit, creating rooms typically 6 to 12 metres wide separated by pillars of similar or somewhat smaller dimensions. The pillars carry the weight of the overlying strata, so their size must increase with depth and with weaker roof rock. In a first pass, extraction ("development") may recover only 40–60 percent of the deposit; in some mines a second phase, "pillar robbing" or retreat mining, removes pillars as the workings are abandoned, allowing the roof to collapse in a controlled way behind the miners — a hazardous operation that has caused fatal accidents when pillars were extracted in the wrong sequence.

Applications

Room and pillar is the standard method for many coal seams in the United States (particularly in Illinois and Appalachia, where continuous miners cut the rooms mechanically), and for deposits of limestone, gypsum, salt, potash, trona, lead, and some metal and uranium ores in flat-lying sedimentary strata. Vast salt and potash mines in Canada, Germany, and elsewhere consist of room-and-pillar workings extending for many kilometres.

Advantages and disadvantages

The method is flexible, can be highly mechanised, and allows selective mining of the best material; development is fast and capital costs are moderate. Its chief disadvantage is low overall recovery: the pillars represent ore or coal permanently or temporarily left behind, and ground failure — pillar collapse, roof falls, or the "bump" of a stressed pillar — is a persistent safety concern. Abandoned workings can subside at the surface, sometimes decades later, damaging buildings above old room-and-pillar mines.

Modern practice designs pillars using empirical strength formulas (such as those developed for coal by Bieniawski and others), monitors stress and convergence, and increasingly uses continuous miners, shuttle cars, and roof bolters to mechanise the cycle of cutting, loading, hauling, and roof support.
