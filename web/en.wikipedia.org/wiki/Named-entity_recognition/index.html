Named-entity recognition

Named-entity recognition (NER) (also known as named-entity identification, entity chunking, and entity extraction) is a subtask of information extraction that seeks to locate and classify named entities mentioned in unstructured text into pre-defined categories such as person names, organizations, locations, medical codes, time expressions, quantities, monetary values, and percentages.

Approaches

Early systems used hand-crafted rule-based methods relying on gazetteers and orthographic patterns. Statistical approaches treat NER as a sequence labelling problem, applying hidden Markov models, maximum entropy models, support vector machines and, most influentially, conditional random fields (CRFs). Modern systems use neural architectures: bidirectional LSTMs with a CRF layer, and transformer models such as BERT fine-tuned for token classification, which achieve state-of-the-art results on standard benchmarks.

Evaluation

Systems are evaluated on annotated corpora such as the CoNLL-2003 shared task dataset and OntoNotes, typically using precision, recall and F1 score computed over exact entity matches. Reported state-of-the-art F1 scores on CoNLL-2003 English exceed 93%.

Applications

NER is used in question answering, semantic search, content recommendation, customer support automation, and biomedical text mining, where variants of the task identify genes, proteins, diseases and chemical compounds.
