Opponent process

The opponent process is a psychological and neurological model that accounts for a wide range of behaviours, including colour vision and drug addiction. The concept was initially proposed by the German physiologist Ewald Hering in 1874, as part of his opponent-process theory of colour vision, and was later generalized to motivation and emotion by Richard Solomon and John Corbit in 1974.

Colour vision

In Hering's theory, colour is coded in three opponent channels: red versus green, blue versus yellow, and black versus white (light versus dark). This explains phenomena such as negative afterimages and why certain colour combinations (a "reddish green") cannot be perceived. Modern visual neuroscience supports opponent processing in retinal ganglion cells and the lateral geniculate nucleus.

Motivation and addiction

Solomon and Corbit proposed that an affective stimulus triggers a primary "a-process" (for example, pleasure or pain) which is opposed by a slower, compensatory "b-process" of opposite valence. With repeated stimulation, the a-process stays roughly constant while the b-process strengthens and lasts longer. This explains tolerance and withdrawal in drug addiction: over time the pleasurable effect diminishes while the opposing craving and dysphoria on abstinence intensify.
