Incarceration in the United States

The United States has one of the largest incarcerated populations in the world. Around 1.8–1.9 million people are held in federal and state prisons, local jails, and other facilities, and several million more are under probation or parole supervision. Although the total has declined from its peak around 2008–2009, the US incarceration rate remains far above that of other developed democracies, a phenomenon often called mass incarceration.

The United States built hundreds of prisons during the late twentieth-century expansion.

Growth

For most of the twentieth century the US imprisonment rate was stable at roughly 100 per 100,000 residents. From the 1970s it climbed steeply for three decades, driven by the war on drugs, mandatory minimum sentences, "three strikes" laws, longer sentences, and reduced use of parole. By 2008 roughly 2.3 million people were behind bars. Since then, bipartisan concern about costs and effectiveness has produced modest reforms, and the population has fallen, though unevenly across states.

Characteristics

The burden of incarceration falls disproportionately on Black and Hispanic Americans, who are imprisoned at several times the rate of white Americans, and on people with low incomes and low educational attainment. A large share of prisoners are held for drug or property offences and non-violent crimes; local jails hold hundreds of thousands of people who have not been convicted, many because they cannot afford bail. The United States also incarcerates more women than any other country, and women's imprisonment grew especially fast during the drug war.

Effects and debate

Scholars debate how much of the 1990s crime decline imprisonment caused; most estimates credit it with a modest share. Critics point to the fiscal cost, the damage to families and communities, and barriers to employment and voting after release. Supporters argue that incapacitation and deterrence protect public safety. Reform efforts include sentencing changes, bail reform, drug decriminalisation in some states, and expanded re-entry programmes.
