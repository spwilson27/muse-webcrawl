Car body style

This article is about the classification of automobile bodies by shape and configuration. For the underlying structure of a vehicle, see vehicle frame and monocoque.

Car body styles

Classified byRoofline, number of doors, seating rows, cargo arrangement, pillar layout
Related termsCoachwork, body-on-frame, unibody, fastback, notchback

Car body styles are the distinct shapes and configurations in which automobiles are built and marketed. A body style describes the overall form of the passenger and cargo space: how many doors the car has, how the roof is shaped, whether the luggage compartment is separate from the cabin or integrated with it, how many rows of seats are fitted, and whether the roof can be opened or removed. Manufacturers, regulators, insurers and reviewers all use body-style terms, although the definitions are not perfectly standardised and marketing language frequently blurs them — a four-door car with a sloping roof may be sold as a "four-door coupé", for example.

The earliest automobiles were essentially motorised horse-drawn carriages, and many of the oldest body-style names — phaeton, landau, cabriolet, tonneau — were inherited directly from carriage building. As closed bodies became normal in the 1920s and 1930s, a more systematic vocabulary emerged around the sedan (saloon), coupé and convertible. The post-war period added the station wagon and hatchback, and from the 1980s onward the minivan, the sport utility vehicle (SUV) and the crossover reshaped the market. Body style is closely connected to car classification, but the two are different: classification usually refers to size and market segment, while body style refers to shape.

Terminology and design elements

Several structural features are used to distinguish body styles:

  • Boxes. Stylists describe cars as one-box, two-box or three-box designs. A three-box car (a traditional sedan) has separate volumes for the engine, the passenger cabin and the boot (trunk). A two-box car (hatchback, station wagon, most SUVs) combines cabin and cargo space into one volume behind the engine compartment. A one-box design (many minivans and city cars) merges all three into a single flowing volume.
  • Pillars. The vertical roof supports are lettered from front to back: A-pillars flank the windscreen, B-pillars sit between the doors, C-pillars support the rear of the roof, and wagons and large SUVs may add D-pillars. Whether a car has a B-pillar — and whether the roofline slopes or steps down — historically separated hardtops and coupés from sedans.
  • Back. The rear profile may be a notchback (a distinct step between roof and boot), a fastback (a single slope from roof to tail), a hatchback (a near-vertical or steeply raked rear door hinged at the roof), or a squareback (the upright tail of a wagon).
  • Doors. Counting conventions vary: a hatch or tailgate is often counted as a door, so hatchbacks are sold as "three-door" or "five-door" cars.

Closed body styles

Sedan (saloon)

The sedan, called a saloon in British English, is the classic three-box passenger car: a separate enclosed boot, a fixed roof, and typically four doors with seating for four or five. Sedans dominated the world car market for most of the twentieth century and remain the default form for taxis, police cars and executive transport, though in North America and Europe they have lost ground to SUVs and crossovers since the 2000s. Two-door sedans were once common and are distinguished from coupés by their taller, more upright rear roofline and full-size rear seats.

Coupé

A coupé is a closed car with a sloping rear roofline and, traditionally, two doors and a smaller interior volume than the equivalent sedan. The name derives from the French couper, "to cut" — the body appeared to be cut down. Modern usage is loose: manufacturers market "four-door coupés" with sedan-like proportions, and the term is often applied to any car styled to look sporting. Related forms include the hardtop coupé, which lacks a B-pillar so that the side glass forms one uninterrupted opening, and the three-box notchback coupé of classic grand tourers.

Hatchback

A hatchback has a rear door (the hatch) hinged at roof level that opens upward to give access to a cargo area continuous with the passenger compartment; the rear seats usually fold flat. Offered in three-door and five-door forms, the hatchback became the archetypal European family car from the 1970s onward — the Volkswagen Golf is the best-known example — because it combines a small footprint with wagon-like load capacity. A liftback is a variation with a more gently sloping rear, in which the whole rear panel including the window lifts as one piece.

Station wagon (estate)

The station wagon — estate car in British English — extends the roofline at full height to the back of the car, with a tailgate behind the rear seats. Wagons began as depot hacks for carrying passengers and luggage to railway stations, and became the standard American family vehicle of the 1950s–1970s, often with wood-grain side trim ("woodies"). They were largely displaced by minivans and SUVs in North America but remain popular in Europe. Low-roofed, style-led variants are marketed as shooting brakes, a term revived from horse-drawn hunting vehicles.

Limousine

A limousine is a luxury car with an extended wheelbase and, in its classic form, a partition between the driver and the rear passenger compartment. Stretch limousines are lengthened conversions used for hire, while factory-built limousines such as the Mercedes-Maybach serve as chauffeur-driven executive cars.

Open and convertible body styles

Convertible (cabriolet)

A convertible has a roof that can be folded down or removed. Folding roofs may be soft fabric tops or retractable hardtops. The term cabriolet is used interchangeably in much of Europe. Two-seat open cars with minimal weather equipment are called roadsters or spiders/spyders; a speedster is a further stripped roadster with a low windscreen. Open cars were the default body style until the 1920s, when closed bodies became affordable, and they survive as a niche for sports and leisure cars.

Targa and T-top

A targa top has a removable panel over the front seats with a fixed roll bar behind — a configuration popularised by the Porsche 911 Targa, which owns the trademark. The related T-top (T-bar) uses two removable panels separated by a central bar. Both offer open-air driving with more structural rigidity and rollover protection than a full convertible.

Utility and multi-purpose styles

Sport utility vehicle (SUV) and crossover

The sport utility vehicle combines a tall two-box body, high ground clearance and — originally — a truck-based body-on-frame chassis with off-road capability. The crossover applies the same styling and seating position to a car-based unibody platform; the distinction is now largely invisible to buyers, and "SUV" is used for both. By the early 2020s SUVs and crossovers accounted for roughly half of new-car sales in many markets, a shift criticised on safety and environmental grounds because of their size and weight. Compact, subcompact and full-size variants span nearly every price class.

Pickup truck

A pickup truck has an enclosed cab and an open cargo bed at the rear, separated by a fixed bulkhead. Cab configurations include single (regular) cab, extended cab with small rear seats or rear-hinged half doors, and crew cab with four full doors. Pickups dominate the North American market — the Ford F-Series has long been the best-selling vehicle line in the United States — and range from compact unibody models to heavy-duty work trucks. The Australian ute is a car-derived relative with an integrated tray.

Minivan (MPV)

The minivan, called a people carrier or MPV (multi-purpose vehicle) in Europe, is a tall one-box or two-box vehicle with sliding rear doors and three rows of seats, typically for seven or eight passengers. Launched in modern form in 1983–84 by the Chrysler minivans (Dodge Caravan/Plymouth Voyager) and the Renault Espace, minivans defined family transport in the 1990s before losing ground to three-row SUVs.

Van and panel van

A van is a boxy vehicle designed primarily for cargo, usually with a single row of seats and a large enclosed load area accessed by rear or sliding doors. Passenger versions overlap with minivans, while cutaway and chassis-cab variants serve as bases for minibuses, ambulances and motorhomes.

Historical body styles

Many early body styles have fallen out of use. The phaeton was a light open car with minimal weather protection; the touring car its larger five- or seven-seat successor. A landaulet had a folding roof over the rear seats only. Town cars enclosed the passengers while leaving the driver exposed. The hardtop — a pillarless closed body styled to resemble a convertible — was a major American style of the 1950s–1970s in both two-door and four-door forms. Wood-framed woodie wagons, cycle-fendered runabouts, and coachbuilt bodies by firms such as Mulliner, Pininfarina (in its early coachbuilding role) and Figoni et Falaschi belong to the era when manufacturers sold a rolling chassis and independent coachbuilders supplied the body — the origin of the word coachwork for car bodies generally.

Regulation and marketing

Body-style terms are rarely defined precisely in law, but they interact with regulation in several ways: safety standards treat convertibles and trucks differently from passenger cars, emissions and fuel-economy rules in the United States have historically given light trucks (including most SUVs and pickups) a separate, less stringent category, and insurance and registration classes sometimes distinguish cars from cargo vehicles. Because the boundaries are porous, marketing departments exploit them — sloping-roofed SUVs are sold as "coupé SUVs", and raised hatchbacks as crossovers — so published lists of body styles should be read as conventions rather than strict taxonomy.
