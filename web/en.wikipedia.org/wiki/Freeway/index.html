Freeway

A freeway is a type of highway that has been designed for high-speed vehicular traffic, with all traffic flow regulated and ingress/egress limited. In North America and Australia, freeways are a type of controlled-access highway; elsewhere terms such as motorway (United Kingdom, Ireland, Pakistan), autobahn (Germany), autostrada (Italy) and expressway are used. Freeways are distinguished from other roads by fully grade-separated interchanges, medians or barriers between opposing directions of traffic, and the absence of at-grade intersections, driveways or traffic signals.

Characteristics

Freeways typically have two or more lanes in each direction, wide shoulders, and design features — broad curves and gentle grades — that permit sustained high speeds. Access is provided only at interchanges via on- and off-ramps. Pedestrians, bicycles and slow vehicles are generally prohibited. "Freeway" originally implied freedom from tolls and from cross traffic, though many modern freeways include tolled sections (sometimes called tollways or turnpikes).

History

Early examples included the parkways of New York and the Long Island Motor Parkway (1908), and Italy's autostrade of the 1920s. Germany's autobahn network, begun in the 1930s, and the American Interstate Highway System, authorised in 1956, set the template for national freeway networks. Freeways reshaped cities and suburbs, enabling mass automobility, but also attracted criticism for dividing neighbourhoods, encouraging sprawl, and generating pollution — grievances that fuelled "freeway revolts" in cities such as San Francisco, Boston and Toronto.

Effects

Freeways have among the lowest fatality rates per distance travelled of any road type, thanks to their controlled access and separation of traffic, yet high traffic volumes mean they account for large absolute numbers of collisions. Capacity expansions can induce additional demand, a phenomenon widely studied in transport planning.
