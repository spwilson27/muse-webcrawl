Zero tolerance

Zero tolerance
TypeEnforcement policy
Applied inPolicing, schools, immigration, workplaces
FeaturePredetermined, non-discretionary punishment for infractions
Related theoryBroken windows theory

Zero tolerance is a policy of imposing a predetermined, typically severe, punishment for a prohibited behaviour or infraction, with no discretion given to authorities to consider context or mitigating circumstances. The term became prominent in the United States from the 1980s onward, first in drug enforcement and policing, and later in schools, immigration, and corporate compliance.

Origins and policing

The phrase was popularised during the Reagan-era war on drugs, including a 1988 programme of seizing vessels and vehicles found with any quantity of drugs. In policing, zero tolerance became associated with the "broken windows" theory of Wilson and Kelling, which holds that visible disorder encourages serious crime. New York City's aggressive enforcement against fare evasion, public drinking and vandalism under Mayor Giuliani and Commissioner Bratton in the 1990s was widely credited — and disputed — as a cause of the city's crime decline.

In schools

The Gun-Free Schools Act of 1994 required US schools receiving federal funds to expel for at least one year any student bringing a firearm to school, and zero tolerance spread to drugs, fighting, and even minor disruptions. Highly publicised cases — children suspended for toy weapons, nail clippers, or medication — fuelled criticism that the policies were inflexible and disproportionately applied to minority and disabled students, feeding what critics call the school-to-prison pipeline. Many districts have since replaced automatic penalties with restorative or discretionary approaches.

Other applications

Governments and organisations invoke zero tolerance for corruption, sexual harassment, drink-driving, doping in sport and immigration violations — notably the 2018 US "zero tolerance" border policy that mandated criminal prosecution of all unlawful entrants and led to family separations. Supporters argue that certainty and severity of punishment deter misconduct and ensure equal treatment; opponents respond that removing discretion produces injustice in individual cases and often drives behaviour underground rather than eliminating it.
