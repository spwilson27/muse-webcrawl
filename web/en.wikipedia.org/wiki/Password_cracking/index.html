Password cracking

In cyber security, password cracking is the process of recovering passwords from data that has been stored in or transmitted by a computer system, typically in hashed form. A common approach is the guessing attack: an attacker submits many candidate passwords, hashes them, and compares the results against stolen password hashes.

Methods

  • Brute-force attack — systematically trying every possible combination of characters.
  • Dictionary attack — trying words from lists, often derived from previous data breaches, with mangling rules applied.
  • Rainbow table attack — using precomputed chains of hashes to trade storage for time; defeated by salting.
  • Credential stuffing — replaying username/password pairs leaked from other services.

Tools and speed

Popular tools include Hashcat and John the Ripper, which exploit GPUs to test billions of guesses per second against fast hashes such as MD5 or NTLM. Slow, memory-hard password hashing functions such as bcrypt, scrypt, and Argon2, together with per-password salts, are the standard defenses against offline cracking.

Time estimates

The feasibility of cracking depends on password length and entropy. Short or human-chosen passwords fall quickly, while long random passphrases resist even large GPU clusters. Password strength meters and compromised-password screening are common preventive measures.
