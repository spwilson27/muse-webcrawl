Langar (Punjabi: ਲੰਗਰ, "kitchen") is the term used in Sikhism for the community kitchen in a gurdwara where a free vegetarian meal is served to all visitors, without distinction of religion, caste, gender, economic status or ethnicity. The tradition embodies the Sikh principles of seva (selfless service), equality and the sharing of food (vand chakko).

History

The practice was initiated by Guru Nanak, the founder of Sikhism, who used resources from his farming to feed the hungry at Kartarpur, and was continued and expanded by Guru Angad. The third Guru, Amar Das, formalised the institution at Goindwal and required all visitors, including — according to tradition — the Mughal emperor Akbar, to sit and eat together in rows (pangat) on the floor before meeting the Guru, a powerful rejection of caste hierarchy. The Mughal emperor is said to have been so impressed that he granted land supporting the langar.

Practice

Every gurdwara operates a langar, staffed by volunteers who cook, serve and wash up. The largest, at the Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib) in Amritsar, serves tens of thousands of people daily — often cited as around 50,000 to 100,000, making it among the largest free kitchens in the world. The food is vegetarian so that everyone may eat it. In recent decades Sikh organisations have taken langar beyond the gurdwara, serving meals to homeless people, disaster victims and communities in crisis around the world.
