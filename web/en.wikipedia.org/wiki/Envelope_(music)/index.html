Envelope (music)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In sound and music, an envelope describes how a sound changes over time. It may relate to elements such as amplitude (volume), frequency (with filters), or pitch. Envelopes are a key feature of synthesizers, where envelope generators shape the character of a tone from the moment a note is triggered until it fades away.

ADSR

The most common envelope type is ADSR, named for its four stages:

  • Attack — the time taken for the level to rise from zero to its peak when the key is first pressed.
  • Decay — the time taken for the level to fall from the peak to the designated sustain level.
  • Sustain — the level maintained while the key is held (a level, not a time).
  • Release — the time taken for the level to fall from sustain to zero after the key is released.

ADSR envelopes were popularized by synthesizers such as those designed by Robert Moog in the 1960s. A plucked string typically has a fast attack and no sustain, whereas an organ has a fast attack, full sustain, and near-immediate release.

Other envelope types

Variants include AHDSR (adding a hold stage), multi-segment envelopes with many breakpoints, and looping envelopes. In modern digital instruments and DAWs, envelopes can modulate almost any parameter, not only amplitude.
