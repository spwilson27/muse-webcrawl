The IceCube Neutrino Observatory is a neutrino observatory constructed at the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station in Antarctica. The project is a cubic-kilometre neutrino telescope made of thousands of sensors buried beneath the Antarctic ice, distributed over a cubic kilometre — the largest neutrino detector in the world.

Strings of optical modules are frozen into the Antarctic ice, detecting Cherenkov light from neutrino interactions.

Construction and design

Between 2005 and 2010, hot-water drills melted 86 vertical holes 1,450–2,450 metres deep, into which strings of 60 digital optical modules (DOMs) each were deployed — 5,160 in total. When a high-energy neutrino interacts with a nucleus in the ice, charged secondary particles emit Cherenkov radiation that the DOMs record, allowing reconstruction of the neutrino's energy and direction. A surface array, IceTop, detects cosmic-ray air showers, and a denser infill, DeepCore, lowers the energy threshold.

Scientific results

In 2013 IceCube announced the first detection of extremely high-energy cosmic neutrinos, including events nicknamed "Bert" and "Ernie" with energies above a petaelectronvolt — the opening of high-energy neutrino astronomy. In 2017–2018, a multimessenger campaign associated a ~290 TeV neutrino (IceCube-170922A) with the flaring blazar TXS 0506+056, the first identified likely source of high-energy neutrinos and cosmic rays. IceCube also measures neutrino oscillations, searches for dark matter and sterile neutrinos, and observes neutrinos from the Galactic plane.
