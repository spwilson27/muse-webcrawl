John Edgar Hoover (January 1, 1895 – May 2, 1972) was an American law-enforcement administrator who served as the first Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI). Appointed director of the Bureau of Investigation — the FBI's predecessor — in 1924, he held the post for 48 years until his death, building the FBI into a large, professional and powerful agency. He is credited with modernising American policing through centralised fingerprint files, forensic laboratories and agent training, but is also widely criticised for abuses of power, secret files on politicians and activists, and the covert harassment programme COINTELPRO.

Early career

Hoover was born in Washington, D.C., and worked at the Library of Congress while earning law degrees from George Washington University. He joined the Department of Justice in 1917 and rose rapidly during the First Red Scare, heading the General Intelligence Division and compiling files on radicals; he organised the Palmer Raids of 1919–1920, in which thousands of suspected radicals were arrested and hundreds deported.

Building the FBI

Appointed acting director of the Bureau of Investigation in 1924 amid the scandals of the Harding administration, Hoover professionalised the Bureau: he imposed strict standards for agents, established a centralised fingerprint identification division, founded the FBI Laboratory (1932) and the FBI National Academy (1935), and oversaw the creation of the Uniform Crime Reports. In the 1930s the Bureau won fame for pursuing gangsters such as John Dillinger and for its publicity-conscious image of the incorruptible "G-Man". The agency was renamed the Federal Bureau of Investigation in 1935.

Surveillance and controversy

During the Second World War and the Cold War, Hoover directed the FBI against espionage and domestic "subversion", amassing extensive files on public figures. Under COINTELPRO (1956–1971), the FBI surveilled and attempted to disrupt civil rights, anti-war and other movements; Hoover pursued a long campaign against Martin Luther King Jr., including an anonymous 1964 letter urging King toward suicide, which became public through Senate investigation. His secret "Official and Confidential" files, destroyed after his death by his secretary Helen Gandy, fuelled speculation that he deterred presidents from removing him. Eight presidents retained him; after his death, Congress limited FBI directors to a single ten-year term.

Death and legacy

Hoover died in his sleep of a heart attack on 2 May 1972 and lay in state in the U.S. Capitol rotunda. The FBI headquarters building in Washington, completed in 1975, bears his name — a designation periodically debated in light of the documented abuses. The Church Committee of 1975–76 exposed many of the Bureau's covert programmes and led to new domestic-intelligence guidelines.
