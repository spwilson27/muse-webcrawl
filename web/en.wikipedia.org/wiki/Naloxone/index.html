Naloxone is a medication used to reverse or reduce the effects of opioids, most importantly to reverse life-threatening respiratory depression in opioid overdose. It is a competitive opioid receptor antagonist that binds to opioid receptors — especially the μ-opioid receptor — with higher affinity than most agonists, displacing them and rapidly restoring breathing. Naloxone has essentially no effect in people who have not taken opioids.

Naloxone nasal spray (Narcan), widely distributed for layperson overdose reversal.

Medical uses

Naloxone is used in emergency medicine to treat opioid overdose from drugs such as heroin, morphine, oxycodone, and synthetic opioids like fentanyl. It is given by injection into a vein, muscle, or under the skin, or as a nasal spray. Effects begin within about two minutes intravenously and last 30 to 90 minutes; because many opioids outlast it, repeat doses and medical observation are often required. It is also combined with buprenorphine (as Suboxone) to deter misuse by injection, and included in some formulations with oxycodone.

Public access

In response to the opioid epidemic, naloxone has been made widely available to laypeople through community distribution programmes, pharmacy standing orders, and harm-reduction organisations. In March 2023 the US Food and Drug Administration approved Narcan nasal spray for over-the-counter sale, the first naloxone product approved for non-prescription use. The World Health Organization includes naloxone on its List of Essential Medicines and recommends that people likely to witness an overdose have access to it.

Side effects and mechanism

Naloxone works by competitively antagonising opioid receptors. In people physically dependent on opioids it can precipitate acute withdrawal symptoms — agitation, pain, nausea, sweating, and rapid heart rate — which are unpleasant but rarely life-threatening in adults. It was patented in 1961 by Jack Fishman and Mozes Lewenstein and first approved by the FDA in 1971.
