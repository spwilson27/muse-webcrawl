Apophony

Apophony (also known as ablaut, vowel gradation, vowel mutation, vowel alternation, internal modification, and internal inflection) is any sound change where a vowel is altered in order to mark grammatical contrasts, typically between related but distinct words or word forms. The classic example in English is the alternation in verb pairs such as sing / sang / sung, where the vowel change itself signals tense.

Ablaut patterns mark tense in Germanic strong verbs such as sing–sang–sung.

Terminology

The term apophony comes from Ancient Greek apo- ("away, from") and phōnē ("sound"). The synonym ablaut was coined in the early nineteenth century by the linguist Jacob Grimm, from German ab- ("off, away") and Laut ("sound"). Although the two terms are often used interchangeably, some linguists reserve ablaut for the specific Indo-European vowel gradation system and use apophony as the broader term covering analogous processes in any language, such as the vowel alternations of Semitic languages or the stem changes in many Native American languages.

History

Apophony in Indo-European was first described in Sanskrit grammars such as the work of Pāṇini, and it became a central topic of comparative linguistics in the nineteenth century. Franz Bopp and Jacob Grimm systematised the correspondences between the vowel grades found in Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and the Germanic languages, showing that the alternations were inherited from Proto-Indo-European rather than being independent innovations.

Proto-Indo-European ablaut

In Proto-Indo-European, ablaut consisted of a system of vowel alternations, typically between a full grade with the vowel *e, an o-grade with *o, and a zero grade with no vowel at all. Lengthened grades *ē and *ō also occurred. These grades were originally conditioned by the position of the accent, and they became morphologised: the e-grade might mark a present tense, the o-grade a perfect or a derived noun, and the zero grade a reduced or unstressed form. A well-known example is the root *ph₂tér- "father", whose strong cases show the e-grade and whose weak cases show the zero grade.

Germanic languages

The Germanic strong verbs preserve ablaut most transparently. English retains about 180 irregular verbs whose principal parts descend from the Germanic ablaut classes: drive / drove / driven, begin / began / begun, fall / fell / fallen, and choose / chose / chosen. German shows parallel patterns, as in singen / sang / gesungen. Because the system is no longer productive, new verbs entering English almost always take the regular weak ("-ed") conjugation, and over time some strong verbs have become weak.

Other language families

Apophony-like processes occur widely. In Semitic languages, roots consisting of consonants are interwoven with vowel patterns that mark tense, voice, and derivation. In the Athabaskan languages, stem-final vowel and tone alternations mark aspect. Some languages, such as certain varieties of Berber, use vowel changes to mark number or aspect. Umlaut, by contrast, is a historically different phenomenon: a vowel assimilation to a following sound, which in Germanic languages was later morphologised into plural and diminutive markers.
