Coalition government

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A coalition government, or coalition cabinet, is a government formed by multiple political parties which usually agree not to run against each other (at least in the main arena of the parliamentary vote) and instead negotiate a common policy programme and share ministerial posts. Coalitions are the norm under proportional representation — where single-party majorities are rare — and form when no party alone commands a parliamentary majority (a hung parliament).

Formation and dynamics

Formation involves bargaining over portfolios (allocated roughly in proportion to seats — "Gamson's law"), policy agreements (formal coalition contracts in Germany and the Netherlands, looser confidence-and-supply deals elsewhere), and the premiership. Political science distinguishes minimal-winning, surplus and minority coalitions, and studies their duration: coalitions typically govern effectively but are vulnerable to partner vetoes, blame-shifting and electorates punishing junior partners — dramatic cases include wartime national governments (grand coalitions), Germany's repeated CDU/CSU–SPD governments, and the UK's Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition (2010–15), the first peacetime British coalition since the 1930s.
