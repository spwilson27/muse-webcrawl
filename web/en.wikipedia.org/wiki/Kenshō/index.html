Kenshō

Zazen meditation, the context in which kenshō is typically sought

Kenshō (Japanese: 見性, "seeing one's true nature") is a term from Zen Buddhism for an initial insight or awakening experience: a glimpse into the true nature of oneself and reality, closely related to — but usually regarded as less complete than — satori, full enlightenment. Kenshō is central to the Rinzai school, where it is pursued through koan study, and is also recognised in the Sōtō school.

Nature of the experience

Kenshō is typically described as a sudden, transient experience of non-duality — the dissolution of the perceived separation between self and world, and an insight into emptiness (śūnyatā) and Buddha-nature. It may be triggered by meditation, a koan, a sound, or a teacher's action or words. Teachers traditionally verify a student's claimed insight in private interview (dokusan), and training continues afterward: the experience must be deepened and integrated into daily life, a process sometimes described as "polishing" the insight.

Kenshō and satori

The relationship between kenshō and satori is understood differently across traditions. In much Japanese usage the terms are near-synonyms for awakening, with satori sometimes reserved for a deeper or more permanent realisation, while kenshō denotes the first breakthrough. Hakuin, the 18th-century reviver of Japanese Rinzai, insisted that kenshō was essential but only the beginning of practice.

Influence

Through writers such as D. T. Suzuki and popular accounts of Zen in the West, kenshō became one of the best-known concepts of Zen practice, and the term is sometimes used loosely for any sudden insight experience.
