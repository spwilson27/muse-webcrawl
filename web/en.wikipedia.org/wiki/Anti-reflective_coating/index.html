Anti-reflective coating

An anti-reflective or anti-glare coating is a type of optical coating applied to the surface of lenses, other optical elements, and photographic equipment to reduce reflection. In typical imaging systems, this improves efficiency, since less light is lost. In complex systems such as cameras, binoculars, telescopes, and microscopes, the reduction in reflections also improves the contrast of the image by elimination of stray light. This is especially important in planetary astronomy. In other applications, the primary benefit is the elimination of the reflection itself, such as a coating on eyeglass lenses that makes the eyes of the wearer more visible to others, or a coating to reduce the glint from a covert viewer's binoculars or telescopic sight.

Other namesAnti-glare coating, AR coating
UsesEyeglasses, camera lenses, telescopes, solar cells, displays
Common materialsMagnesium fluoride, fluoropolymers, nanoporous silica
Typical thicknessQuarter wavelength of light (~100 nm)

Principle

The coating works by exploiting the wave nature of light. A thin layer of material with a refractive index between that of the substrate (such as glass) and air causes reflections from the two surfaces of the layer to interfere destructively. Ideally, the layer is a quarter of a wavelength thick for the wavelength of light to be suppressed (usually green light near 550 nm, in the middle of the visible spectrum), so that the reflection from the second surface travels half a wavelength further than the reflection from the first surface and the two cancel.

The ideal refractive index of the coating is the geometric mean of the indices of the surrounding media. Because few materials have suitable indices and mechanical properties, modern coatings are often multilayer stacks of alternating high- and low-index materials, which can suppress reflection over a broader band of wavelengths and angles.

History

Anti-reflective coatings were first noticed in the 19th century when Lord Rayleigh observed in 1886 that tarnished glass transmitted more light than clean glass, because the tarnish layer had a lower refractive index than the glass. Harold Dennis Taylor of Cooke developed a chemical method of producing such a surface in 1896. Alexander Smakula, working for Carl Zeiss in 1935, invented the first deliberate thin-film anti-reflective coating using magnesium fluoride, a breakthrough initially kept as a German military secret. John Strong in the United States also developed vacuum-deposition coating methods in the late 1930s.

Applications

Eyeglasses

Anti-reflective coatings are widely applied to spectacle lenses, where they reduce reflections, improve night driving vision, and make the wearer's eyes more visible. They are frequently combined with scratch-resistant and hydrophobic layers. Residual reflection from a single-layer magnesium fluoride coating often appears as a faint green or purple tint.

Photography and optics

Camera lenses use multi-coating on most or all air-to-glass surfaces to maximise contrast and minimise flare and ghosting. Similar coatings are used on binoculars, microscopes, and telescopes.

Solar cells and displays

Photovoltaic cells use anti-reflective layers, often of silicon nitride, to increase the fraction of incident light absorbed. Displays and touchscreens use anti-glare treatments, either as coatings or as microscopically etched surfaces.

Moth-eye coatings

A different approach copies the structure of moth eyes, whose surfaces are covered with a regular array of tiny bumps smaller than the wavelength of light. This produces a gradual change in refractive index and suppresses reflection over a very wide range of wavelengths and angles. Such nanostructured surfaces are used in some high-performance optics and are researched for solar panels.
