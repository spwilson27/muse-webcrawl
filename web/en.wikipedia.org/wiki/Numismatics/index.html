Numismatics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Ancient coins are a primary source for numismatic study.

Numismatics is the study or collection of currency, including coins, tokens, paper money, medals and related objects. The word derives from the French numismatique, from the Latin numisma, itself from the Greek nomisma (νόμισμα), "current coin, custom", ultimately from nomizein, "to hold or own as a custom or law". A specialist in the field is a numismatist.

Scope

Numismatics spans several subfields. The study of coins proper covers their production, iconography, inscriptions, metrology (weight standards) and circulation, and serves historians and archaeologists as evidence for chronology, trade, political propaganda and religion. Exonumia concerns coin-like objects such as tokens and medals; notaphily is the study of paper money; and scripophily covers old stocks and bonds. Numismatists distinguish the obverse (front or "heads"), reverse (back or "tails") and edge of a coin, and grade condition using scales such as the Sheldon scale for early American coins.

History of the discipline

Coin collecting was practised by Roman emperors — Augustus is said to have given old and foreign coins as gifts — and by Renaissance rulers and scholars, for whom collections of ancient coins were a link to classical antiquity. The discipline took scholarly form in the early modern period with catalogues and cabinets in Vienna, Paris, London and St. Petersburg. National collections such as those of the British Museum and the Smithsonian Institution, and learned bodies such as the Royal Numismatic Society (founded 1836) and the American Numismatic Society (1858), established modern standards of research and publication.

Coins as historical evidence

Because coins are durable, mass-produced and often bear dates, portraits and mint marks, they are among the most informative artefacts of the ancient and medieval worlds. Hoards — groups of coins buried together — reveal patterns of trade, warfare and monetary crisis; overstrike and imitative issues trace political change; and otherwise unknown rulers and cities are sometimes attested only by their coinage.
