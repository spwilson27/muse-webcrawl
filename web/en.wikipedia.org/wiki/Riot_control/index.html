Riot control

Riot control comprises the measures taken by police, military or security forces to control, disperse or arrest crowds involved in a riot, demonstration or protest that has turned unlawful or violent. It balances the maintenance of public order against rights of assembly and expression, and its tactics range from communication and negotiated management to formations, barriers and less-lethal weapons.

Tactics and equipment

Riot police typically deploy in trained formations — lines, wedges and cordons — wearing helmets with visors, body armour and carrying large shields and batons. Tools for dispersal include water cannon, tear gas (CS gas) and other chemical irritants, pepper spray, rubber and plastic bullets, bean bag rounds, stun grenades and mounted or vehicle support. "Kettling" (containment of a crowd in a confined area) is a controversial tactic used in some countries. Modern research on crowd psychology has influenced a shift in many European forces toward "dialogue policing" and graded, communication-first approaches, on evidence that indiscriminate force tends to escalate disorder.

History

Organized riot control developed with modern professional police in the nineteenth century; Britain's Reading of the Riot Act (1714–1973) formally required a proclamation before force could be used against assemblies. Dedicated units such as France's CRS and gendarmerie mobile, Germany's Bereitschaftspolizei and various national gendarmeries institutionalized the function in the twentieth century.

Criticism

Riot control practices are frequently criticized by human rights organizations for excessive force, misuse of chemical agents, and injuries from kinetic projectiles; international guidance, including UN principles on the use of force, stresses necessity, proportionality and accountability.
