World Federalism

World federalism is the idea of a global federal government: a worldwide system of governance in which states delegate defined, limited powers—typically over war, weapons of mass destruction and global commons—to democratic institutions at the planetary level, while retaining autonomy in other matters. Supporters argue that only enforceable world law can prevent war and manage global problems; critics question its feasibility and fear concentration of power.

History

Precursors include Dante's De Monarchia, Immanuel Kant's Perpetual Peace (1795) and nineteenth-century peace societies. The movement surged after the Second World War: the 1947 Montreux congress founded the World Movement for World Federal Government, and figures such as Albert Einstein and Bertrand Russell endorsed world government as a response to nuclear weapons. Books like Emery Reves's The Anatomy of Peace (1945) popularised the case.

Proposals and organisations

Federalists have proposed reforming the United Nations into a federation, or building regional federations—such as the European Union—as stepping stones. Successor organisations include the World Federalist Movement/Institute for Global Policy and campaigns for a United Nations Parliamentary Assembly. Related but distinct ideas include cosmopolitan democracy and confederal "league of nations" models without direct world lawmaking power over individuals.
