Loop quantum cosmology (LQC) is a finite, symmetry-reduced application of loop quantum gravity that studies the very early universe. Developed principally from 1999 onward by Martin Bojowald and collaborators, and given a rigorous formulation by Abhay Ashtekar and others in 2006, LQC applies loop quantisation techniques to cosmological models such as the homogeneous, isotropic Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker universe.

The Big Bounce

In LQC the classical Big Bang singularity is resolved: the Wheeler–DeWitt differential equation of older quantum cosmology is replaced by a difference equation, and quantum geometry effects generate a repulsive force at densities near the Planck density (about 0.41 times the Planck density in the standard effective dynamics). As a result the universe's contraction reaches a minimum volume and re-expands — a "Big Bounce" — so that the evolution can be extended deterministically through what was classically the singularity. The effective Friedmann equation acquires a correction term that becomes negligible at low density, recovering general relativity.

Applications

LQC has been used to study the onset and duration of cosmic inflation (finding that a bounce can set natural initial conditions for a subsequent inflationary phase), the behaviour of cosmological perturbations, and black-hole-like models. Its predictions for primordial perturbation spectra are studied as a possible window for observational tests.

Criticism and open questions

Because LQC quantises after symmetry reduction rather than deriving cosmology from full loop quantum gravity, questions remain about how faithfully the minisuperspace models capture the full theory, and about ambiguities in the quantisation. Debate continues on the physical status of the bounce and on distinguishing LQC signatures from those of other early-universe scenarios.
