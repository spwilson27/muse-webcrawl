The Western Schism, also known as the Papal Schism of 1378, was a split within the Roman Catholic Church lasting from 1378 to 1417 in which bishops residing in Rome and Avignon — and after 1409 a third at Pisa — simultaneously claimed to be the true pope, dividing Western Christendom into rival obediences.

Origins

After the Avignon Papacy (1309–77), Pope Gregory XI returned to Rome. On his death in 1378 a Roman mob pressed the conclave, which elected the Italian Urban VI. The mostly French cardinals, alienated by Urban's harsh conduct, withdrew, declared his election coerced and elected Clement VII, who settled at Avignon. France and its allies recognised Clement; England, the Empire and Italy recognised Urban — a division that mirrored the Hundred Years' War.

Attempts at resolution

Successors in both lines hardened the split. Theologians at Paris promoted conciliarism — the doctrine that a general council could judge a pope. Cardinals from both sides convened the Council of Pisa (1409), which deposed both claimants and elected Alexander V; the result was three popes. Finally the Council of Constance (1414–18), backed by Emperor Sigismund, secured the resignation of Gregory XII (Rome), deposed John XXIII (Pisa) and Benedict XIII (Avignon, who refused and held out in Peñíscola), and elected Martin V in 1417, restoring unity — and setting a lasting precedent in conciliar theory that popes then spent decades rolling back.
