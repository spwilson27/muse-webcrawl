Hip

In vertebrate anatomy, the hip (or coxa in medical terminology) is the region or joint on the lateral side of the pelvis. The hip joint, formed by the articulation of the rounded head of the femur with the cup-shaped acetabulum of the pelvis, is a ball-and-socket synovial joint that bears the weight of the upper body in both static postures and walking.

Structure

The acetabulum is formed by the fusion of the ilium, ischium, and pubis, the three bones of the hip bone. The joint is enclosed in a strong fibrous capsule reinforced by the iliofemoral, pubofemoral, and ischiofemoral ligaments; the iliofemoral ligament is among the strongest in the body and helps maintain upright posture with minimal muscular effort. Inside the joint, the ligamentum teres carries a small artery to the femoral head.

Movement

The hip permits flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and medial and lateral rotation. Powerful muscles act on it, including the gluteal group, the iliopsoas, and the adductors of the thigh. Its range of motion is less than that of the shoulder, a trade-off for stability under load.

Clinical significance

Hip fractures, especially of the femoral neck, are a major cause of morbidity in the elderly. Osteoarthritis of the hip is common and is a leading indication for total hip replacement, one of the most successful operations in modern medicine. Developmental dysplasia of the hip is screened for in infancy.
