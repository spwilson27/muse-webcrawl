Semantic change (also semantic shift, semantic progression, semantic development, or semantic drift) is a form of language change regarding the evolution of word usage — usually to the point that the modern meaning is radically different from the original usage. In diachronic (or historical) linguistics, semantic change is a change in one of the meanings of a word.

Every word has a variety of senses and connotations, which can be added, removed, or altered over time, often to the extent that cognates across space and time have very different meanings. The study of semantic change can be seen as part of etymology, onomasiology, semasiology, and semantics.

Types of semantic change

Linguists commonly classify semantic changes into several types:

  • Broadening (generalization): the meaning becomes more general, as with holiday, originally a holy day.
  • Narrowing (specialization): the meaning becomes more specific, as with meat, originally any food.
  • Amelioration: the meaning acquires a more positive connotation, as with knight, originally a servant or boy.
  • Pejoration: the meaning acquires a more negative connotation, as with silly, originally meaning blessed or happy.
  • Metaphor: a shift based on similarity, as in the computer mouse.
  • Metonymy: a shift based on association, as in the crown for the monarch.

Causes

Semantic change is driven by factors including linguistic causes (such as ellipsis and analogy), psychological factors (such as taboo and euphemism), sociocultural change, and cultural causes, including technological innovation that requires new terms or repurposes old ones.

Examples

The word awful once meant "inspiring awe" but now usually means "very bad". Nice derives from Latin nescius, "ignorant", and passed through senses including "foolish" and "precise" before reaching its current meaning of "pleasant".
