One China

One China is the principle that there is only one sovereign state under the name China. It is the diplomatic position held by both the People's Republic of China (PRC), which controls mainland China, and — in its own interpretation historically — the Republic of China (ROC), which controls Taiwan and nearby islands. The principle is central to cross-strait relations and to the diplomatic recognition of China by other states.

Background

After the Chinese Civil War, the ROC government retreated to Taiwan in 1949 while the Communists proclaimed the PRC in Beijing. Both governments claimed to be the legitimate government of all China. In 1971, United Nations General Assembly Resolution 2758 recognized the PRC as the sole legitimate representative of China to the UN, and the ROC lost the Chinese seat. Most states subsequently switched diplomatic recognition from Taipei to Beijing.

Interpretations

The PRC's "One China principle" holds that Taiwan is an inalienable part of China and that the PRC is the sole legitimate government. The ROC historically agreed there was one China but held that the ROC was that government; successive Taiwanese governments have taken varying positions. The 1992 Consensus, as described by the Kuomintang, refers to a claimed understanding of "one China, respective interpretations". The United States follows a "One China policy" that acknowledges the Chinese position without endorsing it, maintains unofficial relations with Taiwan under the Taiwan Relations Act, and practices strategic ambiguity regarding Taiwan's defence.

Diplomatic practice

States maintaining formal relations with Beijing must generally sever official ties with Taipei; Taiwan's formal diplomatic allies number only a small group of states and the Holy See. Taiwan participates in many international bodies under names such as "Chinese Taipei". International organizations and most countries' official statements reflect One China formulations of varying precision.
