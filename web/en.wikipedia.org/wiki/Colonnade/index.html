Colonnade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In classical architecture, a colonnade is a long sequence of columns joined by their entablature, often free-standing or part of a building. A colonnade may be straight or curved, single or double, and may support a roof, forming a covered walkway akin to a portico or stoa.

Gian Lorenzo Bernini's Tuscan colonnades (1656–1667) frame St Peter's Square in the Vatican.

History

Colonnades originated in the temples and stoas of ancient Greece and were adopted extensively by Roman builders for fora, basilicas, theatres, and colonnaded streets — the great processional avenues of cities such as Palmyra, Jerash, and Ephesus were lined with them. In Islamic architecture, hypostyle mosques such as that of Córdoba extend the colonnade principle into forests of columns.

Notable examples

The most famous colonnade is Bernini's in St Peter's Square, Rome: two semicircular arms of four rows of Doric/Tuscan columns, crowned with statues of saints, designed to embrace visitors "like the arms of the Church". Other celebrated examples include the east front of the Louvre (Perrault's Colonnade, 1667–1670), the colonnades of the Place de la Concorde, the curved ranges of Bath's Royal Crescent (in attached form), and modernist works such as Mies van der Rohe's widely spaced steel columns.

Related forms

A colonnade differs from an arcade in that the columns support a flat entablature rather than arches. A row of columns on one side only is a portico when attached to a building's entrance; a fully surrounding colonnade around a temple is a peristyle, and around a courtyard, a peristylium.
