Cowboy boot
TypeFootwear
MaterialLeather (often exotic skins)
OriginAmerican West, 19th century

A cowboy boot is a distinctive style of riding boot, historically worn by cowboys, characterised by a high shaft, an angled heel, a rounded to pointed toe, and decorative stitching. The style evolved in the American West during the nineteenth century from earlier riding boots worn by vaqueros and cavalrymen.

Traditional cowboy boots are usually made of cowhide leather, although premium and fashion versions may use exotic skins such as alligator, snake, ostrich, lizard, eel, or stingray. The boot is designed for work on horseback: the tall shaft protects the rider's leg, the narrow toe slips easily into a stirrup, and the angled "cowboy heel" helps keep the foot from sliding through the stirrup.

History

Riding boots had been worn for centuries, but the recognisable cowboy boot emerged in the 1870s and 1880s in cattle towns such as Coffeyville, Kansas, and Abilene, Texas, where bootmakers adapted Civil War-era cavalry boots to the needs of working cowboys. Makers such as Charles Hyer of Olathe, Kansas, and Justin Boots, founded in Texas, helped standardise the style.

Design

Key features include a shaft at least 10 inches (25 cm) tall, often decorated with stitched patterns; an underslung heel of about 1.5 to 2 inches; and pull straps ("boot pulls") at the top to help draw the boot on. Modern variants include the roper boot, with a shorter shaft and lower heel suited to working on foot, and fashion boots in a wide range of colours and materials.

In popular culture

Cowboy boots became an enduring symbol of Western wear and are widely worn in country music, rodeo, and Western fashion worldwide.
