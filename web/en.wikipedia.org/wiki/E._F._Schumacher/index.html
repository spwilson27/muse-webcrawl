Ernst Friedrich Schumacher (16 August 1911 – 4 September 1977) was a German-born British economist and statistician who served as chief economic adviser to the British National Coal Board for two decades. He is best known for his 1973 book Small Is Beautiful: A Study of Economics As If People Mattered, one of the most influential critiques of mainstream growth-oriented economics of the twentieth century, and for founding the Intermediate Technology Development Group (now Practical Action) to promote appropriate technology in developing countries.

Early life

Schumacher was born in Bonn, Germany. He studied at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar and later at Columbia University in New York. During the Second World War, he was interned in England as an enemy alien, but his statistical work came to the attention of John Maynard Keynes, who helped secure his release; Schumacher contributed to wartime economic planning and to the intellectual background of the Beveridge Report.

Career

After the war, Schumacher worked with the British Control Commission in Germany, helping to rebuild the German economy, and from 1950 to 1970 he was chief economic adviser to the National Coal Board, then one of Britain's largest employers. An assignment in Burma in 1955 led him to develop the principles he called "Buddhist economics," arguing that development should aim at human well-being and the purification of character rather than the maximization of consumption.

In 1965 he founded the Intermediate Technology Development Group, dedicated to small-scale technologies that poor communities could build, maintain and afford. His ideas of decentralization, "enoughness," and economics as if people mattered made Small Is Beautiful a bestseller during the 1970s energy crises and an enduring influence on the environmental and appropriate-technology movements.

Later life

Schumacher converted to Catholicism in 1971. His final book, A Guide for the Perplexed (1977), a philosophical work on the levels of knowledge, appeared in the year of his death. He died of a heart attack while on a lecture tour in Switzerland in 1977.
