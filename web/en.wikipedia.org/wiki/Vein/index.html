Vein

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A vein is a blood vessel that carries blood towards the Heart. Most veins carry oxygen-poor blood from the tissues back to the heart; the pulmonary veins, returning freshly oxygenated blood from the lungs, are the main exception. Veins have thinner walls and wider lumens than arteries and operate at low pressure.

Structure and function

Vein walls have the same three layers as arteries but with much less muscle and elastic tissue. Many veins, especially in the legs, contain one-way valves that prevent backflow; blood is pushed along by surrounding skeletal muscle contractions (the muscle pump) and by breathing movements. Superficial veins lie near the skin — visible as blue-ish lines because of how light scatters in skin — while deep veins run with arteries.

Clinical significance

Veins are the usual site for blood sampling and intravenous infusions. Varicose veins are swollen, twisted superficial veins with failing valves. A clot forming in a deep vein (deep vein thrombosis) can break loose and lodge in the lungs as a pulmonary embolism, a medical emergency.
