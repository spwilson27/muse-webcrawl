Cost-of-living index

[Image: Cost-of-living index]

A cost-of-living index is a theoretical price index that measures relative cost of living over time or regions. It is an index that measures differences in the price of goods and services, and allows for substitutions with other items as prices change.

Applications

Cost-of-living indices are used by employers and governments to adjust salaries and benefits when employees relocate between areas with different price levels, and to provide cost-of-living adjustments (COLA) to wages, pensions and social security benefits. Surveys such as those published by the Economist Intelligence Unit and Mercer compare the cost of a basket of goods and services across world cities for expatriate compensation purposes.

Methodology

Constructing such an index requires selecting a representative basket of goods and services, gathering prices in each location, and aggregating them, often with weights reflecting typical expenditure patterns. Theoretical work by economists distinguishes the Konüs index — the ratio of minimum expenditures needed to reach a given level of utility at two price sets — from practical approximations such as the consumer price index.

Problems

Difficulties include substitution bias, changes in the quality of goods, the introduction of new products, and differences in tastes and climate between locations. Because households can substitute cheaper goods when prices rise, fixed-basket indices tend to overstate the true change in the cost of living.
