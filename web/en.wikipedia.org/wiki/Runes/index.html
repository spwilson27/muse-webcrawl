Runes are the letters in a set of related alphabets known as runic alphabets, used by Germanic peoples to write various Germanic languages before the adoption of the Latin alphabet, and for specialised purposes thereafter. The Scandinavian variants are also known as futhark (or fuþark), after the first six letters of the alphabet — F, U, Þ, A, R, K — while the Anglo-Saxon variant is called futhorc after the sound changes in Old English.

History

The runes emerged around the 1st or 2nd century CE, probably inspired by Old Italic scripts (such as the Raetic or Etruscan alphabets) as Germanic peoples came into contact with the Roman world. The earliest generally accepted inscription, on the Vimose comb, dates to about 160 CE. The original alphabet, the Elder Futhark, had 24 letters arranged in three groups of eight (ættir).

In Scandinavia the alphabet was reduced to the 16-letter Younger Futhark around the 8th century, reflecting changes in the Old Norse language; this is the script of the Viking Age runestones, of which thousands survive, especially in Sweden. In England the script expanded into the Anglo-Saxon Futhorc with up to 33 letters, used in manuscripts such as the rune poem and in inscriptions like those on the Franks Casket and the Ruthwell Cross. Medieval Scandinavia added further dotted and staveless runes; runes lingered in folk use in parts of Sweden into the early twentieth century.

Names and lore

Every rune had a name, chosen to begin with its sound — for example fehu ("wealth, cattle"), uruz ("aurochs"), thurisaz ("giant"), and ansuz ("god"). Poems in Old English, Old Norse, and Norwegian preserve these names and their traditional associations. In Norse mythology, the god Odin was said to have won the runes by hanging wounded on the world-tree Yggdrasil for nine nights, as told in the Hávamál; runes were also used for memorial inscriptions, ownership marks, messages, and — according to saga literature — for charms and magic.
