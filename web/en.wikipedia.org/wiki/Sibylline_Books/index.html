The Sibylline Books (Latin: Libri Sibyllini) were a collection of oracular utterances, set out in Greek hexameter verses, that according to tradition were purchased from a sibyl by the last king of Rome, Tarquinius Superbus, and consulted at momentous crises through the history of the Roman Republic and Empire.

The legend of acquisition

In the famous story told by Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Aulus Gellius, an old woman (the Cumaean Sibyl) offered Tarquin nine books of prophecy at a high price. When he refused, she burned three, then offered the remaining six at the same price; refused again, she burned three more, and Tarquin finally bought the last three for the original price of all nine.

Use and custody

The books were kept in a stone chest beneath the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill, guarded by a college of priests — first two men (duumviri), later ten, and under Sulla fifteen (quindecimviri sacris faciundis). They could be consulted only by order of the Senate, usually after prodigies or disasters, and prescribed ritual remedies rather than predicting events. Consultations led Rome to import foreign cults, including those of Ceres, Apollo, and the Great Mother (Magna Mater) in 204 BCE. The original collection burned with the temple in 83 BCE; a replacement was assembled by envoys sent to oracles across the Mediterranean and re-edited by Augustus. The surviving books were finally burned by the general Stilicho around 405 CE.

Distinction

The Sibylline Books should not be confused with the surviving Sibylline Oracles, a later Jewish and Christian pseudepigraphical collection in fourteen books.
