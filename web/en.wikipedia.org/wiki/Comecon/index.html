The Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (CMEA or Comecon; Russian: Совет экономической взаимопомощи, СЭВ) was an economic organisation of communist states founded under Soviet leadership in 1949. It coordinated trade, industrial specialisation, and development aid within the Eastern Bloc and with allied socialist countries such as Cuba, Mongolia, and Vietnam, serving as the Eastern counterpart to Western institutions like the Marshall Plan's Organisation for European Economic Co-operation and later the European Economic Community.

History

The founding members — the Soviet Union, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Poland, and Romania — established the council in Moscow in January 1949, partly in response to the Marshall Plan, participation in which Eastern European states had been forbidden. Albania joined later in 1949 (ceasing participation in 1961), East Germany in 1950, Mongolia in 1962, Cuba in 1972, and Vietnam in 1978.

Comecon promoted national specialisation within the bloc — for example, Cuban sugar and Soviet oil were exchanged under long-term agreements — and coordinated pricing through the "transferable rouble," an accounting unit settled through the International Bank for Economic Co-operation (founded 1963). After the 1971 Comprehensive Programme, it pushed deeper integration and joint projects such as the Druzhba oil pipeline. Unlike the EEC, Comecon never achieved a genuine common market: trade remained largely bilateral and barter-based, and the organisation dissolved on 28 June 1991 as its member states turned to market economies.
