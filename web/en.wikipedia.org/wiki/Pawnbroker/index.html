Pawnbroker

A pawnbroker is an individual or business (a pawnshop or pawn shop) that offers secured loans to people, with items of personal property used as collateral. The items pawned are called pledges or pawns; if the loan and interest are repaid within the agreed period, the customer redeems the item, and if not, the pawnbroker may sell it to recover the debt.

History

Pawning is one of the oldest forms of lending, practised in ancient China, Greece and Rome. In medieval Europe, charitable pawnshops known as monts-de-piété were established to lend at low interest to the poor, while Lombard bankers and later the Medici family were associated with pawnbroking; the three golden balls traditionally hung outside pawnshops are often linked to Lombard and Medici heraldry. The three-ball symbol remains the international sign of the trade.

Operation

A customer brings an item — commonly jewellery, electronics, tools, or musical instruments — which the pawnbroker appraises and offers a loan against, typically a fraction of the resale value. The customer receives a pawn ticket recording the item, the loan amount, interest, and the redemption deadline. Unredeemed items are offered for retail sale in the shop. Pawnshops also commonly buy items outright for resale.

Regulation

Pawnbroking is regulated in most jurisdictions: brokers are licensed, interest rates and holding periods are prescribed by law, and shops must keep records and report transactions to police to deter the disposal of stolen goods. In the United States, pawnshops are regulated at state level and are also used as a source of short-term credit by people without access to conventional banking.
