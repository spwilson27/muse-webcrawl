Impairment (financial reporting)


Impairment in financial reporting

Impairment in financial reporting occurs when the carrying amount of an asset exceeds its recoverable amount, requiring the entity to recognize an impairment loss that reduces the asset's book value. The concept ensures that assets are not carried on the balance sheet at more than the economic benefits they are expected to generate.

International standards

Under International Financial Reporting Standards, IAS 36 Impairment of Assets governs the impairment of most non-financial assets. An entity must assess at each reporting date whether there is any indication of impairment; goodwill and indefinite-lived intangible assets must be tested annually regardless of indicators. The recoverable amount is the higher of an asset's fair value less costs of disposal and its value in use, the latter being the present value of expected future cash flows. Where an individual asset cannot be tested alone, impairment is assessed at the level of the cash-generating unit to which the asset belongs.

US GAAP

Under U.S. generally accepted accounting principles, long-lived assets held and used are tested for recoverability using undiscounted cash flows; if the carrying amount is not recoverable, an impairment loss is measured as the excess of carrying amount over fair value. Goodwill impairment is addressed under ASC 350, which permits a qualitative assessment followed, if necessary, by a quantitative test comparing the fair value of a reporting unit with its carrying amount.

Reversals

IAS 36 permits the reversal of impairment losses for assets other than goodwill when estimates change, but reversals are prohibited for goodwill. U.S. GAAP generally prohibits reversals of impairment losses for assets held and used.
