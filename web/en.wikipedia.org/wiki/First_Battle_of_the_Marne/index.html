The First Battle of the Marne was a battle of the First World War fought from 5 to 12 September 1914. The German army had invaded Belgium and northern France and was within 30 miles of Paris. The French and British counterattacked along the Marne, forced the Germans to retreat to the Aisne, and ended Germany's hopes of a swift victory in the west.

Background

Under the Schlieffen Plan, Germany sought to wheel through Belgium and envelop Paris. After the Battle of the Frontiers and the Great Retreat, the Allied armies fell back to the Marne. General Joffre, the French commander-in-chief, prepared a counteroffensive, while the German First Army under von Kluck turned inward, exposing its flank to the Paris garrison.

The battle

On 6 September, General Maunoury's Sixth Army attacked Kluck's flank from Paris — in the famous episode of the "Taxis of the Marne", Parisian taxi cabs helped ferry reinforcements to the front. A gap opened between the German First and Second Armies into which the British Expeditionary Force and French Fifth Army advanced. Unable to close the gap and overextended, the Germans began a general retreat on 9 September to positions along the Aisne, where they dug in.

Aftermath

The battle was a decisive strategic victory for the Allies: the Schlieffen Plan had failed, and Germany faced the long two-front war it had sought to avoid. The subsequent "Race to the Sea" extended the trench lines to the Channel, beginning the stalemate of the Western Front. Casualties were heavy — roughly a quarter of a million on each side.
