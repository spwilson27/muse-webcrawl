Optical printer

An optical printer is a device consisting of one or more film projectors mechanically linked to a movie camera. It allows filmmakers to re-photograph one or more strips of film. The optical printer is used for making special effects for motion pictures, or for copying and restoring old film material.

Uses

Common optical effects include fade outs and fade ins, dissolves, slow motion, fast motion, and matte work. More complicated work can involve dozens of elements, all combined into a single scene. Optical printing also permits format conversions, the addition of titles and subtitles, and the creation of freeze frames by repeatedly printing a single frame.

History

Simple optical printers were in use in the 1920s. Linwood G. Dunn expanded the concept in the 1930s, and during World War II photographic units of the U.S. armed forces commissioned printers for copying aerial reconnaissance film. The machine remained the centrepiece of visual effects production — used extensively for films such as Star Wars (1977), whose effects involved complex multi-element composites — until digital compositing and the digital intermediate process largely replaced it in the 1990s and 2000s.
