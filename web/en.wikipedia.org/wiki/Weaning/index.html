Weaning

Weaning is the process of gradually introducing an infant or young mammal to a diet independent of its mother's milk, and withdrawing the supply of milk. In human infants, weaning encompasses the introduction of complementary foods and, eventually, the end of breastfeeding.

Human infants

The World Health Organization recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life, with continued breastfeeding alongside complementary foods up to two years or beyond. Complementary foods are commonly introduced around six months, beginning with purées or soft finger foods; baby-led weaning, in which infants feed themselves soft pieces of family food, is an alternative approach. The timing of introducing allergenic foods such as peanut and egg has been revised in light of studies suggesting early introduction may reduce allergy risk.

Cultural and historical practice

Weaning ages vary widely across cultures and historical periods, from under a year to three or four years of age. Historically, wet nursing, animal milks and cereal paps played significant roles, with poor outcomes before safe milk substitutes and sanitation existed.

Animal husbandry

In livestock production, weaning age is a major management decision: calves, piglets and lambs are separated from their dams at ages chosen to balance growth, dam condition and economics, and abrupt versus gradual (fence-line) weaning methods are studied for their effects on stress.
