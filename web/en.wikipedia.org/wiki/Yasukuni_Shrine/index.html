Yasukuni Shrine (靖国神社, Yasukuni Jinja, "Peaceful Country Shrine") is a Shinto shrine in central Tokyo that commemorates those who died in service of Japan, from the Boshin War of 1868–69 through the Second World War. Founded by Emperor Meiji, it enshrines the spirits of more than 2.46 million people. Because the enshrined include convicted war criminals, official visits by Japanese leaders have repeatedly provoked controversy and protests from China and South Korea.

History

The shrine was founded in 1869 as the Tokyo Shōkonsha to honour loyalists who died in the Boshin War, and renamed Yasukuni in 1879. It became the central national site for commemorating war dead; the names, origins and dates of death of the enshrined are recorded in its registers. Under State Shinto the shrine was administered by the military. After 1945 it became an independent religious corporation under the Allied-imposed separation of religion and state.

Enshrinement of war criminals

In 1978, fourteen men convicted as Class-A war criminals by the Tokyo Tribunal — among them wartime prime minister Hideki Tojo — were secretly enshrined. The revelation in 1979 made the shrine a lasting diplomatic issue: no Japanese emperor has visited since, and visits or offerings by prime ministers (notably Junichiro Koizumi's annual visits, 2001–2006, and Shinzo Abe's in 2013) have drawn formal protests from Beijing and Seoul and strained relations.

Grounds and museum

The shrine precinct in Kudan includes the main halls, a great steel torii, statues — including that of Ōmura Masujirō, founder of the Imperial Japanese Army — and the Yūshūkan, a war museum whose exhibits present a narrative of Japanese history criticised abroad as revisionist. The shrine is also famous for its cherry blossoms: Tokyo's official sample tree for declaring the sakura season stands in its grounds.
