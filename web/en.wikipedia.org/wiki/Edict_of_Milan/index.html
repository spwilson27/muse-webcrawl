Edict of Milan

Edict of Milan
Date313 CE
AuthorsConstantine I and Licinius
EffectLegalised Christianity in the Roman Empire; restored confiscated church property

The Edict of Milan was an agreement reached in February 313 CE between the Roman emperors Constantine I and Licinius, proclaimed in Nicomedia in June of that year, which granted religious toleration throughout the Roman Empire and specifically legalised Christianity, ending the persecutions begun under Diocletian.

Background

The Diocletianic Persecution (from 303) had attempted to suppress Christianity. In 311 the dying emperor Galerius issued the Edict of Serdica, grudgingly tolerating Christian worship. Constantine, after his victory at the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312 — which Christian tradition linked to a vision of the cross — met Licinius at Milan, where Licinius married Constantine's sister Constantia.

Provisions

The agreement, preserved in the writings of Lactantius and Eusebius, declared that Christians and all others should have freedom to follow whatever religion they chose, so that the supreme divinity might be favourable to the empire. It ordered the prompt restitution, without payment, of churches, cemeteries and other property confiscated from Christians.

Significance

The edict did not make Christianity the state religion — that came later, with the Edict of Thessalonica (380) — but it transformed the church's legal position, paving the way for Constantine's patronage, the calling of the Council of Nicaea (325) and the creed it issued, the Nicene Creed.
