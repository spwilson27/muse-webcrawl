Elastic collision

An elastic collision is an encounter between two bodies in which the total kinetic energy of the two bodies remains the same, in contrast to an inelastic collision, in which kinetic energy is converted into other forms of energy such as heat or deformation. In an ideal, perfectly elastic collision there is no net conversion of kinetic energy into other forms. Momentum is conserved in both elastic and inelastic collisions.

Equations

For two bodies of masses m₁ and m₂ with initial velocities u₁ and u₂ along a line, conservation of momentum and of kinetic energy give the final velocities:

v₁ = [(m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂)] u₁ + [2m₂/(m₁ + m₂)] u₂

v₂ = [2m₁/(m₁ + m₂)] u₁ + [(m₂ − m₁)/(m₁ + m₂)] u₂

A useful equivalent property is that the relative velocity of approach equals the relative velocity of separation. In the special case of equal masses, the bodies simply exchange velocities.

Applications

Nearly elastic collisions occur in the kinetic theory of gases, where collisions between gas molecules are treated as elastic. In nuclear physics, neutron moderation relies on elastic scattering of neutrons by light nuclei such as hydrogen and carbon. Newton's cradle demonstrates approximately elastic collisions between steel balls. At the atomic scale, Rutherford scattering — the elastic deflection of alpha particles by atomic nuclei — provided key evidence for the structure of the atom.

Relativistic case

Under special relativity, momentum and total energy (including rest energy) are conserved, and the Newtonian formulas are replaced by expressions obtained from the Lorentz-invariant conservation laws; in the limit of speeds much less than the speed of light, these reduce to the classical results.
