The Missoula floods (also known as the Spokane floods or the Bretz floods) were cataclysmic glacial lake outburst floods that swept periodically across eastern Washington and down the Columbia River Gorge at the end of the last ice age. The floods were the result of periodic sudden ruptures of the ice dam on the Clark Fork River that created Glacial Lake Missoula in present-day Montana.

Glacial Lake Missoula drained catastrophically many times, sending walls of water across the Columbia Plateau.

Geology

During the last glaciation, the Cordilleran Ice Sheet advanced southward and a lobe of ice blocked the Clark Fork River near the Idaho panhandle, impounding Glacial Lake Missoula, which contained as much as 2,100 cubic kilometres (500 cu mi) of water — roughly half the volume of modern Lake Michigan. As the lake deepened, pressure on the ice dam increased until the dam floated or fractured, releasing the entire lake in a matter of days. Peak discharge is estimated at around 17 million cubic metres per second, tens of times the combined flow of all modern rivers.

Effects on the landscape

The floodwaters raced across the Columbia Plateau, stripping soil and carving the coulees, cataracts, and channels of the Channeled Scablands. Enormous gravel bars, giant current ripples, and erratic boulders were deposited far downstream. Floodwaters ponded behind the narrow Wallula Gap and in the Willamette Valley, briefly forming temporary lakes, and carried sediment all the way to the Pacific Ocean.

Frequency

Rhythmic sediment layers indicate that the lake filled and emptied dozens of times — perhaps 40 or more floods occurred between roughly 15,000 and 13,000 years ago, with intervals of decades between events as the ice dam re-formed.

Scientific acceptance

Geologist J Harlen Bretz proposed the catastrophic-flood explanation in the 1920s, but it was resisted for decades because it conflicted with the prevailing uniformitarian view that landscapes change only gradually. Evidence assembled by Bretz, J. T. Pardee, and later researchers eventually won acceptance, and the floods are now a classic example of megaflood geology, with analogues identified on Mars.
