Dowry

Dowry
DirectionFrom the bride's family to the groom or his family
ContrastBride price (groom's family to bride's family); dower
RegionsHistorically Europe, South Asia, parts of Africa and the Middle East

A dowry is a payment, such as property or money, paid by the bride's family to the groom or his family at the time of marriage. Dowry contrasts with bride price, which is paid by the groom or his family to the bride's family, and with dower, which is property settled on the bride herself by the groom's family. Traditionally, a dowry was intended to help establish the new household and to provide the bride with some financial security; it could represent her share of the family inheritance, settled at marriage rather than at her parents' death.

History

Dowries were customary in ancient Greece and Rome, throughout medieval and early modern Europe — where elaborate dowry funds, such as Florence's Monte delle doti, were created to finance them — and in colonial America. In Europe the practice declined during the 19th and 20th centuries as women's property rights expanded. In South Asia, dowry remains widespread, particularly in India, where it developed from the traditional gift of stridhan (a woman's own property) into, in many communities, large payments demanded by the groom's family.

Dowry in India and dowry deaths

Dowry demands have been linked to serious social harms in India, including harassment, violence and "dowry deaths" — the killing or driven suicide of young brides over inadequate dowries. India prohibited dowry in the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961 and created specific offences for dowry death and dowry-related cruelty in the Indian Penal Code, though enforcement remains difficult and the practice persists. Similar debates occur in Pakistan and Bangladesh, which have their own dowry-prohibition laws.

Related customs

Related marriage payments include the Islamic mahr, paid by the groom to the bride herself, and the trousseau of household goods a bride traditionally brought to her new home.
