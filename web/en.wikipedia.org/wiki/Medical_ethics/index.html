Medical ethics

Medical ethics is an applied branch of ethics which analyses the practice of clinical medicine and related scientific research. Medical ethics is based on a set of values that professionals can refer to in the case of any confusion or conflict. It is closely related to bioethics, and includes questions of patient autonomy, confidentiality, consent and the just allocation of medical resources.

Principles

A widely used framework, set out by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress, identifies four prima facie principles: autonomy (respecting the patient's self-determination), beneficence (acting in the patient's best interest), non-maleficence ("first, do no harm") and justice (fair treatment and fair distribution of resources). In practice these principles can conflict, and ethics committees help to resolve difficult cases.

History

The most famous ancient code is the Hippocratic Oath (c. 5th–3rd century BC). Modern medical ethics was reshaped by the abuses of the Second World War: the Nuremberg Code (1947) established requirements for research on human subjects, and the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964) set ethical standards for medical research, emphasising informed consent. The Tuskegee syphilis study scandal led in the United States to the Belmont Report and institutional review boards.

Topics

Medical ethics addresses abortion, euthanasia and assisted dying, organ transplantation, genetic testing, reproduction, end-of-life care, medical confidentiality, conflicts of interest, and equitable access to treatment.
