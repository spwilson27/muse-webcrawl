Polynesian languages

The Polynesian languages form a branch of the Oceanic subfamily of the Austronesian language family. There are about 38 Polynesian languages, spoken across the Polynesian triangle — bounded by Hawaii, Easter Island (Rapa Nui) and New Zealand — as well as in Polynesian outlier communities in Melanesia and Micronesia.

Classification

Polynesian is commonly divided into two main branches: Tongic (Tongan and Niuean) and Nuclear Polynesian, which contains all the rest, including Samoan and the Ellicean group (Tuvaluan and the outlier languages) and the Eastern Polynesian languages such as Tahitian, Hawaiian, Rapa Nui, Cook Islands Māori and New Zealand Māori. The family is notable for its small phoneme inventories (some languages have as few as ten consonants and five vowels) and strictly open syllables.

History

Proto-Polynesian is reconstructed as having been spoken in the Tonga–Samoa area roughly 2,000 years ago. From there, seafaring settlers carried the languages eastward across the Pacific in one of the last great episodes of human maritime migration, reaching Hawaii, Rapa Nui and New Zealand by about 1200–1300 CE. Many Polynesian languages are now endangered, though revitalization efforts, notably for Hawaiian and Māori, have had notable success.
