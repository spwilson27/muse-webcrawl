Pledge of Allegiance

The Pledge of Allegiance of the United States is an expression of allegiance to the flag of the United States and the republic of the United States of America. Such a pledge was first composed, with a text different from the current one, in the middle of the 19th century by Francis Bellamy in 1892 and formally adopted by Congress as the pledge in 1942. The official name of The Pledge of Allegiance was adopted in 1945. The most recent alteration of its wording came on Flag Day in 1954, when the words "under God" were added.

Text

The current text of the pledge, as codified in United States law (4 U.S.C. § 4), reads:

I pledge allegiance to the Flag of the United States of America, and to the Republic for which it stands, one Nation under God, indivisible, with liberty and justice for all.

The pledge should be rendered by standing at attention facing the flag with the right hand over the heart. When not in uniform, men should remove any non-religious headdress with their right hand and hold it at the left shoulder, the hand being over the heart. Persons in uniform should remain silent, face the flag, and render the military salute.

History

Origins

The original pledge was composed in August 1892 by Francis Bellamy (1855–1931), a Baptist minister and Christian socialist, who worked for The Youth's Companion, a family magazine. James B. Upham, his superior at the magazine, had been organising a national celebration of the 400th anniversary of Christopher Columbus's voyage, and the pledge was published as part of a patriotic programme for public schools to recite in unison:

I pledge allegiance to my Flag and the Republic for which it stands, one nation, indivisible, with liberty and justice for all.

The pledge was first recited in public schools on 12 October 1892 during Columbus Day observances organised to coincide with the opening of the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago.

Changes

In 1923, at the first National Flag Conference in Washington, D.C., delegates feared that immigrant children might confuse "my flag" with the flags of their native countries, and the words "my Flag" were changed to "the Flag of the United States." The following year, "of America" was added. On 22 June 1942, Congress formally recognised the pledge by including it in the United States Flag Code, although the code at the time specified that civilians should recite it with the right hand outstretched toward the flag; because of its resemblance to the Nazi salute then in use, the gesture was changed later that year so that the hand was held over the heart throughout.

In 1954, in response to the perceived threat of secular Communism, President Dwight D. Eisenhower encouraged Congress to add the words "under God." The amendment was adopted and signed into law on Flag Day, 14 June 1954.

Controversies

Recitation of the pledge in public schools has been the subject of repeated litigation. In Minersville School District v. Gobitis (1940), the Supreme Court upheld compulsory flag salutes, but reversed itself three years later in West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette (1943), ruling that students could not be compelled to salute the flag or recite the pledge. Later cases have challenged the phrase "under God" as an establishment of religion; the Ninth Circuit's 2002 ruling in the case brought by Michael Newdow was ultimately decided by the Supreme Court on standing grounds in Elk Grove Unified School District v. Newdow (2004), leaving the phrase in place.
