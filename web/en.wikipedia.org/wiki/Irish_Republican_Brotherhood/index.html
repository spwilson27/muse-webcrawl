The Irish Republican Brotherhood (IRB) was a secret oath-bound organisation dedicated to the establishment of an "independent democratic republic" in Ireland. It was founded in Dublin on St Patrick's Day, 17 March 1858, by James Stephens, John O'Mahony and Michael Doheny, in parallel with the Fenian Brotherhood founded in New York by O'Mahony and Doheny among Irish-American emigrants. Members of the two organisations were commonly known as Fenians.

Origins and early years

The IRB grew out of the failure of the Young Ireland rising of 1848 and the conviction of a generation of Irish nationalists that only a secret, military organisation funded by the Irish diaspora could break British rule. Stephens organised the movement into cells ("circles") headed by "centres", with Stephens himself as head centre. American money and veterans of the American Civil War provided crucial support.

The movement's newspaper, The Irish People, was suppressed in 1865 and its leaders arrested. An attempted rising in March 1867 fizzled out, but Fenian violence continued — notably the Manchester Martyrs incident and the Clerkenwell bombing of 1867. In the 1870s and 1880s the IRB declined in influence as constitutional nationalism under Isaac Butt and Charles Stewart Parnell, and the Land League agitation, offered more promising avenues, though IRB men were involved in both.

Revival and the Easter Rising

From about 1907 a younger generation — including Tom Clarke, Seán Mac Diarmada, Bulmer Hobson and Denis McCullough — revived the IRB, using its network within the Gaelic League, the GAA and the Irish Volunteers. In 1915 the IRB's Military Council resolved to stage a rising while Britain was at war. The Easter Rising of April 1916, proclaimed in the name of the Irish Republic, was largely an IRB operation: all seven signatories of the Proclamation were IRB members.

War of Independence and decline

After 1916 the IRB's supreme council reconstituted itself as the government of the Irish Republic, and Michael Collins became its dominant figure, simultaneously president of the IRB, Minister for Finance in the Dáil government and Director of Intelligence of the IRA during the War of Independence (1919–1921). After the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921, Collins's supporters and the anti-Treaty side disputed the IRB's legitimacy. The organisation was revived briefly during the Civil War but was wound up in 1924, its purpose having been overtaken by the creation of the Irish Free State.
