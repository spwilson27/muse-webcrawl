Linear temporal logic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic, linear temporal logic (LTL) is a modal temporal logic with modalities referring to time. It is used to encode statements about the future of paths in a system, such as "a request will eventually be granted" or "an event always eventually occurs." LTL was proposed by Amir Pnueli in 1977 for the formal verification of computer programs.

Syntax and operators

LTL extends propositional logic with unary temporal operators: X φ ("next": φ holds at the next moment), G φ ("globally": φ always holds from now on), F φ ("finally/eventually": φ will hold at some future moment), and the binary operator φ U ψ ("until": φ holds continuously until ψ becomes true). Formulas are evaluated over infinite sequences of states (paths), which is what makes the logic "linear": at any moment the future is a single sequence rather than a branching tree.

Applications

LTL is the principal specification language of the model checker SPIN and is widely used in hardware and software verification, together with its branching-time counterpart CTL. Properties such as safety (nothing bad ever happens, G¬bad) and liveness (something good eventually happens, F good) are canonically expressed in LTL. Model checking an LTL formula against a finite-state system is PSPACE-complete; the standard algorithm translates the negated formula into a Büchi automaton and checks emptiness of its product with the system.
