Smoking (cooking)

Smoking is the process of flavouring, browning, cooking, or preserving food — especially meat, fish and cheese — by exposing it to smoke from burning or smouldering material, most often wood. It is one of the oldest food-preservation techniques, and in many cultures it developed distinct regional traditions, from North American barbecue to European smoked fish and sausages.

History

Smoking food likely arose in prehistory, when early humans noticed that meat hung near a fire kept longer and tasted better. The antimicrobial and antioxidant compounds in wood smoke, combined with the drying effect of the process, slow spoilage by inhibiting bacterial growth and delaying the oxidation of fats. Traditional smoking was often combined with curing (salting or brining) before the food was hung in a smokehouse or chimney.

Methods

Cold smoking

Cold smoking exposes food to smoke at temperatures typically between 20 and 30 °C (68–86 °F), away from the heat source. The food is flavoured and partially dried but not cooked, so perishable items such as salmon are usually cured first. Cold-smoked products include lox and some cheeses and sausages.

Hot smoking

Hot smoking takes place at roughly 52–80 °C (126–176 °F), cooking the food while it absorbs smoke. It is the method used for most barbecue: brisket, ribs, pulled pork and smoked poultry are hot-smoked for hours until tender.

Smoke roasting and other variants

Smoke roasting (barbecuing) uses still higher temperatures, while liquid smoke — produced by condensing smoke and dissolving it in water — is used industrially to impart smoke flavour without the traditional process.

Woods used

Different woods give different flavours. Hardwoods are preferred; resinous softwoods such as pine are generally avoided. Hickory gives a strong, bacon-like flavour; mesquite is intense and common in the American Southwest; fruit woods such as apple and cherry are milder and sweeter; alder is traditional for salmon in the Pacific Northwest; oak is a versatile medium smoke.

Health considerations

Smoked foods can contain polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), some of which are carcinogenic, formed when fat drips onto the fire or from the smoke itself. Modern commercial smoking controls temperatures and smoke exposure to limit PAH formation, and health authorities advise moderate consumption of heavily smoked and charred foods.
