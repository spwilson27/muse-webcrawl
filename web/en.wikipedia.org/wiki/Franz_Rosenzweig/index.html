Franz Rosenzweig (25 December 1886 – 10 December 1929) was a German-Jewish philosopher, theologian, and historian. His magnum opus, The Star of Redemption (Der Stern der Erlösung, 1921), is a foundational work of twentieth-century Jewish thought and of dialogical philosophy.

Life

Rosenzweig was born into an assimilated Jewish family in Kassel. He studied medicine and then history and philosophy, completing a dissertation on Hegel published as Hegel and the State (1920). In 1913, after a famous night-long conversation in Leipzig with his friend Eugen Rosenstock-Huessy, he resolved against converting to Christianity and instead embraced Judaism, an experience sealed by his attendance at an Orthodox Yom Kippur service in Berlin.

He served in the German army during the First World War, writing much of The Star of Redemption on military postcards sent home from the front. In 1920 he founded and directed the Freies Jüdisches Lehrhaus, an institute of adult Jewish education in Frankfurt. In 1922 he was diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, which progressively paralyzed him; he continued writing with the aid of his wife Edith and a specially adapted typewriter.

Thought

The Star of Redemption opposes the totalizing systems of German idealism with a "new thinking" grounded in lived experience, speech, and revelation. Its structure moves from the elements God, world, and man through creation, revelation, and redemption, culminating in the six-pointed star formed by Judaism and Christianity, which Rosenzweig treated as complementary paths.

Translation work

With Martin Buber, Rosenzweig undertook a celebrated German translation of the Hebrew Bible, begun in 1925 and left unfinished at his death in 1929; Buber later completed it.
