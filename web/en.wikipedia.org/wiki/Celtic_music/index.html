Celtic music is a broad grouping of music genres that evolved out of the folk traditions of the Celtic peoples: Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Brittany, Cornwall, the Isle of Man and Galicia, together with their diaspora communities, notably in Atlantic Canada and the United States. The term is used both for traditional forms — dance tunes, airs and ballads — and for modern fusions drawing on them.

Forms and instruments

The core dance repertoire consists of reels, jigs, hornpipes, strathspeys and polkas, played in sessions on fiddle, wooden flute, tin whistle, button accordion, concertina and pipes (uilleann pipes in Ireland, Great Highland bagpipes in Scotland), with rhythmic backing from bodhrán, guitar or bouzouki — the latter two adopted during the 20th-century revival. Song traditions include Irish sean-nós ("old style") singing, Scottish Gaelic puirt à beul (mouth music) and waulking songs, and the Welsh plygain carol tradition. The Celtic harp is among the oldest associated instruments, once central to bardic culture in Ireland, Scotland and Wales.

History and revival

Collections of Irish and Scottish tunes were printed from the 18th century (for example the Neal collection of 1724 and Edward Bunting's manuscripts from the 1792 Belfast Harp Festival). Emigration carried the music to North America, where it influenced old-time and bluegrass music. A major folk revival from the 1960s — with groups such as The Dubliners, The Chieftains, Planxty and Scotland's Battlefield Band — brought Celtic music to world audiences; later acts fused it with rock, jazz and world music, and "Celtic" became a successful commercial category, though its use as a single label for distinct national traditions is sometimes debated.
