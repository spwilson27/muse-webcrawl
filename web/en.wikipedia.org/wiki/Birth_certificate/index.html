Birth certificate

TypeVital record / identity document
Issued byCivil registration authority
RecordsDate and place of birth, name, parents' names
Used forProof of identity, citizenship, age

A birth certificate is a vital record that documents the birth of a person. The term "birth certificate" can refer to either the original document certifying the circumstances of the birth or to a certified copy of, or representation of, the ensuing registration of that birth. Depending on the jurisdiction, a record of birth might or might not contain verification of the event by a health professional such as a midwife or doctor.

The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child guarantees every child the right to be registered immediately after birth, to a name, and to acquire a nationality. Birth registration is the first step in establishing a person's legal identity, and an unregistered child may face obstacles in accessing education, healthcare, and legal protection.

Contents

A full ("long form") birth certificate typically records the child's full name, sex, date and place of birth, and the names — and often the occupations, birthplaces, and ages — of the parents. Abridged or short-form certificates contain only the child's details. Certified copies issued by a registry office or vital statistics agency usually carry a raised seal or other security features.

Uses

Birth certificates are foundational identity documents. They are commonly required when applying for a passport or driver's licence, enrolling in school, proving citizenship or nationality, claiming pensions and inheritance, and for genealogical research. In many jurisdictions the original registration is retained permanently by the civil registrar, and individuals obtain certified copies as needed throughout life.

History

Systematic civil registration of births developed in Europe in the 19th century, replacing or supplementing church baptismal registers. England and Wales established compulsory civil registration in 1837 (enforced from 1875), and most countries now maintain national vital registration systems, though coverage remains incomplete in parts of the developing world.
