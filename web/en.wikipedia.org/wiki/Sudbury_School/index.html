Sudbury school

A Sudbury school is a type of school that practices a form of democratic education in which students individually decide what to do with their time and learn as a by-product of ordinary experience rather than through classes or a standard curriculum. Students are held solely responsible for their education, and the school is run by a direct democracy in which students and staff have an equal vote. The model is named for the Sudbury Valley School, founded in 1968 in Framingham, Massachusetts.

Principles

Sudbury schools rest on the beliefs that children are naturally curious and learn best what they need when they need it; that freedom with personal responsibility develops initiative and self-discipline; and that democratic participation teaches the skills and values of self-government. There are no required classes, no tests, no grades, and no homework. Learning occurs through play, conversation, exploration, apprenticeships, and projects initiated by students.

Governance

The School Meeting, comprising all students and staff, each with one vote, makes all major decisions: it enacts rules, elects officers, allocates budgets, and grants diplomas. A Judicial Committee, typically rotating and age-mixed, investigates complaints and enforces rules. Staff are hired (or not rehired) annually by vote of the School Meeting.

History and spread

Sudbury Valley School was founded by Daniel and Hanna Greenberg, Mimsy Sadofsky, and others. Its writings — including Free at Last (1987) — inspired schools worldwide; dozens of schools identify with the model in North America, Europe, Israel, Japan, and elsewhere. The founders deliberately declined to franchise, so each "Sudbury school" is independent and adapts the philosophy locally.

Outcomes and criticism

Follow-up studies published by the school report that alumni attend college at substantial rates and express satisfaction with their self-directed preparation. Critics argue that unstructured environments may underserve some children, that outcomes data are self-selected, and that lack of required literacy and numeracy instruction risks gaps. Supporters respond that students reliably learn reading and arithmetic when they perceive a need, and that coercion undermines motivation.

The model shares roots with, but differs from, A. S. Neill's Summerhill School, which retains offered (optional) classes, and from unschooling, which is home-based.
