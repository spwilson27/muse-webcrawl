Whale vocalization

Whale vocalization is the sounds made by whales and other cetaceans to communicate. The word "song" is used in particular to describe the pattern of regular and predictable sounds made by some species of whales — notably the humpback whale — in a way that is reminiscent of human singing. Other cetaceans produce clicks and whistles: toothed whales use rapid bursts of high-frequency clicks for echolocation and social signalling.

Production of sound

Cetaceans lack vocal cords. Toothed whales force air through phonic lips in the nasal passage, focusing clicks with the fatty melon; baleen whales appear to produce sound with a specialised larynx and recycle air without exhaling — allowing a humpback to sing for long periods underwater without releasing bubbles.

Humpback song

Male humpbacks sing complex songs lasting 10–20 minutes, repeated for hours, structured in themes that all males in a population share and which evolve gradually over seasons — a form of cultural transmission. Songs are heard mainly on breeding grounds and are thought to relate to mating, whether attracting females or mediating male interactions. First described by Roger Payne and Scott McVay in 1971, whale song recordings helped galvanise the "Save the Whales" movement. Blue and fin whales produce among the lowest-frequency, loudest sounds of any animal, travelling whole ocean basins, while the mysterious 52-hertz whale sings at an unusual frequency. Rising ocean noise from shipping is a growing concern for cetacean communication.
