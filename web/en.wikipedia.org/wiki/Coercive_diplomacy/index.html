Coercive diplomacy

Coercive diplomacy is a strategy in international relations that combines threats of force, and if necessary the limited and selective use of force, with diplomacy in order to induce an adversary to comply with one's demands. It differs from brute force or pure deterrence in that the coercer seeks to persuade the target to change its behaviour while the target still has a choice, rather than simply overwhelming it militarily.

Forces deployed in a crisis can serve as instruments of coercive diplomacy, signalling resolve without full-scale war.

Theory

The concept was developed most prominently by the political scientist Alexander L. George, who defined coercive diplomacy as the use of threats and limited force to back up a demand made on an adversary, with the aim of persuading the adversary to comply rather than bludgeoning it into submission. Whereas deterrence seeks to dissuade an adversary from taking an action it has not yet taken, compellence — of which coercive diplomacy is a diplomatic form — seeks to make an adversary stop or undo an action already under way. Thomas Schelling's work on the strategy of conflict provided much of the underlying logic: the power to hurt is bargaining power, and violence can be most effective when held in reserve as a threat.

Strategies

George identified several variants of the strategy, including:

  • Try-and-see — a single, limited threat or action, after which the coercer waits to see whether the target complies before escalating.
  • Turning of the screw — gradual, incremental increases in pressure, giving the adversary repeated opportunities to comply.
  • Ultimatum — a clear demand accompanied by a deadline and a threat of punishment for non-compliance; George distinguished tacit ultimatums, explicit ultimatums, and a "gradual turning of the screw" combined with a deadline.

In all variants, successful coercive diplomacy usually pairs the threat with positive inducements or assurances — sometimes described as "carrots" alongside the "sticks" — so that compliance is more attractive than defiance.

Conditions for success

Research on coercive diplomacy suggests it is more likely to succeed when the demand is clear and limited, when the threat is credible and potent enough to convince the target that the costs of defiance exceed the costs of compliance, when the target believes further escalation will follow non-compliance, and when the coercer offers the target a face-saving way to comply. It tends to fail when demands touch the target regime's survival, when the coercer's resolve is doubted, or when the target can absorb the threatened punishment.

Historical cases

Frequently cited cases include the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962, in which the United States combined a naval "quarantine" of Cuba with diplomacy to secure the withdrawal of Soviet missiles; the international response to Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990–91, where coercive demands backed by a large coalition deployment failed to dislodge Iraqi forces and gave way to war; and the 1999 Kosovo crisis, in which NATO's air campaign was intended in part to coerce Yugoslav president Slobodan Milošević into accepting a settlement. The 1994 Haiti crisis, in which a looming invasion induced the military junta to step aside at the last moment, is often treated as a clearer success.
