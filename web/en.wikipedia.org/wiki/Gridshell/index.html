Gridshell

A gridshell is a structure that derives its strength from a doubly curved shell surface formed by a lattice, or grid, of members. The name was coined by the architect and engineer Frei Otto, whose Mannheim Multihalle (1975), built with Ove Arup, became the classic example: a timber lattice laid flat on the ground as a loose mat and then lifted and deformed into a free-form shell spanning a large exhibition space.

Construction and examples

Timber gridshells exploit the flexibility of thin wooden laths connected at crossing points so the grid can shear and bend into double curvature before being braced and fixed. Later examples include the Weald and Downland Gridshell (2002) in England, a laminated larch structure housing a museum conservation workshop, and the shimmering steel-and-glass gridshell of the Great Court roof at the British Museum (2000) by Foster + Partners and Buro Happold. Metal gridshells, in which the lattice is fabricated directly in its curved form, are used for station roofs, atria and free-form building envelopes. Gridshells are material-efficient but geometrically and structurally complex, and their design relies heavily on form-finding computation.
