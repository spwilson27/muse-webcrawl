Écriture féminine (French for "women's writing") is a concept in feminist literary theory, developed principally by the French writer and philosopher Hélène Cixous in the 1970s, which holds that women's writing differs fundamentally from the dominant masculine literary tradition and that women should write from and through the body, inscribing their own experience and difference into language.

Origins

Cixous introduced the term in her manifesto-essay "Le rire de la Méduse" ("The Laugh of the Medusa", 1975), urging women to "write herself" and to break the rules of the phallocentric symbolic order. Related ideas were developed independently by Luce Irigaray, who argued that Western philosophy is built on a masculine subject and proposed a syntax of sexual difference, and by Julia Kristeva, whose theory of the semiotic — a pre-symbolic rhythm and drive within language — influenced notions of a feminine disruption of rational discourse.

Characteristics

Écriture féminine is characterised by fluidity, multiplicity, non-linearity, cyclical time, and a resistance to binary oppositions and fixed meaning. Drawing on psychoanalysis, its theorists held that because girls' psychosexual development differs from boys', women's libidinal economy — described as more diffuse and generous, an "economy of the gift" — produces a distinct relation to language. Importantly, Cixous insisted it is not restricted to biological women: she cited Jean Genet as an example of a man who wrote in a feminine mode.

Criticism

The concept has been criticized as essentialist — as fixing women to a biological or psychic essence — notably by materialist feminists such as Monique Wittig and Christine Delphy, and by Toril Moi in Sexual/Textual Politics (1985), who nonetheless recognized its strategic power. It remains a cornerstone of French feminist theory and of feminist literary studies.
