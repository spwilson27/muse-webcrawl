Waterway

A waterway is any navigable body of water. A body of water, such as a river, canal or lake, is navigable if it is deep, wide and calm enough for a watercraft (such as boats) to pass safely. Such a navigable water is called a waterway, specifically a navigable waterway. It must be deep enough to accommodate the draft of the vessels and not be narrow enough to restrict the beam of the vessel.

Navigability is also constrained by the velocity of the flow of water and obstructions such as low bridges, weirs, locks, dams, submerged rocks and seasonal ice. Tributaries that cannot be used by vessels are not counted as waterways in the commercial sense, though they may be used by smaller craft such as kayaks and pleasure boats.

Types of waterway

Waterways can be natural, such as rivers, lakes, estuaries and seas, or artificial, such as canals, reservoirs and channelised river sections. Inland waterways, particularly canals and improved rivers, played a central role in the growth of industrial trade before the expansion of the railway networks, and remain important for the bulk carriage of goods such as aggregates, fuel and containers.

Definitions by jurisdiction

In the United States, a "navigable waterway" is defined as a waterway over which Congress has Commerce Clause authority to regulate, including waters usable for interstate or foreign commerce. In the United Kingdom, the term is linked to the existence of a public right of navigation, which may arise at common law on tidal waters and by statute or prescription on certain non-tidal waters.
