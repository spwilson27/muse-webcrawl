Whirlwind I

Whirlwind I was an early digital computer developed at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology between 1945 and 1951, under the direction of Jay Wright Forrester. Originally conceived as a flight simulator for the U.S. Navy (Project Whirlwind), it became the first computer capable of real-time computation, and it profoundly influenced the design of later machines.

Development

The project began in MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory as an analogue flight trainer before Forrester, inspired by the ENIAC, converted it to a digital, stored-program design. Robert Everett was the project's chief engineer. Whirlwind ran its first program in 1949 and was formally dedicated in April 1951. It occupied two floors of the Barta Building, using around 5,000 vacuum tubes, with a 16-bit word length and an instruction rate of about 20,000 instructions per second — extraordinarily fast for its era.

Innovations

Whirlwind pioneered several foundational technologies. Frustrated with unreliable electrostatic storage tubes, Forrester invented magnetic-core memory, first installed in 1953, which became the industry standard for two decades. The machine used parallel rather than serial arithmetic, displayed output on cathode-ray tubes, and was operated interactively with light guns and keyboards — an ancestor of modern interactive computing.

Legacy

Whirlwind's real-time reliability made it the prototype for the U.S. Air Force's SAGE air-defence system, whose AN/FSQ-7 computers were built by IBM using Whirlwind's architecture. Its technology seeded Digital Equipment Corporation (founded by Whirlwind alumnus Ken Olsen), and its transistorized successor, the TX-0 and TX-2 at Lincoln Laboratory, led directly to the PDP line of minicomputers.
