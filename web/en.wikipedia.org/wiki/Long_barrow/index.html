Long barrow
PeriodEarly Neolithic, c. 4000–3000 BC
RegionBritish Isles and Atlantic Europe
FunctionCommunal burial monument
MaterialsEarth (earthen long barrows) or stone chambers (chambered long barrows)

A long barrow is a rectangular or trapezoidal tumulus, or burial mound, built during the Neolithic period. Long barrows are among the oldest surviving stone and earth monuments in Britain and are widely distributed across the British Isles and along the Atlantic fringe of Europe, with related forms in northern Europe.

Forms

Archaeologists distinguish earthen long barrows, elongated mounds of earth often flanked by ditches, which cover timber or turf mortuary structures, from chambered long barrows, in which stone-built chambers (as in megalithic tombs such as West Kennet Long Barrow and Wayland's Smithy) received successive burials. Mounds range from about 20 to over 100 metres in length.

Burials and interpretation

The barrows typically contain the disarticulated remains of many individuals — often several dozen — suggesting they served as communal tombs and places of ancestor veneration for early farming communities, rather than as graves of single elites. Some skeletons show signs of excarnation (exposure of the body before burial) and deliberate rearrangement of bones.

In later folklore many long barrows acquired legends linking them to giants, saints, or the Devil.
