Qu (poetry)

A page of qu verse in a classical anthology.

Qu (Chinese: 曲; pinyin: qǔ) is a type of classical Chinese poetry form, consisting of words written in one of a number of certain, set tone patterns, based upon the tunes of various songs. Thus qu poems are lyrics with lines of varying longer and shorter lengths, set according to the certain and specific, published patterns of rhyme and tone of conventional musical pieces upon which they are based; and, confounding qu lyrics set to the same tune may have entirely different content. Sanqu and zaju are two commonly performed forms of qu.

History

The qu form arose during the Jin dynasty and flourished in the Yuan dynasty (1271–1368), when it became one of the era's defining literary achievements, alongside Yuan drama. Whereas the earlier ci poetry of the Song dynasty was written to pre-existing melodies within strict courtly conventions, the qu drew on popular songs of the streets and pleasure quarters, admitting colloquial language, wit, and vivid narrative into classical verse.

Forms

Sanqu ("scattered songs") are individual songs or suites not attached to a drama, ranging from short lyric songs (xiaoling) to longer suites (santao) sharing a single musical mode. Zaju ("variety plays") are full dramatic works in which the dialogue is interspersed with qu arias, all sung by the lead role. Masters of the form include Guan Hanqing, Ma Zhiyuan, and Zhang Yanghao, whose meditations on history and transience are among the most famous sanqu ever written.

Characteristics

Unlike shi and ci, qu permits padding characters (chenzi) — extra words inserted for rhythm and flavour without regard to the strict syllable count — giving the verse a flexibility and liveliness close to spoken language. The tonal patterns derive from the musical modes of the repertoire, catalogued in rhyme books such as Zhou Deqing's Zhongyuan Yinyun (1324).
