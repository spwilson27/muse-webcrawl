Also calledPorcelain enamel

Vitreous enamel, also called porcelain enamel, is a material made by fusing powdered glass to a substrate by firing, usually between 830 and 870 °C (1,530 and 1,600 °F). The powder melts, flows, and hardens into a smooth, durable vitreous coating. The word vitreous comes from the Latin vitrum, meaning glass.

Enamelling is an old and widely adopted technology. The ancient Egyptians applied enamel decorations to pottery and stone ornaments, and the Greeks, Chinese, and Russians also practised the art. Since the 19th century the process has been used industrially on metals such as steel and cast iron to protect them from corrosion and to give them an attractive, easily cleaned surface.

Composition and application

The ground coating of a vitreous enamel is a frit: a mixture of silica (sand), soda ash, borax, and other fluxes and metal oxides that is smelted and quenched to form a glass, then milled into a fine powder. Pigments such as cobalt oxide (blue), chromium oxide (green), and iron oxide (red) give the enamel its colour. The powder is applied to a cleaned metal surface by spraying, dipping, or dusting, and the workpiece is then fired in a furnace so that the glass melts and bonds chemically to the metal.

Properties

Vitreous enamel is hard, scratch-resistant, chemically inert, and resistant to heat and corrosion. Its surface is non-porous and hygienic, which makes it suitable for cookware, bathtubs, sinks, and hospital equipment. Its main weakness is brittleness: a sharp impact can chip the coating and expose the substrate. The thermal expansion of the glass must be carefully matched to that of the metal, otherwise the enamel may crack or craze on cooling.

Decorative techniques

Artists have developed many decorative enamelling techniques, including cloisonné, in which wires outline coloured compartments; champlevé, in which cells are hollowed out of the metal; plique-à-jour, a translucent stained-glass effect; and painted enamel, perfected at Limoges in France. Famous works include the medieval Limoges enamels, Byzantine icons, and the jewelled eggs made by the House of Fabergé.

Industrial uses

Industrial porcelain enamel is used on major appliances such as stoves, washing machines, and water heaters; on architectural panels and signage (including street signs and advertising signs prized by collectors); on chemical reactor vessels; and on cookware such as enamelled cast-iron pots. Its durability outdoors, with excellent colour retention, has made it a standard material for public signage.
