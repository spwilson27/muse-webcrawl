Cold front

Cold front
SymbolBlue line with triangles pointing in direction of movement
EffectTemperature drop, wind shift, showers or thunderstorms
Associated cloudsCumulonimbus, cumulus, altocumulus

A cold front is the leading edge of a cooler mass of air at ground level that replaces a warmer mass of air and lies within a pronounced surface trough of low pressure. It often forms behind an extratropical cyclone and is usually preceded by a warm front. The colder air is denser and wedges beneath the warmer air, lifting it and frequently generating precipitation and thunderstorms.

Characteristics

As a cold front passes, temperatures can fall sharply — sometimes by more than 15 °C (27 °F) within the first hour. Winds shift, typically from southerly to westerly or northwesterly in the Northern Hemisphere, and gust strongly near the front. Atmospheric pressure falls ahead of the front, reaches a minimum at the passage, and rises steadily behind it. The band of weather along a cold front is usually narrower than that of a warm front.

Clouds and precipitation

The steep slope of a cold front forces warm, moist air to rise rapidly, producing towering cumulus and cumulonimbus clouds, heavy showers and thunderstorms, occasionally with hail or tornadoes. A squall line may develop ahead of the front. Behind the front, skies often clear quickly, with scattered cumulus in the cold, unstable air mass.

Types

An anafront has warm air actively rising over the frontal surface with precipitation behind the surface front, while a katafront has descending motion and weaker, more fragmented precipitation. When a cold front overtakes a warm front, the result is an occluded front.
