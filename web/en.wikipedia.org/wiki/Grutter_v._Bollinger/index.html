Grutter v. Bollinger

Grutter v. Bollinger, 539 U.S. 306 (2003), was a landmark case of the Supreme Court of the United States concerning affirmative action in student admissions. The Court held that the University of Michigan Law School's admissions program, which considered race as one of many factors in an individualised review of each applicant, did not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The decision upheld the principle, first articulated by Justice Lewis Powell in Regents of the University of California v. Bakke (1978), that student body diversity is a compelling state interest.

Background

Barbara Grutter, a white Michigan resident with strong academic credentials, was denied admission to the University of Michigan Law School and sued, arguing that the school's use of race gave minority applicants a significantly greater chance of admission and discriminated against her. The case was decided alongside Gratz v. Bollinger, in which the Court struck down the university's undergraduate admissions system, which automatically awarded points to underrepresented minority applicants.

Majority opinion

Justice Sandra Day O'Connor, writing for a five-justice majority, held that the Law School had a compelling interest in attaining a diverse student body and that its program, which treated race as a flexible "plus" factor within a highly individualised review, was narrowly tailored to that interest. O'Connor famously wrote that the Court expected "that 25 years from now, the use of racial preferences will no longer be necessary to further the interest approved today."

Later developments

In 2006 Michigan voters adopted a state constitutional amendment banning affirmative action in public education, employment, and contracting, which the Supreme Court upheld in Schuette v. Coalition to Defend Affirmative Action (2014). In 2023, in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard and its companion case, the Supreme Court effectively overruled Grutter, holding that the race-conscious admissions programs at Harvard and the University of North Carolina violated the Equal Protection Clause.
