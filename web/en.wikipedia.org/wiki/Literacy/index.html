Literacy

Literacy is the ability to read and write. In modern contexts the word is often broadened to include the ability to use language, numbers, images and other means to understand, communicate and gain useful knowledge in a society. Education systems worldwide treat literacy as a foundational skill and a basic human right.

Definitions

UNESCO defines literacy as the ability to identify, understand, interpret, create, communicate and compute, using printed and written materials associated with varying contexts. The traditional definition—a person's ability to read and write a short simple statement about everyday life—has been supplemented by concepts such as functional literacy, numeracy, digital literacy and media literacy.

History

Writing emerged independently in several ancient civilisations, including Mesopotamia (cuneiform), Egypt (hieroglyphs), China and Mesoamerica. For most of history literacy was confined to small elites of scribes, clergy and administrators. The invention of the printing press in the 15th century, the spread of cheap paper, and compulsory public schooling in the 19th and 20th centuries drove mass literacy. In 1820 only about 12% of the world's population could read and write; by the early 21st century the figure exceeded 85%.

Modern literacy

By region

Adult literacy rates approach 99% in most of Europe, North America and East Asia. Rates remain lowest in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, and in nearly every country women are less likely to be literate than men—a gap attributed to unequal access to schooling.

Effects

Literacy is associated with better health outcomes, higher earnings, greater civic participation and reduced poverty. International Literacy Day is observed on 8 September.
