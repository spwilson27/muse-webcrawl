Thomas Worthington Whittredge (22 May 1820 – 25 February 1910) was an American landscape painter of the second generation of the Hudson River School. Trained in Europe, he became known for tranquil woodland interiors and western scenes distinguished by their subtle treatment of light filtering through trees. He served twice as president of the National Academy of Design in New York and was a central figure in the American art world of the later nineteenth century.

Early life and training

Whittredge was born on a farm in Springfield, Ohio, and grew up near Cincinnati, where he began his career painting portraits and landscapes with little formal instruction. In 1849 he sailed for Europe and settled in Düsseldorf, Germany, studying for several years at the Düsseldorf Academy under Emanuel Leutze and absorbing the precise, naturalistic landscape style of the Düsseldorf school. He travelled widely in Germany, Switzerland and Italy, spending time in Rome with fellow Americans including Albert Bierstadt and Sanford Robinson Gifford, with whom he journeyed back to the United States in 1859.

Career

Settling in New York, Whittredge was elected to the National Academy of Design in 1861 and served as its president from 1874 to 1876. In 1866 he travelled west with General John Pope's expedition across the Great Plains to New Mexico, producing sketches that informed large studio paintings of the Rocky Mountains and the plains; he returned west again, including a trip to Colorado with Gifford in 1870. In his later decades he painted chiefly the woods and streams of New England, Rhode Island and New Jersey, developing the intimate, sun-dappled forest interiors — such as The Trout Pool (1870) — for which he is best remembered.

Legacy

Whittredge's autobiography, edited and published posthumously, is a valuable memoir of nineteenth-century American art life. His works are held by major American museums including the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Thyssen-Bornemisza collection and the Cincinnati Art Museum. Although his reputation dimmed in the early twentieth century along with that of the Hudson River School generally, scholarship since the 1970s has restored him to a leading place among American landscape painters of his generation.
