Tarantula Nebula

The Tarantula Nebula (also known as 30 Doradus and NGC 2070) is a vast H II region in the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC), a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way. It is the most active starburst region known in the Local Group of galaxies and one of the largest and most luminous nebulae known — so bright that, were it as close to Earth as the Orion Nebula, it would cast visible shadows.

Observational history

The nebula was catalogued by Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille during his expedition to the Cape of Good Hope in 1751–52, and later recorded by John Herschel, who noted the loops of nebulosity that suggested the legs of a spider — the origin of its popular name. It lies in the southern constellation Dorado and is visible to the naked eye from dark southern skies as a fuzzy patch within the Large Magellanic Cloud.

Structure and star formation

At its heart lies R136, an extraordinarily dense super star cluster only about 1–2 million years old. R136 contains some of the most massive and luminous stars known, including R136a1, a Wolf–Rayet star with a mass estimated at around 200 times that of the Sun and a luminosity millions of times greater. The intense ultraviolet radiation and stellar winds from these stars ionise and sculpt the surrounding gas, driving continuing star formation throughout the nebula.

Fantastic arcs, pillars, and bubbles of gas and dust extend hundreds of light-years from the core. The nebula's total luminosity is so great that if it were placed at the distance of the Orion Nebula (about 1,300 light-years), it would occupy a large patch of the sky and cast shadows at night.

Supernova 1987A

On the outskirts of the Tarantula Nebula lies the remnant of Supernova 1987A, the closest observed supernova since the invention of the telescope, whose light reached Earth in February 1987. It remains one of the most studied objects in astronomy and confirmed key predictions of supernova theory, including the detection of neutrinos from the collapsing core.

Significance

Because of its proximity (in extragalactic terms) and its extreme star formation, the Tarantula Nebula serves as a local laboratory for the starburst phenomena seen in distant young galaxies, allowing astronomers to study how the most massive stars form and influence their environment.
