Maillard reaction

The Maillard reaction (pronounced /maɪˈjɑːr/) is a chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars that gives browned food its distinctive flavour. It is a form of non-enzymatic browning which typically proceeds rapidly from around 140 to 165 °C (284 to 329 °F). The reaction is named after French chemist Louis Camille Maillard, who first described it in 1912 while attempting to reproduce biological protein synthesis.

Chemistry

The reaction begins with the condensation of a carbonyl group of a sugar with an amino group of an amino acid, forming a Schiff base that rearranges to Amadori (or Heyns) products. These intermediates then undergo a complex cascade of reactions — dehydration, fragmentation, and polymerization — producing hundreds of flavour and aroma compounds, including furans, pyrazines, and thiophenes, and finally the brown nitrogenous polymers called melanoidins, which give seared meat, bread crusts, roasted coffee, and grilled foods their colour.

In cooking

Maillard browning (often conflated with caramelization, a distinct reaction of sugars alone) is responsible for the flavours of searing, roasting, baking, and frying. It is influenced by temperature, time, pH (alkaline conditions accelerate it), and water activity. Cooks exploit it by drying food surfaces, using high heat, or adding reducing sugars and amino-rich ingredients. The crust of bread, the sear on a steak, the roast of coffee beans, and the colour of beer malts all depend on it.

Health aspects

The reaction also occurs slowly in the human body, where sugar–protein products ("advanced glycation end-products") accumulate in tissues with age and are implicated in complications of diabetes. In cooked foods, one Maillard by-product, acrylamide, formed from the amino acid asparagine at high temperatures in starchy foods, has attracted regulatory attention, though the overall dietary significance remains studied.
