Human height

Human height
Global average (adults)About 171 cm (men), 159 cm (women), varying widely by country

Human height is the distance from the bottom of the feet to the top of the head in a fully grown human standing erect. It varies substantially between individuals, sexes and populations, and is used—as an easily measured summary of net nutrition and health in childhood—as an indicator of living standards in anthropometric history and public health.

Determinants

Within a population, height is highly heritable: twin and genome-wide studies attribute roughly 60–80 percent of height variation to genetic differences where nutrition is adequate, with thousands of common variants each contributing tiny effects. Between populations and across time, environmental factors dominate: protein and total energy intake, childhood disease burden (which diverts resources from growth), sanitation, maternal health and access to healthcare. Growth occurs mainly through the lengthening of long bones at the epiphyseal plates, driven by growth hormone and, at puberty, sex steroids, which ultimately fuse the plates. Men are on average about 7–8 percent taller than women within the same population.

Variation across populations and history

The tallest national averages are found in the Netherlands and Montenegro (men around 183 cm); the shortest in parts of Southeast Asia and Latin America (for example Timor-Leste, where men average around 160 cm, and Guatemalan women around 149 cm). Average heights fell in many places during the early Industrial Revolution despite rising incomes—part of the "antebellum puzzle" literature—before climbing through the 20th century as nutrition and public health improved; gains of 10 cm or more per century occurred in countries such as South Korea and Iran. In several rich countries, including the United States, average height has plateaued since the late 20th century.

Measurement and extremes

Height is measured with a stadiometer, ideally in the morning, as spinal compression reduces stature by 1–2 cm across the day. The tallest reliably measured man was Robert Wadlow (272 cm), whose growth resulted from pituitary gigantism; extreme short stature (dwarfism, below about 147 cm in adults) most often results from achondroplasia or growth-hormone deficiency. Height is associated in studies with earnings, health outcomes and disease risks—taller stature correlates with lower cardiovascular risk but higher risk of some cancers—though such associations are statistical rather than deterministic.
