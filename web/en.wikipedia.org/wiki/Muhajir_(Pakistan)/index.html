Muhajir (Urdu: مہاجر, "migrant") is the term used in Pakistan for the Muslim migrants who moved — or whose ancestors moved — from India to Pakistan during and after the Partition of British India in 1947, and for their descendants. The word echoes the Muhajirun, the early followers of Muhammad who migrated from Mecca to Medina. Most Muhajirs settled in the urban centres of Sindh, above all Karachi and Hyderabad, and are defined less by a single region of origin than by a shared migration experience and, usually, by Urdu as a mother tongue; they came from the United Provinces, Delhi, Hyderabad Deccan, Gujarat, Rajasthan, Bombay and elsewhere.

History

At Partition, some seven million Muslims migrated to Pakistan; the urban, educated refugees who arrived in Sindh filled the vacuum left by departing Hindu and Sikh professionals and dominated Pakistan's early civil service, professions and commerce. Karachi, the country's first capital, became overwhelmingly Muhajir. From the 1970s, however, ethnic tensions in Sindh sharpened — over language (the 1972 Sindhi language riots), quotas in government employment, and competition with Sindhis, Punjabis, Pashtuns and later Afghan refugees.

In 1984 students and activists founded the Muhajir Qaumi Movement (MQM), which mobilised Karachi's Muhajirs and became a major political force, amid years of severe ethnic and political violence in the city in the 1980s and 1990s. The party later renamed itself the Muttahida Qaumi Movement ("United National Movement"), presenting a broader identity, though "Muhajir" remains the common ethnic designation for the community.

Culture

Muhajir culture is strongly urban and literary, centred on Urdu poetry, cuisine adapted from Delhi, Lucknow and Hyderabad, and a civic identity built around Karachi. Although largely integrated into Pakistan after three generations, Muhajirs continue to be counted separately in the census-era ethnic geography of Sindh, where they form a plurality in Karachi.
