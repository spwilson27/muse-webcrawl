Bystander effect

The bystander effect, or bystander apathy, is a social psychological phenomenon in which individuals are less likely to offer help to a victim when other people are present. The greater the number of bystanders, the less likely it is that any one of them will help. The effect is usually explained by a perceived diffusion of responsibility — each person assumes someone else will act — and by social influence, as bystanders monitor one another's inaction to judge whether intervention is needed (pluralistic ignorance).

Research

The effect was named and investigated by social psychologists Bibb Latané and John Darley following the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York City, which press reports (later found to be substantially exaggerated) said was witnessed by 38 people who did nothing. In Latané and Darley's classic experiments, participants who believed they were the only witness to an emergency — such as smoke filling a room, or a person apparently having a seizure over an intercom — helped quickly, while those who believed others were present were much slower or did not help at all. Darley and Latané's model proposes five steps to intervention: noticing the event, interpreting it as an emergency, assuming personal responsibility, knowing how to help, and deciding to act; the bystander effect can interrupt any step.

Moderating factors and later findings

The effect is weaker when the emergency is unambiguous and dangerous, when bystanders know one another or the victim, when a bystander has relevant competence, or when someone is singled out for responsibility. A large 2019 study of real CCTV footage of public conflicts across three countries found that bystanders intervened in about 90 per cent of cases and that larger groups increased victim-level odds of receiving help — suggesting the classic effect, while robust in laboratory settings, is moderated strongly in naturalistic settings.

Applications

Understanding the bystander effect informs first-aid training (instructing helpers to assign tasks to specific individuals, e.g. "You — call an ambulance"), school and workplace anti-bullying and sexual-assault prevention programmes, and research on online behaviour, where similar diffusion of responsibility appears in forums and comment threads.
