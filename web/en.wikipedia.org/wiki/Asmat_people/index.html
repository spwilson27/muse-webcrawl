The Asmat are an ethnic group of New Guinea, inhabiting the swampy lowlands of the Casuarina Coast in South Papua, Indonesia, along the Arafura Sea. Traditionally semi-nomadic hunter-fisher-gatherers centred on sago, the Asmat are internationally renowned for their sophisticated woodcarving tradition, considered among the finest in the Pacific.

Culture

Asmat society was organised around men's houses (jeu) and elaborate ritual cycles. The most famous are the carved bis (mbis) poles — tall stacks of human and animal figures erected for feasts honouring the dead, traditionally connected with headhunting raids that were suppressed only in the mid-20th century. Shields, drums, canoes and ancestor figures are also richly carved with motifs drawn from nature and the spirit world.

History

Sustained outside contact came late; the region drew international attention in 1961 with the disappearance of Michael Rockefeller, son of New York governor Nelson Rockefeller, who was collecting Asmat art and vanished near the coast. Catholic missionaries and the Indonesian government subsequently encouraged settlement in permanent villages. Asmat art is held by museums worldwide, including the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the town of Agats hosts an annual Asmat cultural festival.
