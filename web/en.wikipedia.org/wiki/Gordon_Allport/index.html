Gordon Allport

Gordon Willard Allport (November 11, 1897 – October 9, 1967) was an American psychologist. He was one of the first psychologists to focus on the study of personality, and is often referred to as one of the founding figures of personality psychology. His work emphasized the uniqueness of the individual and the importance of traits, and his book The Nature of Prejudice (1954) became a landmark in the study of intergroup relations.

Life and career

Allport was born in Montezuma, Indiana, the youngest of four sons. He studied at Harvard, earning his doctorate in 1922 under Herbert Langfeld. A formative anecdote of his career was his visit to Sigmund Freud in Vienna in 1920: when Allport recounted a tidy observation about a young boy's fear of dirt, Freud replied, "And was that little boy you?" — an exchange Allport later cited as teaching him that psychology could probe too deeply and overlook surface, conscious motivation.

After teaching at Dartmouth College, Allport joined the Harvard faculty in 1930, where he remained for the rest of his career. He helped found the Society for the Psychological Study of Social Issues, served as president of the American Psychological Association in 1939, and edited the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology.

Trait theory

In Personality: A Psychological Interpretation (1937), Allport proposed that personality is organized by traits, which he classified as cardinal traits (dominating a life), central traits (the five to ten descriptors that capture a person), and secondary traits (narrow preferences). With Henry Odbert he compiled some 4,500 trait words from the dictionary — the lexical study that later fed into the Big Five tradition. He also introduced the concepts of the proprium (the self) and functional autonomy of motives.

Prejudice and the contact hypothesis

The Nature of Prejudice (1954) systematized research on stereotyping, scapegoating, and discrimination. In it Allport formulated the contact hypothesis: prejudice between groups can be reduced by intergroup contact under conditions of equal status, common goals, intergroup cooperation, and support of authorities. The book influenced American civil-rights-era social science and is still widely cited.
