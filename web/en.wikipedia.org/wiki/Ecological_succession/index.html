Ecological succession

Ecological succession is the process by which the mix of species and habitat in an area changes over time. Gradually, this process results in a community of species that is in equilibrium with the environment, known historically as a climax community. Succession may be initiated either by formation of new, unoccupied habitat — primary succession, as on a lava flow or retreating glacier — or by a disturbance that alters an existing community without destroying it entirely — secondary succession, as after fire, flood, or logging.

Types and stages

The earliest colonisers of a disturbed area are called pioneer species; they are typically hardy, fast-growing, and tolerant of harsh conditions, and they modify the environment in ways that allow later species to establish. A sequence of intermediate communities, called seres, then follows, often progressing from grasses to shrubs to fast-growing trees and finally to a mature forest in many temperate regions.

Theory and history

The concept was developed by botanists including Henry Chandler Cowles, who studied succession on the Indiana Dunes, and Frederic Clements, who proposed that communities develop like organisms toward a single climax determined by climate. Henry Gleason countered with the individualistic concept, holding that each species responds to the environment independently. Modern ecology treats succession as a more open-ended process influenced by disturbance regimes, invasive species, and climate change, without necessarily converging on a single stable endpoint.
