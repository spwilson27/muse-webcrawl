Extrasensory perception

Extrasensory perception or ESP, also called a sixth sense, is a claimed paranormal ability pertaining to reception of information not gained through the recognized physical senses, but sensed with the mind. The term was adopted by Duke University botanist J. B. Rhine to denote psychic abilities such as intuition, telepathy, psychometry, clairvoyance, clairaudience, clairsentience, empathy and their trans-temporal operation as precognition or retrocognition.

Second sight is an alleged form of extrasensory perception whereby a person perceives information, in the form of a vision, about future events before they happen, or about things or events at remote locations.

History

In the 1930s, at Duke University in North Carolina, J. B. Rhine and his wife Louisa E. Rhine conducted investigation into extrasensory perception. While Louisa Rhine concentrated on collecting accounts of spontaneous cases, J. B. Rhine worked largely in the laboratory, carefully defining terms such as ESP and psi and designing experiments to test them. A simple set of cards, designed by psychologist Karl Zener, was used. The cards had five different symbols on them, and participants were asked to guess which symbol was on a card chosen at random, with the probability of a correct guess being one in five.

Scientific reception

The scientific consensus is that there is no credible scientific evidence for the existence of ESP. Parapsychology, the study of purported ESP and other psi phenomena, has been criticized for continuing investigation despite a lack of convincing experimental results after more than a century of research. Critics point to problems with experimental design, publication bias, the file-drawer effect, and failures of replication, notably in the Ganzfeld experiments and in Daryl Bem's 2011 precognition studies, which prompted major reforms in psychological research methods.
