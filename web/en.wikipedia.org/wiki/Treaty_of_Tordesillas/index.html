The Treaty of Tordesillas, signed at Tordesillas, Spain, on 7 June 1494, divided the newly discovered lands outside Europe between the Kingdom of Portugal and the Crown of Castile along a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands, off the west coast of Africa. Lands east of the line belonged to Portugal; lands west of it belonged to Spain.

Background

After Christopher Columbus's first voyage in 1492, Spain obtained papal bulls (Inter caetera, 1493) from the Spanish-born Pope Alexander VI drawing a line 100 leagues west of the Azores and granting Spain the lands beyond it. John II of Portugal, dissatisfied, negotiated directly with Ferdinand and Isabella, producing the bilateral treaty that moved the line much further west—possibly, historians speculate, because Portugal already suspected land (Brazil) lay within its side.

Effects

The line ran through the eastern bulge of South America, which is why Brazil—claimed by Pedro Álvares Cabral in 1500—became Portuguese while most of the Americas became Spanish. The treaty's logic was extended around the globe by the Treaty of Zaragoza (1529), which set an anti-meridian in the Pacific, assigning the Moluccas to Portugal and the Philippines to Spain. Other European powers, once they became seafaring rivals, never accepted the division; the French king Francis I is said to have asked to see the clause in Adam's will that excluded him from a share of the world.

Legacy

The treaty shaped the linguistic and colonial map of South America—Portuguese-speaking Brazil surrounded by Spanish-speaking states—and is frequently cited as an early landmark of international law and of European partition of the non-European world without regard to its inhabitants. The original document is preserved in the Archivo General de Indias in Seville, with the Portuguese copy in Lisbon; both were inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register.
