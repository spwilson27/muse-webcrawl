British School at Rome

The British School at Rome (BSR; Italian: Scuola Britannica a Roma) is an interdisciplinary research centre supporting British and Commonwealth scholars in the fields of archaeology, art, architecture and the humanities in Italy. Founded in 1901, it is one of the British International Research Institutes supported by the British Academy, and is based in the Valle Giulia in Rome.

History

The school was founded in 1901 as a school of archaeology, following the model of the foreign schools already established in Rome, such as the American Academy and the French Academy. In 1911 it moved to its present home, a building designed by the British architect Sir Edwin Lutyens, which had originally served as the British Pavilion at the Rome International Exhibition of that year. The building was adapted and extended for the school's use.

During the early twentieth century the school became a centre for the study of Roman topography and Italian archaeology under directors including Thomas Ashby, whose work on the aqueducts and roads of Roman Italy remains influential. After the Second World War the school broadened its remit to include fine art and architecture residencies, and it now supports artists as well as scholars through competitive awards.

Activities

The BSR awards residential fellowships and scholarships, including the Rome Prize in architecture and visual arts awards, allowing researchers and practitioners to live and work in Rome for periods of three to twelve months. It maintains a major research library of more than 110,000 volumes specialising in Italian art, archaeology and history, and organises lectures, conferences, exhibitions and concerts open to the public.

Its archaeological fieldwork has included long-running excavation and survey projects in Italy and North Africa, most notably at South Etruria, where the Tiber Valley Project investigated the landscape north of Rome.
