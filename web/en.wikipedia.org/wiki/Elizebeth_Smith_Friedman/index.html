Elizebeth Smith Friedman (August 26, 1892 – October 31, 1980) was an American cryptanalyst and author who deciphered enemy codes in both World Wars and helped convict rum-runners during Prohibition. She has been called America's first female cryptanalyst, and the law-enforcement cryptology unit she built for the U.S. Coast Guard became foundational to later American signals intelligence.

Career

Hired in 1916 at Riverbank Laboratories in Illinois — initially to work on the theory that Francis Bacon authored Shakespeare's plays — she and her husband William F. Friedman became pioneering codebreakers. During the First World War she trained Army officers in cryptanalysis. In the 1920s and 1930s, as head of a small Coast Guard codebreaking unit, her decrypts produced evidence that convicted major rum-running syndicates, and she famously testified in court explaining her solutions.

During the Second World War her Coast Guard unit, by then absorbed into naval intelligence, broke German Enigma traffic from clandestine Abwehr radio networks in South America, work that remained classified and largely uncredited for decades.

Legacy

Friedman's contributions were overshadowed in her lifetime by classification and by credit given to others, notably J. Edgar Hoover's FBI. Later scholarship, NSA recognition (a headquarters auditorium named jointly for her and her husband), and a U.S. Coast Guard cutter named in her honour have restored her reputation as a founder of American cryptology.
