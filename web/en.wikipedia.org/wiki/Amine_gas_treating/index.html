Amine gas treating, also known as amine scrubbing, gas sweetening and acid gas removal, refers to a group of processes that use aqueous solutions of various alkylamines (commonly referred to simply as amines) to remove hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and carbon dioxide (CO2) from gases. It is a common unit process used in refineries, petrochemical plants, natural gas processing plants and other industries.

Process description

Gases containing H2S or CO2 — called sour gases or acid gases — enter the bottom of an absorber tower and flow upward, contacting a descending stream of "lean" amine solution. The amine reacts chemically with the acid gases, producing a "rich" amine stream that leaves the bottom of the absorber; the treated ("sweet") gas exits the top. The rich amine is heated in a regenerator (stripper), where the acid gases are driven off and the lean amine is recycled. The removed H2S is typically converted to elemental sulfur in a Claus process.

Typical amines

  • Monoethanolamine (MEA) — highly reactive, used for low-pressure gas treating and post-combustion CO₂ capture.
  • Diethanolamine (DEA) — common in refinery and gas plant service.
  • Methyldiethanolamine (MDEA) — selective for H₂S over CO₂, lower regeneration energy.
  • Diisopropanolamine (DIPA) and diglycolamine (DGA) — used in specific licensed processes.

Applications

Beyond oil and gas, amine scrubbing is used to upgrade biogas to biomethane and is the leading technology for post-combustion carbon capture and storage. Operational challenges include corrosion, solvent degradation, foaming and the energy demand of regeneration.
