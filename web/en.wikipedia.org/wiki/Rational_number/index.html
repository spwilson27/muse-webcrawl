Rational number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A number line densely marked with fractions between integers]

In mathematics, a rational number is a number that can be expressed as the quotient or fraction p/q of two integers, with the denominator q not equal to zero. The set of rationals is denoted Q (for quotient). Every rational has a decimal expansion that terminates or repeats.

Rational numbers are dense on the number line — between any two lies another — yet they are countable: Cantor's enumeration argument shows there are no more rationals than natural numbers, while the reals are strictly more numerous.

History and structure

Fractions were computed in ancient Egypt (unit fractions in the Rhind papyrus) and Babylon; the discovery that √2 is not rational — traditionally credited to the Pythagoreans and the legend of Hippasus — is a founding crisis of Greek mathematics, spurring Eudoxus's theory of proportion.

Formally, rationals form the smallest field containing the integers: closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division (except by zero). Integers are rationals with denominator 1, so Z ⊂ Q ⊂ R.
