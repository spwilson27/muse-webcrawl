TCP/IP

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A layered diagram of the Internet protocol suite: application, transport, internet, link]

The Internet protocol suite, commonly known as TCP/IP, is the set of communication protocols used in the Internet and similar computer networks. It provides end-to-end data communication specifying how data should be packetised, addressed, transmitted, routed, and received.

Developed in the 1970s for the US Department of Defense's ARPANET programme, chiefly by Vinton Cerf and Bob Kahn (their foundational paper appeared in 1974), TCP/IP was adopted by ARPANET on 1 January 1983 ('flag day') and became the standard networking model, displacing rivals such as the OSI model in practice.

Layers

The suite is organised in four layers: the link layer (Ethernet, Wi-Fi) moves frames locally; the internet layer (IP) routes packets between networks — IPv4's 32-bit addresses are being supplemented by IPv6's 128-bit; the transport layer provides TCP (reliable, ordered streams) and UDP (fast, unreliable datagrams); the application layer carries HTTP, DNS, SMTP, and thousands of other protocols.

Its design principles — packet switching, the end-to-end argument (intelligence at the edges, a simple network core), and layered modularity — enabled the internet's growth from a research network to global infrastructure.
