Cohabitation (government)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cohabitation is a phenomenon in semi-presidential systems, above all France's Fifth Republic, in which the president and the prime minister belong to opposing political camps, because the president's party lacks a majority in the national assembly. The term (la cohabitation) was coined for the French case: it occurred in 1986–88 (President François Mitterrand, Prime Minister Jacques Chirac), 1993–95 (Mitterrand, Édouard Balladur) and 1997–2002 (Chirac, Lionel Jospin).

Cohabitation forces a redistribution of power: the prime minister controls domestic policy through the assembly majority, while the president retains influence in foreign affairs and defence (the informal “domaine réservé”) and the powers of the presidency itself. The 2000–02 reforms — the five-year presidential term (quinquennat) and aligning legislative elections immediately after presidential ones — were designed to make cohabitation unlikely, and it has not recurred since. The concept generalises to other semi-presidential systems where dual executives can fall into rival hands.
