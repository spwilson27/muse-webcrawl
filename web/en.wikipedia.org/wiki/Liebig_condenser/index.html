Liebig condenser

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Liebig condenser, also simply called a straight condenser, is a piece of laboratory glassware used to cool and condense hot vapour into liquid, most commonly as part of a distillation apparatus. It is named after the German chemist Justus von Liebig, who popularised the design in the nineteenth century, although similar water-cooled condensers had been described earlier by Christian Weigel, Pierre-Isaac Poissonnier, and Justus Liebig's contemporary Johan Gadolin.

Design

The condenser consists of two concentric glass tubes. Vapour passes through the straight inner tube, while a coolant—almost always tap water—flows through the outer jacket in the opposite direction, entering at the lowest point and leaving at the highest so that the jacket stays full. The two hose connections are short sidearms fused to the jacket. In a distillation, the condenser is mounted at a downward angle between the still head and the receiving adapter; for reflux it is mounted vertically above the reaction flask.

Use and efficiency

The Liebig is the simplest and least expensive condenser design, and it is effective for liquids with boiling points well above ambient temperature. Because its cooling surface and coolant capacity are limited, it is poorly suited to low-boiling solvents such as diethyl ether, for which condensers with larger surfaces—such as the Allihn (bulb) condenser or the coiled Graham condenser—or circulated ice water are preferred. Modern versions are made of borosilicate glass and fitted with ground-glass joints for quick assembly.
