Dutch spelling

Dutch spelling is the system of orthography used for the Dutch language in the Netherlands, Flanders (Belgium) and Suriname. Dutch spelling is comparatively regular and largely phonemic, though it preserves morphological consistency: words are spelled according to their underlying form even when final devoicing changes their pronunciation.

Key features

Vowel length is marked systematically: long vowels are doubled in closed syllables (as in maan, "moon"), while in open syllables a single vowel letter is already long (as in ma-nen). A central rule governs consonant doubling and vowel doubling when suffixes are added, keeping syllable structure correct — for example kat → katten and boot → boten. Verbs are famous for the 't kofschip rule, which determines whether past-tense and participle endings take -t or -d.

Standardisation and reforms

Modern regulation dates from the 19th century (the De Vries–Te Winkel spelling of 1863). The Netherlands and Flanders adopted a common official spelling in 1946–47, and the Dutch Language Union (Taalunie), founded in 1980, now maintains the official word list, the Groene Boekje ("Green Booklet"). Reforms in 1995 and 2005 adjusted rules for loanwords, capitalisation and the notorious "pannenkoek" rule on the linking -n- in compounds, which remained controversial.

Alphabet

The Dutch alphabet uses the 26 Latin letters; the digraph IJ is often treated as a single letter and capitalised as a unit. Loanwords bring in letters such as c, q, x and y, while native words make heavy use of digraphs like sch, ng and eu.
