The Book of Job (Hebrew: אִיּוֹב, ʾIyyōv) is a book of the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament, among the most celebrated works of ancient wisdom literature. It addresses the problem of theodicy — why the righteous suffer — through the story of Job, a blameless and wealthy man who loses his children, property and health, and refuses either to curse God or to accept his friends' doctrine that suffering must be punishment for sin.

Structure

The book consists of a prose prologue and epilogue framing a long poetic dialogue. In the prologue, Satan ("the Adversary") challenges Job's piety in the heavenly court, and God permits him to strip Job of everything. Three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar — argue in cycles of speeches that Job must have sinned; Job answers that he is innocent and demands a hearing from God. A young man, Elihu, then speaks in God's defence. Finally God answers Job out of the whirlwind, describing the wonders of creation — Behemoth and Leviathan among them — without directly explaining Job's suffering. Job repents "in dust and ashes", and in the epilogue his fortunes are restored twofold.

Composition

Scholars generally regard the prose frame as an older folktale into which the poetic dialogues were set, dating the completed book somewhere between the 7th and 4th centuries BC. The Elihu speeches are often considered a later addition. The book's Hebrew is notably difficult and rich in rare vocabulary and Aramaic colouring.

Interpretation and influence

Job has been read as a protest against retributive theology, a meditation on the limits of human knowledge, and a drama of faith amid unexplained evil. It has inspired commentary from the rabbis and Gregory the Great to modern works such as Jung's Answer to Job, and the phrase "the patience of Job" entered common usage — though the Job of the dialogues is anything but patient.
