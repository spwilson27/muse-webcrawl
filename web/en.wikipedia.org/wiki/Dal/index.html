Dal

In Indian cuisine, dal (also spelled daal or dhal; from Sanskrit: दाल, meaning "split") are dried, split pulses — lentils, peas, and beans — and, by extension, the dishes prepared from them, most commonly a thick stew or soup. The term is used throughout India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka. Dal is a staple food across the subcontinent and, combined with cereals such as rice or roti, provides a complete source of protein in largely vegetarian diets.

Varieties

Common dals include toor dal (split pigeon peas), masoor dal (red lentils), moong dal (split mung beans, with or without skin), urad dal (black gram), and chana dal (split chickpeas). After cooking, usually by boiling until soft, the dal is finished with a tempering (tadka or chaunk) of spices — cumin and mustard seeds, garlic, dried red chillies, asafoetida, curry leaves — fried in ghee or oil and poured over the top. Well-known preparations include dal tadka and dal fry of North India, the sweet-and-sour Gujarati dal, the sambar of South India, and dal bhat, the lentil soup served with rice that is the national everyday meal of Nepal.

Preparation and nutrition

Unlike whole pulses, split dals generally need no soaking and cook relatively quickly, often in a pressure cooker. They are rich in protein, dietary fibre, folate, and iron, and low in fat. Whole, unhulled pulses are usually distinguished by other names (for example, whole black gram is sabut urad, used for dal makhani with butter and cream).
