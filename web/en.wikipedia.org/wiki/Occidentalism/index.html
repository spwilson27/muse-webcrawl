Occidentalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Occidentalism is a term used to describe prejudiced or stereotyped views of the West, often expressed by non-Westerners, in which Western societies are portrayed as materialistic, morally decadent, individualistic, spiritually empty, or hostile to religion and tradition. It is frequently discussed as a counterpart or inversion of Orientalism, Edward Said's term for Western stereotyping of the East, although scholars debate how exact the parallel is.

Origins and usage

The concept gained prominence through the work of scholars such as Chen Xiaomei, whose book Occidentalism: A Theory of Counter-Discourse in Post-Mao China (1995) examined how Chinese intellectuals constructed images of the West for domestic political purposes. The term was further popularised by Ian Buruma and Avishai Margalit in Occidentalism: The West in the Eyes of Its Enemies (2004), which traced hostile ideas about the West through several intellectual traditions, including German Romanticism, Slavophilism, Japanese nationalism, and Islamism.

Themes

Buruma and Margalit identified recurring motifs in Occidentalist thought: the image of the Western city as a soulless, rootless metropolis; the portrayal of the West as worshipping money and machines; the depiction of Westerners as arrogant, cowardly, or decadent; and the idealisation of heroic sacrifice and spiritual purity against Western materialism. They argued that Occidentalism is not simply the mirror image of Orientalism, because it can involve a dehumanising contempt rather than merely exoticisation.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether the concept draws false equivalences between the power relations involved in Orientalism and those involved in anti-Western sentiment, and whether it lumps together quite different movements — religious fundamentalism, nationalism, and anti-globalisation critique — under a single label. Others note that criticism of Western policies or culture is not in itself Occidentalist, and that the term should be reserved for essentialising and hostile stereotyping.
