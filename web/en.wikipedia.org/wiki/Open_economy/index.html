Open economy

Field: International economics, macroeconomics

Opposite: Closed economy

Key measures: Trade openness ratio ((exports + imports) / GDP)

An open economy is an economy in which there are economic activities between domestic and foreign communities. People and businesses in an open economy can trade in goods and services with people and businesses in other countries and generally can borrow or lend on world financial markets. This contrasts with a closed economy, in which international trade and finance cannot take place.

Characteristics

In an open economy, consumers and firms may choose between domestic and foreign goods, domestic saving can be invested abroad, and capital can flow across borders. The expenditures identity for an open economy is usually written as Y = C + I + G + NX, where NX is net exports (exports minus imports). A country's balance of payments records these transactions, with the current account and capital account summing (with errors) to zero.

Degree of openness

No economy is completely open or completely closed; openness is a matter of degree. A common measure is the trade-to-GDP ratio. Small economies such as Singapore, Ireland, and the Netherlands have very high ratios, while large economies such as the United States and Japan have comparatively low ones. Governments influence openness through tariffs, quotas, capital controls, and exchange-rate arrangements.

Models

The Mundell–Fleming model extends the IS–LM framework to an open economy with capital mobility, showing how the effectiveness of monetary and fiscal policy depends on the exchange-rate regime — the so-called "impossible trinity" of a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and independent monetary policy. Open-economy macroeconomics also studies exchange-rate determination, purchasing power parity, and the effects of trade deficits and surpluses.

Advantages and disadvantages

Openness is generally associated with the gains from trade — specialization according to comparative advantage, access to larger markets, technology transfer, and cheaper imports. Critics note that openness can expose an economy to external shocks, contagious financial crises, and pressure on domestic industries and employment, motivating debates about globalization and protectionism.
