In Greek mythology, Astyanax (Greek: Ἀστυάναξ, "lord of the city") was the infant son of the Trojan prince Hector and his wife Andromache. His birth name was Scamandrius, after the river Scamander, but the Trojans called him Astyanax because his father was the city's chief defender.

In the Iliad

Astyanax appears in one of the Iliad's most famous scenes (Book 6), when Hector returns from battle to bid his family farewell. The baby is frightened by the bronze helmet and horsehair crest of his father, who laughs, removes the helmet, and prays that his son may grow up a greater warrior than himself — a prayer the audience knows is doomed. Andromache already foresees that the child may be hurled from Troy's walls.

Death and later tradition

After the fall of Troy, the victorious Greeks, fearing the boy would grow up to avenge his father, killed Astyanax — in the dominant tradition he was thrown from the city walls, an act attributed to Neoptolemus (or, in some versions, Odysseus urged it). His death is lamented in Euripides' tragedy The Trojan Women. A minority medieval tradition, followed in some French romances, had Astyanax survive and become an ancestor of later dynasties.
