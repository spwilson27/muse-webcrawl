Flynn effect

The Flynn effect is the substantial and long-sustained increase in both fluid and crystallised intelligence test scores measured in many parts of the world over the twentieth century. It is named after New Zealand researcher James Flynn, who documented the phenomenon in the 1980s.

Magnitude

Typical gains were around three IQ points per decade on standardised tests, with the largest increases on fluid-reasoning tests such as Raven's Progressive Matrices. The effect forced periodic re-norming of IQ tests so that the average remains 100.

Explanations

Proposed causes include improved nutrition, better and longer schooling, greater familiarity with testing, more cognitively demanding environments, and reduced family size. The gains appear too rapid to be genetic.

Reversal

Since the 1990s, some developed countries, including Norway, Denmark, Finland and France, have reported stagnation or decline in scores — sometimes called a "negative Flynn effect".
