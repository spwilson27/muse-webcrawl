A sport utility vehicle (SUV) is a car classification that combines features of road-going passenger cars with those of off-road vehicles, such as raised ground clearance and four-wheel drive. There is no universally agreed definition; broadly, an SUV has a tall, upright body, a high seating position, and an interior that combines passenger and cargo space in a single volume, usually with a hatchback rear door.

Construction types

Traditional SUVs are built on a light-truck chassis using body-on-frame construction, with rugged suspension and low-range gearing for off-road use — examples include the Jeep Wrangler, Toyota Land Cruiser, and Land Rover Defender. Most SUVs sold today, however, use unibody (monocoque) construction derived from passenger cars; these lighter, road-oriented models are commonly marketed as crossover SUVs or simply crossovers. The line between the categories is blurred, and in common usage "SUV" covers both.

History

Early antecedents include the military Willys MB "Jeep" of the Second World War and post-war civilian utility vehicles such as the Land Rover (1948) and the Jeep Wagoneer (1963), among the first to pair off-road ability with car-like comfort. The Range Rover (1970) added luxury. The segment expanded rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s with models such as the Jeep Cherokee (XJ) and Ford Explorer, aided in the United States by regulatory treatment of "light trucks" under fuel-economy and safety rules. By the 2010s and 2020s SUVs and crossovers had become the best-selling vehicle category in many markets, accounting for close to half of global car sales, with many manufacturers dropping traditional sedans and hatchbacks.

Popularity

Buyers cite the high driving position, perceived safety, spacious and flexible interiors, ease of entry, and all-weather capability. SUVs typically command higher prices and profit margins than equivalent cars, which has encouraged manufacturers to expand their SUV line-ups.

Criticism and safety

SUVs are criticized for greater fuel consumption and CO2 emissions than comparable cars, and for their impact on road safety: their greater mass increases harm to occupants of other vehicles in collisions, and their tall, blunt front ends are associated with a substantially higher risk of death or serious injury for pedestrians, especially children, whom drivers may also fail to see in large front blind spots. Historically, tall body-on-frame SUVs also had a higher rollover risk, though electronic stability control has greatly reduced it. Their size also increases demand for road and parking space. These concerns have led some cities to propose higher parking charges or restrictions for large SUVs.
