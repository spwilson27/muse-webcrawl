Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry

A whole-body DEXA scanner in a clinical setting

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA, or DEXA) is a method of measuring bone mineral density (BMD) using spectral imaging. Two X-ray beams with different photon energies are directed at the patient's bone; by comparing the absorption of each beam after it passes through the body, the density of bone can be separated from that of soft tissue and quantified.

Uses

DXA is the most widely used and most thoroughly studied bone density measurement technique. It is primarily used to diagnose osteoporosis and to assess an individual's risk of fragility fracture. Measurements are usually taken at the lumbar spine and the hip (femoral neck), and results are reported as T-scores and Z-scores: the T-score compares the patient's bone density with that of a healthy young adult reference population. A T-score of −2.5 or lower at the femoral neck defines osteoporosis under World Health Organization criteria.

DXA can also estimate body composition — total fat mass, lean mass and bone mineral content — and is used in research on obesity, sarcopenia and sports science.

Radiation exposure and technique

The effective radiation dose from a DXA scan is very low, comparable to a day or less of natural background radiation, and far lower than a standard chest X-ray. The patient lies supine while a scanning arm passes over the body. Earlier dual-photon absorptiometry (DPA) instruments used a radioisotope source (gadolinium-153); modern DXA systems use X-ray tubes with filtration or rapid kVp switching to produce the two energy spectra.

Limitations

DXA measures areal (two-dimensional) rather than true volumetric density, so results are influenced by bone size. Degenerative changes, aortic calcification and prior fractures can falsely elevate spine readings. Measurements are also affected by the patient's soft-tissue composition, and scores from scanners made by different manufacturers are not directly interchangeable without cross-calibration.
