Persian Constitution of 1906

The Persian Constitution of 1906 (Persian: قانون اساسی مشروطه) was the first constitution of Iran (then called Persia) and a product of the Persian Constitutional Revolution. It was written quickly and partly modelled on the Belgian constitution, and was followed in 1907 by a Supplementary Fundamental Law that supplied the bill-of-rights provisions the first document lacked.

History

After mass protests in 1905–1906 against foreign concessions, misgovernment, and the bastinado of Tehran sugar merchants, Muzaffar al-Din Shah Qajar agreed to establish a National Consultative Assembly (the Majlis) and signed the electoral law and the Fundamental Law in December 1906, days before his death. His successor, Mohammad Ali Shah, grudgingly signed the Supplementary Fundamental Law in October 1907. The constitution survived the ensuing civil war — in which the Majlis was bombarded in 1908 — and remained Iran's constitution, with amendments, until the Islamic Revolution of 1979.

Provisions

The Fundamental Law of December 1906 (51 articles) chiefly defined the powers of the Majlis and the Senate and the procedures of legislation. The Supplementary Fundamental Law of 1907 (107 articles) declared Twelver Shi'a Islam the official religion, guaranteed equality before the law, personal security, property rights, freedom of the press and association (subject to religious limits), and separated the executive, legislative, and judicial powers. It also provided for a committee of mujtahids to vet legislation for conformity with Islamic law — a forerunner of the modern Guardian Council.

Legacy

The 1906–1907 constitution established the principle that sovereignty derives from the people, though in practice royal power reasserted itself under the Pahlavi dynasty. Its supplementary law's blend of popular rights and clerical oversight anticipated tensions that continued through the twentieth century.
