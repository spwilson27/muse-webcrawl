Urban heat island

Surface temperatures peak over built-up areas and ease over parks and water.

An urban heat island (UHI) is an urban area that is significantly warmer than its surrounding rural areas because of human activities and the built environment. The temperature difference is usually larger at night than during the day and is most pronounced in calm, clear weather. Luke Howard documented the phenomenon for London in the 1810s, one of the earliest observations in urban climatology.

Causes

The main causes are: dark, heat-absorbing surfaces such as asphalt and roofing; the replacement of vegetation (which cools by evapotranspiration) with impervious materials; waste heat from vehicles, industry, and air conditioning; and urban geometry — tall buildings and narrow streets trap radiation and reduce wind flow. Surface UHIs measured by satellites can exceed air-temperature UHIs, which typically run 1–3 °C on average and can be much higher on individual nights.

Effects

UHIs increase energy demand for cooling, elevate air pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions, impair water quality, and harm human health: heat islands amplify heat waves, which are among the deadliest weather events, with effects falling hardest on low-income districts that often have the least tree canopy.

Mitigation

Mitigation strategies include increasing tree cover and green space, green and cool (high-albedo) roofs, reflective and permeable pavements, urban forestry programs, and building codes that reduce waste heat. Cities such as Los Angeles have experimented with cool pavements, while Singapore integrates greenery throughout its built fabric.
