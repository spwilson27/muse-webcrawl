Military engineering is loosely defined as the art, science and practice of designing and building military works and maintaining lines of military transport and communications. It is generally considered the oldest of the engineering disciplines, encompassing the construction of fortifications, bridges, roads, airfields and defensive positions, as well as demolitions, mine warfare and the breaching of enemy obstacles. Soldiers who perform these tasks are called combat engineers, sappers or pioneers in various armies.

Branches of activity

Military engineering is conventionally divided into:

  • Combat engineering — supporting forces in the field: mobility (bridging rivers, clearing minefields, building roads), counter-mobility (laying mines, demolitions, obstacles) and survivability (field fortifications, camouflage).
  • Construction engineering — building bases, airfields, ports and infrastructure in rear areas and for occupation or peace-support operations.
  • Topographic and geospatial engineering — surveying, terrain analysis and map production.

History

Military engineering began with the first fortified cities and siege warfare of the ancient world: Roman legions were famous for their roads, camps and siegeworks, and the medieval period saw elaborate castle-building and siegecraft. The gunpowder age produced the star fort and systematised siegecraft of engineers such as Vauban in the service of Louis XIV. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries added railways, telegraphy, pontoon bridging, land mines, military diving and explosive ordnance disposal. Modern corps — the British Royal Engineers, the United States Army Corps of Engineers (which also manages major civil water infrastructure), and their counterparts — retain both combat and construction roles, and military engineering support is a staple of disaster relief and peacekeeping missions.
