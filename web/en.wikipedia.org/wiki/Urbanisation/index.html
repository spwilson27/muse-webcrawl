Urbanisation

By 2007, for the first time, more people lived in urban than rural areas.

Urbanisation (or urbanization) is the population shift from rural to urban areas, the corresponding decrease in the proportion of people living in rural areas, and the ways in which societies adapt to this change. It is the process by which towns and cities are formed and grow as more people begin living and working in central areas.

History

Cities are as old as the agricultural revolution — Mesopotamian and Indus Valley settlements date to the 4th millennium BC — but for most of history urban shares stayed below about 10%. The Industrial Revolution transformed this: Britain became majority-urban by the mid-19th century, and steam power, factories, and railways drew mass migration. In 1800 only about 3% of the world's population lived in cities; by 1900 it was roughly 13%; in 2007 the urban share passed 50% for the first time, and the United Nations projects about 68% by 2050, with nearly all growth in Africa and Asia.

Causes

Urbanisation is driven by rural-to-urban migration (pushed by land scarcity and pulled by jobs, services, and education), natural population increase within cities, and the reclassification and annexation of settlements. Economists associate it with structural transformation — labor moving from agriculture to industry and services — and with agglomeration economies that make cities more productive.

Effects

Urbanisation brings higher average incomes, better access to health and education, and lower fertility, but also congestion, pollution, housing shortages, and informal settlements when growth outpaces planning. Urban areas account for the majority of global energy use and carbon emissions, making sustainable urbanisation central to climate policy. Related phenomena include suburbanisation, counterurbanisation, and the formation of megacities and megalopolises.
