Treason Act 1351

The Treason Act 1351 (25 Edw. 3 Stat. 5, c. 2) is an Act of the Parliament of England that codified the crime of high treason. Enacted under Edward III, it remains, in amended form, one of the oldest statutes still in force in the United Kingdom.

Provisions

The Act declared it treason: to compass or imagine the death of the king, queen, or their eldest son and heir; to violate the king's companion (the queen), the king's eldest daughter unmarried, or the wife of the king's eldest son; to levy war against the king in his realm or adhere to his enemies; to counterfeit the great seal or privy seal or the king's money; and to kill the chancellor, treasurer, or certain judges while performing their offices. It distinguished high treason from the lesser offence of petty treason, and reserved doubtful cases to Parliament.

Significance

By defining treason exhaustively, the statute limited the ability of judges and the Crown to extend the offence by construction—a protection later reinforced by the Treason Act 1695, which added procedural safeguards such as the requirement of two witnesses. Later statutes expanded and then contracted the law; the last execution for high treason in Britain was of William Joyce ("Lord Haw-Haw") in 1946 under this Act's clause on adhering to the king's enemies. The death penalty for treason was abolished by the Crime and Disorder Act 1998.
