Bandicoot

OrderPeramelemorphia
FamiliesPeramelidae, Chaeropodidae, Thylacomyidae
RangeAustralia and New Guinea
DietInsects, worms, larvae, fungi

Bandicoots are a group of about twenty species of small to medium-sized terrestrial marsupials in the order Peramelemorphia, native to Australia and New Guinea. They have a pointed snout, a compact body, large hind feet and coarse fur, and are mostly nocturnal omnivores that dig distinctive conical holes in the soil while searching for insects and other invertebrates.

Biology

Bandicoots combine features seen in few other marsupials: they are polyoestrous breeders with one of the shortest gestation periods of any mammal — about 12.5 days in some species — and they possess a chorioallantoic placenta in addition to the yolk-sac placenta typical of marsupials, a trait otherwise found in the koala and wombats among marsupials. Females have a rear-facing pouch that keeps soil away from the young while digging. The forefeet bear strong claws for excavating, and the elongated hind foot, with a syndactylous second and third toe, is used for grooming.

Genera

Living genera include the long-nosed bandicoots (Perameles), short-nosed bandicoots (Isoodon), the bilbies (Macrotis, family Thylacomyidae) and several New Guinean genera such as Echymipera and Rhynchomeles. The pig-footed bandicoot (Chaeropus) is extinct.

Conservation

Several species, such as the eastern barred bandicoot and the western barred bandicoot, have declined severely since European settlement through habitat loss and predation by foxes and cats, and are subjects of reintroduction programmes.
