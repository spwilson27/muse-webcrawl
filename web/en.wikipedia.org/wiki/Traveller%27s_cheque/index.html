Traveller's cheque

A traveller's cheque is a medium of exchange that was widely used by travellers on holiday in foreign countries instead of cash. Issued by financial institutions in fixed denominations in major currencies, traveller's cheques were prepaid and could be replaced if lost or stolen, making them a secure way to carry money before the spread of payment cards and ATMs.

History

The first traveller's cheques were issued in 1772 by the London Credit Exchange Company for use in selected European cities. The modern product was developed by American Express employee Marcellus Fleming Berry, who devised the countersignature system; American Express issued its traveller's cheques from 1891 and became the dominant issuer, alongside Thomas Cook and major banks. Travellers signed each cheque when purchasing it and countersigned it in the presence of the payee when spending it; matching signatures verified the user.

Decline

From the 1990s onwards, the growing worldwide acceptance of credit and debit cards, and the spread of ATMs allowing cash withdrawal abroad, caused the use of traveller's cheques to fall sharply. Many retailers stopped accepting them because of fraud and handling costs, and issuers progressively withdrew products, though existing cheques generally remain valid indefinitely and can be redeemed.
