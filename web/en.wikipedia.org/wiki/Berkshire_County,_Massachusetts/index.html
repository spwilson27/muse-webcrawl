Berkshire County, Massachusetts

Berkshire County is the westernmost county in the U.S. state of Massachusetts, bordering New York, Vermont and Connecticut. It takes its name from the Berkshire Hills, part of the Appalachian chain, and is popularly known simply as "the Berkshires" — a rural, mountainous region famed for its cultural institutions, including the Tanglewood music festival and Jacob's Pillow dance festival.

Geography

The county is a highland region of the Berkshire Hills and the Taconic Mountains, drained by the Housatonic and Hoosic rivers. Mount Greylock, in the northwest, is the highest point in Massachusetts. The county contains two cities, Pittsfield and North Adams, and 30 towns, including Great Barrington, Stockbridge, Lenox and Williamstown.

History

The area was the homeland of the Mahican people before English settlement in the 18th century. The county was incorporated in 1761. In 1786–87 it was touched by Shays' Rebellion. In the 19th century the region industrialised around textile mills, paper and electrical manufacturing — General Electric's Pittsfield works became a major employer — while its scenery drew writers such as Herman Melville, who wrote Moby-Dick at Arrowhead in Pittsfield, and Edith Wharton, who built The Mount in Lenox.

Culture

The Berkshires are a major summer cultural destination: Tanglewood in Lenox is the summer home of the Boston Symphony Orchestra; Jacob's Pillow in Becket is the oldest international dance festival in the United States; the Massachusetts Museum of Contemporary Art (MASS MoCA) in North Adams is among the largest contemporary art centres in the country. Williams College, in Williamstown, is a leading liberal arts college.
