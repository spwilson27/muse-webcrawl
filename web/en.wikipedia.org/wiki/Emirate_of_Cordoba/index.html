Emirate of Córdoba

The Emirate of Córdoba (Arabic: إمارة قرطبة, Imārat Qurṭubah), also spelled Cordova, was an independent Islamic emirate in the Iberian Peninsula between 756 and 929. It was founded by Abd al-Rahman I, a member of the Umayyad dynasty who had escaped the overthrow of the Umayyad Caliphate by the Abbasids in 750 and fled across North Africa to al-Andalus.

History

After landing in Iberia in 755, Abd al-Rahman defeated the existing wali, Yusuf al-Fihri, at the Battle of Córdoba in May 756 and proclaimed himself emir. Although the territory had been conquered by Muslim forces beginning in 711, it was only with Abd al-Rahman's accession that a unified, independent state emerged. He initially maintained nominal ties to the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad but later asserted full independence.

The early emirate faced repeated internal revolts, Berber uprisings, and pressure from the Christian Kingdom of Asturias in the north. Under Abd al-Rahman II (r. 822–852), Córdoba flourished culturally, drawing on Abbasid models; the court of the musician Ziryab became a byword for refinement. Hisham II and his successors contended with the growing power of the Frankish kingdoms and of León.

Caliphate

In 929, Abd al-Rahman III proclaimed himself caliph, transforming the emirate into the Caliphate of Córdoba. The caliphate endured until 1031, when it fragmented into the taifa kingdoms.
