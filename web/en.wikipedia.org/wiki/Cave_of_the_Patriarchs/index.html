The Cave of the Patriarchs or Tomb of the Patriarchs, known to Jews as the Cave of Machpelah (Hebrew: מערת המכפלה) and to Muslims as the Sanctuary of Abraham or the Ibrahimi Mosque (Arabic: الحرم الإبراهيمي), is a series of caves situated in the heart of the old city of Hebron in the West Bank. According to the traditions of the Abrahamic religions, the cave complex is the burial place of the patriarchs and matriarchs Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Rebecca, Jacob and Leah, and is therefore one of the holiest sites in both Judaism and Islam.

History

According to the Book of Genesis, Abraham purchased the field of Machpelah and its cave from Ephron the Hittite for 400 shekels of silver as a burial plot after the death of Sarah. The massive rectangular enclosure with walls of huge dressed limestone blocks is attributed to the building programme of Herod the Great in the 1st century BCE. During the Byzantine period a basilica was built inside the enclosure; after the Muslim conquest in the 7th century the site became a mosque. Crusaders converted it back into a church in the 12th century, and after Saladin's reconquest in 1188 it returned to use as a mosque. For centuries Jews were permitted to ascend only to the seventh step of the exterior staircase.

Structure

The complex is dominated by the Herodian retaining walls, up to 15 metres high, built of massive stone blocks with the characteristic margins of Herodian masonry. Above the walls rises an Islamic-period structure with two minarets, crenellations and a series of domes. Inside, large cenotaphs (memorial tombs) of the patriarchs and matriarchs stand in separate chambers; the actual caves lie beneath the building and are generally inaccessible. The site has been divided between a mosque section and a synagogue section since the arrangements following the 1994 Cave of the Patriarchs massacre, in which 29 Muslim worshippers were killed by an extremist settler.

Religious significance

In Judaism the cave is the second holiest site after the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, and the associated city of Hebron is one of the four holy cities. In Islam, Abraham (Ibrahim) is revered as a prophet, and the site functions as a major mosque. Pilgrims of all three faiths have visited the tombs for well over a thousand years; travellers' accounts survive from the early medieval period onward.
