The St. Francis Dam was a curved concrete gravity dam built between 1924 and 1926 in San Francisquito Canyon, north of Los Angeles, to store water from the Los Angeles Aqueduct. It was designed and supervised by William Mulholland, chief engineer of the city's Bureau of Water Works and Supply. Shortly before midnight on 12 March 1928, less than two years after completion and hours after Mulholland had inspected it, the dam failed catastrophically.

The disaster

The collapse released some 45,000 acre-feet (about 12.4 billion US gallons) of water in a flood wave initially over 100 feet high, which scoured the canyon and swept down the Santa Clara River valley through Fillmore and Santa Paula to the Pacific Ocean near Ventura. At least 431 people were killed — some estimates run considerably higher — making it one of the worst civil engineering disasters in American history and the second-deadliest disaster in California history after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake.

Causes and aftermath

Investigations blamed a fatal combination of design and geology: the dam was founded on an ancient landslide on one abutment and on a water-soluble conglomerate on the other, and its height had been raised twice during construction without widening the base. Mulholland accepted full responsibility and retired; the failure ended his career. The disaster prompted California to strengthen state supervision of dam safety and helped spur the creation of professional licensing review for engineers. The dam was never rebuilt; a surviving central block, nicknamed "The Tombstone", was demolished in 1929, and the site is now a California Historical Landmark within Angeles National Forest.
