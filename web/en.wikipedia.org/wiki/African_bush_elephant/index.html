African bush elephant

The African bush elephant (Loxodonta africana), also known as the African savanna elephant, is one of two extant species of African elephant and, along with the smaller African forest elephant, one of the largest living terrestrial animals. Bulls stand up to about 3.3 m (10.8 ft) tall at the shoulder and can weigh around 6 tonnes. Bush elephants inhabit grasslands, savannas, and bushlands across sub-Saharan Africa.

Description

African bush elephants have grey, wrinkled skin, very large ears shaped somewhat like the African continent, and a concave or swayed back. Both males and females usually grow large curved tusks of ivory, elongated incisor teeth that grow throughout life. The trunk is a fusion of the nose and upper lip with two finger-like projections at the tip, used for breathing, smelling, grasping, and drinking. The species has four molar teeth in each jaw quadrant over its lifetime, replaced progressively as they wear—an adaptation to a coarse plant diet.

Distribution and habitat

Bush elephants range across much of sub-Saharan Africa, from Mali and Ethiopia to South Africa, though populations are fragmented. The largest remaining populations are in Botswana, Zimbabwe, Tanzania, Kenya, and South Africa. They occupy savanna, grassland, semi-desert, and open woodland, migrating seasonally in search of water and forage.

Behaviour

Elephants live in matriarchal family units led by the oldest female; related families form bond groups and clans. Adult bulls leave their natal herds at puberty and live alone or in bachelor groups, joining female herds to mate. Elephants communicate through vocalisations including infrasound rumbles that travel long distances, as well as through seismic signals, scent, and touch. They are highly intelligent, showing self-awareness, empathy, and long memories. An elephant may eat 150 kg or more of vegetation per day, shaping ecosystems by uprooting trees, dispersing seeds, and digging water holes used by other animals.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the African bush elephant as Endangered; numbers fell sharply during the twentieth century due to poaching for the ivory trade. Continental populations declined from millions historically to roughly 400,000–500,000 in recent decades, though some southern African populations have recovered under protection. Threats include poaching, habitat loss and fragmentation, and human–wildlife conflict. International commercial ivory trade is banned under CITES Appendix I listings for most populations.
