Neuroglia

Neuroglia, also called glial cells (from Greek γλία, "glue") or simply glia, are non-neuronal cells in the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system that do not produce electrical impulses. They maintain homeostasis, form myelin, and provide support and protection for neurons. In the central nervous system, glial cells include oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, ependymal cells and microglia, and in the peripheral nervous system they include Schwann cells and satellite cells.

Glia were first described in 1856 by the pathologist Rudolf Virchow, who conceived of them as a kind of connective "nerve glue" (Nervenkitt). The four main glial types of the central nervous system were characterised by later histologists including Santiago Ramón y Cajal and Pío del Río Hortega.

Types

Macroglia of the CNS

  • Astrocytes — star-shaped cells that regulate the blood–brain barrier, recycle neurotransmitters, buffer ions, and support synapses.
  • Oligodendrocytes — produce myelin sheaths around CNS axons.
  • Ependymal cells — line the ventricles and central canal, helping circulate cerebrospinal fluid.

Microglia

Microglia are the resident immune cells of the brain, derived from embryonic yolk-sac macrophages. They survey the tissue, prune synapses during development, and respond to injury and infection.

Glia of the PNS

  • Schwann cells — myelinate peripheral axons and support unmyelinated ones.
  • Satellite glial cells — surround neuronal cell bodies in sensory and autonomic ganglia.

Numbers

For much of the 20th century it was widely repeated that glia outnumber neurons ten to one. Modern cell-counting methods, notably the isotropic fractionator developed by Suzana Herculano-Houzel, show that the human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons and a comparable number of glial cells — a ratio close to 1:1 overall, though the ratio varies greatly between structures (the cerebral cortex has more glia per neuron; the cerebellum has far fewer).

Functions

Beyond structural support, glia regulate extracellular potassium and glutamate, supply metabolic substrates to neurons, modulate synaptic transmission (the "tripartite synapse"), guide neuronal migration and axon growth during development, and participate in repair after injury, where reactive astrogliosis and glial scarring both protect tissue and impede regeneration.

Clinical significance

Tumours arising from glial cells are called gliomas and account for a large share of primary brain tumours. Glial dysfunction is implicated in multiple sclerosis (loss of oligodendrocyte myelin), epilepsy, chronic pain, and neurodegenerative diseases.
