Second International Congress on Education of the Deaf

Date6–11 September 1880
LocationMilan, Italy
Attendance164 delegates; only one was deaf
OutcomeResolutions favoring oral education over sign language

The Second International Congress on Education of the Deaf, universally known as the Milan Conference or Milan Congress of 1880, was an international meeting of educators of the deaf held in Milan, Italy, in September 1880. It is regarded by the Deaf community as one of the most damaging events in Deaf history.

The resolutions

Of the 164 delegates — drawn mainly from Italy and France, with smaller numbers from Britain, the United States, and elsewhere — only one, the American James Denison, was deaf. Influenced by oralist organizers, the congress passed eight resolutions declaring "the incontestable superiority of speech over signs" for educating deaf children and recommending that the oral method — speech and lip-reading, with sign language excluded from the classroom — be adopted universally.

Consequences

The resolutions swept through Europe and North America: schools dismissed deaf teachers, banned sign language in instruction, and punished students for signing. The proportion of deaf teachers fell drastically, educational outcomes for deaf students declined for generations, and sign languages were driven underground, surviving in dormitories and Deaf clubs. Alexander Graham Bell's advocacy lent oralism further prestige in the United States. American delegates Edward Miner Gallaudet and others opposed the resolutions but were outvoted.

Reassessment

From the 1960s, linguistic research led by William Stokoe established that sign languages are full natural languages, and bilingual-bicultural education gained ground. In 2010, the 21st International Congress on Education of the Deaf, meeting in Vancouver, formally rejected the 1880 resolutions and apologized for their effects — a symbolic repudiation 130 years after Milan.
