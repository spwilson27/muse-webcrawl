Dihedral angle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dihedral angle is the angle between two intersecting planes or half-planes. In chemistry, it is defined as the angle between planes through two sets of three atoms, having two atoms in common. In solid geometry, it is defined as the union of a line, the edge, and two half-planes that have this line as a common edge. When the two planes are specified by vectors, the dihedral angle can be computed from their normal vectors: if n1 and n2 are unit normals, then

cos φ = n1 · n2.

In three dimensions, the equivalent of a plane angle is the solid angle; the angle between two planes is sometimes called a dihedral angle to emphasise the analogy. Notable examples include the angles between faces of polyhedra: the regular tetrahedron has dihedral angle arccos(1/3) ≈ 70.53°, the cube 90°, and the regular dodecahedron about 116.57°.

In chemistry

In stereochemistry, the dihedral (or torsion) angle between four atoms A–B–C–D is the angle between the planes ABC and BCD, with a sign convention determined by the direction of rotation. Torsion angles describe conformations: staggered arrangements of ethane correspond to dihedrals of 60°, 180°, and 300° (−60°), while eclipsed arrangements are at 0°, 120°, and 240°. In proteins, the backbone dihedrals φ (phi), ψ (psi), and ω (omega) characterise secondary structure and are visualised on a Ramachandran plot; side-chain angles are denoted χ. Molecular mechanics force fields include explicit torsional energy terms penalising unfavourable dihedral values, since rotation about bonds governs molecular shape and flexibility.

In aviation and other fields

In aeronautics, "dihedral" refers to the upward angle of an aircraft's wings relative to the horizontal, which contributes to lateral (roll) stability; the opposite, downward angle, is anhedral. Dihedral angles also appear in crystallography, in the folding of sheet materials and origami mathematics, and in computer graphics, where the angle between adjacent mesh faces helps detect sharp edges and compute smooth shading.
