Roman consul

A consul was the highest elected political office of the Roman Republic and a prestigious position under the Roman Empire. Two consuls were elected each year, serving together as heads of state and commanders of the army; each could veto the other's actions, a check intended to prevent the concentration of power that Romans associated with their expelled kings.

History

According to Roman tradition, the consulship was established in 509 BC after the overthrow of Tarquinius Superbus, the last king of Rome, with Lucius Junius Brutus and Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus as the first consuls. Initially the office was restricted to patricians; after the Licinian-Sextian laws of 367 BC, one of the two consulships was opened to plebeians, and the first plebeian consul took office in 366 BC.

Powers and duties

Consuls held imperium, the right of command, presided over the Senate and the assemblies, proposed legislation, conducted elections, and led Rome's armies in war. Within the city their power was limited by the right of appeal (provocatio) and by the tribunes of the plebs; in the field each consul typically commanded two legions. Consuls entered office on dates that varied over time before settling on 1 January, and the year was named after the two "ordinary" consuls (consules ordinarii). If a consul died or resigned, a suffect consul was elected to complete the term.

The insignia of the office included the toga praetexta, the curule chair, and twelve lictors carrying fasces. After their year in office, ex-consuls (consulares) formed the senior ranks of the Senate and commonly governed major provinces as proconsuls.

Eligibility

The cursus honorum set a ladder of offices — quaestor, aedile or tribune, praetor, then consul — with minimum ages codified by the Lex Villia Annalis (180 BC) and tightened by Sulla, who set the minimum consular age at 42. In practice the consulship was dominated by a narrow circle of noble families, and a man from a family without consular ancestors was a novus homo ("new man"), like Marius and Cicero.

Under the Empire

Under Augustus and his successors the consulship lost real power to the emperor but retained immense prestige. Emperors frequently held the office themselves, ordinary consuls served shortened terms, and lists of suffect consuls lengthened. The office survived into late antiquity; the last consul held office in 541 AD under Justinian I, after which the title was absorbed into the imperial titulature. Lists of consuls, the fasti consulares, form the backbone of Roman chronology.
