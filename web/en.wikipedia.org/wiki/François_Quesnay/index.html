François Quesnay

BornJune 4, 1694, Méré, France
DiedDecember 16, 1774 (aged 80), Versailles, France
OccupationPhysician, economist
SchoolPhysiocracy

François Quesnay (June 4, 1694 – December 16, 1774) was a French economist and physician who founded the Physiocratic school of economic thought. His Tableau économique (1758) was one of the first attempts to model the workings of an entire economy.

Life

Quesnay was born near Paris, the son of a small landowner. Trained as a surgeon, he rose to become physician to Madame de Pompadour and to King Louis XV at Versailles. In his sixties he turned increasingly to economic questions, gathering a circle of disciples — including Mirabeau, Dupont de Nemours, and Mercier de la Rivière — who became known as the Physiocrats (the économistes).

The Tableau économique

Drawing an analogy with the circulation of blood, Quesnay's Tableau traced the flows of goods and money between three classes: the productive class (agricultural laborers), the proprietary class (landowners), and the sterile class (artisans and merchants). Physiocrats held that land and agriculture were the sole source of a society's net product (produit net), and therefore advocated a single tax on land, free trade in grain, and the removal of feudal and mercantilist restrictions — policies summarized in the phrase laissez-faire, laissez-passer.

Influence

Adam Smith visited Quesnay's circle in Paris and was influenced by Physiocratic ideas, though he rejected the doctrine that manufacture and commerce were "sterile." The Tableau is regarded as a forerunner of modern input–output analysis and national income accounting.
