Belle Époque

Belle Époque
Period: c. 1871/1880 – 1914
Region: France and Western Europe
Ended by: outbreak of the First World War
Equivalent eras: Gilded Age (USA), Edwardian era (Britain), Gründerzeit (Germany)

The Belle Époque (French for "Beautiful Era") was a period of French and European history, usually dated from the end of the Franco-Prussian War in 1871, or from about 1880, to the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. It was characterized by optimism, peace, economic prosperity, and rapid progress in technology, science, and the arts.

Culture and society

Paris was the cultural centre of the era. The city hosted the Expositions Universelles of 1889 — for which the Eiffel Tower was built — and 1900, and its cabarets, cafés, and dance halls, such as the Moulin Rouge, flourished. Artistic movements including Impressionism's successors, Post-Impressionism, Art Nouveau, and early modernism transformed painting, architecture, and design. Writers and artists such as Marcel Proust, Claude Monet, Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, and Alphonse Mucha defined the period's image.

Progress and its limits

The era saw the spread of electricity, the telephone, the automobile, aviation, cinema, and mass-circulation newspapers. At the same time, the retrospective label — coined after 1918 — glosses over poverty, labour unrest, colonial violence, and political scandals such as the Dreyfus affair. The Belle Époque ended abruptly with the guns of August 1914.
