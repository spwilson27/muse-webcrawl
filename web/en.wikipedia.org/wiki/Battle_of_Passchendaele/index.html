The Battle of Passchendaele (also known as the Third Battle of Ypres) was a campaign of the First World War, fought by the Allies against the German Empire. The battle took place on the Western Front from July to November 1917, for control of the ridges south and east of the Belgian city of Ypres in West Flanders, as part of a strategy decided by the Allies at conferences in November 1916 and May 1917. It became infamous for the scale of casualties and the mud — churned-up clay, shattered drainage and the wettest autumn weather in decades — in which much of the fighting took place.

Objectives

General Sir Douglas Haig, commander of the British Expeditionary Force, sought to break through the German defences in Flanders, liberate the Belgian coast and its U-boat bases at Ostend and Zeebrugge, and relieve pressure on the French army, weakened by mutinies after the Nivelle Offensive. Critics in London, including Prime Minister David Lloyd George, authorised the offensive only reluctantly.

Course of the battle

The offensive opened on 31 July 1917 with the Battle of Pilckem Ridge, after a massive artillery bombardment. Early gains were slowed by rain that began the same afternoon. In September, General Herbert Plumer's Second Army, using carefully limited "bite-and-hold" tactics, won notable victories at the Menin Road Ridge (20 September), Polygon Wood (26 September) and Broodseinde (4 October).

Rain returned in October; attacks at Poelcappelle (9 October) and the First Battle of Passchendaele (12 October) foundered in the mire. The Canadian Corps under Arthur Currie, brought in to take the village itself, captured Passchendaele ridge in a series of assaults between 26 October and 10 November, at a cost of some 16,000 Canadian casualties.

Aftermath and memory

The Allies gained roughly eight kilometres of devastated ground at enormous cost; German losses were also very heavy, and the comparative casualty figures and the battle's strategic value remain subjects of historical debate. Passchendaele has become a byword for the perceived futility of the First World War: the poet Siegfried Sassoon wrote of it, "I died in hell — (they called it Passchendaele)." The Tyne Cot Cemetery, the largest Commonwealth war cemetery in the world, lies on the ridge. The village is now part of Zonnebeke; the rebuilt settlement is spelled Passendale.
