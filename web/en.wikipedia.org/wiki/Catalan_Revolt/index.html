Catalan Revolt

The Catalan Revolt (Catalan: Guerra dels Segadors, "War of the Reapers"; 1640–1659) was a conflict in which the Principality of Catalonia rebelled against the Spanish monarchy of Philip IV during the Franco-Spanish War. Sparked by grievances over the billeting of Castilian troops and the fiscal and constitutional policies of the Count-Duke of Olivares, the revolt led Catalonia to place itself under French protection and, briefly, to proclaim a Catalan Republic.

Causes and outbreak

Olivares's "Union of Arms" scheme sought to distribute the military burden of the monarchy more evenly, but Catalans resisted demands they saw as violating their constitutions. When war with France began in 1635, Spanish troops were quartered in Catalonia and clashes with the peasantry multiplied. On 7 June 1640, the feast of Corpus Christi, reapers entering Barcelona rose in the bloody Corpus de Sang, killing the viceroy, the Count of Santa Coloma. The revolutionary government of Pau Claris, president of the Generalitat, sought French aid and in January 1641 proclaimed a Catalan Republic, soon transformed into allegiance to Louis XIII as Count of Barcelona.

Course of the war

French and Catalan forces defeated the Spanish at the Battle of Montjuïc in January 1641, but the war dragged on amid plague, occupation abuses by the French, and divided loyalties. Barcelona fell to Don John of Austria in 1652 after a long siege. The war formally ended with the Treaty of the Pyrenees in 1659, by which Spain ceded the northern Catalan counties of Roussillon and part of Cerdanya to France — a partition of Catalonia that endures today — while the rest of the principality returned to Spanish rule, its constitutions formally confirmed.

Legacy

The revolt is a defining episode in Catalan national memory. The song Els Segadors ("The Reapers"), originating in the conflict, is now the national anthem of Catalonia.
