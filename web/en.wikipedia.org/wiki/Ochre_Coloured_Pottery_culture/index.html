Ochre Coloured Pottery culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ochre Coloured Pottery culture (OCP) is a Bronze Age culture of the Indo-Gangetic Plain, generally dated to roughly 2000–1500 BCE (dates vary by site and author). It is named for its characteristic pottery, which is often poorly fired and has a wash that leaves an ochre-like stain on the fingers when handled. The culture is found mainly in the Doab region between the Ganges and Yamuna rivers in present-day Uttar Pradesh, with sites extending into Haryana, Rajasthan and Punjab.

Characteristics

OCP sites are usually small settlements near rivers. The pottery is wheel-made, of medium to fine fabric, in shapes including dishes, bowls, jars and vases, sometimes with incised or painted decoration. Associated finds include terracotta figurines, stone tools and, famously, copper objects: the culture overlaps in distribution with the Copper Hoards, which include anthropomorphic figures, harpoons, antennae swords and flat axes, though the exact relationship between the two remains debated.

Interpretation

The OCP culture has been variously interpreted as a late or degenerate phase of the Indus Valley Civilisation, as a contemporary but independent culture of the Ganges plain, or as an early culture ancestral to later Iron Age cultures of the region. Some scholars have attempted to link it to early Indo-Aryan speakers, but this identification is not generally agreed. Many OCP sites show signs of flood destruction, and the culture seems to have faded out by the middle of the second millennium BCE, followed in the region (after a gap at many sites) by the Painted Grey Ware culture.

Notable sites

Sites associated with the culture include Ahichhatra, Atranjikhera, Hastinapura (in its earliest levels), Lal Qila and Saipai, where a copper hoard was found in an OCP context.
