SALT I is the common name for the first round of the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks between the United States and the Soviet Union on limiting strategic nuclear weapons, and for the two agreements concluded from them in 1972: the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty and the Interim Agreement on Certain Measures with Respect to the Limitation of Strategic Offensive Arms. The talks formed a centrepiece of the superpower détente of the early 1970s.

Negotiations

Discussions on limiting missile defences and offensive arsenals began under President Lyndon B. Johnson, whose 1967 Glassboro Summit with Premier Alexei Kosygin raised the issue, but substantive negotiations opened in Helsinki in November 1969 under President Richard Nixon. Sessions alternated between Helsinki and Vienna, with the American delegation led by Gerard Smith and the strategy shaped in Washington by National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger, whose back-channel talks with Soviet ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin proved decisive.

Agreements

At the Moscow Summit of May 1972, Nixon and Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev signed two instruments on 26 May 1972:

  • The ABM Treaty limited each side to two anti-ballistic missile deployment sites (later reduced to one by a 1974 protocol), each with no more than 100 interceptors — a recognition that nationwide missile defence was destabilising to deterrence.
  • The Interim Agreement froze, for five years, the number of fixed land-based ICBM launchers and submarine-launched ballistic missile launchers at levels then operational or under construction. It did not limit bombers, warhead numbers, or multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles (MIRVs), and permitted the Soviets a numerical advantage in launchers, offset by American technological leads.

Reception and legacy

SALT I was hailed as a landmark of détente, but criticised in the United States by Senator Henry "Scoop" Jackson and others for freezing in Soviet numerical advantages; the Jackson Amendment pressed for parity in any future permanent treaty. The interim freeze was later extended by mutual declarations while negotiations continued, leading to the Vladivostok framework of 1974 and SALT II in 1979. The ABM Treaty endured until the United States withdrew from it in 2002.
