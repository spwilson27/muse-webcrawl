Great Dismal Swamp

Bald cypress in the Great Dismal Swamp.

The Great Dismal Swamp is a large swamp in the Coastal Plain region of southeastern Virginia and northeastern North Carolina, between Norfolk, Virginia, and Elizabeth City, North Carolina. Once covering close to one million acres, it has been reduced by drainage and logging to a remnant administered in large part as the Great Dismal Swamp National Wildlife Refuge, established in 1974 and covering about 113,000 acres. At its heart lies Lake Drummond, a shallow natural lake of some 3,100 acres.

History

The swamp formed after the last ice age and was long inhabited by Indigenous peoples. In the colonial era George Washington surveyed it and promoted the Dismal Swamp Company, which planned to drain the land and harvest its timber; Washington Ditch, one of the many canals dug — largely by enslaved labourers — still bears his name. The Dismal Swamp Canal, completed in 1805, linked Chesapeake Bay with Albemarle Sound and remains the oldest continuously operating man-made canal in the United States.

The swamp's dense, nearly impenetrable interior also made it a famous refuge for people escaping slavery. Communities of Great Dismal Swamp maroons lived in the swamp for generations, and it figured in abolitionist literature, including Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's poem "The Slave in the Dismal Swamp" and Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel Dred (1856). Archaeological surveys since the 2000s have documented dozens of maroon settlement sites on islands within the swamp.

Ecology

The swamp supports black bears, bobcats, river otters, some 200 bird species and extensive stands of Atlantic white cedar, bald cypress and water tupelo. Its peat soils store large quantities of carbon, and wildfires in 2008 and 2011 burned for months in the dried peat. Restoration efforts re-wet drained areas to restore the historic hydrology.
