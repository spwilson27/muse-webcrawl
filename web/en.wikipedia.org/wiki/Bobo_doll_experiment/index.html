Bobo doll experiment

The Bobo doll experiment (or experiments) is the collective name for a series of experiments performed by psychologist Albert Bandura in 1961, 1963, and 1965 to test his social learning theory. The studies are considered some of the most famous in psychology and demonstrated that children learn aggressive behavior through observing and imitating adult models — a process Bandura called observational learning.

A child imitating an adult model's aggression toward the inflatable Bobo doll

Experiment of 1961

In the first experiment, 36 boys and 36 girls from the Stanford University Nursery School, aged between 3 and 6 years, were tested. The children were divided into groups. The experimental groups watched an adult male or female model behaving aggressively toward a large inflatable Bobo doll — punching it, hitting it with a mallet, tossing it in the air, and shouting phrases such as "Sock him in the nose!" and "Pow!". A control group observed a non-aggressive model who played quietly with other toys, and another control group saw no model at all.

Afterwards, each child was taken to a playroom containing attractive toys, but was told after a few minutes that those toys were reserved for other children — a manipulation intended to frustrate the child and maximize the chance of aggression. The child was then left for 20 minutes in a room containing aggressive toys (a mallet, dart guns, and the Bobo doll) and non-aggressive toys (a tea set, crayons, dolls). Observers behind a one-way mirror recorded the child's behavior in 5-second intervals.

Results

Children who had observed the aggressive model made far more imitative aggressive responses than those in the non-aggressive or control groups. Boys were more likely to imitate physical aggression, while girls were more likely to imitate verbal aggression. There was also evidence that children were influenced by same-sex models. The results supported Bandura's social learning theory over existing explanations, showing that behavior could be learned by observation alone, without direct reinforcement to the learner.

Experiments of 1963 and 1965

In 1963, Bandura and colleagues showed that children exposed to filmed models — including a cartoon character — imitated aggression nearly as much as those who saw a live model, demonstrating the power of media models. In 1965, a follow-up study introduced vicarious reinforcement: children who saw the model rewarded for aggression imitated more, and children who saw the model punished imitated less, though when offered incentives the children could reproduce the aggressive behavior they had seen, showing that learning and performance are distinct.

Criticisms

The experiments have been criticized on ethical grounds (deliberately exposing children to aggression) and methodological grounds: it has been argued that the Bobo doll is designed to be hit and that children's behavior may have reflected playfulness or demand characteristics rather than genuine aggression. Some researchers have also questioned whether short-term imitation of a novel act generalizes to real-world aggression.

Significance

Despite criticism, the Bobo doll experiments remain landmark studies. They helped shift psychology away from a strict behaviorist emphasis on direct reinforcement and toward cognitive theories of learning, and they informed debates about the effects of media violence on children.
