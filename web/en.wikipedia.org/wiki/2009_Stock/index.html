The 2009 stock market recovery refers to the rebound of global equity markets beginning in March 2009, after the crash associated with the 2007–2008 financial crisis. From its closing low of 676.53 on 9 March 2009, the S&P 500 index began a sustained advance that inaugurated the longest bull market in United States history, lasting until the COVID-19 crash of February–March 2020.

Background: the crash

Between October 2007 and March 2009, the S&P 500 lost roughly 57 per cent of its value as the collapse of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 triggered a global financial panic, a credit freeze and the Great Recession. By early 2009 markets were pricing in the possible failure of major banks including Citigroup and Bank of America, and unemployment in the United States was climbing toward 10 per cent.

The turn

Several developments converged around the March low: the US Treasury's bank "stress tests" promised clarity about bank capital; the Federal Reserve expanded its quantitative easing programme on 18 March 2009, committing over a trillion dollars to mortgage and Treasury purchases; the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act provided fiscal stimulus; and a memo from Citigroup's CEO suggesting the bank had been profitable in early 2009 sparked a sharp rally on 10 March. The S&P 500 finished 2009 up about 23.5 per cent despite rising unemployment — an early sign of the decade-long divergence between equity markets and wage growth that followed.
