A Mersenne prime is a prime number that is one less than a power of two, i.e. of the form Mn = 2n − 1 for some integer n. They are named after Marin Mersenne, a French Minim friar who studied these numbers in the early 17th century. If Mn is prime, then n itself must be prime; the converse is false (e.g. 211 − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89). The smallest Mersenne primes are 3, 7, 31 and 127 (n = 2, 3, 5, 7).

Perfect numbers

Mersenne primes are intimately connected with perfect numbers. Euclid proved that if 2n − 1 is prime, then 2n−1(2n − 1) is a perfect number, and Euler proved that every even perfect number has this form; thus finding Mersenne primes and even perfect numbers are equivalent problems. It is unknown whether any odd perfect numbers exist, or whether there are infinitely many Mersenne primes — both are famous open problems in number theory.

Testing and the search

The Lucas–Lehmer primality test (developed by Édouard Lucas and Derrick Henry Lehmer) makes Mersenne numbers far easier to test than general integers of comparable size, which is why the largest known prime number has almost always been a Mersenne prime. Since 1996 the search has been coordinated by the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS), a distributed computing project whose volunteers have found the most recent Mersenne primes. In October 2024 GIMPS announced the 52nd known Mersenne prime, 2136,279,841 − 1, with 41,024,320 decimal digits — the first found using GPU computing in the project.
