Isabella Augusta, Lady Gregory (née Persse; 15 March 1852 – 22 May 1932) was an Irish dramatist, folklorist and theatre manager. With W. B. Yeats and Edward Martyn, she co-founded the Irish Literary Theatre and the Abbey Theatre, and wrote numerous short plays that were among the Abbey's most popular productions. She is regarded as a central organisational force of the Irish Literary Revival.

Life and work

Born into the Anglo-Irish gentry at Roxborough, County Galway, she married in 1880 Sir William Gregory, a former MP and Governor of Ceylon, 35 years her senior; his death in 1892 left her mistress of Coole Park, near Gort, whose house and lake (the "seven woods" and wild swans of Yeats's poems) became a gathering place of the Revival — Yeats, Synge, Shaw, O'Casey and others were regular summer guests, and visitors carved initials on the famous "autograph tree".

With Yeats she collected folklore from the Galway countryside, writing her retellings in the English dialect of the Kiltartan district ("Kiltartanese"): Cuchulain of Muirthemne (1902), hailed by Yeats as "the best book that has come out of Ireland in my time", and Gods and Fighting Men (1904). Her own one-act plays — comedies such as Spreading the News (1904), The Rising of the Moon (1907), Hyacinth Halvey and the tragedy The Gaol Gate — were Abbey staples, and she directed the theatre's affairs for decades, defending controversial work including The Playboy of the Western World. Among her family, her son Robert Gregory, a painter and airman killed in 1918, is memorialised in Yeats's "An Irish Airman Foresees His Death". She also campaigned successfully for the return to Ireland of the Lane Bequest of Impressionist paintings left by her nephew Hugh Lane, who died on the Lusitania. Her journals and autobiography (Seventy Years, published posthumously) are major sources for the Revival.
