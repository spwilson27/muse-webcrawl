International Style (architecture)

The International Style is a major architectural style of modernism that emerged in Europe and the United States in the 1920s–1930s, emphasising volume over mass, regularity, glass and steel, and the rejection of ornament.

Origins

The term was popularised by Henry-Russell Hitchcock and Philip Johnson's 1932 MoMA exhibition "The International Style: Architecture Since 1922." Key figures include Le Corbusier, Walter Gropius and the Bauhaus, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, and J.J.P. Oud.

Characteristics and spread

Hallmarks are flat roofs, smooth white or curtain-wall surfaces, ribbon windows, pilotis and open plans. After World War II it dominated corporate architecture worldwide — e.g. the Seagram Building (1958) and Lever House — before criticism from postmodernists for sterility and placelessness.
