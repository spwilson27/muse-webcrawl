The lateral pterygoid muscle (or external pterygoid muscle) is a muscle of mastication with two parts, an upper (superior) head and a lower (inferior) head. It lies in the infratemporal fossa and is the only muscle of mastication that opens the jaw.

Structure

The superior head arises from the infratemporal surface and infratemporal crest of the greater wing of the sphenoid bone, while the inferior head arises from the lateral surface of the lateral pterygoid plate. The fibres pass backwards and laterally: the inferior head inserts chiefly into the pterygoid fovea on the front of the neck of the mandible's condyle, and the superior head inserts into the articular disc and capsule of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ). The maxillary artery and the pterygoid venous plexus are closely related to the muscle, and the buccal nerve typically passes between its two heads.

Function

Contracting together, the two lateral pterygoid muscles protrude the mandible and help depress it, opening the mouth. Acting alternately, they produce the side-to-side movements used in grinding food. The superior head is active during closing and clenching, stabilising the articular disc against the articular eminence; the inferior head is active during opening and protrusion.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the lateral pterygoid, particularly spasm or disharmony with the disc of the TMJ, has been implicated in temporomandibular joint disorders and in anterior displacement of the articular disc. The muscle is also an important landmark in surgery of the infratemporal fossa and in regional anaesthetic techniques.
