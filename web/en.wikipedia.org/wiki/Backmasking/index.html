Backmasking

Backmasking is a recording technique in which a sound or message is recorded backward onto a track that is meant to be played forward. The technique was popularised in the 1960s by bands experimenting with tape manipulation, notably the Beatles on the album Revolver (1966).

History

Deliberate backmasking as an artistic device grew out of musique concrète and studio experimentation with reel-to-reel tape. The Beatles' "Rain" (1966) featured a backward vocal passage, and reversed instrumental parts appeared on several songs of the era. Later artists used the technique for comic, atmospheric, or hidden-message effects.

Controversy

In the 1980s, some religious groups in the United States claimed that rock records contained backmasked satanic messages intended to influence listeners subconsciously. The allegations fuelled moral panic, legislative proposals for warning labels, and the 1990 civil trial involving Judas Priest, in which the band was found not liable. Psychologists regard most alleged hidden messages as products of suggestion and auditory pareidolia: listeners presented with a supposed transcript readily "hear" the phrase in ambiguous reversed audio.

Related techniques

Backmasking differs from simply reversing a whole track for effect, and from turntablism techniques in hip hop. It is also distinct from claimed phonetic reversal phenomena in phenomena such as "reverse speech".
