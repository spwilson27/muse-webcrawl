Shame

Shame is an unpleasant self-conscious emotion often associated with a negative evaluation of the self, withdrawal motivations, and feelings of distress, exposure, mistrust, powerlessness, and worthlessness. It arises when a person perceives that they have failed to live up to social or personal standards, or fears that others will regard them as flawed, inadequate, or dishonourable. Shame is usually distinguished from the related emotion of guilt: in the influential formulation of psychologist Helen B. Lewis, shame concerns the whole self ("I am bad"), while guilt concerns a specific behaviour ("I did something bad").

Psychology

Researchers such as June Price Tangney have found that a disposition to shame (shame-proneness) is linked to withdrawal, avoidance, externalization of blame, and psychological symptoms including depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem, whereas guilt-proneness — when not fused with shame — correlates more with empathy and reparative action. Displays of shame, including gaze aversion, head lowering, and blushing, appear to be recognized across cultures and may function as appeasement signals that acknowledge violated norms. Developmental psychologists place the emergence of shame in the second or third year of life, with the growth of self-awareness. Chronic or toxic shame is a focus of clinical work on trauma, addiction, and eating disorders, popularized by writers such as John Bradshaw and Brené Brown.

Social and cultural aspects

Anthropologist Ruth Benedict famously contrasted "shame cultures," said to regulate behaviour mainly through fear of external disgrace (her example was Japan), with "guilt cultures" governed by internalised conscience. The dichotomy is now considered oversimplified, since shame operates in all societies, but the role of honour and shame codes remains important in the study of Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and other cultures. Shame also has social functions: shaming punishments — from pillories and scarlet letters to modern public denunciations, "naming and shaming" campaigns, and online pile-ons — deliberately harness the emotion to enforce norms, a practice debated by legal scholars and ethicists.

Shame and stigma

Public-health researchers note that shame attached to illness, poverty, addiction, or sexuality can discourage people from seeking help, and campaigns against HIV/AIDS, mental illness, and obesity have explicitly tried to reduce stigma. Conversely, some moralists argue that an appropriate capacity for shame is essential to social life and its erosion a cultural loss.
