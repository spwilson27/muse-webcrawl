Infrasound

Infrasound is sound at frequencies below the lower limit of human hearing, conventionally below about 20 hertz. Although generally inaudible, infrasound can be felt as vibration at high intensities and can travel very long distances with little attenuation, since low-frequency waves lose energy slowly in air and water.

Sources

Natural sources include earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, ocean waves (which generate "microbaroms"), severe weather, avalanches, and meteors. Human-made sources include explosions, large machinery, wind turbines, and jet and rocket engines. The 1883 eruption of Krakatoa produced infrasound that circled the globe several times and was recorded on barometers worldwide.

Detection and uses

Infrasound is monitored by a global network of infrasound stations operated as part of the International Monitoring System of the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty, which listens for atmospheric nuclear explosions; the same arrays detect volcanic eruptions, bolides, and rocket launches. Many animals — including elephants, whales, and pigeons — produce or perceive infrasound, using it for long-distance communication and navigation.

Effects on humans

High-intensity infrasound can cause discomfort, but dramatic effects claimed in popular culture (such as a "brown note") are not supported by evidence. Controlled studies suggest some people report unease in the presence of strong infrasonic tones, a phenomenon explored in experiments on haunted locations and concert demonstrations.
