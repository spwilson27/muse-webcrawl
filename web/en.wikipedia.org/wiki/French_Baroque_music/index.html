French Baroque music

French Baroque music flourished at the court of Louis XIV.

French Baroque music is the music composed in France during the Baroque era, roughly from the early 17th century to the mid-18th century. It is characterised by elaborate ornamentation, dance-derived rhythms, a distinctive overture form, and a close association with the absolute monarchy of Louis XIV, who made music a central instrument of court propaganda and prestige.

History

The style crystallised under Louis XIV, who founded institutions such as the Académie Royale de Musique (1669) and employed Jean-Baptiste Lully, an Italian-born composer who effectively dictated French musical taste for decades. Lully created the tragédie en musique, a five-act operatic form combining overture, recitative modelled on French declamation, airs, choruses and extensive ballet divertissements. His collaboration with librettist Philippe Quinault set the template followed by later composers including Marc-Antoine Charpentier, Marin Marais, Michel Richard Delalande and André Campra.

In the 18th century Jean-Philippe Rameau revolutionised French opera and music theory with works such as Hippolyte et Aricie (1733) and his Traité de l'harmonie (1722). The Querelle des Bouffons of 1752–54, sparked by performances of Italian comic opera in Paris, pitted defenders of the French tradition against advocates of Italian style and foreshadowed the end of the French Baroque.

Characteristics

Distinctive features include notes inégales (the unequal performance of paired notes), a rich vocabulary of ornaments (agréments), the French overture with its dotted, majestic opening and fugal second section, and dance suites built from allemandes, courantes, sarabandes and minuets. Keyboard music, especially for harpsichord, reached a high point in the pièces de clavecin of François Couperin, whose character pieces influenced composers across Europe.

Major composers

  • Jean-Baptiste Lully (1632–1687)
  • Marc-Antoine Charpentier (1643–1704)
  • François Couperin (1668–1733)
  • Marin Marais (1656–1728)
  • Michel Richard Delalande (1657–1726)
  • Jean-Philippe Rameau (1683–1764)
