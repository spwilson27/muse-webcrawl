Reef knot

The reef knot, or square knot, is an ancient and simple binding knot used to secure a rope or line around an object. It is most famously used to "reef" sails — gathering in and tying down part of a sail — and for tying packages, bandages and shoelace bows. The knot is symmetrical, consisting of two overhand knots tied one on top of the other in opposite directions ("right over left, then left over right").

Use and misuse

The reef knot is a binding knot, designed to hold tight against an object under even tension and to be easy to untie. It is notoriously unsafe as a bend — a knot joining two ropes together — because it can capsize and spill under load or when one end is jerked, and it can jam badly in synthetic rope. It should never be used to join ropes in load-bearing or life-critical situations; a proper bend such as the sheet bend should be used instead.

Mistakes in tying produce the inferior granny knot (both halves tied the same way), which slips and twists, or the thief knot, which looks similar but has its ends emerging on opposite sides. The reef knot's proper use in first aid for tying bandage ends is one reason it is taught to scouts worldwide.

History

The knot was known to the ancient Egyptians, Greeks and Romans; the knot's form appears in classical sources, where it was sometimes called the Heraclean knot. Its association with sailing is centuries old, and the name "reef knot" has been in English use since at least the early 19th century.
