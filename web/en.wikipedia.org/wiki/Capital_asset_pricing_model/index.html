Capital asset pricing model

The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) is a model in financial economics describing the relationship between systematic risk and expected return for assets, particularly stocks. It is widely used to estimate the cost of capital and evaluate portfolio performance.

FormulaE(Ri) = Rf + βi (E(Rm) − Rf)
Developed byTreynor, Sharpe, Lintner, Mossin (1960s)

The model

Expected return equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium. Beta (β) measures an asset's sensitivity to market movements: a beta above 1 implies greater volatility than the market. William Sharpe received the Nobel Prize in Economics (1990) partly for this work, building on Harry Markowitz's portfolio theory.

Assumptions

CAPM assumes rational, risk-averse investors, frictionless markets, a single investment period, homogeneous expectations and that investors hold diversified portfolios, so only systematic (non-diversifiable) risk is rewarded.

Criticisms and extensions

Empirical tests found anomalies — size and value effects — leading to multi-factor models such as the Fama–French three-factor model. Critics, notably Richard Roll, questioned whether the true market portfolio is observable. CAPM nevertheless remains a standard textbook tool. See Modern portfolio theory and Beta.
