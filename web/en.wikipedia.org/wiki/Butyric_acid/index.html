Butyric acid

IUPAC nameButanoic acid
FormulaC4H8O2
Molar mass88.11 g/mol
AppearanceColourless liquid, rancid odour
Melting point−7.9 °C
Boiling point163.5 °C

Butyric acid (from Ancient Greek βούτυρον, meaning "butter"), also known under the systematic name butanoic acid, is a straight-chain alkyl carboxylic acid with the chemical formula CH3CH2CH2COOH. It is an oily, colourless liquid with an unpleasant smell — the characteristic odour of rancid butter and of human vomit. Butyric acid is a fatty acid occurring in the form of esters in animal fats and plant oils and is one of the short-chain fatty acids produced by gut bacteria.

Occurrence

Butyric acid is found naturally in butter (at about 3–4% in the form of triglycerides), milk, and parmesan cheese, and is generated when butter goes rancid. It is produced in the human colon by bacterial fermentation of dietary fibre and resistant starch, and it is also found in small amounts in many fermented foods. Certain anaerobic bacteria, including Clostridium butyricum, produce it as a principal fermentation product.

Production

Industrially, butyric acid is manufactured by the oxidation of butyraldehyde, itself obtained from propylene by hydroformylation. It can also be produced by fermentation of sugars using Clostridium species, a route of renewed interest for making the acid from renewable feedstocks.

Uses

Butyric acid is used in the manufacture of butyrate esters: low-molecular-weight esters such as methyl butyrate and ethyl butyrate have pleasant fruity smells and are used as flavourings and perfume additives, in striking contrast to the acid itself. The acid is also a precursor to cellulose acetate butyrate plastics, used in coatings and tool handles, and its salts (butyrates) are sold as animal-feed supplements and nutritional supplements.

Biological role

Butyrate is the preferred energy source of colonocytes, the cells lining the colon, supplying a large share of their fuel. It acts as a histone deacetylase inhibitor, influencing gene expression, and has anti-inflammatory effects in the gut; diminished butyrate production is associated with inflammatory bowel conditions. Butyrate also signals through receptors such as GPR41, GPR43, and GPR109A, and participates in regulating appetite and insulin sensitivity.

History

Butyric acid was first observed in impure form by Liebig and Pelouze in 1843 and was isolated from butter by Liebig in 1844; its name derives from the Latin butyrum, butter.
