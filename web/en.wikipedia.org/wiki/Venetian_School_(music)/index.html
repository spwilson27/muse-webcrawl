Venetian School (music)

In music history, the Venetian School was a group of composers working in Venice from about 1550 to around 1610. They were at the forefront of the transition from the Renaissance style to the Baroque. The school is most famous for the development of polychoral music — works written for two or more separated groups of singers and players (cori spezzati) that exploited the unusual acoustics and architecture of St Mark's Basilica.

History

The Flemish composer Adrian Willaert, appointed maestro di cappella of St Mark's in 1527, is considered the founder of the school. His successors included Cipriano de Rore, Gioseffo Zarlino, Claudio Merulo, Andrea Gabrieli and his nephew Giovanni Gabrieli, whose elaborate works for multiple choirs, brass and organ represent the culmination of the style. Claudio Monteverdi, who became maestro di cappella in 1613, carried Venetian practice into the early Baroque.

Style and influence

Venetian composers developed the polychoral motet, the concertato style contrasting groups of voices and instruments, and rich instrumental writing, including early independent parts for brass and strings. Venetian music publishing, centred on firms such as those of Gardano and Scotto, spread the style throughout Europe, and composers such as Hans Leo Hassler travelled to Venice to study with the Gabrielis, carrying the style to Germany.
