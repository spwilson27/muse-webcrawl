Fermentation in food processing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In food processing, fermentation is the conversion of carbohydrates to alcohol or organic acids by microorganisms — yeasts, bacteria, and moulds — under anaerobic or partially anaerobic conditions. One of humanity's oldest food technologies, fermentation preserves perishable foods, creates distinctive flavours and textures, and can improve nutritional value and digestibility.

Types and products

Alcoholic fermentation by Saccharomyces yeasts produces beer, wine, and spirits. Lactic acid fermentation by lactobacilli and relatives yields yogurt, cheese, sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, and sourdough bread, whose acidity suppresses spoilage organisms. Acetic acid fermentation gives vinegar. Moulds and mixed cultures produce soy sauce, miso, tempeh, and blue cheeses; combined fermentations produce kefir, kombucha, and lambic beer.

History and science

Fermented foods date back thousands of years — evidence for fermented beverages exists from Neolithic China and Mesopotamia. The process was long attributed to a mysterious life force until Louis Pasteur demonstrated in the 1850s–60s that fermentation is caused by living microorganisms ("life without air"), founding modern food microbiology. Modern industry uses selected starter cultures for consistency and food safety, while traditional and artisanal ferments rely on wild, spontaneous microbial communities.

Health and safety

Fermentation can break down lactose and phytates, synthesise B vitamins, and deliver live microbes marketed as probiotics, though evidence for specific health benefits varies by product. Controlled fermentation is generally safe; uncontrolled ferments carry risks such as botulism or biogenic amines, managed in industry by hygiene, acidity, and salt control.
