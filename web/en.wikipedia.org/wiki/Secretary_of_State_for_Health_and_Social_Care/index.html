Secretary of State for Health and Social Care

The Secretary of State for Health and Social Care, informally the Health Secretary, is a senior minister of the Crown in the Government of the United Kingdom, responsible for the Department of Health and Social Care and for the National Health Service in England. The office is one of the most prominent in the cabinet: its holder oversees the largest single item of British public spending and the country's largest employer.

Responsibilities

The Health Secretary sets the strategic direction and budget of NHS England, answers to Parliament for the performance of the health service, and is responsible for public health, adult social care policy in England, and the department's arm's-length bodies, which include NHS England, the UK Health Security Agency, the Care Quality Commission, and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). Health is a devolved matter, so the secretary's remit extends only to England; Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland have their own health ministers, though UK-wide coordination became unusually prominent during the COVID-19 pandemic.

History of the office

The post descends from the Minister of Health, created in 1919 when the new Ministry of Health absorbed the Local Government Board and the health functions of several other bodies. In 1968 the ministry was merged with social security into the Department of Health and Social Security (DHSS) under a Secretary of State; the DHSS was split in 1988 into separate health and social security departments. The title "and Social Care" was added in January 2018 by Theresa May, when Jeremy Hunt — the longest-serving modern holder of the office (2012–2018) — retained it, formally recognising the department's adult social care remit.

Memorable holders include Aneurin Bevan, as Minister of Health the founder of the NHS in 1948, and, in the pandemic era, Matt Hancock (2018–2021) and his successor Sajid Javid. The office has often been regarded as both a proving ground for future leaders and a political liability, owing to the NHS's permanent salience with voters.
