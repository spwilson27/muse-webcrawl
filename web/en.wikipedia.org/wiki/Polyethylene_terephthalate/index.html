Polyethylene terephthalate

Polyethylene terephthalate (or poly(ethylene terephthalate), abbreviated PET, PETE, or the obsolete PETP/PET-P) is the most common thermoplastic polymer resin of the polyester family. It is used in fibres for clothing (where it is known as polyester), containers for liquids and foods, thermoforming for manufacturing, and in combination with glass fibre for engineering resins. In most of the world, PET is produced mainly for synthetic fibres and for bottle production.

Properties

PET is a hard, stiff, strong and dimensionally stable material that absorbs very little water. It has good gas barrier properties and good chemical resistance except to alkalis. Its melting point is about 260 °C and its glass transition temperature is around 70–80 °C. PET may be produced in amorphous (transparent) or semi-crystalline (white or opaque) forms depending on processing and thermal history. It bears the resin identification code 1.

Production

PET is produced from ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid (or dimethyl terephthalate) by polycondensation. It was first developed in 1941 by John Rex Whinfield and James Tennant Dickson at the Calico Printers' Association in the United Kingdom, where it was marketed as Terylene; DuPont developed the same polymer in the United States as Dacron. The two-dimensional film form, biaxially oriented PET, is sold as Mylar and Melinex and is used in packaging, insulation, and magnetic tape.

Applications and recycling

The best-known use of PET is the plastic bottle for water and soft drinks, produced by stretch blow moulding. PET is one of the most widely recycled plastics: collected bottles are washed, flaked, and remade into fibre, strapping, sheet, or new food-grade containers. Because repeated melt processing degrades the polymer, much recycled PET is downcycled into textiles and other products.
