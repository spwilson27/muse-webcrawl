Epic fantasy is a subgenre of fantasy defined by the epic nature of its setting, themes and plot. It is frequently treated as synonymous with high fantasy, a term popularised by Lloyd Alexander in 1971 to describe fantasy set in an entirely fictional ("secondary") world, as distinct from "low fantasy" set in the real world with magical intrusions. Epic fantasy typically concerns a struggle between good and evil whose outcome decides the fate of a world, pursued through long quests by large casts of characters across multi-volume series.

Characteristics

Epic fantasies are set in detailed invented worlds with their own histories, geographies, peoples, languages and systems of magic; maps and appendices are common. The hero is often an apparently ordinary person — a farm boy, an orphan, a hobbit — who is drawn into events of world-historical importance, a pattern associated with the monomyth and the coming-of-age story. Narratives unfold at great length, frequently across trilogies or longer sequences, with multiple points of view. Themes include the corrupting nature of power, sacrifice, providence, and the passing of an age.

History

The subgenre descends from epic poetry such as the Epic of Gilgamesh, the Iliad and Beowulf, from medieval romance, and from the fairy-tale and fantasy traditions of William Morris and Lord Dunsany. Its modern form was established by J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) and The Lord of the Rings (1954–55). The commercial success of Tolkien's imitators in the 1970s — including Terry Brooks's The Sword of Shannara (1977) and Stephen R. Donaldson's Chronicles of Thomas Covenant — consolidated epic fantasy as the dominant commercial form of the genre.

Later authors expanded its scope and complexity: Ursula K. Le Guin's Earthsea books, Robert Jordan's The Wheel of Time (1990–2013), George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire, Steven Erikson's Malazan Book of the Fallen, and the works of Brandon Sanderson. Since the 2000s the subgenre has diversified in voice and setting, drawing on non-European mythologies, and has reached mass audiences through screen adaptations such as The Lord of the Rings film trilogy and Game of Thrones.

Relationship to other genres

Epic fantasy contrasts with sword and sorcery, which centres on personal stakes and sword-wielding heroes in shorter, more episodic tales, and with grimdark, which retains epic scale but rejects moral clarity. Its worldbuilding approach overlaps with science fiction, and the boundary between the two is debated; some works, like Anne McCaffrey's Dragonriders of Pern, combine both.

Notable examples

  • The Lord of the Rings by J. R. R. Tolkien
  • The Wheel of Time by Robert Jordan (completed by Brandon Sanderson)
  • A Song of Ice and Fire by George R. R. Martin
  • The Belgariad by David Eddings
  • The Riftwar Saga by Raymond E. Feist
  • The Stormlight Archive by Brandon Sanderson
  • Earthsea by Ursula K. Le Guin
