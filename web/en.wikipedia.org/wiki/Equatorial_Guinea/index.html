Equatorial Guinea

Equatorial Guinea, officially the Republic of Equatorial Guinea, is a country on the west coast of Central Africa, with an area of about 28,000 square kilometres (11,000 sq mi). It consists of a mainland region, Río Muni, bordered by Cameroon and Gabon, and five inhabited islands, of which Bioko — where the capital Malabo is located — is the largest. A new capital, Ciudad de la Paz (formerly Oyala), is under construction on the mainland. Formerly the colony of Spanish Guinea, it gained independence from Spain in 1968.

History

The mainland was colonised by Spain in the 19th century; the islands had earlier Portuguese and briefly British involvement. The first post-independence president, Francisco Macías Nguema, ruled as a brutal dictator until he was overthrown in 1979 by his nephew Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo, who remains president and is among the world's longest-serving leaders.

Economy and demographics

The discovery of large offshore oil reserves in the 1990s transformed the economy, giving Equatorial Guinea one of Africa's highest GDPs per capita, though wealth is very unevenly distributed and most of the population of roughly 1.7 million lives in poverty. The country is unusual in Africa for having Spanish as an official language (alongside French and Portuguese). The population is predominantly Fang, with Bubi people native to Bioko; Roman Catholicism is the dominant religion.
