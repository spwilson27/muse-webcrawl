Mormon pioneers

The Mormon pioneers were members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS Church), also known as Latter Day Saints, who migrated beginning in the mid-1840s across the United States from the Midwest to the Salt Lake Valley in what is today the US state of Utah. The journey, undertaken under conditions of religious persecution, is remembered as a defining episode in Mormon and Utah history.

Background

After the murder of church founder Joseph Smith in 1844 and escalating conflict in Illinois, church leaders under Brigham Young decided to remove the Latter-day Saints beyond the United States frontier. In February 1846 the first companies crossed the frozen Mississippi River from Nauvoo, Illinois, and established Winter Quarters in present-day Omaha, Nebraska.

The trek west

On April 5, 1847, the vanguard company under Brigham Young set out from Winter Quarters; the party of 148 reached the Salt Lake Valley on July 24, 1847 — a date commemorated in Utah as Pioneer Day. Over the following two decades, roughly 70,000 Latter-day Saints travelled the Mormon Trail by wagon and, from 1856, many by handcart. The Willie and Martin handcart companies of 1856 suffered severe losses when caught by early winter storms in Wyoming, the deadliest disaster of the migration.

Legacy

The pioneers founded Salt Lake City and a chain of settlements across the Intermountain West in the region they called Deseret. Their arrival is commemorated by the This Is the Place Monument, reenactments, trek experiences for youth, and numerous monuments along the Mormon Pioneer National Heritage Area.
