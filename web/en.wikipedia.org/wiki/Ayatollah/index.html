Ayatollah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ayatollah (Persian: آیت‌الله, from Arabic āyat Allāh, “sign of God”) is a high rank in the Twelver Shia ulama, conferred informally by consensus of scholars and students on senior jurists who have reached the level of ijtihad (independent legal reasoning) and teach advanced seminarians. Above it stands the rank of Grand Ayatollah, whose holders may serve as marja' al-taqlid — “sources of emulation” followed by lay believers, who pay religious taxes (khums) that fund the seminaries and clerical networks, largely independent of states.

The title's political significance became global after Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini led the Iranian Revolution of 1979 and became Supreme Leader of the Islamic Republic of Iran under the doctrine of velayat-e faqih (guardianship of the jurist). Notable marja's, such as Ali al-Sistani in Najaf, have by contrast kept to a quieter political role while intervening at decisive moments. The title is honorific, not conferred by examination or appointment, and its use is regulated only by scholarly convention and (in Iran) by political sensitivity — a fluidity visible in the rapid promotion of Ali Khamenei to ayatollah (and marja' status, disputed by many) on becoming Supreme Leader in 1989.
