Luminosity function

The term luminosity function is used in several fields of science for the number of objects per unit luminosity interval.

In astronomy

In astronomy, a luminosity function gives the number of stars or galaxies per unit luminosity interval — for example, the number of galaxies with absolute magnitude between M and M + dM. It is a key observable describing the population of a system and its evolution.

The galaxy luminosity function is commonly described by the Schechter function (Paul Schechter, 1976), which combines a power law at faint luminosities with an exponential cut-off above a characteristic luminosity L*. The stellar luminosity function in a star cluster is closely related to the initial mass function, and the globular cluster luminosity function is approximately Gaussian.

Luminosity functions are used to estimate the total light (and, with a mass-to-light ratio, the total mass) of galaxies and clusters, and to trace star formation and galaxy evolution across cosmic time.

In photometry

In photometry, "luminosity function" (also called the luminous efficiency function, V(λ)) describes the average spectral sensitivity of human visual perception to light of different wavelengths. The CIE 1924 photopic luminosity function peaks at 555 nm (green light) and underlies the definitions of the lumen and candela. A separate scotopic function, V′(λ), peaking at 507 nm, describes night vision; the shift between them is the Purkinje effect.
