Ultrasuede

Ultrasuede is the trade name for a synthetic ultra-microfiber fabric invented in 1970 by Dr. Miyoshi Okamoto, a scientist at the Japanese company Toray Industries. It is often described as an artificial substitute for suede leather; typical Ultrasuede is a non-woven material made of about 80% polyester (or recycled polyester) microfiber and 20% polyurethane, with a soft, brushed surface resembling natural suede.

Type: Synthetic microfiber fabric
Invented: 1970
Manufacturer: Toray Industries
Composition: ~80% polyester, 20% polyurethane

Development

Okamoto's work on microfibres at Toray led in 1970 to the product sold in Italy as Alcantara (produced with ENI) and elsewhere as Ultrasuede. The fabric combines extremely fine split fibres with a polyurethane binder, producing a material that is soft, drapable, breathable, washable and more uniform and stain-resistant than animal suede.

Uses

Ultrasuede is used in fashion (famously by designer Halston in the 1970s), footwear, upholstery and automotive interiors, sports gloves, jewellery pouches, and covers for electronics. Its durability and ease of care made it a standard for high-end furniture and car seats. Toray markets related grades including versions made partly from plant-derived and recycled raw materials, and the fabric remains a flagship example of microfibre technology.
