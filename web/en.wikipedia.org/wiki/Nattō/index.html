Nattō (Japanese: 納豆) is a traditional Japanese food made from whole soybeans fermented with the bacterium Bacillus subtilis var. natto. It is characterised by a strong smell, a distinctive savoury flavour, and a sticky, stringy texture: stirring the beans produces long mucilaginous threads. Nattō is most commonly eaten for breakfast over hot rice, seasoned with soy sauce, karashi mustard, and chopped spring onions.

History

The origins of nattō are obscure; one popular legend attributes it to the samurai Minamoto no Yoshiie in the 11th century, whose troops' boiled soybeans, stored in straw, fermented en route. Straw naturally harbours B. subtilis, and nattō was traditionally produced by packing steamed soybeans in rice straw. Commercial production using pure cultures began in the early 20th century, and the Mito area of Ibaraki Prefecture became its best-known centre.

Nutrition and reception

Nattō is rich in protein, vitamin K2, and the enzyme nattokinase, and is widely regarded in Japan as a health food associated with longevity. Its pungent ammonia-like odour and slimy texture make it polarising: it is historically more popular in eastern Japan (Kantō and Tōhoku) than in the west, and surveys regularly place it among the foods foreign visitors find most challenging. Annual consumption in Japan nevertheless amounts to hundreds of thousands of tonnes.
