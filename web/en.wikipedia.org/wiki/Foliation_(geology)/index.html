Foliation (geology)

FieldStructural geology / petrology
DefinitionPlanar arrangement of mineral grains or structural features in a rock
Typical rocksSlate, schist, gneiss

Foliation in geology refers to the repetitive layering or planar fabric in metamorphic rocks. Each layer can be as thin as a sheet of paper or over a metre thick. The word is derived from the Latin folium, meaning "leaf", and describes the tendency of such rocks to split along parallel planes.

Formation

Foliation develops when a rock is shortened along one axis during metamorphism. Under directed pressure, platy minerals such as mica and elongate minerals such as amphibole rotate or regrow so their long axes align perpendicular to the direction of maximum compression. Processes include mechanical rotation, pressure solution, and the preferential nucleation and growth of new minerals in the direction of least stress.

Types

Examples include slaty cleavage in slate, which allows the rock to be split into thin sheets; schistosity in schist, defined by visible aligned mica grains; and gneissic banding, in which light and dark minerals segregate into distinct layers. Foliation is usually distinguished from the original sedimentary bedding, which may survive as a separate fabric, and from joints and faults, which are fractures rather than fabrics.
