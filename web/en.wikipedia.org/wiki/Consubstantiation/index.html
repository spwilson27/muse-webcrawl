Consubstantiation

Consubstantiation
TraditionAssociated (contested) with Lutheran theology
SubjectEucharistic theology; the real presence of Christ
Contrasted withTransubstantiation, memorialism

Consubstantiation is a Christian theological doctrine that (like transubstantiation) describes the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist. It holds that during the sacrament, the substance of the body and blood of Christ are present alongside the substance of the bread and wine, which remain present. It was part of the doctrines of Lollardy, and it was commonly, though erroneously, associated with the teachings of Martin Luther and the Lutheran churches. Lutherans themselves generally reject the label, preferring to speak of the "sacramental union" of the risen body and blood of Christ with the bread and wine.

History

In England, the doctrine was associated with the Lollard followers of John Wycliffe in the 14th and 15th centuries. The term gained currency during the Reformation debates over the mode of Christ's presence in the sacrament. Roman Catholic critics of Luther attributed consubstantiation to him by analogy with transubstantiation: where Catholic doctrine held that the substance of bread is converted into Christ's body (the accidents remaining), the attributed Lutheran view held that the substance of bread coexists with the substance of Christ's body.

Lutheran position

Luther and the Lutheran confessions taught the true, substantial presence of Christ's body and blood "in, with, and under" the forms of bread and wine — a phrase popularly used to summarise the doctrine, though not itself a confessional formula. The Formula of Concord (1577) affirms the sacramental union while declining to explain the mode of presence philosophically. Anglican and Methodist theologians have at times used consubstantiation more favourably; the doctrine of the real presence in some Anglican divines resembles it.

Analogy

A classic analogy used by proponents is that of a red-hot iron: the iron and the heat (or fire) are united in one glowing mass, yet the iron remains iron and the fire remains fire — two substances present together without either being converted into the other. This contrasts with the Aristotelian substance-and-accidents framework used in the doctrine of transubstantiation defined at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and reaffirmed at the Council of Trent.
