Warsaw Convention

The Warsaw Convention is an international convention which regulates liability for international carriage of persons, luggage or goods performed by aircraft for reward. Formally the Convention for the Unification of Certain Rules Relating to International Carriage by Air, it was signed in Warsaw on 12 October 1929 and came into force in 1933.

Provisions

The Convention established uniform rules on the documents of carriage (passenger tickets, baggage checks and air waybills) and created a regime of presumed carrier liability for death or injury of passengers, destruction or loss of baggage and cargo, and damage caused by delay. Crucially, it limited that liability: for passenger death or injury the limit was originally set at 125,000 Poincaré francs (about US$8,300 at the time), unless the carrier had been guilty of wilful misconduct.

Amendments

The original limits came to be seen as far too low. The Hague Protocol (1955) doubled the passenger limit to 250,000 francs and simplified documentation. Later instruments — the Guadalajara Convention (1961), the Guatemala City Protocol (1971, never in force) and the four Montreal Protocols (1975) — modified cargo and documentation rules. In the United States, an intercarrier agreement in 1966 raised limits for flights touching the US to US$75,000.

Montreal Convention

In 1999 the Montreal Convention consolidated and modernised the Warsaw system, introducing a two-tier, effectively unlimited liability regime for passenger death or injury (with strict liability up to a first tier expressed in Special Drawing Rights) and updating limits for baggage, cargo and delay. It has been ratified by the great majority of states and has largely superseded the Warsaw Convention among its parties, though the older treaty still governs carriage between states that have not joined the Montreal regime.
