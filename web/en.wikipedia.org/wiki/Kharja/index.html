Kharja

A kharja (from Arabic kharja, "final"; Spanish: jarcha) is the closing refrain of the muwashshah, a strophic poem form that developed in al-Andalus (Islamic Iberia). Although the body of a muwashshah was written in classical Arabic or Hebrew, the kharja was often composed in colloquial Andalusi Arabic or in Andalusi Romance (Mozarabic). These short verses — some dating to the 11th century — are among the earliest known lyric poetry in a Romance language and predate most other surviving Romance verse.

Form and themes

The kharja typically consists of two to six short lines placed in the mouth of a persona different from the main speaker of the poem — most often a young woman lamenting an absent lover, frequently addressing her mother or sisters with the cry ya mamma. The shift of voice and register at the poem's end gives the muwashshah a dramatic, song-like climax; the kharja often appears to be a pre-existing popular refrain around which the courtly poem was built.

Discovery and significance

Although Hebrew muwashshahat with Romance kharjas had been noted earlier, scholarly recognition of the corpus came in 1948, when S. M. Stern published Romance kharjas preserved in Arabic manuscripts. Some sixty or more kharjas are now known. Because they mix Arabic script with Romance words, their transcription and interpretation remain contested, but their importance is widely acknowledged: they push back the documented history of Iberian lyric, link the Andalusi tradition to the later Galician-Portuguese cantigas de amigo and Castilian villancicos, and figure in debates about the origins of European troubadour poetry.

Example

One of the best-known kharjas, attributed to the circle of the 11th-century poet Yusuf al-Katib, begins in Romance: "Tant' amare, tant' amare, / habib, tant' amare! / Enfermiron welios nidios / e dolen tan male" — "So much loving, so much loving, beloved, so much loving! Bright eyes have sickened and hurt so much."
