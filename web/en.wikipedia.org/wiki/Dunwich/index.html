Dunwich

Dunwich is a village and civil parish in the East Suffolk district of Suffolk, England, on the North Sea coast. In the Anglo-Saxon and medieval periods Dunwich was a major port and one of the largest towns in England — possibly the capital of the Kingdom of East Anglia in the seventh century — but coastal erosion and a series of great storms, beginning with the storm surge of 1286 and continuing in 1328 and 1347, destroyed its harbour and much of the town.

History

Dunwich was recorded in the Domesday Book of 1086 as a substantial borough with several churches. At its height it had as many as eight parish churches, two monasteries, a mint, and a large fleet. As the sea consumed the cliffs, the town dwindled; despite its decay it continued to return two Members of Parliament until the Reform Act 1832, making it one of the notorious "rotten boroughs". Buildings lost to the sea include all but one of its churches — the last, All Saints', fell over the cliff in the early twentieth century — and the ruins of the Greyfriars friary and a leper chapel survive on the cliff top.

Today Dunwich is a small village popular with visitors for its beach, heathland (a National Nature Reserve), the Dunwich Museum, and the legend of church bells ringing beneath the sea. Offshore surveys have mapped the submerged streets and church ruins of the medieval city on the seabed.
