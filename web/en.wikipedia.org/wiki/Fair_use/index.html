Fair use

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fair use is a doctrine in United States copyright law that permits limited use of copyrighted material without permission from the rights holder, for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship and research. Codified in § 107 of the Copyright Act of 1976 from common-law roots (Justice Story in Folsom v. Marsh, 1841), it is decided case by case on four factors: (1) the purpose and character of the use (including commerciality and “transformativeness”); (2) the nature of the work; (3) the amount used; (4) the effect on the work's market.

Leading cases mark its reach: Campbell v. Acuff-Rose (1994) protected commercial parody (2 Live Crew's “Pretty Woman”) and enthroned transformative use; Authors Guild v. Google (2d Cir. 2015) upheld mass book digitisation for search and snippets; Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023) narrowed the first factor where the secondary use shares the original's purpose and commercial character. Most other countries use narrower, enumerated “fair dealing” exceptions (UK, Commonwealth) or statutory lists (EU), making fair use a distinctly American flexibility — exported in debates over AI training data, where its application remained contested in the mid-2020s.
