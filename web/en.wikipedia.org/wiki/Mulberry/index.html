Mulberries are the fruit of trees in the genus Morus, a group of flowering plants in the family Moraceae. The genus comprises roughly 10 to 16 species of deciduous trees commonly known as mulberries, growing wild and under cultivation in many temperate regions of the world. The best-known species include the white mulberry (Morus alba), the black mulberry (Morus nigra), and the red mulberry (Morus rubra).

Description

Mulberry trees are small to medium-sized, typically growing 10–15 metres (30–50 feet) tall. The leaves are alternately arranged, simple, and often lobed, with a serrated margin. The fruit is a multiple fruit, 2–3 centimetres (0.8–1.2 in) long, composed of many small drupes clustered around a central stem. Immature fruits are white, green, or pale yellow; as they ripen they turn pink or red and finally dark purple or black in most species.

Cultivation and uses

The white mulberry is famous as the primary food plant of the silkworm (Bombyx mori), and its cultivation spread along with sericulture from China across Asia and Europe. Mulberry fruit is eaten fresh and is used in jams, jellies, pies, and wines. The fruit contains anthocyanins and other compounds and has been used in traditional medicine. Mulberry wood is durable and is used for furniture and fence posts; in Japan and India, mulberry bark fibre has been used to make paper.

Distribution

Black mulberry is native to southwestern Asia, red mulberry to eastern North America, and white mulberry to central and eastern Asia, particularly China, where it has been cultivated for more than 4,000 years. White mulberry has become naturalised — and is considered invasive — in many parts of North America, where it hybridises with the native red mulberry.
