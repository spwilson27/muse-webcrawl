Dihybrid cross

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dihybrid cross is a mating experiment between two organisms that are identically hybrid for two traits. A hybrid organism is heterozygous, meaning it carries two different alleles at a particular genetic locus. The dihybrid cross is used to demonstrate Gregor Mendel's law of independent assortment, which states that alleles of different genes separate independently during the formation of gametes, provided the genes are unlinked.

Mendel's experiment

Mendel crossed pea plants that differed in two traits: seed shape (round vs. wrinkled) and seed color (yellow vs. green). The first filial (F1) generation was uniformly heterozygous for both genes and showed both dominant traits (round, yellow seeds). When the F1 plants were self-pollinated, the F2 generation showed four phenotypic combinations in the characteristic ratio of 9:3:3:1.

PhenotypeProportion
Round, yellow9/16
Round, green3/16
Wrinkled, yellow3/16
Wrinkled, green1/16

The appearance of the two recombinant phenotype combinations (round green and wrinkled yellow), each at 3/16, demonstrated that the two genes assort independently. If the genes had been completely linked, only the two parental combinations would have appeared.

Punnett square

The cross is usually represented with a 4 × 4 Punnett square, since each heterozygous parent (AaBb) produces four types of gametes (AB, Ab, aB, ab) in equal proportions. The sixteen equally likely zygote combinations yield nine distinct genotypes grouped into the four phenotypes above.

Exceptions

The 9:3:3:1 ratio assumes complete dominance at both loci, no gene interaction, and unlinked genes. If two genes are linked on the same chromosome, parental phenotypes predominate, and the degree of deviation allows construction of genetic linkage maps. Epistasis can modify the ratio into forms such as 9:7, 9:3:4, 12:3:1, or 15:1.
