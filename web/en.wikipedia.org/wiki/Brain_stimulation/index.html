Brain stimulation

Brain stimulation is a group of medical techniques that use electrical, magnetic, or other forms of energy to modulate the activity of neurons in the brain. Brain stimulation therapies are used to treat a range of neurological and psychiatric conditions, including Parkinson's disease, major depression, epilepsy, chronic pain, and obsessive–compulsive disorder. The field encompasses both non-invasive methods applied through the scalp and invasive methods involving surgically implanted electrodes.

Techniques

Non-invasive techniques

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) uses a pulsed magnetic field generated by a coil placed against the scalp to induce electrical currents in underlying cortical tissue. Repetitive TMS (rTMS) was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2008 for treatment-resistant depression. Transcranial direct-current stimulation (tDCS) and transcranial alternating-current stimulation (tACS) deliver weak electrical currents through scalp electrodes and are widely used in research on cognition and rehabilitation. Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), the oldest brain stimulation treatment still in use, induces a brief controlled seizure under general anesthesia and remains highly effective for severe depression and catatonia.

Invasive techniques

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) involves the surgical implantation of electrodes into specific deep brain structures, such as the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus, connected to an implanted pulse generator. DBS is an established treatment for advanced Parkinson's disease, essential tremor, and dystonia, and is under investigation for psychiatric disorders. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) delivers pulses to the vagus nerve in the neck and is approved for epilepsy and treatment-resistant depression. Responsive neurostimulation detects abnormal electrical activity and delivers stimulation to abort seizures.

Mechanisms

The mechanisms of brain stimulation are incompletely understood. Acute effects include depolarization or hyperpolarization of neuronal membranes near the stimulation site, while longer-term therapeutic effects are thought to involve changes in synaptic plasticity, network oscillations, and neurotransmitter systems. Stimulation can both excite and inhibit neural activity depending on parameters such as frequency, intensity, and pulse pattern.

History

Electrical stimulation of the nervous system dates to antiquity, when electric fish were applied to treat pain. Modern brain stimulation began with ECT in the 1930s, followed by the development of DBS in the 1980s and 1990s and the introduction of TMS by Anthony Barker and colleagues in 1985.

Risks and side effects

Non-invasive techniques are generally well tolerated; TMS can cause scalp discomfort and, rarely, seizures, while tDCS may cause skin irritation. Invasive techniques carry surgical risks including hemorrhage and infection, and stimulation itself can produce mood, speech, or cognitive side effects that are usually reversible by adjusting parameters.
