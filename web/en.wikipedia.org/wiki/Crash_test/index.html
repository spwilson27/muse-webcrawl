Crash test

PurposeEvaluating vehicle crashworthiness and occupant safety
Common typesFrontal impact, side impact, rollover, offset barrier
Key bodiesNHTSA, IIHS, Euro NCAP, ANCAP
Test devicesAnthropomorphic test devices (crash test dummies)

A crash test is a form of destructive testing used to evaluate the crashworthiness of automobiles and other vehicles, and the safety performance of their restraint systems. In a typical test, a vehicle is propelled into a fixed barrier or another vehicle at a specified speed and angle, while instrumented crash test dummies record accelerations and forces that predict injury risk to occupants.

Types of tests

Regulatory and consumer-information programmes use several standard configurations. Frontal full-width tests drive the vehicle into a rigid barrier; offset tests, such as the moderate-overlap and small-overlap tests popularised by the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS), strike only part of the front structure and are more demanding of the passenger compartment. Side-impact tests use a moving deformable barrier or a pole. Rollover tests, rear-impact (whiplash) tests, and pedestrian-protection tests assess other crash modes.

Rating programmes

The New Car Assessment Programmes (NCAP) — including Euro NCAP, ANCAP, and the US NHTSA programme — publish star ratings that have driven substantial improvements in vehicle safety design. The IIHS confers "Top Safety Pick" awards. Tests measure dummy injury criteria such as the Head Injury Criterion (HIC), chest acceleration, and femur loads, as well as structural intrusion.

History

Systematic crash testing began in the 1930s–1950s with early dummy research (such as "Sierra Sam") and university studies; consumer-oriented testing expanded greatly from the late 1970s onward. Modern testing increasingly complements physical tests with computer simulation and evaluates crash-avoidance technologies such as automatic emergency braking.
