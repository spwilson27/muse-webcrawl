Bound state

In quantum physics, a bound state is a composite of two or more particles whose total energy is less than the sum of the energies of the separated particles, so that the constituents remain confined together indefinitely unless disturbed from outside. Bound states form a discrete spectrum of energy levels, in contrast to the continuous spectrum of unbound (scattering) states.

Examples

Familiar bound states include the electron and proton bound in a hydrogen atom, nuclei bound by the strong nuclear force, molecules bound by chemical bonds, and excitons in semiconductors. In the mathematics of the Schrödinger equation, a bound state corresponds to a square-integrable (normalisable) wavefunction that decays at large distances, associated with a negative energy eigenvalue relative to the ionisation threshold. A potential well of sufficient depth and width in one or three dimensions supports at least one bound state; in three dimensions a minimum strength is required.

Bound states in field theory

In quantum field theory, bound states appear as poles of the S-matrix and are described formally by the Bethe–Salpeter equation. The positivity of a particle's binding energy distinguishes stable bound states from resonances, which are quasi-bound states that eventually decay. Some theories predict exotic bound states such as positronium (an electron–positron pair) and bound states in the continuum, in which a localised state coexists with extended states of the same energy.
