Michaelis–Menten kinetics

Placeholder image: The Michaelis–Menten saturation curve

In biochemistry, Michaelis–Menten kinetics, named after Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten, is the simplest case of enzyme kinetics, describing the rate of enzymatic reactions by relating reaction rate v to the concentration of a substrate [S]. Its formula is given by the Michaelis–Menten equation:

v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])

where Vmax is the maximum rate achieved when the enzyme is saturated with substrate, and Km (the Michaelis constant) is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax. Km is often interpreted as a measure of the enzyme's affinity for its substrate — a low Km indicates high affinity.

History

In their 1913 paper "Die Kinetik der Invertinwirkung," Michaelis and Menten (building on the earlier work of Victor Henri) derived the equation for the action of invertase on sucrose, using the assumption of rapid equilibrium between enzyme and substrate. The more general steady-state treatment, in which the concentration of the enzyme–substrate complex is assumed to be roughly constant during the initial phase of the reaction, was published by G. E. Briggs and J. B. S. Haldane in 1925 and is now the standard derivation.

Derivation and assumptions

The model treats the scheme E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, where E is enzyme, S substrate, ES the enzyme–substrate complex, and P product. Assuming that ES is in a steady state (d[ES]/dt ≈ 0) and that product formation is effectively irreversible in the initial-rate period, algebra yields the equation above, with Km = (k−1 + k2)/k1 and Vmax = k2[E]0. The catalytic rate constant k2 is called kcat or the turnover number.

Determination and applications

Parameters were historically estimated from double-reciprocal (Lineweaver–Burk) plots of 1/v against 1/[S], though modern practice favours non-linear regression because reciprocal plots distort errors. Deviations from Michaelis–Menten behaviour arise with allosteric (sigmoidal) enzymes, multiple substrates, and enzyme inhibition — competitive, non-competitive, and uncompetitive inhibitors each modify the apparent parameters in characteristic ways, a framework fundamental to pharmacology and toxicology.
