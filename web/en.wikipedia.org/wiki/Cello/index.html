The cello (pronounced /ˈtʃɛloʊ/), formally the violoncello, is a bowed string instrument of the violin family. Its four strings are tuned in perfect fifths — C, G, D and A — one octave below the viola. It is played upright between the knees, supported by an endpin, and its music is written mainly in the bass and tenor clefs. The cello's warm, wide-ranging tone, close to the human voice, has made it one of the most popular solo instruments as well as a cornerstone of the orchestra and chamber music.

History

The cello emerged in northern Italy in the early 16th century from the violin family, at first chiefly as a bass continuo instrument (the violoncello meaning "little violone"). Makers such as Andrea Amati and, later, Antonio Stradivari — whose "Batta" and "Davidov" cellos remain famous — standardised its form; Stradivari's smaller "forma B" model became the pattern. In the 18th century cellists like Luigi Boccherini advanced virtuoso technique, and Bach's six Cello Suites (c. 1720) became the instrument's most celebrated solo works. The endpin became standard in the 19th century, improving mobility and projection.

Construction and playing

A cello body is about 121 cm long, built of spruce (top) and maple (back, ribs and neck) with two f-holes, a bridge and a sound post. Players draw the bow across the strings (arco), pluck (pizzicato), or strike with the wood (col legno); left-hand techniques include vibrato, harmonics, and playing in thumb position in the upper register. Famous cellists include Pablo Casals, Mstislav Rostropovich, Jacqueline du Pré and Yo-Yo Ma.

Repertoire

Major concertos include those by Haydn, Schumann, Dvořák, Saint-Saëns, Elgar and Shostakovich. The cello is central to string quartets and piano trios, and pairs of cellos anchor the orchestral string bass line with the double basses.
