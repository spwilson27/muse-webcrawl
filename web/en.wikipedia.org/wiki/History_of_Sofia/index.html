History of Sofia

The history of Sofia, the capital of Bulgaria, spans some 7,000 years, from Neolithic settlements in the Sofia Valley to the Roman city of Serdica, the medieval Bulgarian city of Sredets, five centuries of Ottoman rule, and its selection as capital of the modern Bulgarian state in 1879.

Antiquity

The area of Sofia was settled by the Thracian tribe of the Serdi, who gave their name to Serdica. The city was conquered by Rome under Marcus Licinius Crassus in 29 BCE and became a prosperous Roman municipium, later the capital of the province of Dacia Mediterranea. Serdica was a favourite residence of Emperor Constantine the Great, who reportedly called it "my Rome". The Edict of Serdica, issued by Emperor Galerius in 311 CE, granted toleration to Christians shortly before Constantine's Edict of Milan. Surviving Roman remains include the Rotunda of Saint George, the Church of Saint Sophia (which gave the modern city its name), and parts of the ancient city walls and streets beneath the modern centre.

Serdica was devastated by the Huns in 447 and rebuilt by Justinian I. It was incorporated into the First Bulgarian Empire under Khan Krum in 809.

Middle Ages

Under the name Sredets, the city was an important fortress and administrative centre of the Bulgarian Empire, changing hands repeatedly between Bulgaria and Byzantium. It was held by the Byzantines from 1018 until 1194, when it was restored to the Second Bulgarian Empire. The Boyana Church, on the city's outskirts, contains frescoes from 1259 considered masterpieces of medieval art. In 1382 the city fell to the Ottoman Turks after a long siege.

Ottoman period

Under Ottoman rule, now called Sofia after the Church of Saint Sophia, the city became the seat of the Beylerbey of Rumelia, the governor of Ottoman Europe, and an important trading centre on the route between Belgrade and Constantinople. Mosques such as the Banya Bashi Mosque (1576) were built, and the city had a mixed population of Turks, Bulgarians, Jews, Armenians, and others. Bulgarian national revival activity grew in the 19th century, and the city was liberated by Russian forces during the Russo-Turkish War on 4 January 1878.

Capital of Bulgaria

On 3 April 1879, Sofia was chosen as the capital of the newly autonomous Principality of Bulgaria, in part for its central position in the Bulgarian lands and its ancient history. The city then had about 18,000 inhabitants. It grew rapidly under Prince Alexander of Battenberg and King Ferdinand, acquiring boulevards, the National Assembly building, the Royal Palace, the Alexander Nevsky Cathedral (completed 1912), and Sofia University. The city's motto, "Grows but does not age", reflects this expansion.

Sofia was heavily bombed by Allied aircraft in 1943–44 during the Second World War. Bulgaria fell under communist rule in 1944, and the socialist period brought large housing estates, the monumental Largo complex, and industrialization. After the fall of communism in 1989, Sofia became the political and economic centre of the Republic of Bulgaria, joining the European Union with the country in 2007, and growing into a regional hub for technology and services.
