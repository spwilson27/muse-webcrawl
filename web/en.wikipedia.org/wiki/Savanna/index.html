Savanna

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A savanna is a mixed woodland-grassland ecosystem characterised by trees spaced widely enough that the canopy does not close, allowing continuous grass cover. Savannas cover about a fifth of Earth's land surface, in tropical and subtropical regions of Africa (the largest expanse), South America (the Brazilian cerrado, the Llanos), Australia and Asia.

Savannas exist where rainfall is seasonal and intermediate — too dry for forest, too wet for desert — and are maintained by a balance of rain, fire and herbivory: grasses regrow quickly after burning and grazing, while tree seedlings are cropped back. Their wildlife assemblages are among the most spectacular on Earth — the Serengeti's wildebeest migration, elephants, lions and giraffes in Africa; kangaroos in Australia — and their soils store significant carbon.

Humanity evolved in African savannas, and the biome remains home to hundreds of millions of pastoralists and farmers. It is squeezed today by agricultural conversion, woody encroachment as CO₂ rises, and shifting rainfall — pressures tracked for emblematic reserves by conservation science (see conservation biology and biogeography).
