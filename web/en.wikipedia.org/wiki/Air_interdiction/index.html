Air interdiction

Air interdiction (AI), also known as deep air support, is the use of preventive tactical and strategic aircraft missions against enemy targets that are not in direct contact with friendly ground forces. Its purpose is to destroy, neutralise or delay the enemy's military potential — reinforcements, supplies, vehicles and infrastructure — before it can be brought to bear on friendly forces.

Doctrine

Interdiction targets are typically located deep enough behind the front line that attacking them does not require detailed integration with the fire and movement of friendly forces, distinguishing AI from close air support. Battlefield air interdiction targets targets in the vicinity of the battle area that still affect ongoing operations. Attacks on lines of communication — bridges, rail yards, road convoys and fuel depots — aim to strangle the enemy's logistics, while strikes on second-echelon forces aim to delay or disrupt reinforcements.

History

Interdiction matured in the Second World War: Allied air forces isolated the Normandy battlefield in 1944 through the Transportation Plan against French railways and bridges, and interdiction campaigns were central to fighting in Italy and Burma. In Korea, Operation Strangle attempted to cut communist supply lines with mixed results. In Vietnam, campaigns such as Commando Hunt against the Ho Chi Minh Trail consumed enormous resources against an enemy using dispersed, low-volume logistics. Modern precision-guided munitions, persistent surveillance and stealth have greatly increased the effectiveness of interdiction, as demonstrated in the 1991 Gulf War.
