Motion Picture Association film rating system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The current MPA rating symbols.

The Motion Picture Association film rating system is used in the United States and its territories to rate a motion picture's thematic and content suitability for certain audiences, based on its content. Administered by the Motion Picture Association (MPA, formerly the MPAA) through its Classification and Rating Administration (CARA), the system was introduced on November 1, 1968, replacing the earlier Hays Code of self-censorship.

Current ratings

RatingMeaning
GGeneral audiences — all ages admitted. Nothing that would offend parents for viewing by children.
PGParental guidance suggested — some material may not be suitable for children.
PG-13Parents strongly cautioned — some material may be inappropriate for children under 13.
RRestricted — under 17 requires accompanying parent or adult guardian.
NC-17Adults only — no one 17 and under admitted.

History

MPAA president Jack Valenti devised the system in 1968 with the initial ratings G, M (later GP, then PG), R, and X. The PG-13 rating was introduced on July 1, 1984, following parental concern over films such as Gremlins and Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom; the first film released with the rating was Red Dawn. In 1990 the X rating was replaced by NC-17, in part because X had become associated with pornography and had never been trademarked by the association.

Application

Ratings are advisory rather than legally binding, though most theatre chains enforce the age restrictions for R and NC-17 films, and many newspapers, broadcasters, and retailers decline advertising or shelf space for NC-17 titles — a commercial stigma that leads studios to edit films to avoid it. A film may also be released unrated. Since 2013, rating boxes have included brief descriptors explaining the reason for the rating, such as "Rated R for strong bloody violence and language throughout."
