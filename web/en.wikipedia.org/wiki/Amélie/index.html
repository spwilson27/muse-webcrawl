Amélie (French: Le Fabuleux Destin d'Amélie Poulain, "The Fabulous Destiny of Amélie Poulain") is a 2001 French romantic comedy film directed by Jean-Pierre Jeunet and starring Audrey Tautou in the title role. A whimsical, highly stylised portrait of contemporary Parisian life in Montmartre, it became one of the most commercially successful French-language films ever released internationally and a lasting cultural touchstone.

Plot

Amélie Poulain, a shy and imaginative young waitress at the Café des 2 Moulins, lives a solitary life after a lonely childhood. One evening she discovers, hidden behind a skirting board in her flat, a tin box of childhood treasures left by a boy decades earlier. She resolves to return it anonymously; seeing the now middle-aged owner's joyful reaction, she dedicates herself to discreetly improving the lives of those around her — matchmaking colleagues, defending a bullied grocer's assistant, forging a letter to console a widow, and guiding her reclusive neighbour, a painter with brittle bones who repaints Renoir's Luncheon of the Boating Party every year.

Her own happiness arrives through a trail of photo-booth photographs left by Nino Quincampoix, a young man who collects discarded pictures; after an elaborate cat-and-mouse courtship conducted through notes and intermediaries, Amélie's neighbour persuades her to stop arranging everyone else's life and risk her own, and the two finally meet.

Style and production

The film is distinguished by saturated green, red, and gold colour grading, extensive digital clean-up of Paris locations (removing graffiti and modern clutter), playful voice-over cataloguing characters' likes and dislikes, and a celebrated accordion-and-piano score by Yann Tiersen. Jeunet assembled the stories from anecdotes he had collected since the 1970s; Emily Watson was first cast but withdrew, and the part was rewritten for Tautou.

Reception and legacy

Amélie won four César Awards including Best Film and Best Director, two BAFTAs, and was nominated for five Academy Awards. It grossed over $170 million worldwide on a modest budget, dramatically boosting tourism to Montmartre and the Café des 2 Moulins. Critics praised its charm and visual invention, though some French commentators objected to its picture-postcard, ethnically homogeneous Paris. The film's aesthetic influenced advertising, music videos, and later television; a Broadway musical adaptation opened in 2017.
