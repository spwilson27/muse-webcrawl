The Arithmetica Universalis ("Universal Arithmetic") is a mathematics text by Isaac Newton on algebra, published in 1707. It was edited and published — reportedly without Newton's enthusiasm — by William Whiston, Newton's successor as Lucasian Professor at Cambridge, from Newton's lecture notes as deputy professor.

Contents

The work systematizes algebraic notation, operations, and the theory of equations: rules for manipulating expressions, solving equations, transforming problems from geometry into algebra, and the construction of equations. It states what are now called Newton's identities relating power sums of roots to coefficients, and gives Newton's rule for bounding the number of imaginary roots of a polynomial — an extension of Descartes' rule of signs. Joseph Raphson published an English translation in 1720.

Significance

Drawing on Descartes, Viète, and Wallis, the Arithmetica Universalis helped establish algebra as the "universal arithmetic" of quantities, and it was a standard university text through the 18th century. In it Newton also argued that geometry and algebra should keep distinct roles, famously preferring the geometry of the ancients for constructions.
