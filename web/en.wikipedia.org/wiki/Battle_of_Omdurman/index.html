Battle of Omdurman

The Battle of Omdurman was fought on 2 September 1898 during the Anglo-Egyptian reconquest of Sudan. An army commanded by General Herbert Kitchener decisively defeated the forces of Abdullah al-Khalifa, successor to the Mahdi, outside Omdurman, the Mahdist capital across the Nile from Khartoum. The battle demonstrated the overwhelming firepower of a modern European-style army — Maxim guns, artillery, and gunboats — against a much larger but poorly equipped force.

Background

After the Mahdist state was established in 1885, Britain and Egypt mounted a slow advance up the Nile from 1896, building a railway across the Nubian desert to supply the army. By September 1898 Kitchener's force stood before Omdurman.

The battle

Mahdist attacks across open ground were broken by rifle, machine-gun and artillery fire before they could close. A famous episode was the charge of the 21st Lancers, in which the young Winston Churchill took part as a war correspondent and officer. When Kitchener advanced on the city in the afternoon, a final Mahdist counterattack from the Kerreri hills was repulsed.

Aftermath

Omdurman ended the Mahdist state; the Khalifa fled and was killed the following year at the Battle of Umm Diwaykarat. Sudan came under the Anglo-Egyptian condominium. The treatment of Mahdist wounded after the battle and the desecration of the Mahdi's tomb drew criticism, including from Churchill's account The River War.
