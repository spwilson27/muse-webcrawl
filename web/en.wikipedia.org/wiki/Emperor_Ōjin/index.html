Emperor Ōjin (応神天皇, Ōjin Tennō) was the 15th legendary Emperor of Japan, according to the traditional order of succession. His life and reign are recorded in the eighth-century chronicles Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, which credit him with a reign traditionally dated 270–310 AD, though modern historians generally consider him a legendary or semi-legendary figure, possibly based on a Yamato ruler of the late fourth or early fifth century.

Legendary narrative

According to the chronicles, Ōjin was the son of Emperor Chūai and Empress Jingū. Chūai died before his birth, and Jingū — said to have carried Ōjin in her womb for three years — acted as regent, famously leading an expedition to Korea before returning to give birth in Kyushu; the child was thus named Homutawake, and later Hondawake. On Jingū's death he ascended the throne, ruling from a palace at Karushima in Yamato.

The chronicles credit Ōjin's reign with increased contact with the Korean kingdoms of Baekje and Silla and with the arrival of learned scholars such as Wani, who is traditionally said to have introduced Chinese writing to Japan.

Deification as Hachiman

Ōjin was later deified as Hachiman, the god of war and tutelary deity of warriors, one of the most widely venerated kami in Shinto. Hachiman shrines, numbering in the tens of thousands, include the head shrines at Usa in Ōita and Iwashimizu in Kyoto. His cult merged with Buddhism (as Hachiman Daibosatsu) and he became a protector of Japan and of the Minamoto clan.

Tomb

Ōjin's imperial tomb is traditionally identified with the large keyhole-shaped kofun at Habikino, Osaka Prefecture (the Konda Gobyōyama Kofun), administered by the Imperial Household Agency, though the attribution is not archaeologically verified.
