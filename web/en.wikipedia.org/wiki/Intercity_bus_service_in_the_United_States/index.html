Intercity bus service in the United States

Intercity bus service in the United States comprises scheduled coach services between cities and towns, provided by national networks such as Greyhound Lines and FlixBus, regional carriers, and "Chinatown bus" operators. For much of the 20th century the intercity bus was the dominant form of public transport for small-town and rural America; although ridership fell steeply with the spread of car ownership and cheap air travel, the sector revived in the 2000s with curbside, express services.

History

Bus networks grew rapidly in the 1920s and were consolidated under federal regulation after the Motor Carrier Act of 1935. Greyhound and Trailways became the twin pillars of the industry, serving thousands of communities at mid-century. Deregulation under the Bus Regulatory Reform Act of 1982 let carriers abandon unprofitable rural routes, and networks shrank, though states later subsidized some rural intercity services. In the Northeast, low-cost curbside operators based in Chinatowns pioneered online booking and very low fares from the late 1990s, a model copied by Megabus (from 2006) and BoltBus with "from $1" fares and onboard Wi-Fi.

The modern market

FlixBus entered the US in 2018 and acquired Greyhound in 2021, combining a technology-driven booking platform with the legacy network. Amtrak Thruway buses and regional public operators fill gaps, especially in the West. Intercity buses remain the cheapest intercity mode and the only public service for many rural communities; industry reports put annual trips in the tens of millions, with strong growth on dense corridors such as the Northeast Corridor, Texas Triangle and Florida.
