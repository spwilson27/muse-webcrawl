Gulay-gorod

Gulay-gorod (Russian: гуляй-город, "walking town") was a mobile fortification used by the Russian army from the 16th to the 18th centuries. It consisted of large wooden shields, typically fitted with loopholes for firearms and mounted on carts or sledges, which could be linked together in the field to form a defensive wall.

Description

The shields were prefabricated panels of stout oak boards, each several metres long, pierced with firing ports for arquebusiers, musketeers, and light cannon. Transported by horse-drawn wagons, they could be deployed quickly on open steppe where no natural cover existed, giving infantry and artillery a fortified position against cavalry attack.

History

The gulay-gorod was developed for the wars against the Crimean Tatars and other steppe peoples, whose mounted archers threatened Russian armies operating far from their fortresses. It was used prominently in the 1570s, including in the campaign culminating in the Battle of Molodi in 1572, where a Russian army sheltered behind the mobile walls defeated a much larger Crimean-Tatar force. Similar devices were used in campaigns along the expanding southern frontier lines (the zasechnaya cherta), and elements persisted into the era of Peter the Great.

Comparable devices

The gulay-gorod is analogous to other early modern mobile defences: the Hussite war wagons (wagenburg), the Hungarian and Ottoman wagon laagers, and the mantlets used by Spanish and Italian armies. Like them, it answered the tactical problem of protecting slow-firing infantry and guns from shock cavalry in open terrain.
