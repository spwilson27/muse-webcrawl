Kidney dialysis

Kidney dialysis is the process of removing excess water, solutes and toxins from the blood in people whose kidneys have failed. It substitutes for the filtering function of healthy kidneys and is a form of renal replacement therapy.

Types

In haemodialysis, blood passes through an external dialyser ("artificial kidney") where a semipermeable membrane separates it from dialysate fluid; it is typically done three times a week for about four hours, usually via an arteriovenous fistula. In peritoneal dialysis, the patient's own peritoneum serves as the membrane, with dialysate instilled into the abdomen through a catheter, often performed daily at home.

Indications and outlook

Dialysis is started for end-stage kidney disease or severe acute kidney injury with refractory fluid overload, hyperkalaemia, acidosis or uraemic complications. While life-sustaining, it only partially replaces kidney function; transplantation offers better long-term outcomes for eligible patients.
