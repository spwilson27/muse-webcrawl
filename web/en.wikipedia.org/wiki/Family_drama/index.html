Family drama
GenreDrama (film, television, theatre)
FocusRelationships, conflict and change within families

The family drama is a genre of dramatic storytelling — in film, television, theatre and literature — that centres on the relationships, conflicts and emotional lives of a family. Rather than external action, family dramas typically draw their tension from domestic situations: marriage and divorce, parent–child estrangement, sibling rivalry, inheritance, illness, addiction, and the clash between generations or between tradition and change.

Characteristics

Family dramas are usually realist and character-driven, often set largely in the home and unfolding over significant spans of time as characters age and relationships evolve. Many landmark works of drama belong to the genre, including Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman, Tennessee Williams's The Glass Menagerie and August Wilson's Fences. In cinema, notable examples include Yasujirō Ozu's Tokyo Story (1953), Ingmar Bergman's Fanny and Alexander (1982), and Robert Redford's Ordinary People (1980).

On television

Television family dramas range from long-running domestic serials to prestige series. Examples include Little House on the Prairie, The Waltons, Parenthood, This Is Us, and Six Feet Under, as well as family sagas centred on dynasties and businesses such as Dallas and Succession. In Japan, the long-running asadora morning dramas and home dramas are staples of the format, while Korean family dramas are known for high ratings and long episode counts.
