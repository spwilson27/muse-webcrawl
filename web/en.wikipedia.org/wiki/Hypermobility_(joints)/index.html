Hypermobility (joints)

Hypermobility, also known as double-jointedness, describes joints that stretch farther than normal. It occurs when the tissues holding a joint together, principally ligaments and the joint capsule, are more lax than usual. Joint hypermobility is common, particularly among children and young people, and often causes no symptoms at all; in some people it is associated with joint pain, dislocations, and fatigue.

Hypermobility may affect a single joint, a few joints, or be generalised throughout the body. Generalised joint hypermobility is a feature of several heritable connective tissue disorders, most notably the hypermobile type of Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, as well as Marfan syndrome and osteogenesis imperfecta. It is also common in people with Down syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with hypermobile joints have no problems, and the trait can be an advantage to gymnasts, dancers, musicians, and martial artists. Others experience joint and muscle pain, frequent sprains, subluxations or dislocations, poor proprioception leading to clumsiness, and soft tissue injuries. When hypermobility is accompanied by symptoms in the absence of another connective tissue disorder, it may be diagnosed as a hypermobility spectrum disorder; the older term benign joint hypermobility syndrome is also still used.

Diagnosis

Hypermobility is most often assessed with the Beighton score, a nine-point scale awarding points for being able to bend the little fingers back beyond 90 degrees, touch the thumbs to the forearms, hyperextend the elbows and knees beyond 10 degrees, and place the palms flat on the floor with straight legs. A score of 5 or more in adults (6 or more in children) is commonly taken to indicate generalised joint hypermobility, though thresholds vary with age, sex, and ethnicity. Hypermobility decreases with age, and it is more common in females and in people of Asian and African descent.

Causes

Joint laxity is influenced by the structure of collagen and other proteins in ligaments and tendons, by joint shape, muscle tone, and hormonal factors. It tends to run in families, consistent with a heritable component of connective tissue composition. Acquired hypermobility can also follow injury or result from training, as in ballet dancers and contortionists.

Management

There is no cure for hypermobility itself, but symptoms are managed with physiotherapy aimed at strengthening the muscles around affected joints and improving proprioception, pacing of activity, joint protection techniques, orthotics, and analgesia when needed. People with hypermobility are often advised to keep generally fit and to avoid repeatedly hyperextending joints for display, which may increase injury risk.
