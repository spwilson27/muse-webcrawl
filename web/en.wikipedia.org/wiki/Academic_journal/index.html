An academic journal or scholarly journal is a periodical publication in which scholarship relating to a particular academic discipline is published. They serve as permanent records and forums for the presentation and discussion of research. Academic journals typically contain articles that report original research, review articles that synthesise existing literature, and book reviews. Unlike general magazines, they are written for and by specialists, and their contents are usually subjected to peer review before publication.

History

The first academic journals appeared in 1665: the French Journal des sçavans, edited by Denis de Sallo, and the English Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, edited by Henry Oldenburg, secretary of the Royal Society. These periodicals grew out of the scholarly correspondence networks of the seventeenth century and offered a faster, more public way to announce discoveries and establish priority than books. Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the journal steadily displaced other forms of scientific communication, and by the late twentieth century publishing in refereed journals had become the principal measure of academic productivity and prestige.

Peer review and publication

Most academic journals evaluate submitted manuscripts through peer review, in which the editor sends a paper to independent experts (referees) in the field, who recommend acceptance, revision or rejection and may remain anonymous to the author (single-blind or double-blind review). Accepted articles are edited, assigned bibliographic identifiers such as a Digital Object Identifier (DOI), and published in issues (volumes) of the journal, increasingly online-only and often ahead of print. Journals are ranked informally and through metrics such as the impact factor, which measures average citations to recent articles; such metrics are influential in hiring and funding but are criticised for distorting scholarly incentives.

Economics and open access

Academic publishing is dominated by a small number of commercial publishers (such as Elsevier, Springer Nature and Wiley) alongside university presses and scholarly societies. Because libraries must subscribe to journals their researchers cannot do without, subscription prices rose far faster than inflation in the late twentieth century — the "serials crisis" — while authors and referees generally work unpaid and publishers' profit margins have been a frequent subject of controversy. The open access movement seeks to make research freely available online, through author-pays "gold" open access journals, institutional repositories ("green" open access), and funder mandates such as Plan S. The growth of the internet has also produced predatory journals that charge authors while performing little or no genuine peer review.
