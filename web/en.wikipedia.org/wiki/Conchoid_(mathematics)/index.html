Conchoid (mathematics)

A conchoid is a curve derived from a fixed point, a given curve, and a length. Given a curve, a fixed point called the pole, and a distance, the conchoid is the locus of points lying on lines through the pole that intersect the given curve, at the given distance from the intersection point, on either side of it.

History

The construction is named after the Greek mathematician Nicomedes (c. 280–210 BCE), who used the conchoid of a straight line—now called the conchoid of Nicomedes—to solve the classical problem of trisecting an angle. Nicomedes reportedly designed a mechanical device, a form of marked ruler, for drawing the curve. The name comes from the Greek word for a mussel or conch shell, whose shape the curve resembles.

Conchoid of Nicomedes

When the given curve is a straight line, the resulting curve has two branches separated by the line as an asymptote. In Cartesian coordinates, with the pole at the origin and the line x = a, the curve satisfies (x − a)²(x² + y²) = b²x², where b is the fixed distance. Depending on the relative sizes of a and b, the inner branch may be smooth, have a cusp, or contain a loop.

General conchoids

Conchoids can be formed from any base curve: the conchoid of a circle with the pole on its circumference gives Pascal's limaçon, which includes the cardioid as a special case. Conchoid-like constructions also appear in the theory of linkages and in the design of certain cams.
