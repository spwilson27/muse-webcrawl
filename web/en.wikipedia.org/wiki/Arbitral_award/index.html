Arbitral award

An arbitral award is the decision rendered by an arbitral tribunal at the conclusion of arbitration proceedings. Like a court judgment, a final award determines the parties' rights and obligations and is binding on them; unlike a judgment, it derives its authority from the parties' agreement to arbitrate and from the arbitration law of the seat. Awards may order payment of damages, specific performance, declarations, interest and costs, and are enforceable in most of the world under the New York Convention.

Types of award

Tribunals may render final awards disposing of all issues, partial awards deciding severable questions (such as liability before quantum, or jurisdiction), interim awards granting provisional relief, consent awards recording a settlement so that it becomes enforceable, and default awards where a party fails to participate. In some systems awards on costs are issued separately after the main award.

Form and content

Most arbitration laws and institutional rules require an award to be in writing, signed by the arbitrators (a majority sufficing if the omission of a signature is explained), dated, and to state the seat of arbitration. Unless the parties agree otherwise, the tribunal must give reasons. An award is deemed made at the seat on the date stated. Tribunals may correct clerical or computational errors, interpret an award, or render an additional award on claims presented but not decided, usually only within a short window after issuance, after which the tribunal is functus officio — its mandate exhausted.

Challenge and setting aside

The courts of the seat exercise supervisory jurisdiction and may set an award aside on narrow grounds modelled on Article 34 of the UNCITRAL Model Law: invalid agreement, lack of due process, excess of jurisdiction, irregular tribunal composition or procedure, non-arbitrability, or conflict with public policy. There is ordinarily no review of the merits. In notable departures, a few jurisdictions historically allowed appeal on points of law (England's Arbitration Act 1996, section 69, permits it unless excluded) or expanded review, largely curtailed by decisions such as Hall Street Associates v. Mattel in the United States.

Enforcement

A party may seek recognition and enforcement wherever the losing party holds assets. Under the New York Convention the enforcing court may refuse only on the exhaustive Article V grounds, and recognition rates are high, which is why an arbitral award is generally more portable across borders than a court judgment obtained through litigation. In investor-state dispute settlement under ICSID, awards are enforceable as final domestic judgments in all ICSID contracting states, outside the New York Convention regime.
