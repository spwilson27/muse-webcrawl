Yoshida Kenkō

Yoshida Kenkō (Japanese: 吉田 兼好; c. 1284 – c. 1350), born Urabe Kaneyoshi, was a Japanese author and Buddhist monk of the Kamakura period. His most famous work, Tsurezuregusa (Essays in Idleness, c. 1330–1332), a collection of 243 short essays, anecdotes, and reflections, is one of the most beloved classics of Japanese literature and a defining text of the zuihitsu ("following the brush") essay genre.

Life

Kenkō was born into the Urabe clan, a family of Shinto officials attached to the Yoshida Shrine in Kyoto — hence his later name Yoshida. He served as a chamberlain and guards officer at the court of Emperor Go-Fushimi and was known in court circles as a poet and man of taste. After the death of his sovereign he took Buddhist orders, though stories of a disappointed love — possibly involving a daughter of the regent family — are legendary rather than documented. As a monk he lived in retreat near Kyoto and at Mount Hiei, maintaining friendships with courtiers and warriors alike, and remained a respected arbiter of court tradition and etiquette. He lived through the fall of the Kamakura shogunate and the wars of the Northern and Southern Courts.

Essays in Idleness

The Tsurezuregusa opens by confessing that, having nothing to do, the author sat before his inkstone all day and jotted down "whatever nonsensical thoughts entered his head" — a pose of casual spontaneity that masks a carefully shaped miscellany. Its 243 sections range from aesthetic manifestos to gossip, from Buddhist sermons to practical advice and comic anecdotes. Kenkō's celebrated aesthetic prizes imperfection and impermanence: "Are we to look at cherry blossoms only in full bloom, the moon only when it is cloudless?" Uniformity and completion, he argues, are less moving than suggestion and decay — ideas that link him with the earlier recluse literature of Kamo no Chōmei and anticipate later Japanese aesthetics. At the same time he is nostalgic for the refined old court, sharply funny about pretension, and capable of brisk, practical worldliness.

Legacy

Read and annotated for centuries, the Tsurezuregusa became a fixture of Japanese education and, in translations such as Donald Keene's Essays in Idleness, one of the most widely read premodern Japanese texts abroad. Kenkō is ranked with Chōmei and Sei Shōnagon (author of The Pillow Book) as a supreme master of the Japanese essay.
