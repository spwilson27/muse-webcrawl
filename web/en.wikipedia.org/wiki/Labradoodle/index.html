A Labradoodle (/ˈlæbrəˌduːdəl/) is a crossbreed dog created by crossing a Labrador Retriever and a Standard or Miniature Poodle. The term first appeared in the 1950s, but the cross gained wide popularity in the late 1980s, when Australian breeder Wally Conron of the Royal Guide Dogs Association of Australia produced a litter intended to provide a guide dog suitable for a blind woman whose husband was allergic to dog hair.

History

Conron's 1989 litter produced a puppy, Sultan, whose low-shedding coat passed allergy testing and who went on to work as a guide dog in Hawaii. Conron later publicly expressed regret at having started the "designer dog" craze, noting that many later breeders did not health-test their stock. Multi-generational breeding programmes in Australia produced the developing "Australian Labradoodle," which some breeders have infused with additional breeds such as the Irish Water Spaniel and American and English Cocker Spaniels.

Characteristics

Labradoodles vary greatly in size, coat, and temperament depending on the poodle parent and generation. Coat types are commonly described as hair, fleece, or wool; the latter two are marketed as low-shedding and are often claimed to be better tolerated by allergy sufferers, though no dog is completely hypoallergenic and individual puppies in a litter may differ. The cross is generally energetic, sociable, and trainable, and Labradoodles are used as assistance, therapy, and family dogs.

Health

Like the parent breeds, Labradoodles can be prone to hip and elbow dysplasia, progressive retinal atrophy, and exercise-induced collapse. Responsible breeders screen breeding stock for these conditions.

Recognition

Major kennel clubs such as the American Kennel Club and The Kennel Club (UK) do not recognise the Labradoodle as a breed, though dedicated associations maintain registries and breed standards for multi-generational dogs.
