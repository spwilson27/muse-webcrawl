Autopoiesis

Autopoiesis (from Greek auto "self" and poiesis "creation, production") refers to a system capable of producing and maintaining itself by creating its own parts through a network of processes. The term was introduced by Chilean biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela in the 1970s to define the self-maintaining chemistry of living cells.

Definition

An autopoietic system is organised as a network of processes of production of components that (1) through their interactions continuously regenerate the network that produced them, and (2) constitute the system as a concrete unit in the space in which the components exist. The canonical example is the living cell: the cell membrane is produced by processes within the cell, while those processes occur inside the boundary the membrane creates.

History

Maturana and Varela developed the concept in works including De máquinas y seres vivos (1972) and Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living (1980). Varela later extended the related framework of enactivism in cognitive science. The concept contrasts with allopoiesis, the production of something other than the system itself, as a factory produces cars.

Applications and criticism

Sociologist Niklas Luhmann adapted autopoiesis to social systems theory, treating communication networks as self-reproducing. The concept has also been applied in law, architecture, and artificial life. Critics question whether the definition sharply distinguishes living from non-living systems and whether social applications stretch the term beyond its biological grounding.
