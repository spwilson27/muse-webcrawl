Biopsy

Biopsy
PurposeRemoval and examination of cells or tissue for diagnosis
Common typesCore needle, excisional, incisional, punch, fine-needle aspiration, endoscopic

A biopsy is a medical test commonly performed by a surgeon, interventional radiologist, or interventional cardiologist. The process involves the extraction of sample cells or tissues for examination to determine the presence or extent of a disease. The tissue is generally examined under a microscope by a pathologist and may also be analyzed chemically. When an entire lump or suspicious area is removed, the procedure is called an excisional biopsy; when only a sample of tissue is removed, it is an incisional or core biopsy.

Types

Needle biopsies obtain tissue through a hollow needle and include fine-needle aspiration, which withdraws cells and fluid, and core needle biopsy, which removes a small cylinder of tissue, often guided by ultrasound, CT, or mammography. A punch biopsy uses a circular blade to sample the full thickness of skin. Endoscopic biopsies are taken through instruments passed into the gastrointestinal, respiratory, or urinary tracts. Bone marrow biopsies sample the marrow of the pelvis for hematologic diagnosis, and liquid biopsies analyze circulating tumor DNA in blood.

Analysis

After removal, the specimen is fixed, commonly in formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained—routinely with hematoxylin and eosin—for microscopic examination. Immunohistochemistry, molecular studies, and frozen-section analysis during surgery provide further diagnostic information. Biopsy results are central to the diagnosis and staging of cancer and to distinguishing malignant from benign disease.

History

The word derives from the Greek bios (life) and opsis (a sight). Diagnostic tissue examination grew with nineteenth-century microscopy and cellular pathology, notably the work of Rudolf Virchow, and needle techniques were refined throughout the twentieth century.
