Dandelion

Taraxacum officinale, the common dandelion, is a flowering herbaceous perennial plant in the aster family Asteraceae, native to Eurasia but now naturalised worldwide. Familiar from lawns and roadsides, it has a rosette of toothed leaves (its name derives from the French dent de lion, "lion's tooth"), bright yellow composite flower heads, and wind-borne seeds borne on parachute-like pappi forming the familiar "dandelion clock."

Description and ecology

The plant grows from an unbranched taproot and exudes a milky latex when cut. Its hollow stems bear single flower heads composed of many small ray florets. Most populations reproduce by apomixis — seeds that are clones of the parent — giving rise to hundreds of microspecies. Bees and other insects use dandelions as an early source of nectar and pollen.

Uses

Dandelion greens are eaten as a salad vegetable and cooked green; the flowers are made into wine, the roots into a coffee substitute and herbal preparations. In folk medicine the plant has been used as a diuretic and liver remedy. Gardeners often regard it as a weed, though its deep taproot also breaks up compacted soil.
