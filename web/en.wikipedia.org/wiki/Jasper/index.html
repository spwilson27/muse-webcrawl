Jasper, an aggregate of microgranular quartz and/or cryptocrystalline chalcedony and other mineral phases, is an opaque, impure variety of silica, usually red, yellow, brown or green in colour; and rarely blue. The common colour is red, due to iron(III) inclusions. Jasper develops in veins and cracks in volcanic rocks, often together with chalcedony and agate, and breaks with a smooth surface. It can be highly polished and is used for items such as vases, seals, and snuff boxes.

The density of jasper is typically 2.58 to 2.91, and its refractive index about 1.54. The name jasper comes from an Old French and Latin word for a speckled or spotted stone; jasper was a favourite gem in the ancient world, and its name can be traced back in Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, and Egyptian.

Types

Pattern and colour varieties

Jasper is a highly variable material. Trade and varietal names include picture jasper (with landscape-like banding), ocean jasper from Madagascar, imperial jasper, dalmatian jasper (actually a feldspar-bearing rock), mookaite from Mooka Creek in Western Australia, bloodstone (dark green jasper with red spots of hematite), and brecciated jasper, which consists of angular jasper fragments cemented by chalcedony or quartz.

Basanite is a velvety-black variety of amorphous quartz once used by goldsmiths as a touchstone to test the fineness of gold and silver alloys by the colour of the streak produced.

History

Jasper was known and used in antiquity across the Mediterranean and Near East. The material is mentioned in the epic of Gilgamesh, in descriptions of the breastplate of the High Priest in Exodus, and in the foundations of the New Jerusalem in the Book of Revelation. Minoan seals were carved from jasper around 1800 BC, and jasper intaglios were common in the Roman world. In the ancient world jasper was considered a rain-bringer and a protective amulet; green jasper was used by the Egyptians and in ancient Babylon for amulets.

In the 19th century, imperial jasper objects were carved in the Russian imperial lapidary works at Peterhof, Kolyvan, and Yekaterinburg, including the famous giant jasper vases of the Hermitage.

Jaspilite

Jaspilite is a banded iron formation rock characterised by alternating bands of jasper (red chert) and hematite or magnetite. Banded iron formations of this type, formed in the Precambrian oceans around 2.4–1.8 billion years ago, are among the most important sources of iron ore in the world, mined extensively in Australia, Brazil, and the Lake Superior region.

Picture jaspers

Picture jaspers exhibit combinations of patterns — flow patterns, dendritic inclusions, and banding — resulting from the petrification of mud or ash deposits and subsequent mineralisation, resulting in patterns resembling landscapes or pictures. Noted deposits include Bruneau jasper from Idaho and Oregon, and the porcelain jaspers of the western United States.
