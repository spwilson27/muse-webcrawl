Harvester Judgment

The Harvester Judgment (Ex parte H.V. McKay [1907]) was a landmark decision of the Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration of Australia, delivered by Justice H. B. Higgins. The case established the concept of a basic wage or "living wage" for unskilled labourers in Australia, calculated as the minimum needed to support a man, his wife, and three children in "frugal comfort".

McKay's Sunshine Harvester works, whose wage rates were at issue in the case.

Background

The Excise Tariff Act 1906 imposed an excise on agricultural machinery, remitted for manufacturers who paid their employees "fair and reasonable" wages. Hugh Victor McKay, maker of the Sunshine Harvester, applied for a declaration that his wages were fair and reasonable, and the case came before Higgins as president of the Arbitration Court.

Decision

Higgins rejected wage rates based merely on an employer's capacity to pay or on market bargaining. Instead, he reasoned that the first charge on industry must be a living wage covering "the normal needs of the average employee, regarded as a human being living in a civilised community". For an unskilled labourer he fixed the figure at seven shillings per day (42 shillings per week), based on evidence about the cost of food, housing, clothing and other necessities for a family of about five.

Legacy

Although the enabling tariff legislation was later struck down by the High Court in R v Barger (1908), the Harvester standard endured as the foundation of Australia's centralised wage-fixing system for some sixty years. It shaped the basic wage component of Australian awards until the shift toward total wage concepts in the 1960s, and remains a touchstone in debates about minimum wages.
