Cordite

Type: Smokeless propellant

Place of origin: United Kingdom

Inventors: Frederick Abel and James Dewar (1889)

Principal ingredients: Nitrocellulose, nitroglycerin, petroleum jelly

Cordite is a family of smokeless propellants developed and produced in the United Kingdom from 1889 to replace black powder as a military propellant. Like modern gunpowders, cordite is classified as a low explosive because of its slow deflagration rather than detonation. Pressed into long cords — hence the name — it was the standard propellant for British and Commonwealth small arms and artillery through both world wars.

History

In 1889 the chemists Sir Frederick Abel and Sir James Dewar, working for a government committee on explosives, patented a gelatinized mixture of guncotton (nitrocellulose), nitroglycerin, and petroleum jelly (mineral jelly), extruded into cords and dried with acetone as a solvent. Their patent was contested by Alfred Nobel, whose similar propellant ballistite (patented 1887) used camphor; the ensuing British lawsuit was lost by Nobel largely on the wording of his claims. The original formulation, Cordite Mk I (58% nitroglycerin, 37% guncotton, 5% petroleum jelly), proved erosive to gun barrels and was replaced by Cordite MD ("modified", 1901), which reversed the proportions — roughly 65% guncotton, 30% nitroglycerin, 5% petroleum jelly — reducing barrel wear and flash.

Use

Cordite was manufactured at government factories such as the Royal Gunpowder Factory at Waltham Abbey and, during the First World War, at the vast HM Factory, Gretna, which employed thousands of workers — many of them women — to supply artillery charges. It propelled the .303 British rifle cartridge and a wide range of naval and field artillery shells; in the Second World War further variants (including solventless and flashless types for naval use) were produced across the Commonwealth, including in Canada and India. Production in Britain ended after the Second World War as extruded and granular nitrocellulose-based powders became preferred, though cordite-loaded ammunition remained in service inventories for years.

Properties

Because the strands were long, the burning rate of a charge could be tuned by strand (cord) diameter: thicker cords burned for longer, suiting the long barrels of naval guns, while thinner strands suited small arms. The petroleum jelly acted as a stabilizer and lubricant. Cordite is double-based (nitrocellulose plus nitroglycerin) and therefore more energetic — and more erosive at high nitroglycerin content — than single-based powders.
