Pompey

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Marble bust of Pompey the Great, from a Roman portrait]

Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus (106–48 BC), known in English as Pompey the Great, was a Roman general and statesman, the outstanding military talent of the late Republic's middle years and Julius Caesar's great rival in its final crisis. A teenage commander who raised a private army for Sulla, he won commands against Sertorius in Spain, the pirates of the Mediterranean (cleared in three months, 67 BC) and Mithridates of Pontus, annexing Syria and Judaea (63 BC) and triumphing three times by the age of forty-six. Forming the First Triumvirate with Caesar and Crassus (60 BC), he drifted into the Senate's camp as Caesar's power grew; defeated by Caesar at Pharsalus (48 BC), he fled to Egypt and was murdered on landing.

The young conqueror

Pompey's extraordinary career was built on extraordinary commands granted by popular legislation over senatorial grumbling: the lex Gabinia (67 BC) gave him the whole Mediterranean against the pirates; the lex Manilia (66 BC) the Mithridatic War. His eastern settlement — provinces of Bithynia-Pontus and Syria, client kings from Armenia to Judaea (he entered the Holy of Holies in Jerusalem, to local outrage) — endured for centuries. His first triumph (81 BC, still in his twenties and not yet a senator) set the pattern of a career that bent every republican rule.

Triumvirate and civil war

Blocked by the Senate from ratifying his eastern acts and settling his veterans, Pompey joined Caesar and Crassus in 60 BC, marrying Caesar's daughter Julia (whose death in 54 BC loosened the bond). As sole consul in 52 BC he restored order after street warfare, then aligned with the optimates against Caesar. When Caesar crossed the Rubicon (49 BC), Pompey abandoned Italy, built an army in Greece, and — against his own better judgement, contemporaries said — was forced by his senatorial allies into battle at Pharsalus (9 August 48 BC), where Caesar's veterans destroyed his larger army. In Egypt, the boy-king Ptolemy XIII's advisers had him stabbed as he came ashore; his severed head was later presented to a disgusted Caesar.
