Wood

Wood is a structural tissue found in the stems and roots of trees and other woody plants. It is an organic material — a natural composite of cellulose fibers that are strong in tension and embedded in a matrix of lignin that resists compression. Wood is sometimes defined as only the secondary xylem in the stems of trees, or more broadly to include the same type of tissue elsewhere, such as in roots.

Wood has been used for thousands of years for fuel, construction materials, tools, weapons, furniture, musical instruments, and works of art. As a material under continuous observation and study, wood has been known longer than most other materials, and the study of wood has a rich history in botany, engineering, and forestry.

Physical properties

Wood is strongly anisotropic: its properties vary greatly depending on whether forces and conditions are along the grain or across it. Important physical properties include density, moisture content, thermal and electrical conductivity, and shrinkage during drying. Hardness is commonly measured on the Janka hardness test.

Formation

Wood is produced by the vascular cambium, a lateral meristem that generates new xylem cells inward toward the pith. In many species this growth is seasonal, producing visible annual growth rings. Springwood (earlywood) cells are larger and thinner-walled; summerwood (latewood) cells are smaller and denser.

Types

Woods are broadly divided into hardwoods (from angiosperm trees such as oak, maple, and teak) and softwoods (from gymnosperm trees such as pine, spruce, and cedar). The terms do not strictly correspond to hardness. Heartwood is the darker, older inner wood; sapwood is the lighter outer zone that conducts water in the living tree.

Uses

Wood is used as lumber and timber in construction, as pulp for paper, as fuel and charcoal, and in the manufacture of engineered products such as plywood, particleboard, and laminated veneer lumber. See also wood finishing and wood preservation.

Chemical composition

Dry wood by weight is roughly 50% carbon, 42% oxygen, 6% hydrogen, 1% nitrogen, and 1% other elements. Its structural polymers are cellulose (about 40–50%), hemicellulose (about 25–35%), and lignin (about 20–30%).
