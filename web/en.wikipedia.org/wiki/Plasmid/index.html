Plasmid

A plasmid is a small, extrachromosomal DNA molecule within a cell that is physically separated from the main chromosome and can replicate independently. Plasmids are most often circular double-stranded DNA molecules found in bacteria, though they also occur in archaea and some eukaryotes. They typically carry genes that are not essential for survival but confer advantages, such as antibiotic resistance or the ability to metabolise unusual compounds.

Properties and transfer

Plasmids replicate using the host's machinery from their own origin of replication, and occur in characteristic copy numbers from one to hundreds per cell. Conjugative plasmids (such as the F plasmid) encode the machinery for their own transfer between cells by conjugation; others can be mobilised or moved by transformation and transduction. This horizontal gene transfer lets traits spread rapidly through bacterial populations — the mechanism behind much of the global spread of antibiotic resistance, carried on R plasmids.

Functions

Natural plasmids carry genes for antibiotic resistance, virulence (e.g. the Ti plasmid of Agrobacterium, which causes crown gall in plants), toxin production, heavy-metal resistance, bacteriocins, and the degradation of xenobiotics. Compatibility groups (Inc groups) classify plasmids by their replication control, since closely related plasmids cannot stably coexist in one cell.

Use in biotechnology

Engineered plasmids are the workhorse vectors of molecular biology: a gene of interest is inserted into a plasmid bearing an origin of replication and a selectable marker (often an antibiotic-resistance gene), the plasmid is introduced into bacteria, and the bacteria manufacture copies of the DNA or express the encoded protein — the method used to produce human insulin and countless research reagents. Plasmid maps, multiple cloning sites and reporter genes are standard features of laboratory vectors such as the pUC series.
