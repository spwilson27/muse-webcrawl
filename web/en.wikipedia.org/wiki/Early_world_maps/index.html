Early world maps

Early world maps are the earliest surviving cartographic depictions of the whole known world, dating from the 6th century BC to the great age of discovery in the 16th century AD, after which maps based on new surveys progressively replaced traditional schemes.

Ptolemy's world map, reconstructed from his Geography in the 15th century.

Antiquity

The oldest known world map is the Babylonian Imago Mundi (c. 6th century BC), a clay tablet showing Babylon at the centre of a circular world surrounded by ocean. Greek cartography advanced through Anaximander and Hecataeus to Eratosthenes, who measured the Earth's circumference, and culminated in the work of Claudius Ptolemy (2nd century AD), whose Geography used a coordinate grid and depicted the inhabited world (oikumene) from the Canary Islands to China.

Medieval maps

In medieval Europe the symbolic T and O map placed Jerusalem at the centre of a tripartite world of Asia, Europe and Africa, as in the great mappaemundi of Hereford and Ebstorf. Islamic cartographers such as al-Idrisi, working for Roger II of Sicily, produced more accurate world maps in the 12th century, while Chinese and Korean maps, including the Kangnido map of 1402, integrated knowledge of Africa and Europe. Sailors' portolan charts, beginning with the Carte Pisane (c. 1290), charted coastlines with notable practical accuracy.

The age of discovery

The voyages of Columbus, da Gama and Magellan transformed world mapping. The Cantino planisphere (1502), the maps of Martin Waldseemüller (1507)—the first to name "America"—and Gerardus Mercator's projection of 1569 mark the transition to the modern world map, increasingly based on measured observation rather than inherited tradition.
