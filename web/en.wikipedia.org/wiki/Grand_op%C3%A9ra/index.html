Grand opéra

Grand opéra is a genre of 19th-century opera characterised by large-scale casts and orchestras, spectacular stage effects, historical subjects, and lavish ballet sequences, typically in four or five acts. The genre flourished at the Paris Opéra, particularly between the 1820s and the 1880s.

Characteristics

Grand opéras featured historical themes involving conflict between public spectacle and private emotion, crowded crowd scenes, processions, and — almost obligatory — an extended ballet, usually in the second or third act. Composers such as Daniel Auber (La muette de Portici, 1828) and Giacomo Meyerbeer (Les Huguenots, 1836) defined the style, and works by Rossini, Donizetti, Halévy and the young Verdi also belong to it.

Influence

The genre set the template for epic opera worldwide, influencing Wagner's music dramas, Verdi's Aida, and even modern stage spectacle. Its decline followed the Franco-Prussian War and changing tastes toward the end of the century.
