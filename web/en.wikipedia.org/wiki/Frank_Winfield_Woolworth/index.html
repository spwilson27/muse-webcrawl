Frank Winfield Woolworth (April 13, 1852 – April 8, 1919) was an American entrepreneur, the founder of F. W. Woolworth Company, and the operator of variety stores known as "Five-and-Dimes" (5- and 10-cent stores) which featured a selection of low-priced merchandise. He pioneered the now-common practices of buying merchandise directly from manufacturers, keeping prices fixed, and displaying goods on open counters where customers could handle them without a clerk.

Early life

Woolworth was born in Rodman, New York, to a farming family. At 21 he began working as a stock boy and clerk in a dry-goods store in Watertown, New York, where he observed the popularity of a counter of items priced at five cents. He concluded that an entire store selling nothing over five or ten cents could succeed.

The five-and-dime empire

After a first store in Utica, New York, failed in 1879, Woolworth opened a successful store in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, the same year, and expanded with partners and family members, including his brother Charles Sumner Woolworth. The F. W. Woolworth Company was incorporated in 1911, merging the chains of Woolworth and several associates into a business with hundreds of stores across the United States and abroad, including in Britain, where the first store opened in Liverpool in 1909.

In 1913 Woolworth completed the Woolworth Building in New York City, then the tallest building in the world, paying its roughly $13.5 million cost in cash. The neo-Gothic skyscraper, nicknamed the "Cathedral of Commerce," remained the company's headquarters for decades.

Death and legacy

Woolworth died in 1919 at his estate in Glen Cove, Long Island. His retail model of fixed low prices, self-service browsing, and direct purchasing transformed popular shopping and was widely imitated. The last Woolworth variety stores in the United States closed in 1997, though the name survived longer in some international markets; the corporation he founded continued as Foot Locker, Inc.
