Haplorhini

Haplorhini (from Greek, "simple-nosed") is a suborder of primates containing the tarsiers and the simians (monkeys and apes, including humans). Haplorhines are distinguished from the other primate suborder, the Strepsirrhini ("curly-nosed" primates such as lemurs and lorises), by a number of anatomical features, most notably the structure of the nose and upper lip.

Characteristics

Haplorhines have a dry, hairy nose and a mobile upper lip that is not split, unlike the wet rhinarium and divided upper lip of strepsirrhines. They lack the tapetum lucidum that gives many nocturnal mammals eyeshine, and instead the simians possess a fovea in the retina; most haplorhines are diurnal, the exceptions being the tarsiers and the night monkeys. Haplorhines cannot synthesise vitamin C, having lost the functional gene for the enzyme L-gulonolactone oxidase, and must obtain it from their diet.

Classification and evolution

The suborder divides into the infraorder Tarsiiformes, containing only the tarsiers of Southeast Asia, and the Simiiformes, comprising the platyrrhine monkeys of the Americas and the catarrhines—Old World monkeys and apes—of Africa and Asia. Molecular estimates place the split between haplorhines and strepsirrhines deep in the early Cenozoic or late Cretaceous, and the tarsier–simian divergence at roughly 60–70 million years ago.
