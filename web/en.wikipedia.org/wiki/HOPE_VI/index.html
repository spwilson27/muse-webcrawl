HOPE VI is a programme of the United States Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), created in 1993, that provided grants to local public housing agencies to demolish severely distressed public housing projects and replace them with mixed-income communities, typically of lower density and designed using New Urbanist principles such as street-facing townhouses, porches and "defensible space".

Origins

The programme grew out of the 1992 report of the National Commission on Severely Distressed Public Housing, which identified some 86,000 units as severely distressed. Modelled partly on early experiments such as Atlanta's Techwood/Clark Howell redevelopment for the 1996 Olympics, HOPE VI shifted federal policy away from concentrated high-rise projects toward poverty deconcentration and mixed finance, leveraging private investment alongside federal funds.

Implementation and criticism

By the late 2000s HUD had awarded hundreds of revitalisation grants totalling several billion dollars, and well over 100,000 distressed units had been demolished. Displaced residents received housing choice vouchers or places in the new developments, but because replacement developments contained fewer public-housing units, only a minority of original residents typically returned. Critics charged HOPE VI with reducing the net stock of deeply affordable housing and dispersing poor communities; supporters pointed to improved housing quality, safety and neighbourhood investment. Evaluations by the Urban Institute found outcomes for relocatees varied widely by city.

Legacy

HOPE VI funding effectively wound down around 2010 and the Obama administration's Choice Neighborhoods initiative succeeded it, extending the approach beyond public housing to surrounding neighbourhood services and schools.
