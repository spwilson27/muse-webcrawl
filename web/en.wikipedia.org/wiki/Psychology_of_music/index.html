The psychology of music is the branch of psychology and musicology concerned with how music is perceived, created, remembered and responded to emotionally and physiologically. It spans music cognition (pitch, rhythm, harmony and expectancy), the development of musical ability, musical preference and identity, performance anxiety and expertise, and the therapeutic uses of music. The field draws on experimental psychology, neuroscience, acoustics and ethnomusicology.

History

Scientific study of musical sensation began with Hermann von Helmholtz's On the Sensations of Tone (1863), which grounded consonance in the physiology of the ear, and Carl Seashore's psychometrics of musical talent in the early 20th century. Gestalt psychologists studied melody as a perceptual whole; Leonard B. Meyer's Emotion and Meaning in Music (1956) founded expectation-based theories of musical meaning, and the cognitive revolution from the 1970s — aided by brain imaging from the 1990s — made music a model system for studying auditory cognition.

Principal topics

Perception. Research examines absolute pitch, the octave equivalence of tones, stream segregation in polyphony, and enculturation into the scales and rhythms of one's culture. Emotion. Music reliably evokes emotion through mechanisms catalogued by Juslin and Västfjäll — brainstem reflexes, rhythmic entrainment, evaluative conditioning, contagion, imagery, episodic memory and the fulfilment or violation of expectation — and is used deliberately for mood regulation. Memory and learning. Musical training is associated with enhanced auditory working memory, though claims of broad transfer (the "Mozart effect") have not held up. Preference. Taste is predicted by exposure (the mere-exposure effect), personality, social identity and an inverted-U relationship with complexity.

Applications

Findings inform music education, music therapy (for dementia, autism and rehabilitation after stroke), performance training, and the design of streaming recommendation systems and film scoring.
