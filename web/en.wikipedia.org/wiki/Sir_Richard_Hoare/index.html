Sir Richard Hoare (1648 – 6 January 1719) was an English goldsmith and banker, the founder of C. Hoare & Co., one of Britain's oldest private banks, and a prominent City of London politician who served as Lord Mayor of London.

Early life and the bank

Hoare was apprenticed to the goldsmith John Colvill and became a freeman of the Goldsmiths' Company in 1672. In that year he acquired the business of his master at the sign of the Golden Bottle in Cheapside, moving shortly afterwards to Fleet Street — the site Hoare's Bank occupies to this day. Like other goldsmith-bankers of Restoration London, he took deposits, lent money, discounted bills and issued notes, building a clientele among the aristocracy and gentry that the bank retained for generations. The partnership became known as Hoare's Bank; unusually among the great goldsmith houses, it survived the financial storms of the eighteenth century and remains a family-owned private bank, with partners drawn from Hoare's descendants into the twenty-first century.

Civic and political career

Hoare rose through the City hierarchy: sheriff of London in 1703–04, knighted by Queen Anne in 1702, alderman, and finally Lord Mayor in 1712–13. A Tory in politics, he sat in Parliament for the City of London in the early eighteenth century and was known for his defence of the City's privileges and of the goldsmiths' trade against the growing power of the Bank of England, in which he nonetheless became a substantial stockholder. He was also a governor of Christ's Hospital and other City charities.

Family and legacy

Hoare married Susanna Austin in 1672; among their descendants were the diarist and banker Henry Hoare ("the Magnificent") of Stourhead. Richard Hoare died in January 1719 and was buried in London. His journal and correspondence survive among the bank's archives and are a valued source for the early history of English banking.
