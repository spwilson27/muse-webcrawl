Nothing

FieldPhilosophy (metaphysics, ontology)
Key thinkersParmenides, Plato, Aristotle, Descartes, Leibniz, Hegel, Heidegger, Sartre, Carnap
Related conceptsVoid, emptiness, vacuum, zero, non-being, ex nihilo

Nothing, or nothingness, is the complete absence of anything: the state of non-being. In everyday language "nothing" is a pronoun denoting the absence of any thing, but philosophers since antiquity have puzzled over whether nothing can be thought about, talked about, or said to exist at all — questions touching logic, physics, theology and the fear of oblivion.

Ancient philosophy

Parmenides argued that "what is not" can neither be thought nor spoken of, and that non-being is impossible. Leucippus and Democritus, by contrast, made the void — empty space in which atoms move — a fundamental reality, and Epicurus and Lucretius taught that "nothing can be created from nothing". Plato wrestled with non-being in the Sophist, and Aristotle denied that a true vacuum exists in nature.

Theology and creation

Jewish, Christian and Islamic theology developed the doctrine of creatio ex nihilo — creation out of nothing — against the idea of eternal pre-existing matter. In several Asian traditions, by contrast, emptiness (Sanskrit śūnyatā, Chinese wu) is a positive and central concept rather than mere absence, denoting interdependence and the lack of fixed essence.

Modern philosophy

Leibniz famously asked "why is there something rather than nothing?", calling it the first question of metaphysics. Hegel treated pure being and pure nothing as identical abstractions whose union is becoming. Martin Heidegger made "the Nothing" central in his 1929 lecture What is Metaphysics?, arguing that nothing is encountered in anxiety — a claim Rudolf Carnap attacked as meaningless pseudo-statement. Jean-Paul Sartre analysed nothingness (le néant) in Being and Nothingness as brought into the world by consciousness.

Physics and mathematics

Physics does not offer absolute nothing: a vacuum seethes with quantum fluctuations and virtual particles, and Lawrence Krauss and others have argued that a universe can arise from such a "nothing". In mathematics, analogous concepts include zero, the empty set, and the null value — rigorous tools that nonetheless are something rather than nothing at all.
