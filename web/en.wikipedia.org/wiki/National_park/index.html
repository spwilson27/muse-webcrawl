A national park is a nature park designated for conservation purposes because of unparalleled national natural, scenic, or cultural resources. National parks are usually created and owned by national governments and are open to the public for recreation and enjoyment, subject to protections that preserve landscapes, wildlife, and ecosystems for future generations. The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) classifies most national parks in its protected-area Category II.

History

The modern concept began in the United States: Yosemite was granted federal protection in 1864, and Yellowstone, established on 1 March 1872, is generally regarded as the world's first national park. The idea spread to Canada (Banff, 1885), Australia (Royal National Park, 1879), and eventually to Europe, Africa, and Asia, where parks such as Kruger (South Africa), Serengeti (Tanzania), and Sagarmatha (Nepal) became globally famous. The U.S. National Park Service, created in 1916, administers more than 400 units including 63 national parks.

Definitions and management

Definitions vary by country. In the American model, land is federally owned and largely uninhabited; in the British model (England, Wales, and Scotland), parks encompass lived-in, privately owned farmland and villages, protected chiefly through planning law. French national parks combine strictly protected core zones with buffer areas. Common management goals include habitat and species conservation, control of invasive species and poaching, fire management, and balancing conservation with tourism, which brings significant economic benefits but also crowding and environmental pressure.
