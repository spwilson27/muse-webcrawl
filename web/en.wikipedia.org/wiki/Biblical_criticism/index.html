Biblical criticism

Biblical criticism is the use of critical analysis to understand and explain the Bible. During the eighteenth century, when it began as an historical method, biblical criticism used the scientific method; since then it has been influenced by a variety of other methodologies and approaches. Biblical criticism is a collection of many different approaches to studying the Bible, each answering questions about the text such as authorship, date, sources, intended audience, and meaning.

History

Biblical criticism originated in the Enlightenment, when scholars began applying the tools of historical and literary analysis to scripture. Baruch Spinoza's Tractatus Theologico-Politicus (1670) argued that the Pentateuch was not written entirely by Moses. In the eighteenth century, Jean Astruc proposed that Genesis was compiled from multiple sources, and Johann Gottfried Eichhorn extended source criticism to the whole Bible.

In the nineteenth century, Julius Wellhausen formulated the documentary hypothesis, proposing that the Pentateuch was woven from four originally independent sources (J, E, D, and P). Meanwhile, form criticism, pioneered by Hermann Gunkel, examined the small units of oral tradition behind the written text, and redaction criticism later studied how editors shaped those traditions.

Methods

Textual criticism

Textual criticism (also called lower criticism) seeks to establish the original wording of a text by comparing the many surviving manuscripts, ancient translations, and quotations in early writers. For the New Testament, thousands of Greek manuscripts survive, plus the Dead Sea Scrolls provide Hebrew texts over a thousand years older than previously known copies.

Source criticism

Source criticism studies the written sources behind a biblical book, such as the sources combined in the Pentateuch or the hypothetical Q source shared by Matthew and Luke.

Form and redaction criticism

Form criticism classifies texts by genre (hymns, parables, miracle stories) and seeks their original setting in life (Sitz im Leben). Redaction criticism examines how the final editors arranged and modified their sources to express a theological viewpoint.

Literary and canonical criticism

Later approaches—including rhetorical criticism, narrative criticism, reader-response criticism, and Brevard Childs's canonical criticism—focus on the text in its final form rather than its prehistory.
