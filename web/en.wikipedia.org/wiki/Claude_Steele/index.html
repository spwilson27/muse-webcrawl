Claude Mason Steele (born January 1, 1946) is an American social psychologist and a professor of psychology at Stanford University. He is best known for his work on stereotype threat and its application to minority student academic performance, and for developing the theory of self-affirmation. His book Whistling Vivaldi: How Stereotypes Affect Us and What We Can Do (2010) summarizes much of his research for a general audience.

Education and career

Steele attended Hiram College, where he studied psychology, and earned his PhD in social psychology from Ohio State University in 1971. He taught at the University of Utah, the University of Washington, and the University of Michigan before joining Stanford in 1987. He later served as dean of the Stanford Graduate School of Education (2009–2011), provost of Columbia University (2011–2014), and executive vice chancellor and provost of UC Berkeley (2014–2016), before returning to Stanford.

Stereotype threat

In a landmark 1995 paper with Joshua Aronson, Steele showed that African-American college students performed worse on standardized tests when the test was presented as diagnostic of intellectual ability — a framing that activated fear of confirming a negative stereotype about their group. When the same test was presented as non-diagnostic, the performance gap largely disappeared. Steele's research program explored stereotype threat among women in mathematics, older adults, and other groups, along with interventions — such as self-affirmation exercises and growth-mindset messaging — that reduce its effects.

Honors

Steele is a member of the National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. He has received numerous awards in psychology, including the William James Fellow Award, and holds honorary degrees from several universities.
