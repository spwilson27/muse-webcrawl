Monocular vision

[Image placeholder: monocular depth cues]

Monocular vision is vision in which each eye is used separately, as opposed to binocular vision in which both eyes are used together. In organisms with monocular vision, the eyes are typically located on opposite sides of the head, giving each eye a largely separate field of view and maximising the total visual field — an arrangement common in prey animals, which gain panoramic surveillance at the cost of depth perception in the overlapping zone.

Monocular cues

Even with a single eye, an observer can judge distance using monocular cues: relative size of familiar objects, occlusion (nearer objects overlap farther ones), linear perspective, texture gradients, aerial perspective, shading and shadow, and motion parallax produced by movement of the head. Artists have exploited these pictorial cues for centuries to create the illusion of depth on a flat surface.

In humans

Humans normally have binocular vision, but monocular vision may result from the loss of an eye, severe amblyopia or strabismus. People with vision in only one eye rely on monocular cues and typically adapt well to most tasks, though stereoscopic depth perception is absent and the visual field on the affected side is reduced; some occupations, such as certain categories of professional driving and aviation, impose restrictions.

In animals

Many herbivores, birds and fish have laterally placed eyes and predominantly monocular vision. Chameleons move their eyes independently and can switch to binocular focusing for striking prey. Some animals, such as certain sharks and rays, also supplement vision with electrosensory systems.
