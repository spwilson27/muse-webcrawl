Solar time is a calculation of the passage of time based on the position of the Sun in the sky. The fundamental unit of solar time is the day, based on the synodic rotation period. Traditionally, there are two types of solar time: apparent solar time (also called true solar time) and mean solar time.

Apparent solar time

Apparent solar time is measured by a sundial: solar noon occurs when the Sun crosses the local meridian and reaches its highest point in the sky. Because the Earth's orbit is elliptical and its axis is tilted, the length of the solar day varies through the year, so apparent solar time runs up to about 16 minutes ahead of, or 14 minutes behind, clock time — a difference charted by the equation of time and traced by the figure-eight analemma.

Mean solar time

Mean solar time smooths out these variations by imagining a fictitious "mean Sun" moving at a constant rate along the celestial equator. When corrected for longitude within a time zone, mean solar time becomes local mean time, the basis of the standard time systems introduced in the 19th century with Greenwich Mean Time as the reference. Modern civil time (UTC) is based on atomic clocks but is kept within 0.9 seconds of mean solar time at Greenwich (UT1) by leap seconds.

On other planets

The concept generalises: on Mars, a solar day is called a sol, lasting 24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds of Earth time, and mission clocks for landers and rovers have tracked local mean solar time at their landing sites.
