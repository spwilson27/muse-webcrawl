Immersion (mathematics)

In differential geometry and topology, an immersion is a differentiable function between differentiable manifolds whose differential (pushforward) is everywhere injective. Explicitly, f : M → N is an immersion if the derivative Dp f : TpM → Tf(p)N is an injective linear map for every point p in M. An immersion need not itself be injective: the image of the manifold may cross or touch itself.

The figure-eight (lemniscate-like) immersion of S¹ in the plane has everywhere nonzero derivative but is not injective.

Properties

Because its differential is injective, an immersion is a local embedding: by the inverse function theorem, every point of M has a neighborhood that f maps diffeomorphically onto its image. Globally, however, distinct points of M can share an image point, as in the figure-eight curve, or even accumulate on themselves, as in an irrational-slope line wrapped densely around a torus (an injective immersion that is not an embedding when the image is given the subspace topology).

Immersions versus embeddings

An immersion that is also a homeomorphism onto its image (with appropriate conditions, such as being proper) is an embedding. Every embedding is an immersion, but not conversely. The famous Whitney embedding theorem states that any smooth n-manifold can be embedded in ℝ2n, while Whitney's immersion theorem guarantees an immersion into ℝ2n−1. The Nash–Kuiper theorem gives surprising C¹ isometric immersions, and Smale's sphere eversion shows that a sphere can be turned inside out through immersions — but not through embeddings — in three-dimensional space.

Regular homotopy

Immersions are studied up to regular homotopy — homotopy through immersions — and the Smale–Hirsch theorem classifies them in terms of bundle monomorphisms, reducing differential topology to homotopy theory. This framework explains phenomena such as sphere eversion and the classification of immersions of the circle in the plane by turning number (the Whitney–Graustein theorem).
