Cooperative banking

Cooperative banking is retail and commercial banking organized on a cooperative basis. Cooperative banks are owned by their customers — typically organized as members holding shares — and governed democratically on a "one member, one vote" principle, in contrast to stock banks owned by external shareholders. The sector includes credit unions, savings and loans associations, and large cooperative banking networks.

History

The model originated in 19th-century Germany with the credit cooperatives promoted by Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen for rural communities and Hermann Schulze-Delitzsch for urban artisans. Similar institutions spread across Europe and beyond; examples include the Raiffeisen banks of Germany, Austria, and Switzerland, Crédit Agricole and Crédit Mutuel in France, Rabobank in the Netherlands, and the cooperative banks of Finland and Italy.

Characteristics

Cooperative banks typically emphasize local relationships, member service over profit maximization, and reinvestment in their communities. Many operate in federal networks, where local banks jointly own central institutions providing liquidity, payments, and specialized services. Although their market share varies by country, cooperative banks are systemically important in several European economies and are noted in studies for relative stability during the 2008 financial crisis, though they have also faced governance and consolidation pressures.
