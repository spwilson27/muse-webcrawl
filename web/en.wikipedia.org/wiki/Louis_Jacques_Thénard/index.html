Louis Jacques Thénard (4 May 1777 – 21 June 1857) was a French chemist. Born to a poor farming family in La Louptière, he was brought to Paris by the patronage of a local landowner and studied under Vauquelin at the École Polytechnique, where he soon became a demonstrator and, at age twenty, a lecturer. He later held chairs at the École Polytechnique, the Collège de France and the University of Paris, becoming one of the most influential chemistry teachers of his generation.

Work

Working closely with Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac, Thénard carried out extensive research supported by Napoleon. Together they improved the method of preparing potassium and sodium by chemical reduction, and in 1808, independently of Humphry Davy, they isolated the element boron by reducing boric acid with potassium. In 1818 Thénard discovered hydrogen peroxide, which he prepared by treating barium peroxide with nitric acid; he named it eau oxygénée ("oxygenated water"). He is also associated with "Thénard's blue", a cobalt aluminate pigment used in porcelain, and with the discovery of thenardite, a sodium sulfate mineral named after him. His multi-volume Traité de chimie élémentaire, théorique et pratique (1813–1816) became a standard textbook across Europe.

Honours

Thénard was elected to the French Academy of Sciences, made a baron in 1825, and served as a deputy and later a peer of France. His name is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower. Antoine Bussy was among his many students.
