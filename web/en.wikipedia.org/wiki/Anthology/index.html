Anthology

Anthology
DefinitionA published collection of writings by various authors
EtymologyGreek anthologia, "flower-gathering"
ExamplesGreek Anthology; The Norton Anthology of English Literature

An anthology is a collection of selected writings by various authors, usually organised by genre, theme, period or language, and published in a single volume. Anthologies are most commonly collections of poems or short stories, but the term also covers collections of plays, songs, essays, films and television episodes. The word derives from the Greek anthologia (ἀνθολογία), literally a "gathering of flowers".

History

The earliest famous anthology is the Greek Anthology, a collection of poems spanning the classical and Byzantine periods, assembled from earlier collections such as the Garland of Meleager of Gadara (1st century BC). Anthologies flourished in many literary cultures: in Japan, imperial poetry collections such as the Kokin Wakashū (c. 905) defined the canon of waka poetry, and in England Tottel's Miscellany (1557) introduced the work of Wyatt and Surrey to a broad readership.

Role in publishing

Anthologies play a central role in establishing literary canons and in education: textbooks such as The Norton Anthology of English Literature are standard in university courses. In genre fiction, especially science fiction, fantasy and horror, anthologies of short stories — both reprint "best of the year" volumes and original anthologies edited around a theme — are an important market for short fiction and a showcase for new writers. The editor of an anthology selects and arranges the contents and typically writes an introduction; a collection of works by a single author is more precisely called a collection or, for a retrospective selection, an omnibus or reader.

Other media

By extension, the term describes works in other media that gather self-contained pieces: anthology films, anthology television series, anthology comics and music compilation albums. In each case the unifying feature is editorial selection across multiple, often independent, contributions rather than a single continuous work.
