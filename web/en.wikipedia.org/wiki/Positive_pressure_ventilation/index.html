Positive pressure ventilation

Positive pressure ventilation is a form of respiratory therapy in which air (or a mixture of gases) is delivered into the airways and lungs under pressure greater than atmospheric pressure, typically by a mechanical ventilator connected to a face mask, supraglottic airway or endotracheal tube.

Mechanism

Unlike spontaneous breathing, in which the diaphragm creates negative intrathoracic pressure, positive pressure ventilation pushes gas into the lungs during inspiration; exhalation is passive, driven by elastic recoil. Settings include tidal volume, respiratory rate, fraction of inspired oxygen and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).

Applications and risks

It is used in anaesthesia, intensive care and emergency medicine for respiratory failure. Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (such as CPAP and BiPAP) can avoid intubation in conditions such as acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema and COPD exacerbations. Risks include ventilator-induced lung injury, barotrauma and ventilator-associated pneumonia.
