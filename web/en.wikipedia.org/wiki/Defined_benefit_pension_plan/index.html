Defined benefit pension plan

A defined benefit pension plan is a type of pension plan in which an employer promises a specified pension payment, lump sum, or combination thereof on retirement, predetermined by a formula based on the employee's earnings history, tenure of service and age, rather than depending directly on individual investment returns. Traditionally, many governmental and public entities, as well as a large number of private employers, provided defined benefit plans, sometimes simply called final salary schemes in the United Kingdom.

Under a defined benefit plan, the retirement income is fixed by formula in advance

How it works

The benefit is usually expressed as a fraction of salary for each year of service. For example, a plan might pay 1/60th of final salary per year of service, so that an employee retiring after 40 years on a final salary of 60,000 would receive an annual pension of 40,000. The employer (and sometimes the employee) contributes to a fund whose assets are invested; the employer bears the investment and longevity risk, and must make good any shortfall. Actuaries value the scheme's liabilities using assumptions about mortality, salary growth, inflation and discount rates.

Funding and protection

Because the promise is long-term, defined benefit schemes are subject to funding regulation. In the United States, the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA) sets minimum funding standards, and the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation insures benefits if a plan terminates with insufficient assets. In the UK, the Pension Protection Fund performs a similar role. A scheme whose assets fall short of its liabilities is said to be in deficit, and sponsoring employers may be required to agree recovery plans with trustees and regulators.

Decline

Since the 1980s, private-sector employers in many countries have closed defined benefit schemes to new entrants or to future accrual, replacing them with defined contribution plans, in which the investment risk falls on the employee. The shift reflects rising longevity, volatile asset returns, low interest rates increasing measured liabilities, and changes in accounting rules that made deficits more visible on corporate balance sheets. Defined benefit provision remains common in the public sector.
