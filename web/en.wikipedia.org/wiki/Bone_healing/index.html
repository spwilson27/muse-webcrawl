Bone healing

Bone healing, or fracture healing, is a complex physiological process in which the body repairs a bone fracture. Unlike most tissues, bone can heal without forming a fibrous scar: under favourable conditions it regenerates bone tissue that is eventually indistinguishable from the original. The process involves inflammation, repair with callus formation, and long-term remodelling.

Stages (secondary healing)

Most fractures heal by secondary (indirect) healing, which proceeds in overlapping stages:

  1. Haematoma and inflammation — bleeding at the fracture forms a clot; inflammatory cells clear debris and release signalling molecules.
  2. Soft callus — over about two weeks, fibroblasts and chondroblasts bridge the gap with fibrocartilage, stabilising the fragments.
  3. Hard callus — the cartilage is replaced by woven bone through endochondral ossification, restoring mechanical continuity over weeks to months.
  4. Remodelling — osteoclasts and osteoblasts reshape the woven bone into lamellar bone along lines of stress, a process that can continue for years.

Primary (direct) healing occurs when fragments are held in rigid, anatomical contact — for example by surgical compression plating — allowing bone to grow directly across the gap with little callus.

Factors affecting healing

Healing is impaired by poor blood supply, infection, excessive movement at the fracture site, smoking, diabetes, malnutrition and certain drugs. Complications include delayed union, non-union (failure to heal) and malunion (healing in a bad position). Treatments of problem fractures include bone grafting, electrical or ultrasound stimulation, and growth factors such as bone morphogenetic proteins.
