Wörgl experiment

The Wörgl experiment was a local-currency scheme carried out in 1932–1933 in the Austrian town of Wörgl during the Great Depression. It is one of the most famous historical examples of a demurrage currency, inspired by the ideas of the German economist Silvio Gesell.

Background

By 1932 Wörgl, a town of about 4,500 people in Tyrol, faced mass unemployment and a bankrupt municipal treasury. Mayor Michael Unterguggenberger persuaded the town council to issue "certificates of work" — local scrip backed by schillings deposited at the local savings bank — as a substitute currency for paying municipal workers and funding public works.

Design

The notes carried a demurrage charge: holders had to buy a stamp worth 1% of the note's face value each month to keep it valid. This penalised hoarding and encouraged rapid spending, dramatically increasing the velocity of money within the town.

Results and suppression

During the experiment, unemployment in Wörgl fell significantly while it rose elsewhere in Austria; the town paved streets, built a bridge and a ski jump, and paid taxes early. News of the "miracle of Wörgl" spread internationally, and hundreds of Austrian communities considered copying it. In 1933 the Austrian National Bank, defending its monopoly on currency issue, obtained a court ruling that shut the experiment down, and Wörgl's economy relapsed.

Legacy

The experiment remains a reference point in debates about complementary currencies, demurrage, and monetary policy in deflationary crises, and is frequently cited by advocates of local exchange systems.
