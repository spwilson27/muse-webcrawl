Australian Aboriginal art encompasses the art made by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples of Australia, a body of work whose rock art traditions are among the oldest continuous art practices in the world. It includes rock paintings and petroglyphs, bark painting, sand and ground drawing, body painting, carved objects, and, since the 1970s, a celebrated movement of acrylic painting on canvas.

Rock art

Rock shelters in regions such as Kakadu, the Kimberley, and the Sydney Basin preserve paintings and engravings spanning tens of thousands of years, depicting ancestral beings, animals (including extinct megafauna in naturalistic styles), hand stencils, and contact-era subjects such as ships. The Gwion Gwion (Bradshaw) figures of the Kimberley are famous for their elegant, dynamic human forms.

Meaning and the Dreaming

Much traditional art encodes Dreaming (Tjukurrpa) narratives — the ancestral law and geography of Country. Designs of concentric circles, tracks, and lines map waterholes, journeys, and sacred sites; because some knowledge is restricted, artists developed publicly shareable versions of designs, a practice that shaped the famous dotted style, in which fields of dots both depict and veil sacred motifs.

Contemporary movement

In 1971–72, teacher Geoffrey Bardon encouraged men at Papunya, a settlement west of Alice Springs, to transfer sand and body designs to board and canvas with acrylic paints, launching the Western Desert art movement and the Papunya Tula artists' cooperative. Artists such as Clifford Possum Tjapaltjarri and later Emily Kame Kngwarreye achieved international acclaim; Aboriginal art became a major sector of the Australian art market, raising ongoing debates about authenticity, cultural protocols, resale royalties, and exploitation.
