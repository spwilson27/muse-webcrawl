Twelve Angry Men (play)

Twelve Angry Men is a play by American playwright Reginald Rose, adapted from his 1954 teleplay of the same title for the CBS Studio One anthology series. The stage version debuted in San Francisco in 1955, and in its revised form premiered in London in 1964, on Broadway in 2004, and has since become one of the most widely performed plays in schools and community theatres.

Plot

The play is set entirely in a jury room in New York City, where twelve jurors must decide the fate of a young man accused of murdering his father. The verdict must be unanimous, and a guilty verdict carries a mandatory death sentence. At the first vote, eleven jurors vote guilty and one — Juror 8 — votes not guilty, insisting that the evidence deserves discussion. Over the course of the deliberation, Juror 8 patiently raises reasonable doubts: the uniqueness of the murder weapon, the reliability of the witnesses, the defendant's supposed shout, and the timeline of events. One by one, the jurors change their votes as their own prejudices, assumptions, and personal histories are exposed, until the final holdout concedes and the jury returns a verdict of not guilty.

Adaptations

The story is best known from the acclaimed 1957 film 12 Angry Men, directed by Sidney Lumet and starring Henry Fonda as Juror 8. A 1997 television film directed by William Friedkin starred Jack Lemmon. Rose's stage adaptation is frequently updated or performed with mixed-gender casts under the title Twelve Angry Jurors or 12 Angry Jurors.

Themes

The play is a study of the American jury system and of reasonable doubt, prejudice, and civic responsibility: each juror embodies a different social type — the bigot, the timid immigrant witness to democracy, the rational stockbroker, the angry father estranged from his own son — and the drama lies in how personal bias must yield to evidence.
