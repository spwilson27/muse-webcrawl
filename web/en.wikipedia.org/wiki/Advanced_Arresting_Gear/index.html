Advanced Arresting Gear (AAG) is an arresting gear system developed by General Atomics for the United States Navy's Gerald R. Ford-class aircraft carriers. It is designed to recover a wider range of aircraft — from heavy manned fighters to lightweight unmanned aerial vehicles — with lower peak loads on airframes than the hydraulic Mark 7 arresting gear used on the Nimitz class.

Design

Unlike the hydraulic rams of the Mk 7 system, AAG absorbs the energy of a landing aircraft with water turbines coupled to large induction motors and power-conditioning electronics. Digital control systems regulate the retarding force throughout the arrestment ("runout"), tailoring deceleration to the weight and speed of each aircraft, which reduces stress on both the aircraft and the ship's structure and allows finer control for light unmanned aircraft that hydraulic systems cannot safely stop.

Development and testing

AAG was developed under the same programme as the Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System (EMALS). Land-based testing was carried out at Joint Base McGuire–Dix–Lakehurst, New Jersey, and the system was installed aboard USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), the first ship of the class. Development suffered delays and cost growth, and shipboard testing with manned aircraft began during the ship's post-delivery test period.

Advantages claimed

The Navy and the manufacturer cite reduced manning and maintenance, self-diagnosing digital controls, higher sortie-generation support through faster reset times, and the ability to recover future air-wing aircraft types across a wider weight envelope.
