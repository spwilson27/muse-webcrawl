Bell's palsy is a type of facial paralysis that results in a temporary inability to control the facial muscles on the affected side of the face. It is caused by dysfunction of the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) of unknown cause, though it is thought to often result from inflammation of the nerve, possibly triggered by a viral infection such as herpes simplex.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually come on over 48 hours and affect one side of the face, including the forehead — a feature distinguishing it from a central cause such as stroke, in which forehead movement is typically spared. They may include inability to close the eye, drooping of the mouth, loss of the nasolabial fold, altered taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, sensitivity to sound (hyperacusis), and pain behind the ear.

Treatment and prognosis

Most people recover fully, with about 70% regaining normal function even without treatment. Corticosteroids such as prednisolone, started within 72 hours, improve the chances of complete recovery; eye protection and lubrication are important when the eyelid cannot close. The condition is named after the Scottish surgeon Sir Charles Bell, who described the facial nerve's role in facial expression.
