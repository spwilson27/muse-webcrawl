Euler's formula

Euler's formula, named after Leonhard Euler, is a mathematical formula in complex analysis that establishes the fundamental relationship between the trigonometric functions and the complex exponential function. It states that for any real number x:

eix = cos x + i sin x

where e is the base of the natural logarithm and i is the imaginary unit.

Interpretation

The formula shows that complex exponentiation traces the unit circle in the complex plane: eix is the point at angle x from the positive real axis. It thus provides the polar form of a complex number, reiθ, and makes multiplication of complex numbers correspond to addition of angles. Setting x = π yields Euler's identity, eiπ + 1 = 0, often called the most beautiful equation in mathematics.

History and proof

A version of the formula was first noted by Roger Cotes in 1714; Euler published it in its present form in his Introductio in analysin infinitorum (1748). The standard proof compares the Taylor series of eix, cos x and sin x: expanding the exponential series and grouping real and imaginary terms reproduces exactly the cosine and sine series. Alternative proofs use calculus, showing that (cos x − i sin x)·eix is constant and equal to 1.

Applications

Euler's formula is indispensable in electrical engineering and signal processing, where sinusoidal signals are represented as phasors; in Fourier analysis; in quantum mechanics, where wavefunctions are complex exponentials; and throughout differential equations and number theory.
