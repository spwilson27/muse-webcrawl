In fluid dynamics, the Reynolds number (Re) is a dimensionless quantity that predicts the flow patterns of a fluid: whether the flow will be laminar (smooth, orderly) or turbulent (chaotic, mixing). It is defined as the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces within the fluid:

Re = ρuL / μ = uL / ν

where ρ is the fluid density, u a characteristic velocity, L a characteristic length (such as pipe diameter), μ the dynamic viscosity and ν the kinematic viscosity.

History

The concept was introduced by George Gabriel Stokes in 1851 but was popularized by Osborne Reynolds, whose 1883 experiments injecting dye into pipe flow demonstrated the transition from laminar to turbulent flow at a critical value of the parameter that now bears his name. Reynolds published the definitive analysis in 1895.

Applications

The Reynolds number is used to characterize flow over aircraft wings, through pipes and pumps, around ships and in weather systems. Flows with geometrically similar boundaries are dynamically similar when their Reynolds numbers match — the principle behind wind tunnel and towing tank model testing. In pipe flow, transition typically occurs around Re ≈ 2,300–4,000. At very low Reynolds numbers (Re ≪ 1), viscous forces dominate and the flow is described by Stokes flow, governing the swimming of microorganisms and sedimentation of fine particles.
