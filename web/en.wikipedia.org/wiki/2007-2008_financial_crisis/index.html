The financial crisis of 2007–2008, also known as the global financial crisis (GFC), was a severe worldwide economic crisis and the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression. It originated in the United States housing market and the collapse of mortgage-backed securities, and nearly brought down the global banking system in the autumn of 2008.

Origins

A long US housing bubble, inflated by low interest rates and the proliferation of subprime and adjustable-rate mortgages, began deflating in 2006. Because risky mortgages had been packaged into mortgage-backed securities and collateralised debt obligations (CDOs) sold worldwide — and certified with high credit ratings — losses spread through the entire financial system. When house prices fell and defaults rose, the value of these securities became impossible to determine, freezing interbank lending from August 2007.

Panic of 2008

In March 2008 Bear Stearns was rescued via a forced sale to JPMorgan Chase. In September the US government took over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac; on 15 September 2008 Lehman Brothers filed the largest bankruptcy in US history, igniting a global panic. Insurer AIG was bailed out with $85 billion the following day. Governments responded with bank rescues, guarantees, the $700 billion US Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), and coordinated interest-rate cuts.

Consequences

The crisis triggered the Great Recession: world trade and output contracted, unemployment rose sharply, and governments accumulated large debts, contributing to the European sovereign debt crisis that followed. Regulatory responses included the Dodd–Frank Act in the US and the Basel III bank capital rules.
