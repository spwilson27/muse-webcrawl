Biplane

Hero image: the Sopwith Camel, the archetypal biplane fighter of the First World War.

A biplane is a fixed-wing aircraft with two main wings stacked one above the other. The first powered, controlled aeroplane to fly, the Wright Flyer, was a biplane, and the configuration dominated aircraft design through the First World War and into the 1920s before being displaced by the cantilever monoplane. Biplanes survive today mainly in aerobatic, agricultural, and vintage roles.

Design and advantages

The two wings of a biplane, connected by struts and bracing wires, form a truss-like box girder that is light and stiff — crucial when wings were built of wood and fabric. The arrangement provides large wing area in a compact span, giving low wing loading, short take-off runs, and excellent maneuverability at low speed. The tradeoff is drag: the struts, wires, and interference between the wings limit top speed. Designers often staggered the wings (as on the de Havilland Tiger Moth) to improve visibility and lift. A biplane with a much smaller lower wing is called a sesquiplane; three-winged variants are triplanes, such as the Fokker Dr.I.

History

Biplanes flourished in the First World War as fighters (Sopwith Camel, SPAD S.XIII), bombers, and trainers. Through the 1920s and early 1930s they served as airliners, mail planes, and crop dusters. Improved structures and streamlining made monoplanes decisively faster by the mid-1930s, though biplanes like the Soviet Polikarpov Po-2 and the Antonov An-2 — the largest single-engine biplane ever built — remained in production and use for decades. The Pitts Special and other modern biplanes are prized for aerobatics because of their roll rate and strength.
