Western fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Western fiction is a genre of literature set in the American frontier, commonly known as the "Old West", roughly between the 1860s and the end of the 19th century. It is one of the most distinctive and enduring American genres, spanning dime novels, short stories, novels, films, television and comics.

History

The genre grew out of early frontier narratives and exploded with the cheap dime novels of the 1860s, featuring folk heroes such as Buffalo Bill and Deadwood Dick. Owen Wister's The Virginian (1902) is usually credited with establishing the literary Western, and Zane Grey made it a mass phenomenon with novels like Riders of the Purple Sage (1912). Pulp magazines of the 1920s-40s, writers like Max Brand, and later Louis L'Amour — one of the bestselling authors of the 20th century — carried the genre to its commercial peak.

Themes

Classic Westerns dramatise the conflict between civilisation and wilderness, law and outlawry, settlers and Native peoples, and the loner hero who enforces an unwritten code of honour. Later "revisionist" works — from the novels of Elmore Leonard to Cormac McCarthy's Blood Meridian (1985) — have questioned those myths, emphasising violence, ambiguity and the cost of conquest.

In other media

The Western was a dominant genre of Hollywood film for half a century and of early television; the Italian "Spaghetti Westerns" of Sergio Leone reinvented it in the 1960s. Elements of the Western have migrated into science fiction and other genres, down to the "space western".
