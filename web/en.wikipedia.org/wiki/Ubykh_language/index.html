Ubykh is an extinct Northwest Caucasian language once spoken by the Ubykh people along the eastern coast of the Black Sea, in what is now the Sochi region of Russia. The language became extinct in 1992 when its last native speaker, Tevfik Esenç, died in Turkey.

The historical Ubykh homeland on the Black Sea coast of the Caucasus.

History

The Ubykhs were deported to the Ottoman Empire in 1864 following the Russian–Circassian War, and their communities in Turkey gradually shifted to Turkish and to the Circassian (Adyghe) language. Linguists including Georges Dumézil recorded Ubykh extensively in the twentieth century, working with Hüseyin Kozan and, finally, Tevfik Esenç (1906–1992), whose death marked the language's extinction as a native tongue. Efforts at revival have since been undertaken by descendants using the recorded corpus.

Phonology

Ubykh is famous among linguists for its exceptionally large consonant inventory — around 80 consonant phonemes in some analyses — combined with an extremely small vowel inventory of only two or three phonemic vowels. It features ejective, pharyngealised, and labialised consonants, and minimal vowel contrasts compensated by consonantal distinctions, giving it one of the largest consonant-to-vowel ratios of any known language.

Grammar

Ubykh is a polysynthetic, agglutinative, ergative language. Verbs can incorporate numerous affixes marking the agent, patient, benefactive, tense, aspect, and spatial relations, forming single words of great complexity. Nouns are comparatively simple, with limited case marking.
