Anglo-Norman literature is the body of literature written in Anglo-Norman, the variety of Norman French used in England after the Norman Conquest of 1066. For three centuries French was the language of the English court, law and aristocracy, and England — not France — produced some of the earliest and finest vernacular French writing, including the earliest surviving French verse narrative and history.

Characteristics

Anglo-Norman developed insular features distinguishing it from continental Old French: a simplified declension, distinctive spellings and vocabulary influenced by English. It remained a literary and administrative language in England long after the aristocracy became bilingual, and was still used in legal contexts ("Law French") into the early modern period.

Major works and genres

Early 12th-century works include Philippe de Thaon's Comput (1113) and Bestiaire, Gaimar's Estoire des Engleis, and the Oxford Song of Roland, the oldest surviving copy of the epic. Wace wrote the Roman de Brut (1155), which introduced King Arthur to French literature, and the Roman de Rou. Marie de France, writing at the English court, composed her Lais, fables and the Espurgatoire Seint Patriz. Benoît de Sainte-Maure produced the vast Roman de Troie and a chronicle of the Norman dukes for Henry II. Later centuries saw romances such as Gui de Warewic and Horn, saints' lives, religious drama (the Jeu d'Adam), satire and the chronicles of Jordan Fantosme and others. By the 15th century English had reasserted itself as the dominant literary language, though Anglo-Norman profoundly shaped Middle English vocabulary and verse forms.
