Alderney camps

The Alderney camps were prison camps built and operated by Nazi Germany during its occupation of the Channel Islands in the Second World War. The island of Alderney, whose civilian population had been almost entirely evacuated to England in June 1940, was the site of four labour camps: Helgoland, Norderney, Borkum and Sylt. They were the only camps of the Nazi camp system established on British soil.

History

After occupying the Channel Islands in 1940, the Germans began fortifying Alderney as part of the Atlantic Wall. From early 1942, thousands of forced labourers — prisoners of war, political prisoners and conscripted workers from across occupied Europe — were brought to the island under the Organisation Todt to build massive concrete fortifications. Conditions were brutal, with inadequate food, housing and medical care, and lethal violence by guards.

Three of the camps (Helgoland, Norderney and Borkum) housed forced labourers under Organisation Todt. The fourth, Lager Sylt, held Jewish prisoners initially under the Organisation Todt and from March 1943 was run by the SS as a subcamp of Neuengamme concentration camp, making it the only SS concentration camp on British soil.

Deaths and investigations

The number of deaths on Alderney remains a subject of historical research and debate. Official British investigations after the war recorded hundreds of deaths; estimates by researchers have ranged from several hundred to more than a thousand. In 2023–2024 an official review commissioned by the British government, led by Lord Eric Pickles, examined archival evidence and concluded that the number of deaths was likely between about 640 and 1,027, and found no evidence of the much higher figures claimed in some accounts.

Aftermath

The camps were evacuated or closed in 1944 as Allied forces advanced in Normandy. After the war, a British military court tried some personnel, though many responsible were never prosecuted. Little physical trace of the camps remains; memorials on Alderney commemorate the prisoners who died there, including at the Hammond Memorial, dedicated to the memory of the foreign workers who perished on the island.
