Marlborough Sounds

The Marlborough Sounds (Māori: Te Tauihu-o-te-Waka) are an extensive network of sea-drowned valleys at the northern end of the South Island of New Zealand. The sounds were created by a combination of land subsidence and rising sea levels after the last ice age, and form a coastline of some 1,200 kilometres of peninsulas, islands and sheltered inlets.

Geography

The principal waterways are Queen Charlotte Sound (Tōtaranui), Pelorus Sound (Te Hoiere) and Kenepuru Sound. The town of Picton, at the head of Queen Charlotte Sound, is the South Island terminal for the inter-island ferries from Wellington. Most of the area is administered by the Marlborough District Council, a unitary authority.

History

Māori traditions hold that the sounds mark the prow of the wrecked waka of the explorer Kupe. Captain James Cook used Ship Cove in Queen Charlotte Sound as a base during his voyages in the 1770s. In the nineteenth century, whaling stations operated at several sites, and Queen Charlotte Sound was the scene of early European settlement.

Economy and environment

Aquaculture — especially Greenshell mussels and king salmon — forestry, farming and tourism are the main industries. Much of the land is accessible only by boat, and many homes rely on marine transport rather than roads. Conservation issues include sedimentation from forestry, the effects of marine farms, and the recovery of native bush and birdlife on predator-free islands studied by marine biologists.
