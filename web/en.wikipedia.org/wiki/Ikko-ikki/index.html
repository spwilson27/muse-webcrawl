Ikkō-ikki

The Ikkō-ikki (Japanese: 一向一揆, "Ikkō-shū uprisings") were rebellious leagues of peasants, monks, and local warriors in 15th- and 16th-century Japan who followed the Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land) sect of Buddhism, also called Ikkō-shū. United by their devotion to the teachings of the priest Rennyo and their opposition to samurai rule, the Ikkō-ikki at their height controlled entire provinces and fielded armies that resisted the warlords of the Sengoku period for decades.

Ikkō-ikki forces combined peasant levies, monks, and dispossessed samurai.

Origins

Jōdo Shinshū, founded by Shinran in the 13th century, taught salvation through faith in Amida Buddha, expressed in the nembutsu chant. Under the charismatic leadership of Rennyo (1415–1499), the sect expanded explosively among peasants and low-ranking warriors. Local congregations (monto) organized themselves into leagues (ikki) bound by oaths, providing mutual defense against taxation and official oppression.

Kaga Province

The Ikkō-ikki's most famous achievement was the conquest of Kaga Province in 1488, when adherents overthrew the shugo (provincial governor) Togashi Masachika. Kaga remained under Ikkō-ikki rule for nearly a century — celebrated in later tradition as "the peasants' kingdom" — until it was conquered by Oda Nobunaga's general Shibata Katsuie in 1580.

Conflict with the warlords

The Ikkō-ikki were centered on fortified cathedral-monasteries such as Ishiyama Hongan-ji in Osaka and Nagashima near Nagoya. They fought both against and alongside Sengoku daimyō, and at times allied with the Hongan-ji's enemies or patrons as strategy dictated. Oda Nobunaga regarded them as a mortal threat and waged a brutal ten-year campaign, the Ishiyama Hongan-ji War (1570–1580), culminating in the negotiated surrender of the fortress. The massacre of the Nagashima Ikkō-ikki in 1574, in which tens of thousands perished, remains one of the bloodiest episodes of the era.

Legacy

Although militarily destroyed as an independent power by Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Jōdo Shinshū survived as a religious institution, and Hongan-ji was later split into eastern and western branches by Tokugawa Ieyasu to limit its influence. The Ikkō-ikki are remembered as a striking example of religiously organized commoner resistance in pre-modern Japan.
