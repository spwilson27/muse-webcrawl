Publius Decius Mus (died 340 BC) was a Roman consul of the Roman Republic remembered for his legendary self-sacrifice — the ritual of devotio — at the Battle of Vesuvius during the Latin War. He was the first of three generations of his family, the Decii Mures, said to have devoted themselves in battle to save the Roman army.

Career

Decius came from a plebeian family that rose to prominence in the 4th century BC. In 352 BC he served on a commission for settling debts, and as military tribune in 343 BC, during the First Samnite War, he saved a Roman army trapped in a defile near Suessula by seizing a hilltop position at night, an exploit celebrated by the historian Livy. He was elected consul for 340 BC together with Titus Manlius Torquatus.

The devotio at Vesuvius

According to Livy, before the decisive battle against the Latins near Vesuvius, both consuls dreamed that the army whose general devoted himself and the enemy host to the gods of the underworld would be victorious. When Decius's wing began to give way, he called the pontifex maximus, veiled his head, repeated the solemn formula of devotion, and rode alone into the enemy ranks, where he was killed. The Romans rallied and won the battle. His colleague Manlius, by contrast, is remembered for executing his own son for disobeying orders — the twin exemplars of Roman discipline and self-sacrifice.

Legacy

The story became one of Rome's most famous moral exempla. Decius's son, also Publius Decius Mus, reportedly repeated the devotio at the Battle of Sentinum in 295 BC, and his grandson at the Battle of Asculum in 279 BC. Historians debate the historicity of these repeated sacrifices, but the tradition powerfully expresses Roman ideals of duty, piety, and devotion to the state above personal survival.
