The Virginia opossum (Didelphis virginiana), also known as the North American opossum, is the only opossum found north of Mexico and the only marsupial native to North America north of the Rio Grande. A solitary and nocturnal animal about the size of a domestic cat, it is a successful opportunist that has expanded its range northward and westward since European colonisation.

Description

Adults weigh roughly 2–6 kilograms (4–14 lb) and have coarse greyish-white fur, a pointed white face with black ears, and a long, hairless, prehensile tail adapted for grasping branches. They have opposable clawless "thumbs" on the hind feet and fifty teeth — more than any other North American land mammal. Their body temperature is relatively low, which makes them poor hosts for the rabies virus.

Behaviour

Virginia opossums are omnivorous scavengers, eating insects, snails, small rodents, carrion, fruit, and garbage. They consume large numbers of ticks, which has drawn interest in their role in limiting Lyme disease. When threatened and unable to flee, an opossum may fall into an involuntary catatonic state resembling death — "playing possum" — complete with drooling, a lolling tongue, and the emission of a foul-smelling fluid, which can last from minutes to several hours.

Reproduction

Females give birth after a gestation of only about 12–13 days to litters of up to twenty or more tiny young, which crawl to the mother's pouch (marsupium) and attach to a teat. Usually no more than thirteen survive, matching the number of teats. After about two months in the pouch, the young ride on the mother's back for a further period before becoming independent. Wild opossums rarely live more than two years.

Distribution

The species ranges from Central America through Mexico and the eastern and central United States into southern Ontario, and introduced populations occur along the Pacific coast. It thrives in woodland, farmland, and suburban habitats near water.
