Cave

A cave is a natural void in the ground, specifically a space large enough for a human to enter. Caves form naturally by the weathering of rock and often extend deep underground. The study of caves is called speleology. Caves are found throughout the world; the longest surveyed system, Mammoth Cave in Kentucky, exceeds 680 kilometres of mapped passages.

Formation types

Solution caves, the most common type, form when mildly acidic groundwater dissolves soluble rock such as limestone, dolomite or gypsum, creating karst landscapes decorated with stalactites, stalagmites and other speleothems. Lava caves (lava tubes) form in flowing basaltic lava. Sea caves are cut by wave action along coasts, glacier caves melt within ice, and talus caves open between fallen boulders.

Ecology and human use

Caves provide habitats for specialised organisms — troglobites such as blind fish and cave crickets — and important roosts for bats. Humans have used caves since prehistory for shelter, burial and ritual art, as in the paintings of Lascaux and Altamira. Today caves are visited for tourism, scientific research and sport caving.
