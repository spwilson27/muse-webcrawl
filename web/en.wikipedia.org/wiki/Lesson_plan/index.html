Lesson plan

Lesson plan
ContextEducation; instructional design
ComponentsObjectives, materials, activities, assessment

A lesson plan is a teacher's detailed description of the course of instruction for a single lesson, or a sequence of lessons. It sets out what students are expected to learn (the objectives), how the material will be taught (activities, explanations, and materials), how long each part will take, and how learning will be checked (assessment). Lesson plans are a basic tool of professional teaching and are typically required of student teachers and inspected in many school systems.

Components

Common models of lesson planning include the "Herbartian" steps (preparation, presentation, association, generalization, application), the format popularized by Madeline Hunter (anticipatory set, objective, input, modeling, checking for understanding, guided and independent practice, closure), and Robert Gagné's nine events of instruction. A typical plan specifies learning objectives—often written using taxonomies such as Bloom's taxonomy—required materials, an opening or "hook", the main teaching sequence with differentiated tasks for different learners, formative checks, homework, and a closing summary. Longer-range planning nests lesson plans within unit plans and schemes of work derived from a curriculum.

Purpose and debate

Advocates argue that planning improves coherence, pacing, and alignment between objectives and assessment ("backward design", as popularized by Wiggins and McTighe, starts from desired outcomes and evidence of learning). Critics, including some experienced teachers, caution that rigid or excessively detailed plans can become bureaucratic exercises that substitute paperwork for responsiveness to students; others note that expert teachers often plan in outline and improvise in the classroom. Digital templates and shared repositories have made lesson-plan exchange common, for better and worse.
