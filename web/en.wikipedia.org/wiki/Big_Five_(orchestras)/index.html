Big Five (orchestras)

The Big Five is a traditional grouping of the five leading American symphony orchestras, a term originating in the mid-twentieth century when these ensembles were widely regarded as standing at the summit of US orchestral life. The five are the New York Philharmonic, the Boston Symphony Orchestra, the Chicago Symphony Orchestra, the Philadelphia Orchestra and the Cleveland Orchestra.

History of the term

The designation reflected the five orchestras' prestige, recording contracts, touring reach and famous music directors — figures such as Toscanini and Bernstein in New York, Koussevitzky and Ozawa in Boston, Reiner and Solti in Chicago, Stokowski and Ormandy in Philadelphia, and George Szell in Cleveland, whose exacting standards made the Cleveland Orchestra a byword for precision. The five also led the field in endowments, salaries and season length.

Criticism and changing landscape

By the late twentieth century, critics argued the label was outdated: orchestras such as the Los Angeles Philharmonic, San Francisco Symphony and Minnesota Orchestra rivalled or surpassed members of the five in innovation, finances or acclaim. A 2008 Gramophone-style critical consensus and shifting economics have made "Big Five" largely a historical term, though it is still used in journalism and marketing.
