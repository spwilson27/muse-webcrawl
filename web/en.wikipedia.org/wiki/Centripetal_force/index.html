Centripetal force

QuantityForce (vector)
DirectionToward the centre of curvature
SI unitNewton (N)
Contrasted withCentrifugal force (fictitious)

A centripetal force (from Latin centrum, "centre", and petere, "to seek") is a force that makes a body follow a curved path. The direction of the centripetal force is always orthogonal to the motion of the body and towards the fixed point of the instantaneous centre of curvature of the path. Isaac Newton described it as "a force by which bodies are drawn or impelled, or in any way tend, towards a point as to a centre".

Formula

For an object of mass m moving with speed v in a circle of radius r, the magnitude of the centripetal acceleration is a = v²/r, so the required net force is:

F = m v²/r = m ω²r

where ω is the angular velocity. "Centripetal force" is not a separate type of force: it is the name given to whatever real force (or component) supplies this inward acceleration — tension in a string, gravity for an orbiting satellite, friction between tyres and road for a turning car, or the normal force of a banked track.

Examples

  • A ball whirled on a string: tension provides the centripetal force; if the string breaks, the ball flies off tangentially.
  • The Moon's orbit: Earth's gravity provides the centripetal force.
  • A car rounding a curve: static friction, supplemented by banking of the road.
  • Electrons in the classical model of the atom: electrostatic attraction.

Relation to centrifugal force

In a reference frame rotating with the object, the object appears at rest and a fictitious (inertial) centrifugal force appears to balance the centripetal force. In the inertial frame no such outward force exists; the sensation of being "flung outward" is the effect of inertia — the body's tendency to continue in a straight line.
