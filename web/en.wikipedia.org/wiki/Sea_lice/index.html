Sea louse

Sea lice are marine ectoparasitic copepods of the family Caligidae, comprising roughly 559 species in 37 genera. Despite the name, they are not related to true lice (insects). They feed on the mucus, epidermal tissue and blood of host fish. The best-known species is the salmon louse, Lepeophtheirus salmonis, which parasitises salmonid fish, along with Caligus species that affect a wide range of marine fish.

Life cycle

Sea lice pass through free-swimming nauplius and copepodid stages before attaching to a host, where they develop through chalimus stages tethered by a frontal filament, then become mobile pre-adults and adults that graze over the fish's surface. Females carry paired egg strings and can produce many generations per year in warm water.

Impact on aquaculture

Sea lice are the most economically damaging parasite of farmed Atlantic salmon in Norway, Scotland, Canada, Chile and Ireland. Heavy infestations cause skin erosion, secondary infection and reduced growth, and lice larvae from farms can infest migrating wild juvenile salmon, a major conservation controversy. Control measures include chemical bath treatments and feed additives (against which resistance has developed), cleaner fish such as lumpfish and wrasse, lice skirts, thermolicers and laser systems.
