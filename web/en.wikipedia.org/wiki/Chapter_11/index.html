Chapter 11, Title 11, United States Code

Chapter 11 of the United States Bankruptcy Code (Title 11 of the United States Code) permits reorganization under the bankruptcy laws of the United States. It is most commonly used by businesses, which remain in control of their operations as a "debtor in possession" while they restructure their debts under the supervision of a bankruptcy court, but it is also available to individuals. A debtor usually proposes a plan of reorganization to keep its business alive and pay creditors over time.

Overview

A Chapter 11 case begins with the filing of a petition, usually voluntary, in a federal bankruptcy court. Filing triggers the automatic stay, which halts most collection actions, lawsuits, foreclosures and repossessions against the debtor. Unlike Chapter 7 liquidation, Chapter 11 does not require the appointment of a trustee in the ordinary course; the debtor remains in possession of its property and operates the business, though a trustee or examiner may be appointed for cause, such as fraud or mismanagement.

Plan of reorganization

The central feature of Chapter 11 is the plan of reorganization. The debtor has an initial exclusivity period (120 days, extendable by the court up to a statutory maximum) in which to file a plan. Creditors are divided into classes, and classes vote on the plan; the court may confirm a plan over the objection of a dissenting class through the "cramdown" provisions if statutory requirements are met, including that the plan does not discriminate unfairly and is fair and equitable. Upon confirmation, the debtor is discharged from most pre-confirmation debts and must make payments according to the plan.

Notable cases

Many of the largest bankruptcies in American history have been Chapter 11 cases, including Lehman Brothers (2008), Washington Mutual, General Motors and Chrysler (2009), Enron (2001) and Texaco (1987). Several major airlines, retailers and energy companies have used Chapter 11 to reject burdensome leases and contracts, restructure pension obligations and emerge as smaller, reorganized companies; some, however, ultimately convert to Chapter 7 liquidation.

Small business and individual cases

The Small Business Reorganization Act of 2019 created Subchapter V of Chapter 11, a streamlined process for small business debtors with a standing trustee who facilitates plan confirmation. Individuals with debts above the Chapter 13 limits may also file under Chapter 11, subject to special rules introduced by the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005.
