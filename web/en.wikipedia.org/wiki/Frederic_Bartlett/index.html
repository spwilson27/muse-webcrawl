Frederic Bartlett

Sir Frederic Charles Bartlett FRS (20 October 1886 – 30 September 1969) was a British psychologist and the first professor of experimental psychology at the University of Cambridge. Best known for his 1932 book Remembering, which treated memory as a reconstructive, schema-driven process, he was one of the forerunners of modern cognitive psychology.

Born: 20 October 1886, Stow-on-the-Wold, England
Died: 30 September 1969, Cambridge, England
Institution: University of Cambridge
Known for: Schema theory; reconstructive memory

Career

Bartlett studied at St John's College, Cambridge, and was influenced by the anthropologist W. H. R. Rivers. He directed the Cambridge Psychological Laboratory from 1922 and in 1931 became the university's first professor of experimental psychology, a chair he held until 1951. During the Second World War he directed applied research on training, fatigue and equipment design for the Royal Air Force, founding the Applied Psychology Unit at Cambridge. He was knighted in 1948, was president of the British Psychological Society, and edited the British Journal of Psychology for many years.

Remembering and schema theory

In Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology (1932), Bartlett reported experiments in which British participants read and repeatedly reproduced an unfamiliar Native American folktale, "The War of the Ghosts". Retellings systematically shortened, rationalised and reshaped the story to fit the participants' own cultural expectations. Bartlett concluded that remembering is not the replaying of fixed traces but an imaginative reconstruction guided by schemas — organised masses of past reactions that actively shape what is recalled. The book was neglected during the behaviourist decades but was rediscovered by cognitive psychologists in the 1960s and 1970s, and its serial-reproduction studies remain classics of memory research. His later work, Thinking (1958), extended the same approach to problem solving.
