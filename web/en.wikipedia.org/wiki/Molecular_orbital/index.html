Molecular orbital

In chemistry, a molecular orbital (MO) is a mathematical function describing the location and wave-like behaviour of an electron in a molecule. Molecular orbitals are constructed from linear combinations of atomic orbitals (LCAO) and are used to predict chemical and physical properties such as bond order, magnetism and electronic spectra. The approach, known as molecular orbital theory, was developed in the late 1920s by Friedrich Hund and Robert S. Mulliken.

Bonding and antibonding orbitals

When two atomic orbitals combine, they produce a bonding orbital (lower in energy, with electron density concentrated between the nuclei) and an antibonding orbital (higher in energy, with a node between the nuclei, designated by an asterisk, e.g. σ*). Orbitals are classified by symmetry as sigma (σ), pi (π) or delta (δ). Electrons fill molecular orbitals according to the Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion principle and Hund's rule, as in atomic structure. The bond order is calculated as half the difference between the numbers of bonding and antibonding electrons.

Applications

Molecular orbital theory famously explains the paramagnetism of oxygen, which valence bond theory fails to predict: the MO diagram of O2 places two unpaired electrons in degenerate π* antibonding orbitals. The theory also underlies the concepts of the HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital), frontier orbital theory, and modern computational chemistry methods such as Hartree–Fock and density functional theory.
