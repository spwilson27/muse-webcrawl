Irish literature

Irish literature is literature written in the Irish language, Latin, and the Irish variety of English on the island of Ireland. It has one of the oldest vernacular traditions in western Europe: Irish-language writing dates from the early medieval period, and Ireland has produced four Nobel laureates in literature — W. B. Yeats, George Bernard Shaw, Samuel Beckett and Seamus Heaney.

Early and medieval literature

The earliest Irish literature consists of ogham inscriptions and, from the 6th century, monastic manuscripts preserving mythological cycles — the Mythological Cycle, the Ulster Cycle (including the epic Táin Bó Cúailnge), the Fenian Cycle and the Cycle of the Kings. Latin works by Irish scholars such as Columbanus circulated across Europe, and illuminated manuscripts like the Book of Kells represent the high point of Insular art.

Early modern to the nineteenth century

The decline of the Gaelic order after the 17th century drove Irish-language poetry into folk and bardic traditions, while English-language writing from Ireland flourished with Jonathan Swift, Oliver Goldsmith, Maria Edgeworth and Bram Stoker, author of Dracula (1897).

The Irish Literary Revival

The late 19th- and early 20th-century revival, led by Yeats, Lady Gregory and J. M. Synge, founded the Abbey Theatre in 1904 and reshaped Irish cultural identity. James Joyce's Ulysses (1922) became a landmark of modernism, while Beckett, writing in English and French, redefined drama with Waiting for Godot.

Contemporary literature

Post-war and contemporary Irish writing includes poets Seamus Heaney and Eavan Boland, novelists John McGahern, Edna O'Brien, Colm Tóibín, Anne Enright and Sally Rooney, and a continuing tradition of Irish-language writing.
