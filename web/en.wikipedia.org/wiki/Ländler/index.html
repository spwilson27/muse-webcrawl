Ländler

The Ländler is a folk dance in 3/4 time popular in Austria, Bavaria, German-speaking Switzerland and Slovenia from the late 18th century into the 19th. Danced by couples, it features hopping, stamping, clapping and close turning figures, and is generally regarded as a precursor of the waltz: slower and more earthy than its ballroom descendant, it kept partners in a close embrace that was considered somewhat scandalous when it first reached towns and courts.

Characteristics

The name likely derives from Land (countryside) or from the Austrian region of Land ob der Enns. The music is typically in a moderate triple meter with a strong accented first beat, played by small ensembles of fiddles, clarinets, brass and accordion. The dance itself is improvised within conventions: couples circle with intricate arm movements, and men show off with Schuhplattler-like stamping, thigh-slapping and yodelling in some regional variants.

In art music

Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven and especially Schubert composed sets of Ländler for piano and for dances in Vienna, where the style coloured the early waltz. Schubert's many Ländler and the dance scenes in his songs helped domesticate the rustic form for the bourgeois salon. Mahler used distorted Ländler in his symphonies as an evocation — sometimes nostalgic, sometimes grotesque — of Austrian folk life. The dance also survives in popular culture: the choreography Maria and Captain von Trapp perform in The Sound of Music is a Ländler, and it features in the ball scene tradition of Alpine folk festivals today.
