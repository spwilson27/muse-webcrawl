Eilmer of Malmesbury (also known as Oliver) was an 11th-century English Benedictine monk from Malmesbury Abbey who is best known for an early attempt at human flight, in which he strapped wings to his hands and feet and leapt from a tower. The attempt, usually dated to around 1010, reportedly carried him some 200 metres (roughly a furlong, in the chronicler's account) before he crashed, breaking both legs.

The flight

The story is recorded by the historian William of Malmesbury in his Gesta Regum Anglorum (c. 1125). William wrote that Eilmer, as a young man, had read and believed the Greek myth of Daedalus, and so fixed wings to his hands and feet and launched himself from the top of a tower at Malmesbury. He flew "for more than a furlong" before losing control; William attributes the crash to "the violence of the wind and the eddies" combined with Eilmer's sudden panic. Eilmer himself reportedly blamed his failure on not having fitted himself with a tail.

Eilmer survived the fall but was lame for the rest of his life. William also records that the elderly Eilmer, in 1066, witnessed Halley's Comet and lamented it as an ill omen — he had seen the same comet as a boy, which places his birth in the late 10th century.

Legacy

Eilmer is often counted among the pioneers of aviation, alongside figures such as Abbas ibn Firnas of al-Andalus, who made a similar gliding attempt two centuries earlier. A stained-glass window at Malmesbury Abbey commemorates his flight, and he is sometimes called "the flying monk."
