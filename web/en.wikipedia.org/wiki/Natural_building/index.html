Natural building

Natural building
FocusSustainable construction from minimally processed, locally available materials
Common materialsEarth (cob, adobe, rammed earth), straw, timber, stone, lime
Related movementsGreen building, permaculture, appropriate technology

Natural building is an approach to construction that emphasises sustainability through the use of minimally processed, plentiful, or renewable materials, ideally sourced close to the building site. Drawing on vernacular traditions from around the world, it seeks to reduce the embodied energy and toxicity associated with industrial materials such as concrete, steel, and synthetic insulation, while creating healthy indoor environments that regulate humidity and temperature passively.

Techniques

Principal methods include cob (monolithic earth mixed with straw and sand), adobe (sun-dried earth bricks), rammed earth (earth compacted in formwork), straw-bale construction (bales used as infill or load-bearing walls, rendered with lime or earth plaster), earthbag building, cordwood masonry, and timber framing with natural infill such as light straw-clay. Finishes are typically breathable lime or clay plasters, and roofs may be thatched, shingled, or planted as living roofs.

History and movement

Although earth and straw building techniques are ancient — a large share of the world's population still lives in earthen homes — the modern natural building movement emerged in the 1970s–1990s, notably through revivalists of cob in Oregon and Britain and the straw-bale revival in the American Southwest. Proponents argue such buildings can match conventional durability (medieval cob houses remain in use in Devon, England) while challenges include building-code acceptance, labour intensity, and performance in wet climates without careful detailing ("good boots and a good hat": raised foundations and generous roof overhangs).
