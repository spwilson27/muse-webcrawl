Asaph Hall III (October 15, 1829 – November 22, 1907) was an American astronomer who is most famous for having discovered the two moons of Mars, Deimos and Phobos, in 1877. He determined the orbits of satellites of other planets and of double stars, the rotation of Saturn, and the mass of Mars.

Biography

Hall was born in Goshen, Connecticut, a son of Asaph Hall II, a clockmaker, and Hannah (Palmer) Hall. His father died when he was 13, leaving the family in financial difficulty, so he left school at 16 to become an apprentice carpenter. He soon afterward enrolled at Central College in McGrawville, New York, where he studied geometry and German, and where he met Angeline Stickney, a student of geometry and German who became his wife in 1856.

After leaving college, the Halls moved to Ann Arbor, Michigan, where Asaph attended the University of Michigan and studied astronomy under Franz Brünnow. Financial problems forced him to leave in 1857, and he took a position at the Harvard College Observatory in Cambridge, Massachusetts, where he became an expert computer of orbits. In 1862 he joined the staff of the United States Naval Observatory in Washington, D.C., and within a year of his arrival he was made a professor. He remained at the Naval Observatory for the rest of his career, retiring in 1891 as a captain in the United States Navy.

Discovery of the moons of Mars

In August 1877, Mars came to a particularly favorable opposition, and Hall resolved to search for Martian satellites using the observatory's 26-inch (66 cm) refracting telescope, then the largest refractor in the world. The chief difficulty was the glare of the planet itself, which drowned out any faint nearby object. Hall devised a method of shielding his eye from the planet's light, and on August 12, 1877, he spotted a faint object near Mars — what would prove to be the outer moon, Deimos. Discouraged by poor weather and faint results, he nearly abandoned the search, but his wife Angeline urged him to continue. On August 17 he found a second, inner object, and on the night of August 17–18 he confirmed that the inner body was moving with extraordinary speed around the planet, completing an orbit in less than a Martian day. This inner moon was Phobos.

The names Phobos ("fear") and Deimos ("panic" or "dread") were suggested by Henry Madan of Eton College, after the attendants of Ares in Book XV of the Iliad. Hall's discovery was one of the celebrated astronomical achievements of the nineteenth century, and it retrospectively lent interest to Jonathan Swift's fictional mention of two Martian moons in Gulliver's Travels (1726) and to a similar speculation by Voltaire.

Other work

Hall made extensive observations of double stars and of the satellites of Saturn, Jupiter, and Uranus. He determined the mass of Mars from the motions of its satellites, measured stellar parallaxes, and in 1892 he determined the rotation period of Saturn from a bright spot observed on the planet. He published numerous papers on celestial mechanics and positional astronomy and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences. A crater on the Moon, Hall crater, and a crater on Phobos are named in his honor, as is the asteroid 2023 Asaph.

Family

Hall's son Asaph Hall Jr. also became an astronomer at the Naval Observatory, and another son, Percival Hall, became president of Gallaudet University. Angeline Stickney Hall, whose encouragement Hall credited for the discovery of the moons, has a crater on Phobos named Stickney after her — the largest crater on that moon.
