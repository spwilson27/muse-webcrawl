Electrostatics

Electrostatics is a branch of physics that studies slow-moving or stationary electric charges. Since classical antiquity it has been known that some materials, such as amber, attract lightweight particles after rubbing. The Greek word for amber, ἤλεκτρον (ēlektron), was the source of the word "electricity". Electrostatic phenomena arise from the forces that electric charges exert on each other, described by Coulomb's law.

History

Thales of Miletus (c. 624–546 BC) is credited with noting that amber rubbed with fur attracts feathers and straw. Systematic study began with William Gilbert's De Magnete (1600), which coined the Latin electricus. In the 18th century Benjamin Franklin proposed the single-fluid theory of charge and the principle of charge conservation; Charles-Augustin de Coulomb used a torsion balance in 1785 to establish the inverse-square law of force between charges. Michael Faraday introduced the concept of the electric field, and Carl Friedrich Gauss formulated the flux law now called Gauss's law.

Coulomb's law

The magnitude of the electrostatic force F between two point charges q1 and q2 separated by distance r is

F = (1 / 4πε₀) · |q₁q₂| / r²

where ε₀ is the permittivity of free space. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs and repulsive if they have the same sign. The Coulomb is an example of an inverse-square law, formally analogous to Newton's law of universal gravitation.

The electrostatic field

The electric field E at a point is defined as the force per unit charge. For static charge distributions the field is conservative and can be written as the negative gradient of a scalar electric potential V: E = −∇V. Gauss's law states that the total electric flux through a closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by ε₀, providing a powerful method for computing fields of symmetric charge distributions.

Applications and phenomena

Electrostatic phenomena include lightning, the attraction of plastic wrap to the hand, electrostatic discharge that can damage electronics, and the operation of photocopiers and laser printers, which use charged drums. Industrial applications include electrostatic precipitators that remove particulates from flue gas, electrostatic painting, and xerography. Triboelectric charging — charge transfer by contact and friction — underlies static cling and is exploited in nanogenerators that harvest mechanical energy.
