John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh (12 November 1842 – 30 June 1919), was an English physicist and mathematician who, with William Ramsay, discovered the element argon — work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1904. He also provided the theoretical explanation of the scattering of light by particles much smaller than its wavelength, now known as Rayleigh scattering, which accounts for the blue color of the sky.

Life and career

Strutt was educated at Harrow and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he graduated as Senior Wrangler in 1865. In 1873 he succeeded his father as Baron Rayleigh and devoted himself to research at a private laboratory at Terling Place, the family seat in Essex. From 1879 to 1884 he succeeded James Clerk Maxwell as Cavendish Professor of Experimental Physics at Cambridge, where he built up the Cavendish Laboratory's teaching program and established precise standards of electrical measurement (the ohm, ampere, and volt). He later served as president of the Royal Society (1905–1908) and chancellor of Cambridge University (1908–1919).

Scientific work

Rayleigh's work spanned an extraordinary range: acoustics, optics, hydrodynamics, electromagnetism, and the theory of vibrations. His two-volume The Theory of Sound (1877–78) remains a classic of mathematical physics. He described surface waves in solids ("Rayleigh waves"), important in seismology; derived the Rayleigh–Jeans law of black-body radiation (1900); and studied fluid instabilities (Rayleigh–Taylor instability) and dimensionless numbers (the Rayleigh number in convection). His discovery of argon arose from meticulous measurements showing that nitrogen prepared from air was denser than nitrogen from chemical compounds — the tiny discrepancy led, with Ramsay, to isolating the inert gas in 1894.

Personal life and legacy

Rayleigh married Evelyn Balfour, sister of Arthur Balfour, in 1871; their son Robert John Strutt became a noted physicist in his own right. Strutt died at Terling Place in 1919. The lunar and Martian craters Rayleigh are named for him, as are numerous physical phenomena and the "rayl," a unit of acoustic impedance. He was the only person to chair both the Royal Society and to preside over the Society for Psychical Research, reflecting his unusually wide interests.
