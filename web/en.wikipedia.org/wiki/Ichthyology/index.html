Ichthyology

Ichthyology is the branch of zoology devoted to the study of fish, including bony fish (Osteichthyes), cartilaginous fish (Chondrichthyes), and jawless fish (Agnatha). The word derives from the Greek ikhthus (ἰχθύς), "fish", and logos, "study". More than 34,000 fish species have been described — more than all other vertebrates combined — and several hundred new species are described each year.

Scope

Ichthyologists study fish taxonomy and systematics, anatomy and physiology, ecology and behaviour, evolution, and conservation, in institutions such as natural history museums, universities, aquariums, and fisheries agencies. The discipline supports fisheries management, aquaculture, and biodiversity conservation, and maintains global catalogues such as the Catalog of Fishes and FishBase.

History

Aristotle recorded observations on more than 100 Mediterranean fish species. Modern systematic ichthyology began with Peter Artedi, the "father of ichthyology", whose posthumous Ichthyologia (1738) structured the classification his friend Linnaeus incorporated into Systema Naturae. Major figures include Marcus Elieser Bloch, Georges Cuvier and Achille Valenciennes (the 22-volume Histoire naturelle des poissons), Albert Günther, and David Starr Jordan. The American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists, founded in 1913, publishes the journal Ichthyology & Herpetology (formerly Copeia).
