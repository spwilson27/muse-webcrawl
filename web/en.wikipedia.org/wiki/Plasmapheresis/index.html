Plasmapheresis

Apheresis equipment used for plasma exchange

Plasmapheresis (from the Greek plasma and aphairesis, "taking away") is the removal, treatment, and return or exchange of blood plasma or its components from and to the blood circulation. It is thus an extracorporeal therapy, a medical procedure performed outside the body.

Method

Blood is withdrawn from the patient and passed through an apheresis device that separates plasma from the cellular components, either by centrifugation or by filtration through a membrane. In therapeutic plasma exchange, the removed plasma is discarded and replaced with a substitute fluid, typically albumin solution or donor plasma; in other forms the plasma is treated and returned. A single session usually processes one to one-and-a-half plasma volumes and takes two to three hours.

Uses

Plasma exchange is used to remove harmful substances circulating in the plasma, especially autoantibodies, immune complexes, and paraproteins. Established indications include Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis in crisis, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, Goodpasture syndrome, and some cases of vasculitis and hyperviscosity syndrome. Plasmapheresis is also the method by which source plasma is collected from donors for manufacture of plasma-derived medicines, such as immunoglobulin and clotting factors.

Complications

Risks include low blood pressure, allergic reactions to replacement fluids, citrate-induced hypocalcaemia (tingling, nausea), infection related to vascular access, and depletion of clotting factors after repeated exchanges with albumin replacement.
