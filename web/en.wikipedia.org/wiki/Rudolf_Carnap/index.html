Rudolf Carnap (18 May 1891 – 14 September 1970) was a German-language philosopher who was active in Europe before 1935 and in the United States thereafter. He was a leading member of the Vienna Circle and an influential exponent of logical positivism (logical empiricism). Carnap made major contributions to logic, the philosophy of science, semantics, and the theory of probability.

Carnap studied physics, mathematics, and philosophy at the universities of Jena and Freiburg, attending Gottlob Frege's lectures on mathematical logic at Jena. He received his doctorate in 1921 with a thesis on the concept of space.

Major works

His Der logische Aufbau der Welt (1928; The Logical Structure of the World) attempted to show how all scientific concepts could be defined on the basis of experience within a single system. In The Logical Syntax of Language (1934) he argued that the logic of science is the syntax of the language of science, and advanced the "principle of tolerance": in logic, there are no morals — everyone may build their own logic, their own language, as they wish.

Vienna Circle and verificationism

Carnap joined the Vienna Circle around Moritz Schlick in 1926 and co-authored its 1929 manifesto. He defended a verificationist criterion of meaning, holding that many traditional metaphysical statements are cognitively meaningless because they are neither empirically verifiable nor analytic. His 1932 essay "The Elimination of Metaphysics Through Logical Analysis of Language" famously applied this verdict to Heidegger's metaphysics.

Later career

Fleeing Nazism, Carnap emigrated to the United States in 1935, teaching at the University of Chicago (1936–1952) alongside Charles Morris, and later at UCLA. His later work included Meaning and Necessity (1947) on semantics and modal logic, Logical Foundations of Probability (1950), which developed inductive logic and the concept of degree of confirmation, and the influential distinction between "internal" and "external" questions in "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950). Carnap's debates with Quine, Popper, and Neurath shaped the course of twentieth-century analytic philosophy.
