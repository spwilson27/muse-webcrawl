ABO blood group system
Discovered1900–1901, by Karl Landsteiner
GroupsA, B, AB, O
GeneABO, chromosome 9q34
ImportanceBlood transfusion compatibility

The ABO blood group system is the most important of the human blood group systems in transfusion medicine. It classifies blood by the presence of A and B antigens on the surface of red blood cells and of anti-A and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. The Austrian immunologist Karl Landsteiner discovered the system in 1900, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930.

The groups

  • Group A: A antigens on red cells; anti-B antibodies in plasma.
  • Group B: B antigens on red cells; anti-A antibodies in plasma.
  • Group AB: both A and B antigens; neither antibody — universal recipients of red cells.
  • Group O: neither antigen; both anti-A and anti-B antibodies — universal red-cell donors (O negative especially).

Transfusing incompatible blood can cause a fatal haemolytic reaction, because the recipient's antibodies attack the donor red cells. The ABO system is inherited: the ABO gene on chromosome 9 encodes glycosyltransferase enzymes that build the A and B antigens from the precursor H antigen.

Distribution and significance

Group O is the most common worldwide, with especially high frequencies among Indigenous peoples of the Americas; group B is most frequent in Central Asia and northern India. Beyond transfusion, ABO type has been associated with modest differences in risk of certain diseases, including cardiovascular disease, some cancers, and susceptibility to infections such as cholera and norovirus.
