The Bengal Fan, also known as the Bengal Deep Sea Fan or Ganges Fan, is the largest submarine fan on Earth. It covers much of the floor of the Bay of Bengal in the northern Indian Ocean, extending roughly 3,000 kilometres (1,900 miles) from the continental shelf off Bangladesh and eastern India southward to latitudes south of Sri Lanka, and up to about 1,430 kilometres (890 miles) wide.

The fan is composed overwhelmingly of sediment eroded from the Himalayas and carried to the sea by the Ganges and Brahmaputra rivers, whose combined sediment load — historically around a billion tonnes per year — is among the largest of any river system on Earth.

Formation

The fan began to form following the collision of the Indian Plate with Asia in the early Eocene, roughly 55–50 million years ago, which raised the Himalayas and initiated large-scale erosion. Sediment delivered to the coast built a delta and was carried offshore, first onto the continental slope and then, via turbidity currents flowing down submarine channels, across the abyssal plain.

Near the shelf edge the accumulated pile of sediment reaches thicknesses of up to about 16.5 kilometres, making it one of the thickest sedimentary accumulations anywhere in the oceans. The presently active channel, the "Swatch of No Ground", incises the shelf and feeds a leveed channel system that runs the length of the fan.

Structure

The Bengal Fan is commonly divided into upper, middle and lower fan regions. The upper fan has a single active channel with natural levees; the middle fan has multiple channels; and the lower fan is a broad, smooth depositional surface. East of the Ninety East Ridge, the continuation of the system is sometimes distinguished as the Nicobar Fan. Buried older channels, cut off as the active channel migrated or was diverted, are preserved within the fan body.

Scientific study

Because its sediments are derived almost entirely from the Himalaya–Tibet orogen, the Bengal Fan provides a long record of mountain erosion and of the South Asian monsoon. It has been drilled by the Deep Sea Drilling Project (Leg 22), the Ocean Drilling Program (Leg 116) and the International Ocean Discovery Program (Expedition 354), whose cores are used to reconstruct the history of Himalayan uplift, weathering and climate over tens of millions of years.
