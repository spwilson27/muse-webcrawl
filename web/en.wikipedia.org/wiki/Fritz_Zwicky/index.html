Fritz Zwicky

Fritz Zwicky (1898–1974).

Fritz Zwicky (14 February 1898 – 8 February 1974) was a Swiss astronomer who worked mostly at the California Institute of Technology and made foundational contributions to supernova research, galaxy clusters and the case for dark matter. He was a prolific and combative thinker who coined several enduring terms of astrophysics and pioneered the study of cosmic rays, jet propulsion and morphological analysis.

Supernovae and neutron stars

With Walter Baade, Zwicky coined the term "supernova" in 1934 and argued that supernovae mark the collapse of ordinary stars into extremely dense neutron stars, and that they accelerate cosmic rays — ideas confirmed decades later. Their systematic supernova searches using the Palomar Observatory telescopes discovered more than 120 supernovae and laid the foundations for modern supernova surveys.

Dark matter

In 1933 Zwicky studied the Coma Cluster of galaxies and found that its member galaxies moved far too fast for the cluster to be held together by the gravity of its visible mass. He concluded that most of the cluster's mass was invisible, calling it dunkle Materie ("dark matter"). The inference was largely ignored for four decades until galaxy rotation curves confirmed the discrepancy. Zwicky also predicted gravitational lensing by galaxies, pioneered the concept of compact galaxies, and published the six-volume Catalogue of Galaxies and of Clusters of Galaxies (1961–68).
