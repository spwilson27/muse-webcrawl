15-minute city
Concept byCarlos Moreno
Popularised2016 onwards; Paris under Anne Hidalgo
Key functionsDwell, work, commerce, healthcare, education, entertainment

The 15-minute city (French: ville du quart d'heure) is an urban planning concept in which most daily necessities and services, such as work, shopping, education, healthcare, and leisure, can be easily reached by a 15-minute walk, bike ride, or public transit ride from any point in the city. This approach aims to reduce reliance on cars, improve public health, and promote sustainability and liveability.

History

The concept was popularized by the French-Colombian scientist Carlos Moreno, a professor at the Sorbonne, in 2016, and was adopted as a central policy by Paris mayor Anne Hidalgo during her 2020 re-election campaign. It draws on older ideas of neighbourhood planning, including Clarence Perry's "neighbourhood unit" of the 1920s and the garden city movement, as well as Jane Jacobs's advocacy for dense, mixed-use neighbourhoods.

Key principles

Moreno identifies six essential urban functions: living, working, commerce, healthcare, education, and entertainment. These are supported by four guiding principles: density, proximity, diversity, and ubiquity (digitalization). The concept is also described as "chrono-urbanism" — organizing urban life around time rather than distance.

Implementation

Cities that have embraced versions of the concept include Paris (France), Melbourne (Australia, with its "20-minute neighbourhoods"), Portland (Oregon, USA), Milan (Italy), Chengdu (China), and Utrecht (Netherlands). Implementations typically involve converting car space to pedestrian and cycle infrastructure, adding local services, and redesigning streets as shared spaces.

Criticism

Critics have argued that the concept may be impractical in low-density suburbs, could raise local housing prices, and may conflict with specialized services that cannot be provided in every neighbourhood. In the early 2020s the concept also became the subject of conspiracy theories claiming it was a pretext for restricting freedom of movement, which planners and fact-checkers have disputed. Supporters respond that the model adds options rather than removing them.
