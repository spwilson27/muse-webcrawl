Foundations of mathematics

The foundations of mathematics are the study of the logical and philosophical basis of mathematics: the basic concepts and principles from which mathematical theories are developed, and the nature of mathematical truth and proof. In modern practice, foundations are usually framed in axiomatic set theory, in particular Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC), which serves as a common framework in which most of mathematics can be formalized.

Historical development

Greek mathematics, exemplified by Euclid's Elements, established the axiomatic method. In the 19th century, the arithmetization of analysis, non-Euclidean geometry, and paradoxes in naive set theory (such as Russell's paradox, 1901) provoked a "foundational crisis". Three schools emerged: logicism (Frege, Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica), which sought to reduce mathematics to logic; formalism (Hilbert), which sought finitary consistency proofs; and intuitionism (Brouwer), which rejected non-constructive methods such as the law of excluded middle for infinite sets.

Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed the limits of formalization: no consistent system strong enough for arithmetic proves all arithmetic truths or its own consistency. Cohen's and Gödel's results on the independence of the continuum hypothesis further shaped the field. Alternative foundations now studied include category theory and type theory (including homotopy type theory).
