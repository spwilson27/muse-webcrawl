Performance art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A lone artist seated motionless in a gallery facing visitors one by one, in the still, durational manner of Marina Abramović's The Artist Is Present (2010)]

Performance art is an artwork presented live, usually by the artist or performers acting on the artist's instructions, in which the artist's body and actions are the medium. With roots in Futurist and Dada cabaret, it took shape in the 1950s–60s happenings, Fluxus events and Viennese Actionism, and by the 1970s — with artists such as Marina Abramović, Chris Burden, Joseph Beuys and Yoko Ono — was an established, if unruly, art form.

Canonical works include Ono's Cut Piece (1964), Beuys's How to Explain Pictures to a Dead Hare (1965) and I Like America and America Likes Me (1974, living with a coyote), Burden's Shoot (1971, shot in the arm by an assistant), and Abramović's Rhythm 0 (1974) and her 736-hour sitting The Artist Is Present at MoMA (2010).

Characteristics

Performance art is generally conceptual, ephemeral and resistant to sale — though photographs, relics and re-performances complicate its afterlife, and museums now programme it centrally. It draws on theatre while denying theatre's fictions: the actions are real, risks sometimes genuinely dangerous, and the audience's presence part of the work.

Developments

From 1980s body and identity-based work (Orlan, Ana Mendieta, Adrian Piper) to today's social-practice and durational pieces livestreamed online, performance remains the art world's laboratory for testing what an artwork — and an audience — is.
