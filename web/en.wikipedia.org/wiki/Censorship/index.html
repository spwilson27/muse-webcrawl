Censorship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Censorship is the suppression of speech, public communication or other information that is considered objectionable, harmful, sensitive or inconvenient by authorities or institutions. It may be exercised by governments (by law, licensing, prosecution or ownership of media), by private bodies (employers, platforms, publishers) and by self-censorship under threat. The office of censor is ancient — Rome's censors policed morals; the Catholic Church's Index Librorum Prohibitorum (1559–1966) listed forbidden books; licensing of printing shaped early modern Europe until Milton's Areopagitica (1644) made the classic case against pre-publication licensing.

Modern censorship operates through many mechanisms: prior restraint and licensing, criminal laws (sedition, blasphemy, “fake news”, lèse-majesté), internet filtering and shutdowns (from China's “Great Firewall” to wartime blocking), economic pressure on media owners and advertisers, and private platform moderation at scale. International law permits only narrow restrictions on expression (ICCPR Article 19(3), proportionate and prescribed by law); press-freedom indices (RSF, Freedom House) rank most states as falling short in some degree. Defenders of particular restrictions cite harm prevention — child protection, incitement, national security — while critics warn of mission creep and the “heckler's veto”.
