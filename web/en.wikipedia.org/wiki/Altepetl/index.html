Altepetl

Altepetl (Nahuatl: āltepētl, from ātl, "water", and tepētl, "hill") was the fundamental political unit of pre-Columbian and early colonial Nahua society in central Mexico — effectively a local city-state or ethnic state.

Organization

Each altepetl was ruled by a hereditary lord, the tlatoani, and comprised a principal town with its market, temple, and palace, together with constituent subdivisions called calpolli. Altepetl owed tribute and labour to the ruler and maintained its own dynastic history and patron deity. Larger powers such as Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlaxcala were themselves composed of or stood at the head of multiple altepetl.

After the conquest

Following the Spanish conquest, altepetl structures underlay the colonial pueblos de indios and municipalities, and Nahua communities continued to organize their records, elections, and landholding around the altepetl for centuries.

Etymology

The paired metaphor "water-hill" evokes the life-sustaining territory of the community; the term is often paired with the honorific atoyatl tepetl in formal speech.
