Tuvan (Тыва дыл, Tyva dyl) is a Turkic language spoken in the Republic of Tuva in south-central Siberia, Russia, and by smaller communities in Mongolia and China. It belongs to the Siberian (Northeastern) branch of the Turkic languages and is closely related to Tofa and Soyot.

Features

Tuvan is notable for its phonemic vowel length and for a system of low/high ("pharyngealized" vs. plain) vowel articulation. It has borrowed extensively from Mongolian, reflecting centuries of contact, and more recently from Russian. The language is agglutinative with vowel harmony, like other Turkic languages.

Writing

Tuvan was written in Mongolian script before the 20th century. A Latin-based alphabet was created in 1930 under the linguist Alexander Palmbach, but was replaced by a Cyrillic-based script in 1943, still in use today.

Culture

Tuvan is the language of the internationally known tradition of throat singing (khöömei), in which a single vocalist produces two or more pitches simultaneously. American physicist Richard Feynman famously developed a fascination with Tuva, documented in the book Tuva or Bust!
