Geʻez

Geʻez (ግእዝ, Gəʿəz), also spelled Ge'ez and sometimes called Ethiopic, is an ancient South Semitic language of the Ethiopian and Eritrean highlands. It originated as the spoken language of the Kingdom of Aksum in what is now northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, and remains in use as the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and the Beta Israel Jewish community.

History

The earliest inscriptions in Geʻez date to the 4th century AD, during the reign of King Ezana of Aksum, whose conversion to Christianity around 330 AD led to the translation of the Bible into Geʻez. These biblical translations, together with original theological, historical, and liturgical works, created a rich literary tradition that continued for more than a millennium.

Geʻez ceased to be a widely spoken vernacular sometime between the 10th and 12th centuries, displaced in everyday speech by Amharic in the south and by Tigrinya and Tigre in the north. It nevertheless survived as a language of scholarship, church ritual, poetry (notably the qene tradition of improvised, double-meaning verse), and royal chronicles, a role comparable to that of Latin in medieval Europe.

Phonology and grammar

Geʻez distinguishes seven vowels and a consonant inventory that includes ejective consonants characteristic of Ethiopian Semitic languages, as well as pharyngeal consonants inherited from earlier Semitic. Nouns mark gender (masculine and feminine) and number, with a broken-plural system alongside suffix plurals. Verbs are built on consonantal roots, typically triliteral, fitted into patterns that encode tense, aspect, mood, and the person, gender, and number of the subject. The basic word order in classical prose is verb–subject–object.

Writing system

Geʻez is written in its own script, an abugida in which each character represents a consonant–vowel syllable. The script developed from the South Arabian consonantal alphabet, adding systematic vowel modifications to the basic consonant shapes. It is written left to right, and is also used to write Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre, and several other languages of Ethiopia and Eritrea.

Literature

Major works in Geʻez include the Bible, the Kebra Nagast (Glory of the Kings), lives of saints, royal chronicles, legal collections such as the Fetha Nagast, and extensive hymnography attributed to Saint Yared. Manuscripts were traditionally copied on parchment and preserved in church and monastic libraries.

Modern use

Today Geʻez is taught in traditional church schools and in universities in Ethiopia, Eritrea, and abroad. It is not a mother tongue, but thousands of priests, deacons, and scholars read and chant it, and new liturgical poetry continues to be composed in it.
