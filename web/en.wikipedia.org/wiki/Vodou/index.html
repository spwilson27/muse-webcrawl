Haitian Vodou

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A Vodou ceremony, centred on serving the lwa (spirits).

Haitian Vodou (Haitian Creole: Vodou) is an African diasporic religion that developed in Haiti between the 16th and 19th centuries, blending the traditions of West and Central African peoples brought to the French colony of Saint-Domingue with Roman Catholic influences from European colonialism. Vodouists (adherents) believe in a sole creator deity, Bondye, and serve a pantheon of subordinate spirits known as the lwa.

Beliefs and practice

Central to Vodou are rituals held in temples (ounfò), led by priests (oungan) or priestesses (manbo), at which drumming, singing and dancing invite a lwa to possess a devotee and communicate with the congregation. Offerings of food, drink and other items are made to the lwa, who are grouped into "nations" such as Rada and Petwo. The religion emphasises healing, community and maintaining harmonious relations with the spirits of ancestors and nature.

History

Vodou coalesced among enslaved Africans in colonial Saint-Domingue and played a role in the Haitian Revolution, which began in 1791 and led to the founding of Haiti in 1804 — a connection traditionally traced to the Bois Caïman ceremony of August 1791. For much of Haiti's history Vodou was suppressed or stigmatised by political and religious authorities, and it was spread abroad by the Haitian diaspora, notably to Louisiana and other parts of the Americas. It received official recognition as a religion in Haiti in 2003.

Misconceptions

Outside Haiti, Vodou has often been sensationalised in popular culture — a distortion reflected in the popularised spelling "voodoo" — with exaggerated emphasis on zombies, dolls and dark magic. Practitioners and scholars stress that such depictions bear little resemblance to the community-centred, ancestral religion actually practised.
