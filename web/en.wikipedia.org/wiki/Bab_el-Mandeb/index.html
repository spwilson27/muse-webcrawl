Bab el-Mandeb

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The narrow strait of Bab el-Mandeb between Yemen's cliffs and Djibouti's shore]
The narrow strait of Bab el-Mandeb between Yemen's cliffs and Djibouti's shore

The Bab el-Mandeb (Arabic: 'Gate of Tears') is the strait connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean, between Yemen on the Arabian Peninsula and Djibouti and Eritrea in the Horn of Africa. About 29 km wide at its narrowest and split by Perim Island into two channels, it is one of the world's great maritime chokepoints: the route between the Mediterranean (via Suez) and Asia passes through it, carrying a substantial share of global trade and oil.

Geography and name

The strait's Arabic name is explained by legend as mourning for those drowned by the earthquake that separated Asia from Africa, or for sailors lost in its treacherous currents. The eastern channel (Bab Iskender) is the deep shipping route; the western passage is shallower and used by local traffic. At the last glacial maximum, sea levels low enough may have made the strait a land crossing — one candidate route for early human dispersal out of Africa.

Strategic significance

Bab el-Mandeb, with Suez and Hormuz, forms a chain of chokepoints for Eurasian trade: roughly a tenth of world trade by volume and several million barrels of oil per day transit it in normal times. The Houthi attacks on shipping from late 2023 — in solidarity, they declared, with Gaza — drove most Western carriers to reroute around the Cape of Good Hope, spiking freight costs and reviving the strait's ancient reputation, and prompting US-led naval operations in the Red Sea.
