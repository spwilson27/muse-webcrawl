Kattegat

The Kattegat (Danish: [ˈkʰætəˌkæt]; Swedish: Kattegatt) is a sea area of about 30,000 km² bounded by the Jutland peninsula of Denmark to the west, the Danish islands to the south, and the Swedish provinces of Bohuslän, Västergötland, Halland and Scania to the east. It connects the Baltic Sea (via the Danish straits) with the Skagerrak and the North Sea.

Geography

The Kattegat is relatively shallow, averaging about 23 metres deep, and is studded with rocky reefs hazardous to navigation. Its principal islands include Samsø, Læsø and Anholt on the Danish side and the Gothenburg archipelago on the Swedish side. Major ports on its shores are Gothenburg, Aarhus, Aalborg and Frederikshavn. Brackish Baltic water flows out at the surface while saltier North Sea water enters beneath, producing a two-layer circulation.

Ecology and history

The Kattegat's ecosystems suffer from nutrient pollution and seasonal oxygen depletion affecting fish stocks. The name, from Dutch for "cat's gate", reflects its narrow, difficult passages; control of the straits was historically contested between Denmark and Sweden, and the sea lanes remain among the busiest in the world for Baltic trade.
