Veal


Veal cutlets, showing the meat's characteristic pale colour

Veal is the meat of calves, in contrast to the beef from older cattle. Veal is typically produced from young male calves of dairy breeds, which do not produce milk and are surplus to dairy herds. It is pale pink, fine-grained, and tender, and features prominently in Italian, French, and other European cuisines.

Types

TypeDescription
Bob vealFrom calves slaughtered at a few days to a few weeks old.
Milk-fed ("special-fed") vealCalves fed a milk or formula diet, slaughtered around 18–22 weeks; flesh is ivory-pale.
Grain-fed vealCalves fed grain and forage after an initial milk period; flesh is darker pink.
Rose veal(UK) Calves reared to higher welfare standards, fed a normal diet; meat is pink rather than white.
Pasture-raised vealCalves raised outdoors with the herd; increasingly marketed as an ethical alternative.

Culinary uses

Classic veal dishes include Italian saltimbocca, osso buco (braised veal shank), and vitello tonnato; Austrian and German Wiener schnitzel is traditionally made with veal; French cuisine uses veal in blanquette de veau, escalopes, and fricandeau. Ground veal is used in meatballs and meatloaf mixtures, and veal bones are prized for light, gelatinous stocks and demi-glace.

Production and welfare controversy

Veal production has long been controversial. Traditional systems kept calves in narrow individual crates that prevented turning around, and fed low-iron milk diets to keep the flesh pale — practices criticised by animal welfare advocates. Veal crates were banned in the United Kingdom in 1990 and across the European Union from 2007; most North American producers have transitioned to group housing. These reforms, together with rose and pasture-raised production, reflect changing consumer expectations.
