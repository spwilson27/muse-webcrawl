Disjunctive sequence

A disjunctive sequence is an infinite sequence over a finite alphabet in which every finite string appears as a substring. For instance, a sequence over the decimal digits is disjunctive if every finite string of digits — 0, 1, 42, 271828, and so on without exception — occurs somewhere in it. A real number whose digit expansion in a given base is a disjunctive sequence is called a disjunctive number in that base; a number disjunctive in every base is called absolutely disjunctive.

Examples and relation to normal numbers

Every normal number is disjunctive in the corresponding base, because normality requires each string of length k to appear with frequency b−k, but the converse is false: disjunctive sequences need not have the uniform frequencies normality demands. Champernowne's constant, formed by concatenating the positive integers (0.123456789101112…), is a classical example of a disjunctive (indeed normal) number in base 10. The Copeland–Erdős constant, formed from the primes, is another. Explicit disjunctive sequences are easy to construct by concatenating all finite strings in order of increasing length, though such constructions are generally not normal.

Properties

Almost all real numbers (in the sense of Lebesgue measure) are disjunctive in every base. Disjunctive sequences appear in symbolic dynamics, where the concept corresponds to a dense orbit in the full shift, and in the theory of pseudorandomness, although disjunctiveness alone is far weaker than randomness: a disjunctive sequence may be highly computable and predictable. Iterating transformations such as the Bernoulli shift on a disjunctive sequence gives rise to concepts like disjunctive words in combinatorics on words.
