Death of a Salesman

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Willy Loman with his two sample cases trudging up the apartment canyon of Brooklyn, in a stage still: the salesman dwarfed by the towers]

Death of a Salesman is a play by Arthur Miller, premiered on Broadway on 10 February 1949. It won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama and the Tony Award for Best Play and is considered one of the greatest plays of the twentieth century: the tragedy of Willy Loman, an ageing Brooklyn travelling salesman whose faith in being 'well liked' collapses, in the last two days of his life, into memory, quarrel and a final, deluded sacrifice.

The action flows between present and remembered past — the play's working title was The Inside of His Head. Willy's sons Biff and Happy, his wife Linda ('attention must be paid to such a person', in her famous plea, paraphrased in most summaries), the neighbour Charley and the ghost of Willy's brother Ben ('the jungle is dark but full of diamonds', in Ben's oft-quoted formulation) people his unraveling.

Plot and meaning

Fired after 34 years, borrowing his salary from Charley while pretending to earn, Willy pins his hopes on Biff — the golden schoolboy athlete who caught him with a woman in a Boston hotel and never recovered. The restaurant scene, the boys' abandonment of their father, Biff's tearful attempt at truth ('I'm a dime a dozen, and so are you') and Willy's suicide — staged to buy the family his life-insurance money and Biff's imagined magnificence — culminate in the Requiem at the grave, with Linda's baffled refrain that they are, at last, free (of debt, of the house payments).

Reception

The premiere famously left audiences stunned and weeping; salesmen wrote to Miller that it was their story. Decades of global productions — including Miller's own 1983 Beijing staging and multiple film and television versions (Lee J. Cobb, Dustin Hoffman, Brian Dennehy, Wendell Pierce among the Willys) — have made it the great American tragedy of ordinary life.
