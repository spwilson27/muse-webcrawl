Oncogene

An oncogene is a gene that has the potential to cause cancer. In tumour cells, these genes are often mutated or expressed at high levels. Most normal cells undergo apoptosis when critical functions are altered; activated oncogenes can cause cells designated for apoptosis to survive and proliferate instead.

Proto-oncogenes

A proto-oncogene is a normal gene that could become an oncogene due to mutations or increased expression. Proto-oncogenes code for proteins that help regulate cell growth and differentiation — growth factors, their receptors, signal transducers such as Ras, and transcription factors such as Myc. A proto-oncogene becomes an oncogene through point mutation, gene amplification, or chromosomal translocation (as in the Philadelphia chromosome, which fuses BCR and ABL1 in chronic myeloid leukaemia).

History

The first oncogene, src, was identified in the Rous sarcoma virus in the 1970s by J. Michael Bishop and Harold Varmus, who showed that viral oncogenes were captured copies of cellular genes; they received the Nobel Prize in 1989. Since then more than a hundred oncogenes have been characterised, and many are targets of modern cancer drugs, such as HER2 in breast cancer and EGFR in lung cancer. Oncogenes contrast with tumour suppressor genes, whose loss of function also drives cancer; malignant transformation generally requires changes in both classes of gene.
