The Wehrmacht (German: [ˈveːɐ̯maxt], lit. "defence force") was the unified armed forces of Nazi Germany from 1935 to 1945. It consisted of the Heer (army), the Kriegsmarine (navy) and the Luftwaffe (air force). The designation "Wehrmacht" replaced the previously used term Reichswehr and was the manifestation of the Nazi regime's efforts to rearm Germany to a greater extent than the Treaty of Versailles permitted.

Origins and build-up

After Adolf Hitler's rise to power in 1933, Germany repudiated the military restrictions of the Treaty of Versailles: conscription was reintroduced in March 1935 and the armed forces were openly renamed the Wehrmacht that May. From 1934 the officer corps swore a personal oath of loyalty to Hitler. In 1938, following the Blomberg–Fritsch affair, Hitler abolished the War Ministry and created the Oberkommando der Wehrmacht (OKW) under Wilhelm Keitel, subordinating the services directly to himself.

Second World War

The Wehrmacht carried out the invasions that opened the Second World War — Poland (1939), Denmark and Norway, the Low Countries and France (1940), Yugoslavia and Greece, and the Soviet Union in Operation Barbarossa (1941). Early victories based on combined-arms "Blitzkrieg" tactics were followed by attritional defeat from 1942–43 onward at Stalingrad, El Alamein and Kursk, and in the air and sea wars. Some 18 million men served in the Wehrmacht during the war; about 5.3 million German military personnel died, the great majority on the Eastern Front.

War crimes and the "clean Wehrmacht" myth

The Wehrmacht was deeply implicated in Nazi crimes, particularly in the East: it cooperated with the SS Einsatzgruppen, implemented the Commissar Order and the criminal "Barbarossa decree", and participated in mass reprisals, starvation policy against Soviet prisoners of war (of whom some 3.3 million died) and anti-partisan warfare that killed vast numbers of civilians. The post-war myth of a "clean Wehrmacht" — blaming atrocities solely on the SS — was promoted by former generals' memoirs and Western Cold War politics, but was dismantled by historians and publicly by the controversial Wehrmacht exhibition (Wehrmachtsausstellung) of the 1990s. The Wehrmacht ceased to exist with Germany's surrender in May 1945 and was formally dissolved by Allied decree in August 1946.
