Torsen is a type of limited-slip differential used in automobiles. The name is a contraction of torque-sensing: the device automatically varies the torque split between wheels in response to differences in traction, without electronic controls or driver input. It was invented by American Vernon Gleasman and patented in 1958; the rights were acquired by the Gleason Corporation, and the design is now produced by Torsen's successor companies (currently under JTEKT).

Operation

Torsen differentials use worm gears and worm wheels whose friction characteristics bind the gear set when a torque imbalance occurs. In the classic T-1 design, crossed-axis helical gears mesh so that under even traction the differential behaves like an open differential, but when one wheel begins to spin, the friction in the gear mesh resists the speed difference and biases torque toward the wheel with more grip. The maximum ratio of torque that can be sent to the high-traction wheel is the torque bias ratio (TBR), typically between about 2:1 and 4:1 depending on design.

A limitation of the Torsen design is that if one wheel has virtually no traction (for example, lifted or on ice), the unit cannot multiply torque beyond its bias ratio of near-zero, so the vehicle may not move unless the spinning wheel is braked (as modern traction control systems do) or the design is supplemented with preload.

Types

  • T-1 – the original design using crossed-axis helical (Invex) gearing; used as a centre differential in Audi's quattro system from the 1980s.
  • T-2 – parallel-axis helical gears; used in many rear-wheel-drive applications and the Humvee/H1.
  • T-3 – a planetary design developed for centre-differential applications with a nominal asymmetric torque split.

Applications

Torsen differentials became famous as the centre differential of Audi quattro models and have been used in vehicles including the Toyota Supra and Land Cruiser, the Mazda MX-5 (in some markets), the Honda S2000, the AM General Humvee, and various performance cars, as axle differentials or centre differentials.
