Tourkokratia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tourkokratia (Greek: Τουρκοκρατία, "Turkish rule") is the historiographical term for the period of Ottoman domination over Greece, conventionally dated from the fall of Constantinople in 1453 — though most of the Greek lands were conquered between the mid-fifteenth and late seventeenth centuries — to the outbreak of the Greek War of Independence in 1821 and the establishment of an independent Greek state.

Background

After 1453 the Ottoman Empire absorbed the remaining Byzantine territories and, over the following two centuries, most Greek-speaking lands, including Crete (1645–1669) after a long war with Venice. The Ionian Islands remained under Venetian rule and never formed part of the Tourkokratia proper.

Ottoman administration

Greek Orthodox Christians were organised within the millet system, under which the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople was recognised as ethnarch of the Orthodox population, exercising civil as well as religious authority. The Phanariots — wealthy Greek families of Constantinople's Phanar district — rose to prominence in the eighteenth century, supplying dragomans to the Porte and hospodars (governors) to the Danubian Principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia. At the local level, primates (kodjabashis) and armed irregulars such as the armatoles played significant roles; the klephts were mountain bandits who in tradition came to symbolise resistance.

Society and economy

The period saw the growth of Greek merchant communities across the Ottoman Empire and Europe, a revival of education in the eighteenth-century Modern Greek Enlightenment, and the emergence of national consciousness that culminated in the founding of the Filiki Eteria in 1814 and the revolution of 1821.

Legacy

In Greek national historiography, the Tourkokratia was long portrayed as centuries of oppression and stagnation; more recent scholarship emphasises institutional continuity, local autonomy, and the complexity of Greek participation in Ottoman structures.
