Heel spur

A heel spur (or calcaneal spur) is a bony outgrowth of the calcaneus (heel bone) in the foot. Spurs form where connective tissue attaches to bone: a plantar (inferior) spur develops at the attachment of the plantar fascia on the underside of the heel, while a dorsal or retrocalcaneal spur develops at the attachment of the Achilles tendon at the back of the heel.

Causes

Calcaneal spurs develop gradually, through repeated stress and microtrauma at the tendon or fascia attachment, which prompts the body to deposit additional bone. Risk factors include prolonged standing, running, obesity, poorly fitting footwear, flat feet or high arches, and aging. They are associated with conditions such as plantar fasciitis, though the relationship is debated.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Many heel spurs cause no symptoms at all and are discovered incidentally on X-rays taken for other reasons; studies find them present in a substantial minority of adults. When symptoms occur, they typically consist of a sharp pain under the heel on taking the first steps in the morning or after rest — a picture that largely overlaps with plantar fasciitis, and clinicians generally consider the fascial inflammation, rather than the spur itself, to be the pain source. Diagnosis is by clinical examination and imaging (X-ray shows the bony projection).

Treatment

Management is usually conservative: rest, ice, stretching exercises, orthotic inserts and supportive footwear, and anti-inflammatory medication. Most cases resolve without procedures. Resistant cases may be treated with corticosteroid injections, shockwave therapy, or, rarely, surgery to release the plantar fascia or remove the spur.
