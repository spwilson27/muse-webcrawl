Hip-hop music

Hip-hop (also spelled hip hop) is a popular music genre that emerged in the early 1970s from African-American and Latino communities in the Bronx, New York City. It is characterised by rhythmic, rhyming vocal delivery — rapping (or MCing) — over beats built from sampling, drum machines, and turntablism. Hip-hop music is the musical expression of the broader hip-hop culture, whose classic elements also include DJing, breakdancing, and graffiti art.

Origins

The genre is conventionally dated to a party on 11 August 1973 at 1520 Sedgwick Avenue in the Bronx, where Jamaican-born DJ Kool Herc used two turntables to extend the percussion "breaks" of funk and soul records while his associate Coke La Rock hyped the crowd — pioneering the breakbeat and MC roles. Afrika Bambaataa and Grandmaster Flash developed the form further, and the Sugarhill Gang's "Rapper's Delight" (1979) brought rap to the pop charts. Run-DMC, LL Cool J, and the Beastie Boys carried it into the mainstream in the 1980s.

Golden age and diversification

The late 1980s and early 1990s "golden age" brought Public Enemy, De La Soul, A Tribe Called Quest, and the sampling artistry of producers such as the Bomb Squad and Dr. Dre, whose G-funk defined West Coast rap alongside Tupac Shakur and Snoop Dogg, while the Notorious B.I.G. led the East Coast scene. Regional styles — Southern, Midwestern, and later trap, characterised by hi-hat patterns and 808 bass from Atlanta producers — successively reshaped the sound.

Global dominance

By the late 2010s hip-hop had become the most-streamed genre in the United States, and artists such as Jay-Z, Kanye West, Eminem, Drake, Nicki Minaj, and Kendrick Lamar — who in 2018 became the first popular musician to win the Pulitzer Prize for Music — achieved worldwide prominence. Localised hip-hop scenes thrive on every continent, adapting the form to local languages and concerns.
