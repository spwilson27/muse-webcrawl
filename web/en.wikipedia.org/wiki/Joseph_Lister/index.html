Joseph Lister

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Joseph Lister (1827–1912), later Lord Lister, was a British surgeon who transformed surgery by introducing antiseptic practice. Applying Louis Pasteur's germ theory, Lister used carbolic acid (phenol) to sterilise instruments, wounds and dressings, and published his results in 1867 — sharply reducing post-operative infections and deaths at a time when surgery was often fatal.

Antiseptic surgery

Working in Glasgow and then Edinburgh and London, Lister treated compound fractures — where infection commonly led to amputation or death — by cleaning wounds with carbolic acid and covering them with treated dressings; his ward results improved dramatically. He later added carbolic sprays, sterile ligatures and hand hygiene, and his methods were adopted worldwide, evolving into the modern aseptic technique that replaced antisepsis itself.

Legacy

Lister's work helped make modern surgery — and the operations it now takes for granted — possible. The mouthwash Listerine (introduced 1879) was named after him, as is the food-poisoning bacterium Listeria. He served as president of the Royal Society (1895–1900) and was surgeon to Queen Victoria.
