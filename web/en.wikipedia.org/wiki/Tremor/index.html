Tremor

SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsInvoluntary, rhythmic shaking of a body part
CausesEssential tremor, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, hyperthyroidism, anxiety, medications

The Archimedes spiral test, used to assess action tremor.

A tremor is an involuntary, somewhat rhythmic muscle contraction and relaxation involving oscillations or twitching movements of one or more body parts. It is the most common of all movement disorders and can affect the hands, arms, head, face, voice, trunk and legs. Most tremors occur in the hands.

Classification

Tremors are classified by when they occur. Resting tremors appear when the affected part is supported and relaxed, as in Parkinson's disease, whose tremor is classically described as "pill-rolling." Action tremors occur during voluntary movement and include postural tremor (holding a position against gravity), kinetic tremor (during movement) and intention tremor, which worsens as the hand approaches a target and points to cerebellar disease.

Causes

All people have a slight physiological tremor, which can be enhanced by anxiety, fatigue, caffeine, fever or hyperthyroidism. Essential tremor is the most common pathological tremor, often familial, typically affecting both hands during action and sometimes the head and voice. Other causes include multiple sclerosis, stroke, Wilson's disease, alcohol withdrawal, and drugs such as lithium and certain asthma medicines.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause. Essential tremor often responds to propranolol or primidone; severe disabling tremor may be treated with deep brain stimulation of the thalamus or with focused ultrasound thalamotomy. Parkinsonian tremor is managed with antiparkinsonian medication. Avoiding triggers such as caffeine helps in many cases.
