Barycentric coordinate system

In geometry, a barycentric coordinate system is a coordinate system in which the location of a point is specified by reference to a simplex (for example, a triangle in the plane or a tetrahedron in space). The barycentric coordinates of a point can be interpreted as masses placed at the vertices of the simplex such that the point is the center of mass (barycenter) of those masses. They were introduced by August Ferdinand Möbius in 1827.

Coordinates on a triangle

For a point P in the plane of a triangle ABC, the barycentric coordinates (λ1, λ2, λ3) satisfy P = λ1A + λ2B + λ3C with λ1 + λ2 + λ3 = 1. Each coordinate is proportional to the area of the subtriangle opposite the corresponding vertex, signed for points outside the triangle. A point is inside the triangle exactly when all three coordinates are positive. Familiar points have simple barycentric coordinates: the centroid is (1/3, 1/3, 1/3), the incenter is proportional to the side lengths (a : b : c), and the circumcenter to (sin 2A : sin 2B : sin 2C).

Applications

Barycentric coordinates are used extensively in triangle geometry, where many centers and lines have elegant expressions in trilinear and barycentric coordinates; in computer graphics for interpolating values (color, depth, texture) across rasterized triangles and for ray–triangle intersection tests; and in finite element analysis on triangular and tetrahedral meshes. Generalized barycentric coordinates extend the idea to arbitrary polygons and polyhedra.
