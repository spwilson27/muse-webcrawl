Expected goals

Expected goals (xG) is a statistical metric used in association football (and adapted in other sports such as ice hockey) that assigns to each shot a probability that it will result in a goal, based on the characteristics of the shot and the play leading up to it. The total xG of a team's shots in a match is used to evaluate the quality of scoring chances created and conceded, independent of the actual scoreline.

Calculation

xG models are trained on large databases of historical shots with known outcomes. Variables commonly used include:

  • Distance from the shot location to the goal
  • Angle of the shot relative to the goal
  • Body part used (foot, head, other)
  • Type of assist or preceding play (through ball, cross, cutback, set piece, rebound)
  • Whether the shot followed a dribble or a fast break
  • Position of defenders and the goalkeeper (in more advanced models)

Each shot receives a value between 0 and 1. A penalty kick, for example, is typically valued around 0.76–0.79 xG, while a long-range shot under pressure may be worth only 0.02–0.03.

Uses

Analysts, clubs, broadcasters, and betting markets use xG to judge team and player performance more reliably than raw goals, which are strongly affected by finishing variance and luck over small samples. Related metrics include expected assists (xA), post-shot expected goals (PSxG, which incorporates shot placement and is used to evaluate goalkeepers), and expected points (xPts) derived from simulated match outcomes.

History

The concept grew out of football analytics work in the 2000s and early 2010s, building on earlier shot-quality research and on expected-value models in ice hockey (such as "expected goals" in NHL analytics). It entered mainstream broadcast coverage in the mid-2010s, notably when the BBC's Match of the Day began showing xG figures in 2017.

Criticism

Critics point out that different data providers produce different xG values for the same shots because their models and variables differ; that xG does not account for game state or the identity of the shooter in most public models; and that aggregated xG can be misread as a claim about what the score "should" have been rather than a probabilistic summary of chance quality.
