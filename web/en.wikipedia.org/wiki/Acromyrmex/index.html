Acromyrmex is a genus of New World ants of the subfamily Myrmicinae, comprising roughly thirty described species. Together with the related genus Atta, they are known as leafcutter ants. They are native to Central and South America and the southern United States.

Fungus growing

Like other members of the tribe Attini, Acromyrmex species do not eat the leaves they cut directly. Instead, fragments of fresh vegetation are carried into underground nests and used as a substrate for a cultivated fungus (usually Leucoagaricus gongylophorus), which the ants tend, weed and feed upon. This obligate mutualism, tens of millions of years old, makes the ants among the few non-human animals to practise agriculture. A bacterial associate on the ants' bodies produces antibiotics that suppress a specialised parasitic mould, Escovopsis.

Colony structure

Colonies are founded by a single queen, who carries a pellet of fungus in her mouthparts during the nuptial flight. Mature colonies may contain hundreds of thousands to millions of workers. Workers are polymorphic, ranging from small gardeners to large soldiers with powerful mandibles capable of cutting through leather. Compared with Atta, Acromyrmex colonies are generally smaller and their workers more spiny.

Economic importance

Leafcutter ants are major herbivores in neotropical ecosystems, but several species are serious pests of crops, pasture and forestry plantations, and are a major agricultural problem in parts of South America.
