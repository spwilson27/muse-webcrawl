Functional food

A functional food is a food claimed to have an additional function—often health-promoting or disease-preventing—beyond basic nutrition, usually by virtue of added or concentrated ingredients. Examples include yoghurts with probiotic bacteria, spreads fortified with plant sterols, eggs enriched with omega-3 fatty acids, and cereals fortified with vitamins and minerals.

Concept and market

The concept originated in Japan in the 1980s; in 1991 Japan introduced the FOSHU (Foods for Specified Health Uses) regulatory category for foods with approved health claims. The global market has grown into the tens of billions of dollars, spanning fortified dairy, beverages with added botanicals, and products marketed around gut health, cognition or immunity.

Regulation and criticism

There is no single legal definition in the United States, where functional foods are regulated as conventional foods, dietary supplements or drugs depending on claims; the EU authorizes specific health claims only after assessment by the European Food Safety Authority, and most submitted claims have been rejected for lack of evidence. Nutrition scientists caution that benefits are often modest, based on surrogate markers, and no substitute for an overall healthy diet; the term is widely regarded as partly a marketing category. Related terms include "nutraceutical" and "medical food".
