Wonton

A wonton (Chinese: 餛飩, húntún; Cantonese: wàhn-tān, 雲吞) is a Chinese dumpling made by wrapping a filling — most commonly minced pork, shrimp, or both — in a thin sheet of wheat dough and pinching it closed. Wontons are usually boiled and served in a clear broth, often with noodles, though they may also be steamed or deep-fried.

History

Wontons have a long history in Chinese cuisine; the name huntun ("primordial chaos") was explained in early texts by the dumpling's sealed, shapeless form, and they were traditionally eaten at the winter solstice. Mentions appear in sources from the Han dynasty onward, and wontons spread throughout China and, with the Chinese diaspora, across Southeast Asia and the world.

Regional varieties

Cantonese wontons, the most internationally familiar, are filled mainly with shrimp and pork, seasoned with flounder powder, and served in a hot broth with yellow egg noodles and chives; Hong Kong wonton noodle soup is considered a signature dish of the city. Sichuan chao shou ("folded hands") are folded differently and served in chilli oil sauce. Shanghai small wontons (xiao huntun) have a scant smear of pork and are drunk with their soup, while large ones carry more filling. Fujian's bianshi use a wrapper made with pounded pork. Deep-fried wontons are also popular, and filled wontons feature in American Chinese cooking, including the cream cheese-filled "crab rangoon".
