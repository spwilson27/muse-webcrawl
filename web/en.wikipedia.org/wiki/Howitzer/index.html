Howitzer

A howitzer is a category of artillery weapon characterised by a relatively short barrel and the use of comparatively small propellant charges to propel projectiles over comparatively high trajectories, with a steep angle of descent. Howitzers form one of the main classes of field artillery, alongside guns (long barrel, flat trajectory) and mortars (very high angle, short range).

Characteristics

Historically the distinction between a gun and a howitzer rested on the firing angle and the construction of the chamber: guns fired at low elevation with full charges, while howitzers could elevate to 45 degrees or more and used variable, reduced charges. By the twentieth century the categories blurred; many modern weapons, termed gun-howitzers, combine the features of both. Most modern howitzers are of 105 mm or 155 mm calibre (152 mm in Russian service) and are towed or self-propelled on wheeled or tracked chassis.

History

The word derives from the Czech houfnice, a fifteenth-century Hussite weapon designed to fire stone shot into dense troop formations. Howitzers were widely used in siege and field warfare from the seventeenth century onward, lobbing explosive shells over walls and earthworks. In the World Wars they became the backbone of divisional artillery: famous examples include the German 15 cm sFH 18, the Soviet 122 mm M1938 (M-30), and the American 105 mm M2A1/M101 and 155 mm M114.

Post-war self-propelled howitzers such as the American M109, the German Panzerhaubitze 2000 and the South Korean K9 Thunder added armoured mobility, automation and ranges beyond 30 km with rocket-assisted projectiles. Precision guided shells such as the M982 Excalibur have since blurred the line between artillery and missile fires.

Types

  • Towed howitzers — e.g. the M777 lightweight 155 mm howitzer.
  • Self-propelled howitzers — e.g. PzH 2000, K9, CAESAR.
  • Pack howitzers — light pieces that can be broken down for animal or air transport.
