The Cortes of Cádiz was the Spanish national assembly that met in the besieged port city of Cádiz during the Peninsular War, from 1810 to 1813. Convened after Napoleon's armies occupied most of Spain and the ruling Council of Regency sought legitimacy, the Cortes proclaimed national sovereignty, abolished many institutions of the old regime and promulgated the Spanish Constitution of 1812, a landmark of European liberalism nicknamed "La Pepa".

Background

After the French invasion of 1808, King Ferdinand VII was held captive in France and the Supreme Central Junta governed in his name. When French forces overran Andalusia in 1810, the Junta dissolved itself in favour of a Regency, which summoned a Cortes. Deputies arrived from across Spain and the Spanish Americas, though American representatives objected to their under-representation, an early grievance feeding independence movements.

Work of the Cortes

The assembly first met on the Isla de León (now San Fernando) on 24 September 1810 and later moved into Cádiz, meeting in the Oratory of Saint Philip Neri while the city endured siege and a yellow-fever epidemic. Deputies included liberals such as Agustín de Argüelles, traditionalists, and clergy. The Cortes decreed freedom of the press, abolished seigneurial jurisdictions and the Inquisition, suppressed guilds, and asserted that sovereignty resided in the nation rather than the king.

The Constitution of 1812

Promulgated on 19 March 1812 — the feast of Saint Joseph, hence the nickname "La Pepa" — the constitution established a constitutional monarchy, a unicameral legislature, broad (though indirect) male suffrage, and legal equality across the Spanish world. When Ferdinand VII returned in 1814 he annulled the constitution and restored absolutism, but the document became a touchstone for liberals in Spain, Italy and Latin America throughout the nineteenth century.
