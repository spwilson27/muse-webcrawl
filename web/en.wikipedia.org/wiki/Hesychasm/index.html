Hesychasm (from the Greek ἡσυχία, hesychia, "stillness, rest, quiet") is a contemplative tradition of prayer in Eastern Orthodox Christianity, practised especially by monks on Mount Athos. Hesychasts aim, through ascetic discipline and the ceaseless repetition of the Jesus Prayer — "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner" — to attain inner stillness and, by grace, a vision of the uncreated light of God, identified with the light of the Transfiguration on Mount Tabor.

The tradition rests on the teaching that humans can participate in the divine energies of God while his essence remains unknowable — a distinction defended theologically by Saint Gregory Palamas in the fourteenth century.

History

Hesychast practice traces its roots to the Desert Fathers of Egypt and to writings such as the Philokalia, an anthology of spiritual texts spanning from the fourth to the fifteenth centuries. Saint John Climacus's Ladder of Divine Ascent and the teachings of Symeon the New Theologian shaped the method, which often joined the prayer to the rhythm of breathing and a seated posture.

In the 1330s–1340s, the Calabrian monk Barlaam attacked the hesychasts' claims and methods, provoking the "Hesychast controversy". Gregory Palamas answered with his Triads, and a series of councils in Constantinople (1341, 1347, and 1351) vindicated Palamite theology, which became official Orthodox doctrine. Hesychasm spread through the Greek and Slavic worlds, influencing Russian monasticism and, much later, Western interest through the nineteenth-century Russian classic The Way of a Pilgrim.

Practice

The hesychast withdraws from distraction, sits with bowed head, controls the breathing, and repeats the Jesus Prayer interiorly, seeking the "prayer of the heart" in which the prayer continues unceasingly, even in sleep. Practitioners are warned of spiritual delusion (prelest) and traditionally work under the guidance of a spiritual father or geron (starets). The goal is theosis — deification, or union with God by grace.
