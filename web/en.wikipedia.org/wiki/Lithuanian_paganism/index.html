Lithuanian paganism

Lithuanian paganism is the polytheistic religion of the ancient Lithuanians, a branch of Baltic paganism. Lithuania was the last country in Europe to adopt Christianity—the Grand Duchy officially converted under Jogaila in 1387, and pagan practices survived in rural areas for centuries afterwards.

Beliefs

The Lithuanian pantheon included Perkūnas, god of thunder and the sky; Žemyna, goddess of the earth; Saulė, the sun goddess; Mėnulis, the moon; and Dievas, a remote sky god. Sacred groves, hills and springs were venerated, and eternal fires were tended by priests (kriviai) and priestesses (vaidilutės). The serpent (žaltys) was kept as a household guardian spirit.

History

The Grand Duchy of Lithuania resisted crusading pressure from the Teutonic Order throughout the 13th and 14th centuries, with the defence of the old faith a major motive for resistance. Grand Duke Algirdas and Kęstutis maintained pagan state rituals. Conversion came with the Union of Krewo (1385) and Jogaila's baptism in 1386–87, though Samogitia was not baptised until 1413. Folk customs, songs (dainos) and festivals such as Joninės (Rasos) preserved many pre-Christian elements into the modern era.

Revival

The modern revival movement, Romuva, reconstructs the old religion from folklore, archaeology and historical sources, and is officially recognised in Lithuania as a traditional religious community.
