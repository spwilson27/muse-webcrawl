In linear algebra, a Jordan normal form, also known as a Jordan canonical form, is an upper triangular matrix of a particular form called a Jordan matrix representing a linear operator on a finite-dimensional vector space with respect to some basis. Such a matrix has each non-zero off-diagonal entry equal to 1, immediately above the main diagonal (on the superdiagonal), and with identical diagonal entries to the left and below them.

Overview

Every square matrix over an algebraically closed field (such as the complex numbers) is similar to a Jordan matrix, and this Jordan form is unique up to the order of its blocks. A Jordan block is a square submatrix with a constant value λ (an eigenvalue) on the diagonal, ones on the superdiagonal, and zeros elsewhere. If all Jordan blocks are of size 1, the matrix is diagonalisable.

Motivation

Not every matrix can be diagonalised: a matrix is diagonalisable if and only if its minimal polynomial splits into distinct linear factors. The Jordan form measures the failure of diagonalisability—blocks of size greater than 1 occur exactly when an eigenvalue's geometric multiplicity is smaller than its algebraic multiplicity, and arise from chains of generalised eigenvectors.

Applications

The Jordan form gives the simplest possible representation of a linear operator and is used to solve systems of linear differential equations, compute matrix exponentials and powers, and classify linear operators up to similarity. Because small perturbations can change the Jordan structure discontinuousously, numerical computation is delicate, and the Schur decomposition is usually preferred in practice.

History

The form is named after the French mathematician Camille Jordan, who presented it in his Traité des substitutions (1870); related results were obtained by Karl Weierstrass for pairs of bilinear forms.
