The flag of Lebanon (Arabic: علم لبنان) consists of two horizontal red stripes separated by a white stripe twice their height, with a green cedar tree centred on the white stripe. It was adopted on 7 December 1943, shortly after Lebanon proclaimed its independence from the French Mandate. Article 5 of the Lebanese constitution describes the flag: “The Lebanese flag shall be composed of three horizontal stripes — white between two red ones — with the cedar in green in the middle of the white stripe.”

Symbolism

The cedar of Lebanon (Cedrus libani) is the country's national emblem, mentioned in the Bible and associated with holiness, eternity, and peace; the tree on the flag must, by constitutional practice and custom, be drawn entirely in green with a brown trunk, and must touch neither red stripe. The red stripes are commonly said to represent the blood shed in the struggle for independence, and the white stripe peace and the snow of Lebanon's mountains. The flag's designers, deputies of the first independent parliament, drew on earlier Lebanese banners, including the red-white standards associated with the independence movement of 1943 and, more distantly, flags used during the Ottoman and Mandate periods.

History

Under the French Mandate (1920–1943), Lebanon used the French tricolour with a cedar on the white band. In November 1943, after the Lebanese government unilaterally amended the constitution to end the Mandate, French forces arrested the president and ministers, provoking protests; a flag without French symbols, sketched by the deputy Henri Pharaon and colleagues, was raised, and its design was formalised by the Chamber of Deputies on 7 December 1943, shortly before full independence took effect on 1 January 1944.

World's largest flag

Lebanon has repeatedly produced record-breaking examples of its flag; in 2010 the Lebanese Army unveiled a flag of about 43,700 square metres at the Rayak air base, setting a world record for the largest flag flown at the time.
