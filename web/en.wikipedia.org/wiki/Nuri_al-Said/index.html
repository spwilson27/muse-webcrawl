Nuri al-Said

Born1888, Baghdad, Ottoman Empire
Died15 July 1958 (aged 69–70), Baghdad, Iraq
OfficePrime Minister of Iraq (14 terms)
Political stancePro-British, monarchist, anti-communist

Nuri al-Said (1888 – 15 July 1958) was an Iraqi politician during the British Mandate of Iraq and the Kingdom of Iraq. He served as Prime Minister of Iraq on fourteen occasions, more than any other figure of the era, and was one of the most influential politicians in the modern history of Iraq. A supporter of close ties with the United Kingdom and of the Hashemite monarchy, al-Said dominated Iraqi politics for much of the period between 1930 and 1958.

Early life and career

Born in Baghdad, al-Said was educated at the Ottoman military academy in Istanbul and served as an Ottoman officer before joining the Arab Revolt during the First World War. He became a close associate of Emir Faisal, who became King Faisal I of Iraq in 1921, and played a central role in building the Iraqi state and its army. He negotiated the Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930, which ended the British mandate while preserving a close alliance with Britain.

Political dominance

Al-Said's politics combined Arab nationalism of a gradualist kind with a strong pro-Western orientation, hostility to communism, and support for the monarchy. He suppressed opposition movements and communist organising, built up the security services, and was associated with the Baghdad Pact of 1955, an anti-Soviet alliance linking Iraq with Turkey, Iran, Pakistan and Britain, which was deeply unpopular with Arab nationalists. In 1958 he helped create the Arab Federation, a union of the Hashemite kingdoms of Iraq and Jordan, as a counterweight to the United Arab Republic of Egypt and Syria.

Death

On 14 July 1958 the Iraqi monarchy was overthrown in a military coup led by Abd al-Karim Qasim and Abdul Salam Arif. King Faisal II and the crown prince were killed. Al-Said went into hiding but was discovered the following day — by many accounts attempting to flee Baghdad disguised in women's clothing — and was shot and killed. His body was dragged through the streets by a mob, an end that symbolised the violent rejection of the old order he had embodied.
