Cartouche

In Egyptian hieroglyphs, a cartouche is an oval with a line at one end at right angles to the oval, indicating that the text enclosed is a royal name. The ancient Egyptian word for it was shenu; in modern Egyptian Arabic usage the French-derived term "cartouche" is also applied to rifle and shotgun cartridges, and to nameplates in other contexts.

History

The cartouche evolved from the shen ring, a circle of rope symbolizing eternity and protection. From the reign of Sneferu in the Fourth Dynasty, royal names — first the throne name (prenomen), later also the birth name (nomen) — were enclosed in cartouches. The device protected the king's name and identified it as royal.

Decipherment

Cartouches played a key role in the decipherment of hieroglyphs: Jean-François Champollion used the cartouches in the Rosetta Stone and other inscriptions, containing the names Ptolemy and Cleopatra, to establish the phonetic values of hieroglyphic signs in the 1820s.

Other uses

In art and architecture, "cartouche" more broadly denotes an ornamental frame or panel with scrolled edges used in decoration, heraldry, cartography, and furniture.
