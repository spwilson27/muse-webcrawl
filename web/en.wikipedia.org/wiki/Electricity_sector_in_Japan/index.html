The electricity sector in Japan supplies power to one of the world's largest economies and is notable for two unusual features: the national grid is divided between 50 Hz service in eastern Japan and 60 Hz in the west, a legacy of German (AEG) generators installed in Tokyo and American (General Electric) generators in Osaka in the 1890s; and, being an island nation poor in domestic fuels, Japan imports nearly all its fossil energy, principally liquefied natural gas (LNG) and coal.

Structure and reform

For most of the post-war era, ten privately owned regional utilities — including Tokyo Electric Power Company (TEPCO), Kansai Electric and Chubu Electric — held monopolies over generation, transmission and retail in their areas. Following the 2011 Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster, which idled Japan's entire nuclear fleet and exposed the fragility of the system, the government enacted phased reforms: a national grid operator (OCCTO) was created in 2015, the retail market fully liberalised in 2016, and transmission and distribution were legally unbundled in 2020. The 50/60 Hz divide persists, bridged only by frequency-converter stations of limited capacity, which hampered power transfers after the 2011 earthquake and tsunami.

Generation mix

Before 2011 nuclear power provided about 30 per cent of Japan's electricity; since then fossil fuels filled the gap while solar power grew rapidly under a feed-in tariff, making Japan one of the world's largest solar markets. Hydroelectricity, geothermal (Japan has major unexploited geothermal resources), wind and biomass contribute smaller shares. Successive energy plans aim to restore nuclear to roughly 20 per cent of the mix by 2030 alongside 36–38 per cent renewables, as reactors gradually restart under post-Fukushima safety rules.
