Brahmavihara

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The four faces symbolise the four brahmaviharas.

The brahmaviharas (Pali: brahmavihārā; Sanskrit: brahmavihāra), literally "abodes of Brahma" or "sublime attitudes", are a series of four Buddhist virtues and the meditation practices used to cultivate them. They are also known as the four immeasurables (Pali: appamaññā) because the goodwill they generate is extended without limit to all beings. The four are:

mettāmaitrīloving-kindness, friendliness
karuṇākaruṇācompassion
muditāmuditāsympathetic or appreciative joy
upekkhāupekṣāequanimity

Meaning

Mettā (loving-kindness) is the wish that all beings be happy and safe. Karuṇā (compassion) is the wish that beings be free from suffering, arising in response to their pain. Muditā (sympathetic joy) is delight in the happiness and success of others, free of envy. Upekkhā (equanimity) is a balanced, even-minded regard for all beings, undisturbed by attraction or aversion, grounded in the understanding that beings are heirs of their own actions (karma). The commentaries describe each state as having a "near enemy" (a quality that resembles it but is actually opposed, e.g. selfish affection for loving-kindness, grief for compassion, exuberance for joy, and indifference for equanimity) and a "far enemy" (its obvious opposite, such as hatred, cruelty, envy, or agitation).

In the early texts

The brahmaviharas are taught throughout the Pali Canon, notably in the Tevijja Sutta and the Mettā Sutta, and are classically developed by radiating each quality in the six directions and to progressively widening classes of beings—oneself, benefactors, neutral persons, hostile persons, and finally all beings without distinction—until the mind becomes "immeasurable". According to the early texts, practising these meditations leads to rebirth in the Brahma realms and supports the attainment of the meditative absorptions (jhānas); combined with insight (vipassanā), they conduce to liberation. Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga gives the most influential Theravada systematisation of the practice.

Wider context

The four qualities are not exclusively Buddhist: versions appear in Hindu texts such as Patañjali's Yoga Sutras (1.33), which advises friendliness toward the happy, compassion for the suffering, joy in the virtuous, and equanimity toward the wicked as means of calming the mind, and in Jain literature. In Mahayana Buddhism the four immeasurables are central to the bodhisattva path and are recited in the widely used prayer: "May all beings have happiness and the causes of happiness; may they be free from suffering and its causes; may they never be parted from the happiness that knows no suffering; may they dwell in equanimity, free from attachment and aversion."
