Bradyseism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The columns of the macellum at Pozzuoli record centuries of bradyseism.

Bradyseism is the gradual uplift or subsidence of part of the Earth's surface caused by the movement of magma or hydrothermal fluids beneath a volcanic caldera. The term derives from the Greek bradýs (βραδύς, "slow") and seismós (σεισμός, "earthquake"), and so literally means "slow earthquake". Unlike the sudden displacement of a tectonic earthquake, bradyseismic deformation unfolds over months, years, or centuries, at rates of millimetres to a few centimetres per year, and is frequently accompanied by swarms of small earthquakes.

Pozzuoli and the Phlegraean Fields

The classic example of bradyseism is the town of Pozzuoli in the Phlegraean Fields caldera west of Naples, Italy. The columns of the Roman market building (the so-called Temple of Serapis, in fact a macellum) bear bands of borings made by marine molluscs (Lithophaga) up to several metres above the floor, demonstrating that the building sank below sea level after Roman times and later rose again. Charles Lyell used the columns as a frontispiece and key exhibit in his Principles of Geology (1830–33) to argue for gradual geological change.

Instrumental measurement in the 20th century documented dramatic modern episodes. Between 1969 and 1972 the ground at Pozzuoli rose by about 1.7 metres, and between 1982 and 1984 it rose by a further 1.8 metres, accompanied by thousands of small earthquakes. The 1982–84 crisis damaged buildings and prompted the evacuation of some 40,000 people from the old town; the ground subsequently subsided slowly. Since about 2005 the caldera has been in a new phase of gradual uplift, amounting to roughly a metre by the mid-2020s, with increasing seismicity that has again raised public and scientific concern. Deflation episodes can be equally rapid: the 1538 eruption of Monte Nuovo was preceded by years of uplift.

Causes and monitoring

Bradyseism is generally attributed to pressure changes in a magma chamber or in an overlying hydrothermal system: the intrusion of magma, the accumulation or release of volcanic gases and heated groundwater, or the cooling and crystallisation of magma can all inflate or deflate the surface. Because sustained uplift can precede eruptions in restless calderas such as the Phlegraean Fields, Yellowstone, and Long Valley, bradyseism is monitored continuously with levelling, GNSS (GPS), satellite radar interferometry (InSAR), gravimetry, and seismic networks. Interpreting the signals is difficult, however, since large deformation may also subside without any eruption.
