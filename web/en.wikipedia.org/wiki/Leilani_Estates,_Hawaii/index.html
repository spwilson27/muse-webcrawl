Leilani Estates is a census-designated place (CDP) and residential subdivision in Puna District, Hawaiʻi County, on the Island of Hawaiʻi, United States. Laid out beginning in the late 1960s on the lower East Rift Zone of Kīlauea volcano, the subdivision became internationally known in May 2018, when a flank eruption of Kīlauea opened more than twenty fissures within and around the community, destroying hundreds of homes and displacing its residents.

Geography

Leilani Estates lies southeast of Pāhoa, between Hawaiʻi Route 130 and the coast, at elevations of roughly 90–240 m (300–800 ft). It is located in Lava Flow Hazard Zone 1 — the highest-risk zone on the island — because it sits directly on Kīlauea's lower East Rift Zone, where the volcano's magma conduit passes close to the surface. The area is lush and rainy, with homesteads carved from ʻōhiʻa forest, and many residents lived off-grid or on catchment water systems.

2018 eruption

On 3 May 2018, following hundreds of earthquakes and the collapse of the Puʻu ʻŌʻō crater floor, the first fissure opened on Mohala Street in the subdivision, spewing lava fountains and toxic sulfur dioxide gas. Mandatory evacuations were ordered for Leilani Estates and neighbouring Lanipuna Gardens. Over the following weeks, 24 fissures opened; Fissure 8 (later named Ahuʻailāʻau) produced a major lava channel that flowed to the ocean at Kapoho, destroying Vacationland and Kapoho Bay. By the eruption's end in September 2018, lava had covered about 35 km², destroyed more than 700 structures — the majority in and around Leilani Estates, where over 300 homes were lost — and created over 3 km of new coastline. Parts of the subdivision remained inaccessible for years, and rebuilding has been slow because of altered roads, lingering heat and the risk of further eruptions.

Demographics

The 2010 census recorded 1,560 residents. The 2020 census, taken after the eruption, recorded roughly 1,100. The community historically attracted retirees, alternative-lifestyle residents and growers drawn by inexpensive land — inexpensive precisely because of the volcanic hazard, a trade-off that was widely discussed in coverage of the disaster.
