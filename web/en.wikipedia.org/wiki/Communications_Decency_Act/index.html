Communications Decency Act

The Communications Decency Act of 1996 (CDA) was the first notable attempt by the United States Congress to regulate pornographic and indecent material on the Internet. It was passed as Title V of the Telecommunications Act of 1996 and signed into law by President Bill Clinton on February 8, 1996. The Act criminalised the knowing transmission of "obscene or indecent" messages to minors and the display of material "patently offensive" in a manner available to minors.

Legal challenge: Reno v. ACLU

Civil-liberties groups, publishers, and Internet companies challenged the indecency provisions as unconstitutionally vague and overbroad under the First Amendment. In Reno v. American Civil Liberties Union (1997), the Supreme Court struck down the Act's anti-indecency provisions, holding that they suppressed speech that adults have a constitutional right to send and receive, and affirming that the Internet merits full First Amendment protection rather than the reduced standard applied to broadcast media.

Section 230

The most enduring part of the CDA is Section 230, which survived the court challenges. Section 230 provides that "interactive computer services" are generally not to be treated as the publisher or speaker of content provided by their users, and shields them for good-faith moderation decisions. This immunity became a foundational legal protection for Internet platforms, forums, and social media, and has been the subject of intense political debate and reform proposals ever since. Congress narrowed it in 2018 with FOSTA-SESTA, which carved out facilitation of sex trafficking.

Aftermath

Congress attempted a narrower successor, the Child Online Protection Act (COPA) of 1998, which was likewise blocked by the courts and never took effect. The CDA remains a landmark in Internet law: its failed censorship provisions defined the limits of online speech regulation, while Section 230 shaped the development of the modern web.
