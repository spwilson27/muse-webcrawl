Vortex lift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Vortex lift is the method by which highly swept wings, such as delta wings, generate lift at high angles of attack. At low angles of attack such wings produce lift in the normal (potential flow) manner; as incidence increases, flow separates at the sharp leading edges and rolls up into stable, conical vortices that remain attached over the wing's upper surface.

Mechanism

The low pressure inside the vortex cores acts on the wing surface beneath them, producing additional "non-linear" lift that grows beyond the point where a conventional wing would stall. Provided the vortices remain coherent and do not burst over the wing, a delta can continue to generate lift to angles of attack of 30° or more, though with steeply rising drag.

Applications

Vortex lift was exploited by the Concorde, whose ogee-planform wing relied on leading-edge vortices for low-speed lift, allowing its slender fuselage to land without a conventional high-lift system of comparable size. Fighter aircraft use the same phenomenon, enhanced by leading-edge extensions (LERX) or strakes that shed controlled vortices over the main wing — visible as vapour cones in humid conditions. The space shuttle's double-delta wing likewise depended on vortex lift during approach.

Vortex breakdown

At excessive angle of attack the vortices burst, first at the trailing edge and progressively forward, causing lift loss, buffeting and pitch-up. Controlling vortex burst location is a central design problem for delta and straked configurations.
