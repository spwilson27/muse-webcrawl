Rotator cuff

Muscles: Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis

Function: Stabilisation and rotation of the shoulder joint

The rotator cuff is a group of four muscles and their tendons that surround the shoulder joint, stabilising the head of the humerus within the shallow socket (glenoid) of the scapula while enabling the arm's wide range of motion. The four muscles — remembered by the mnemonic SITS — all arise from the scapula and insert on the humeral head:

  • Supraspinatus — from the supraspinous fossa; initiates abduction of the arm.
  • Infraspinatus — from the infraspinous fossa; externally rotates the arm.
  • Teres minor — from the lateral scapular border; externally rotates the arm.
  • Subscapularis — from the front of the scapula; internally rotates the arm.

Function

Because the glenoid socket is very shallow, the shoulder depends on the cuff to hold the humeral head centred while larger muscles such as the deltoid move the arm. The cuff muscles act together as a force couple, compressing the joint and steering rotation; they also depress the humeral head during overhead movement so it does not jam against the acromion.

Injury and disorders

Rotator cuff disease is among the most common causes of shoulder pain. It ranges from tendinopathy and subacromial impingement/bursitis to partial and full-thickness rotator cuff tears, which become increasingly common with age and are also frequent in overhead athletes and manual workers. The supraspinatus tendon, passing under the acromion, is the most often torn. Symptoms include pain — often worse at night and with overhead activity — weakness, and reduced range of motion. Diagnosis combines physical examination tests (such as the empty-can and Hawkins–Kennedy tests) with ultrasound or MRI.

Treatment

Most cuff problems are managed conservatively with physiotherapy, activity modification, and anti-inflammatory medication or steroid injection. Chronic, large, or traumatic tears may be repaired surgically, usually arthroscopically, by suturing the tendon back to bone; outcomes are best with early repair of acute tears in younger patients.
