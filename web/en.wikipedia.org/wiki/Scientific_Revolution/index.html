Scientific Revolution

The Scientific Revolution was a series of events that marked the emergence of modern science during the early modern period, when developments in mathematics, physics, astronomy, biology and chemistry transformed views of society and nature. It is conventionally dated from Copernicus's De revolutionibus (1543) to Newton's Principia (1687).

Scientific Revolution
Period: c. 1543–1687
Key figures: Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, Bacon, Descartes, Newton

Astronomy

Copernicus proposed heliocentrism; Tycho Brahe's observations enabled Kepler's laws of planetary motion; Galileo's telescopic discoveries and defence of Copernicanism led to his trial in 1633.

Method and philosophy

Francis Bacon advocated empirical, inductive method; René Descartes championed deductive rationalism and analytic geometry. New institutions — the Royal Society (1660) and the French Academy of Sciences (1666) — and scientific journals formalised communication.

Newton

Isaac Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687) unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics with universal gravitation, a synthesis often seen as the Revolution's culmination, laying groundwork for the Enlightenment.
