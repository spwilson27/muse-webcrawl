The Diolkos (Greek: Δίολκος, from dia "across" and holkos "portage machine") is a paved trackway near Corinth in ancient Greece that enabled boats to be moved overland across the Isthmus of Corinth. The shortcut allowed ancient vessels to avoid the long and dangerous circumnavigation of the Peloponnese peninsula, sailing instead between the Gulf of Corinth and the Saronic Gulf.

History

The Diolkos was built around the end of the 7th or the beginning of the 6th century BC, traditionally during the reign of the tyrant Periander of Corinth (c. 602 BC). Ships were hauled on wheeled cradles along parallel grooves cut into the limestone paving, about 1.6 metres apart — an arrangement sometimes described as an early form of railway. Chiefly smaller vessels and warships were transported; cargoes of larger merchant ships may more often have been unloaded and carried across separately.

The trackway remained in regular use for at least six centuries, until about the middle of the 1st century AD; literary references include Aristophanes, Polybius, Strabo, and Pliny the Elder. Nero later attempted to cut a canal through the isthmus in AD 67, a project completed only in 1893 with the modern Corinth Canal, whose line partly follows the Diolkos near its western end.

Remains

Excavations in the 20th century uncovered substantial sections of the paved track at the western (Corinthian Gulf) end near Poseidonia, including the loading dock area. Part of the monument was damaged during construction of the Corinth Canal and by later road building, and its preservation has been a subject of concern among archaeologists.
