The Kilwa Sultanate was a Swahili sultanate centred on the island city of Kilwa Kisiwani, off the coast of present-day southern Tanzania. Founded around the turn of the second millennium, it became, between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries, the most powerful of the Swahili city-states, controlling much of the East African coast and the lucrative trade in gold from the interior kingdom centred on Great Zimbabwe.

History

Founding

According to the Kilwa Chronicle, the sultanate was founded by Ali ibn al-Hassan Shirazi, a prince said to have come from Shiraz in Persia, who purchased the island from its African inhabitants around 975–1005 AD. Modern historians treat the Shirazi origin story partly as a legitimising myth, while noting genuine links between the Swahili coast and the wider western Indian Ocean world.

Golden age

From the twelfth century Kilwa grew rich on the Indian Ocean trade, taxing commerce in gold, ivory, and slaves between the African interior and Arabia, India, and China. Under the Mahdali dynasty, especially Sultan al-Hassan ibn Sulaiman (r. c. 1310–1333), Kilwa reached its zenith: it issued its own copper coinage, extended control over Mafia Island and possibly as far as Sofala in modern Mozambique — the southern outlet for Zimbabwean gold — and built the great palace of Husuni Kubwa and the enlarged Great Mosque of Kilwa, the largest mosque in sub-Saharan Africa of its time. The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta visited in 1331 and praised Kilwa as one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns in the world.

Decline and Portuguese conquest

Internal dynastic struggles weakened Kilwa in the fifteenth century. In 1500 the Portuguese explorer Pedro Álvares Cabral visited, and in 1505 Francisco de Almeida sacked and occupied the city, building a fort and installing a puppet sultan. Abandoned by the Portuguese in 1512, Kilwa never recovered its former importance, though it later revived modestly under Omani influence in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries as a centre of the slave and ivory trades.

Trade

Kilwa's wealth rested on its position as an entrepôt: gold mined in the Zimbabwe plateau reached the coast at Sofala and passed north through Kilwa to markets in Arabia and India, while imported goods — Chinese porcelain, Persian ceramics, Indian glass beads and cloth — flowed inland. Archaeology at Kilwa Kisiwani, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site (designated 1981, together with Songo Mnara), has recovered imported ceramics and coinage attesting to these networks.
