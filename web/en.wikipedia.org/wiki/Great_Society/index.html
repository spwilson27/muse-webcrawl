The Great Society was a domestic program launched by Democratic President Lyndon B. Johnson in 1964–65. The term was first referenced during a commencement speech given on May 22, 1964, at Ohio University, then again at the University of Michigan. The main goal of the Great Society social reforms was the total elimination of poverty and racial injustice.

New major federal programs that addressed civil rights, education, medical care, urban problems, rural poverty, and transportation were launched during Johnson's presidency. The program and its initiatives were subsequently promoted by him and fellow Democrats in Congress in the 1960s.

War on Poverty

On January 8, 1964, President Johnson declared an "unconditional war on poverty" in his first State of the Union address. The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 created the Office of Economic Opportunity and programs such as Job Corps, VISTA, Head Start, and food stamps.

Health care

In 1965, Congress created Medicare, which provided health insurance for the elderly, and Medicaid, which provided health care for low-income families. These remain among the most significant legacies of the Great Society.

Civil rights

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 prohibited racial discrimination in voting, dramatically increasing black voter registration in the South.

Legacy

Historians debate the Great Society's effectiveness; poverty rates declined sharply during the 1960s, but critics argue many programs were inefficient or expanded federal power excessively. The escalation of the Vietnam War diverted funding and political attention from the domestic agenda.
