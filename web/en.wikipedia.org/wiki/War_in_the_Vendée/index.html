War in the Vendée

The War in the Vendée (French: Guerre de Vendée) was a counter-revolutionary insurrection in the Vendée region of France during the French Revolution. Between 1793 and 1796, royalist and Catholic peasants of the region rose against the Revolutionary government in Paris, forming the Catholic and Royal Army and fighting a guerrilla war through the hedgerowed bocage country.

Causes

The uprising was triggered by the levée en masse decree of February 1793, which ordered the conscription of 300,000 men, and by long-standing resentment of the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, which had subordinated the Catholic Church to the state. The largely rural, devout population of the Vendée resisted the oath imposed on priests and the requisitioning of church property.

Course of the war

The insurgency began in March 1793 with attacks on Republican garrisons. Led by figures such as Jacques Cathelineau, Maurice d'Elbée, François de Charette, and Henri de La Rochejaquelein, the Vendéans captured several towns and won early victories at Thouars and Saumur. In October 1793, after defeat at Cholet, the army crossed the Loire in the Virée de Galerne, marching north toward Granville before being destroyed at Le Mans and Savenay in December.

Repression and legacy

The Republican response was brutal. General Louis Marie Turreau's "infernal columns" devastated the countryside in early 1794, and mass drownings and shootings occurred at Nantes under Jean-Baptiste Carrier. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, from around 170,000 to considerably higher figures. The rebellion was effectively pacified by General Lazare Hoche in 1796, though sporadic risings continued. The war remains one of the most contested episodes of the Revolution, with historians debating whether the repression in the Vendée constituted genocide.
