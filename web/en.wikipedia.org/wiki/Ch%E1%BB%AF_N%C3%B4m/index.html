Chữ Nôm is a logographic writing system formerly used to write the Vietnamese language. It uses Chinese characters (chữ Hán) — borrowed both for their sound and meaning — together with thousands of locally invented characters to represent native Vietnamese words.

History

Vietnamese had traditionally been written in classical Chinese for official purposes. From around the 13th century, scholars developed chữ Nôm to record Vietnamese itself, and it flourished in vernacular literature. The celebrated poet Nguyễn Du composed his masterpiece The Tale of Kiều (early 19th century) in chữ Nôm. The Tây Sơn regime briefly promoted it for official documents, but it never displaced classical Chinese in administration.

Decline

During French colonial rule, the Latin-based Vietnamese alphabet, chữ Quốc ngữ, was promoted and, after reforms in the early 20th century, became the universal script. Today chữ Nôm is studied by scholars and preserved by institutes in Vietnam and abroad; many of its characters have been encoded in Unicode.

Structure

Nôm characters often combine a component hinting at pronunciation with one hinting at meaning, resulting in a very large inventory of graphs — a major reason literacy in the system was historically restricted to a scholarly elite.
