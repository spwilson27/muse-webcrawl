The Vela Pulsar (PSR J0835−4510 or PSR B0833−45) is a radio, optical, X-ray and gamma-ray emitting pulsar associated with the Vela supernova remnant in the southern constellation of Vela. It is one of the brightest gamma-ray sources in the sky and the brightest persistent source at GeV energies.

Characteristics

The pulsar rotates once every 89.33 milliseconds, about eleven times per second, and is gradually slowing down. Its characteristic age of roughly 11,000 years matches estimates for the age of the surrounding supernova remnant, making the pair one of the clearest confirmed links between a pulsar and the remnant of the supernova that created it. The Vela Pulsar lies about 960 light-years from Earth.

Glitches and observations

Vela is famous for its large, fairly regular spin-up glitches, sudden increases in rotation rate that are thought to arise from interactions between the neutron star's crust and its superfluid interior. It was one of the first pulsars detected in gamma rays, by the SAS-2 satellite in the 1970s, and has been studied intensively by later observatories including the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory and the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope. Its optical counterpart is extremely faint, around 23rd magnitude.
