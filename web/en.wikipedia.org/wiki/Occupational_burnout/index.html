Occupational burnout

Burnout
FieldOccupational health psychology
Key dimensionsExhaustion; cynicism/depersonalization; reduced professional efficacy

Occupational burnout is a state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion resulting from prolonged exposure to chronic interpersonal and organizational stressors in the workplace. It is characterized by three dimensions: overwhelming exhaustion, cynicism or detachment from one's job, and a diminished sense of personal accomplishment or professional efficacy. In the ICD-11, the World Health Organization classifies burnout as an "occupational phenomenon" rather than a medical condition, defining it specifically in the occupational context.

Burnout develops gradually under chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.

History

The term was introduced into the scientific literature by psychologist Herbert Freudenberger in 1974, describing the depletion he observed in himself and colleagues working in free clinics. Christina Maslach and colleagues subsequently developed the concept empirically, creating the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) in 1981 and framing burnout as a response to chronic interpersonal stressors, initially in human-service professions such as health care, teaching, and social work. Research later extended the concept to virtually all occupations.

Causes

Research associated with Maslach and Michael Leiter identifies six areas of worklife where mismatches between the person and the job predict burnout: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values. Chronic overload, lack of autonomy, insufficient recognition, breakdown of workplace relationships, perceived unfairness, and conflicts between personal values and job demands each contribute. Individual factors such as perfectionism and neuroticism moderate risk, but organizational factors are considered the primary drivers.

Symptoms and effects

Symptoms include fatigue that rest does not relieve, irritability, sleep disturbance, difficulty concentrating, physical complaints such as headaches, and a negative, callous attitude toward clients, patients, or colleagues (depersonalization). Burnout is associated with job dissatisfaction, absenteeism, turnover, reduced performance, and elevated risk of depression, anxiety, cardiovascular disease, and substance misuse. It overlaps with, but is distinguished from, depression by its specific tie to the work context.

Measurement

The Maslach Burnout Inventory, in its versions for human services, educators, and general occupations, is the most widely used instrument, scoring emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment. Other instruments include the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory, the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, and the Shirom–Melamed Burnout Measure.

Prevention and intervention

Interventions divide into individual approaches — stress-management training, cognitive-behavioural techniques, mindfulness, improved recovery and sleep — and organizational approaches, such as reducing workload, increasing job control and participation in decision-making, improving fairness and recognition, and redesigning jobs. Meta-analyses generally find organizational interventions more durable than purely individual ones, though evidence quality varies. Recovery typically requires sustained changes in working conditions, not merely rest.
