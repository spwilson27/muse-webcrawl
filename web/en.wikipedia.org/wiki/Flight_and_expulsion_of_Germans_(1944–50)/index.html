Flight and expulsion of Germans (1944–50)

The flight and expulsion of Germans during and after World War II (1944–1950) was the forced migration of ethnic Germans from Central and Eastern Europe, the largest of a series of post-war population transfers in Europe. Between 12 and 14.6 million Germans fled or were expelled from the former eastern territories of Germany (East Prussia, Silesia, Eastern Pomerania, and eastern Brandenburg) and from countries such as Czechoslovakia, Poland, Hungary, Romania, and Yugoslavia, resettling in the territories of post-war Germany and Austria.

Phases

The displacement occurred in three overlapping phases. In late 1944 and early 1945, millions of German civilians fled westward ahead of the advancing Red Army, often in winter treks or by ship across the Baltic; the sinking of the evacuation ship Wilhelm Gustloff in January 1945 remains the deadliest maritime disaster in history. A second phase of unorganized, often violent "wild expulsions" followed Germany's surrender in May 1945. The third phase, authorized by the Potsdam Agreement of August 1945, which called for the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary, consisted of organized expulsions lasting until about 1950.

Consequences

The expellees (Vertriebene) formed a major political and social force in post-war West Germany, where they numbered about eight million by 1950 — roughly one-sixth of the population. The events remain a subject of historical and political debate, including over the number of deaths, which demographic studies have estimated as high as two million but which archival-based research has put at roughly 500,000–600,000 confirmed deaths.
