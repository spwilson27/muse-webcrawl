The Great Wall of China (German: Beim Bau der Chinesischen Mauer) is the title given to a collection of short stories by Franz Kafka published posthumously in 1931, edited by Max Brod, and by extension to the title story within it, written in 1917.

The title story

The story is narrated by a scholar who once worked as an overseer on a section of the Great Wall of China. He reflects on the strange, piecemeal method by which the wall is built — in disconnected sections separated by gaps of a thousand metres — and on the remoteness of the imperial court in Peking, whose edicts reach the provinces so slowly that the death of an emperor may be learned of only decades later. The narrator's meditations turn the wall into a parable of bureaucracy, faith, futility and the inscrutability of authority.

Publication history

Kafka left the story unfinished at his death in 1924. Max Brod, defying Kafka's instruction to destroy his manuscripts, prepared the collection for publication by Gustav Kiepenheuer in Berlin in 1931. The first English translation, by Willa and Edwin Muir, appeared in 1933 and introduced Kafka's shorter parables — including "The Great Wall of China," "An Old Manuscript," and fragments related to The Trial — to English-speaking readers.

Themes

Critics read the story as an allegory of the Austro-Hungarian Empire's decaying bureaucracy, of religious faith in an absent deity, and of the limits of historical knowledge. Its final fragment, concerning a message from the dying emperor that can never reach its subject, is among Kafka's most famous parables.
