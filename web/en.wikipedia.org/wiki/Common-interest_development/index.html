Common-interest development

A planned community is a common form of common-interest development.

A common-interest development (CID) is the fastest growing form of housing in the world. They are commonly referred to and seen in the United States as condominiums, homeowners associations (HOAs), planned unit developments and cooperatives. In the United Kingdom they are known as commonhold properties, and in Australia as strata title.

The basic characteristic of a CID is that the individual owners hold title to a private interest (a unit or lot) together with a shared interest in common areas of the property, which are owned and managed collectively, usually through an association.

Types

  • Condominiums — owners hold title to individual units and an undivided interest in common elements.
  • Planned communities — owners hold title to lots, with an association owning or maintaining common areas such as streets, parks and recreation facilities.
  • Stock cooperatives — a corporation owns the property and residents hold shares together with a proprietary lease.

Governance

CIDs are governed by a set of recorded documents — a declaration of covenants, conditions and restrictions (CC&Rs), bylaws and rules — administered by an elected board of the owners' association. Associations levy assessments to maintain common areas and enforce the governing documents.

Criticism

Critics have described some associations as private governments with limited democratic accountability, pointing to foreclosure powers, fines, and restrictions on owners' use of their property. Supporters respond that CIDs protect property values and provide amenities and services that municipalities may not supply.
