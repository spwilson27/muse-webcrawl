Tracery

Tracery is an architectural device by which windows (or screens, panels, and vaults) are divided into sections of various proportions by stone bars or ribs of moulding. Most commonly, it refers to the stonework elements that support the glass in a Gothic window. The term probably derives from the "tracing floors" on which the full-size patterns of the windows were drawn.

Types

Plate tracery, the earliest form, used in early Gothic buildings of the late 12th and early 13th centuries, appears as if openings were punched through a solid stone plate. In bar tracery, introduced at Reims Cathedral and developed from about the 1220s, the window is divided by slender stone bars (mullions) whose mouldings continue into arches, circles, and other shapes above, allowing much larger and more elaborate windows.

Styles

Later forms include geometrical tracery (circles and foils arranged geometrically, characteristic of the Decorated style), curvilinear or flowing tracery (with ogee curves forming flame-like and net-like patterns, culminating in the French Flamboyant style), and the rectilinear tracery of the English Perpendicular style, in which mullions run straight through to the window head in a grid-like pattern. Related devices include blind tracery applied to walls and panel tracery used on screens, fonts, and furniture.
