Delayed puberty is when a person's body does not begin the physical changes of puberty at the usual age. It is conventionally defined as the absence of testicular enlargement in boys by age 14, or the absence of breast development in girls by age 13. Delayed puberty affects roughly 2% of adolescents and is more commonly evaluated in boys.

Causes

The most common cause is constitutional delay of growth and puberty, a temporary slowing of development that often runs in families and resolves spontaneously. Other causes are grouped into:

  • Hypergonadotropic hypogonadism — the gonads themselves fail to respond, as in Klinefelter syndrome or Turner syndrome, with elevated gonadotropin levels.
  • Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism — the hypothalamus or pituitary fails to signal the gonads; this may be congenital, as in Kallmann syndrome, or functional, resulting from chronic illness, malnutrition, inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease or excessive exercise.

Diagnosis

Evaluation typically includes a medical history, growth charts, physical examination with Tanner staging, a bone-age X-ray of the hand, and blood tests measuring LH, FSH, testosterone or oestradiol, thyroid function and prolactin. Distinguishing constitutional delay from congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism can be difficult and sometimes requires observation over time.

Treatment

Many cases need only reassurance and monitoring. When treatment is desired or a pathological cause is found, short courses of sex steroids (testosterone in boys, oestrogen in girls) can induce pubertal development; underlying conditions are treated where possible. Psychological support may be helpful, as delayed puberty can cause distress and social difficulty during adolescence.
