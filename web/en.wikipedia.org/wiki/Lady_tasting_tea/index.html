Lady tasting tea

The lady tasting tea is a famous anecdote and designed experiment in the history of statistics. At a gathering at Rothamsted Experimental Station in England in the 1920s, a lady — later identified as Muriel Bristol — claimed that she could tell, from the taste of a cup of tea, whether the milk had been added to the tea or the tea poured onto the milk. The statistician Ronald Fisher devised a rigorous experiment to test her claim, an account he published in his 1935 book The Design of Experiments. The episode became a foundational example of the logic of hypothesis testing, randomisation, and the null hypothesis.

The experiment

Fisher proposed preparing eight cups of tea, four in which milk was poured first and four in which tea was poured first, identical in every other respect. The lady would be told the composition of the set — four of each — and would taste the cups in a randomised order and classify each as milk-first or tea-first. She therefore had to choose which four of the eight cups were of one kind. There are C(8,4) = 70 equally likely ways to choose four cups from eight under randomisation, so the probability of a perfect classification arising purely by chance is 1/70, about 1.4%. Bristol reportedly identified all eight cups correctly.

Statistical significance

Fisher used the example to explain the concept of a null hypothesis — here, that the lady had no discriminatory ability and was guessing — and argued that an experiment can only ever disprove such a hypothesis, never prove the alternative with certainty. The exact probability calculation underlying the test is the basis of Fisher's exact test for 2×2 contingency tables. The story is frequently retold in statistics teaching as the origin of the conventional 5% significance threshold style of reasoning, though the anecdote's details were recounted by several Rothamsted colleagues with slight variations.

The claimant

Muriel Bristol (1888–1950) was an algologist working at Rothamsted. Her future husband, William Roach, later recalled the episode. Fisher's colleague H. Fairfield Smith also left an account. Bristol's perfect score surprised the sceptical onlookers; she reportedly explained that she could indeed taste the difference, since the order of pouring affects the scalding of the milk and the resulting flavour.
