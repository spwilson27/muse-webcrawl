The Irish Volunteers (Irish: Óglaigh na hÉireann) was a military organisation founded in Dublin in November 1913 by nationalists in response to the formation of the unionist Ulster Volunteers earlier that year. Its declared purpose was to secure and maintain "the rights and liberties common to all the people of Ireland" — in practice, to defend the promised Home Rule settlement and to pressure Britain to implement it. From the Volunteers came, indirectly, the Irish Republican Army (IRA).

Foundation

The organisation was launched at a public meeting in the Rotunda Hospital, Dublin, on 25 November 1913, attended by some 4,000 people. Eoin MacNeill, a Gaelic League scholar, became chief of staff, and The O'Rahilly a leading organiser. The secret Irish Republican Brotherhood (IRB) had promoted the founding and quietly placed its own men on the provisional committee, although MacNeill and others were unaware of the extent of IRB penetration.

Membership grew rapidly through 1914, reaching roughly 180,000. In July 1914 the Volunteers landed rifles at Howth, County Dublin, in imitation of the unionist Larne gun-running.

The split of 1914

On the outbreak of the First World War, Irish Parliamentary Party leader John Redmond urged the Volunteers to enlist in the British Army, in the belief that service would secure Home Rule. The great majority followed him and became the National Volunteers, most of whose members served in Irish regiments during the war. A minority of about 13,500, retaining the name Irish Volunteers under MacNeill, refused, opposing Irish involvement in Britain's war.

Easter Rising and after

The IRB's Military Council used the rump Volunteers — together with the Irish Citizen Army — as the instrument of the Easter Rising of April 1916, keeping the plan secret even from MacNeill, who tried to countermand it. After the Rising's defeat and the executions of its leaders, the Volunteers were reorganised from 1917 under leaders including Michael Collins and Richard Mulcahy, increasingly calling themselves the Irish Republican Army. From 1919 the reorganised force fought the War of Independence as the IRA, and the name "Irish Volunteers" passed into history, though Óglaigh na hÉireann remains the official Irish name of the modern Irish Defence Forces.
