Focal length

Focal length
Common symbolsf
SI unitmetre (m); commonly millimetre in photography
RelatedOptical power (reciprocal, in dioptres)

The focal length of an optical system is a measure of how strongly the system converges or diverges light; it is the inverse of the system's optical power. For a positive (converging) optical system, such as a convex lens, the focal length is the distance over which initially collimated (parallel) rays are brought to a focus. For a negative (diverging) system, the focal length is the distance to the point from which collimated rays appear to diverge after passing through the system.

Thin lens approximation

For a thin lens in air, the focal length is the distance from the centre of the lens to its principal focal point. For a converging lens (for example a biconvex lens), the focal length is positive and is the distance at which a beam of collimated light will be focused to a single spot. For a diverging lens (for example a biconcave lens), the focal length is negative and is the distance to the point from which a collimated beam appears to diverge after passing through the lens. The lensmaker's equation relates focal length to the radii of curvature of the lens surfaces and the refractive index of the material.

In photography

In photography, the focal length of a lens, usually stated in millimetres, determines its angle of view and magnification. Lenses with focal lengths shorter than the diagonal of the image format are called wide-angle lenses; those near the diagonal are "normal" lenses (about 50 mm for the 35 mm full-frame format); and longer focal lengths give telephoto lenses with a narrow angle of view. Because not all cameras use the same sensor size, the "35 mm equivalent focal length" is used to compare angles of view across formats using the crop factor. Zoom lenses have a variable focal length, while prime lenses have a fixed one.
