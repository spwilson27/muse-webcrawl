Metatheatre

[Image: A stage production in which actors perform before a visible on-stage audience seated in boxes, blurring the line between play and spectators]

Metatheatre is a term coined by Lionel Abel in 1963 to describe dramatic works that draw attention to their own nature as theatre — plays about plays, in which characters recognise or exploit their status as performers. Devices include the play within a play, direct address to the audience, asides, choruses commenting on the action, and characters who step outside their roles.

History and examples

Self-referential theatre is ancient: Aristophanes' comedies address the audience in the parabasis, and Roman comedy plays on its own conventions. Shakespeare made extensive use of metatheatrical devices — the players' performance in Hamlet, designed to "catch the conscience of the king"; Prospero's epilogue in The Tempest; and the mechanicals' play in A Midsummer Night's Dream. Calderón's The Great Theatre of the World turned the theatrum mundi metaphor into allegory. Modern landmarks include Luigi Pirandello's Six Characters in Search of an Author (1921), Bertolt Brecht's epic theatre and its alienation effects, and Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead.

Function

Metatheatre is used to interrogate the boundary between fiction and reality, expose theatrical illusion, comment on the act of representation itself, and implicate the audience in the events on stage. The related concept in other media is generally called metafiction.
