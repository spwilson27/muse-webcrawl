Paleogene

The Paleogene Period (also spelled Palaeogene) is the first of three periods of the Cenozoic Era, spanning the time from the end of the Cretaceous Period 66 million years ago to the beginning of the Neogene Period 23.03 million years ago. It comprises the Paleocene, Eocene and Oligocene epochs. The Paleogene is best known as the interval in which mammals — small and marginal during the age of dinosaurs — diversified explosively to fill ecological niches emptied by the Cretaceous–Paleogene mass extinction, the event that opens the period.

Climate

The Paleogene began in the cool aftermath of the end-Cretaceous impact and warmed steadily to the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, c. 56 Ma), a rapid global warming event marked by a large negative carbon-isotope excursion, and then to the sustained hothouse of the Early Eocene Climatic Optimum, when palms and alligators lived well inside the Arctic Circle. From the mid-Eocene the climate cooled in steps, culminating at the Eocene–Oligocene transition (c. 34 Ma) in the first major glaciation of Antarctica and a sharp fall in sea level.

Life

With non-avian dinosaurs gone, mammals radiated into most terrestrial niches: archaic groups such as multituberculates, condylarths and creodonts were joined by the first recognisable members of modern orders — early primates, bats, whales (which evolved from land mammals during the Eocene), horses, and carnivorans. Birds, including giant flightless forms and huge predatory "terror birds" in South America, filled many large-animal roles. In the seas, sharks and bony fish recovered, and the first giant penguins and early pinnipeds appeared by the period's close.

Geography

Continents continued drifting toward their modern positions: the Atlantic widened, India raced north and began colliding with Asia (raising the Himalayas), Australia separated decisively from Antarctica, and the Tethys Sea shrank toward the modern Mediterranean. The opening of Drake Passage around the Eocene–Oligocene boundary helped isolate Antarctica in a circumpolar current, reinforcing global cooling.
