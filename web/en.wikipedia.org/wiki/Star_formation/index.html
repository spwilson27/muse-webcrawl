Star formation

Star formation is the process by which dense regions within molecular clouds in interstellar space, sometimes called "stellar nurseries", collapse and form stars. As a branch of astronomy, star formation includes the study of the interstellar medium and giant molecular clouds as precursors to the process, and the study of protostars and young stellar objects as its immediate products. It is closely related to planet formation. The theory of star formation, accounting for the formation of a single star, is stellar formation theory.

Stellar nurseries

Stars are born inside molecular clouds, cold (10–20 K), dense regions of gas and dust that contain most of the mass of the interstellar medium. A region becomes unstable and collapses under its own gravity when its mass exceeds the Jeans mass — through triggers such as supernova shock waves, galactic spiral-arm compression, or cloud collisions. As a fragment collapses it heats up, radiates in the infrared, and fragments further; conservation of angular momentum flattens the infalling material into a rotating accretion disc around a central protostar.

Protostars and pre-main-sequence stars

The collapsing core first forms a hydrostatic protostar fed by an envelope of gas and dust, often driving bipolar outflows and Herbig–Haro objects along its rotation axis. Objects are classified observationally from Class 0 (deeply embedded) through Class III (disc largely dissipated). Once the envelope clears, the star appears as a pre-main-sequence object — a T Tauri star if of low mass, a Herbig Ae/Be star if more massive — contracting along its Hayashi track while its core temperature rises. When the core reaches roughly ten million kelvin, hydrogen fusion ignites and the star settles onto the main sequence.

Mass limits and feedback

Objects below about 0.08 solar masses never fuse hydrogen and become brown dwarfs; at the high end, radiation pressure and stellar winds limit growth to perhaps 150–300 solar masses. Newly formed massive stars ionise their surroundings into H II regions and, through winds and eventual supernovae, disperse the parent cloud — stellar feedback that both halts and triggers further star formation. Because clouds convert only a few percent of their mass into stars, efficiency is a central problem in the theory.

Observing star formation

Most of the process is hidden by dust at visible wavelengths, so it is studied in the infrared and radio, with space observatories such as Hubble, Spitzer, Herschel and the James Webb Space Telescope revealing embedded protostars and protoplanetary discs, and interferometers such as ALMA resolving disc structure and chemistry. Nearby nurseries include the Orion, Taurus–Auriga and ρ Ophiuchi complexes.
