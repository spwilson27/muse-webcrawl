Toxoid

A toxoid is an inactivated bacterial toxin whose toxicity has been suppressed by chemical or heat treatment, but which retains the ability to stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies (antitoxins). Toxoids are used as vaccines: immunisation with a toxoid teaches the immune system to neutralise the natural toxin produced during infection, preventing the disease symptoms the toxin causes.

Toxoid
TypeVaccine antigen
Made byChemical inactivation of bacterial toxin (e.g. with formaldehyde)
ExamplesTetanus toxoid, diphtheria toxoid

Production

Toxoids are produced by growing the toxin-producing bacterium in culture, purifying the exotoxin, and treating it with formaldehyde, which cross-links the protein and destroys its toxic activity while preserving its antigenic structure. The toxoid is usually adsorbed onto an aluminium salt adjuvant to enhance the immune response.

Uses

The best-known toxoid vaccines protect against tetanus and diphtheria, diseases whose pathology is caused almost entirely by a single potent exotoxin. They are commonly given in combination products such as DTaP and Td. Because immunity wanes over time, booster doses of tetanus toxoid are recommended at intervals of about ten years. Toxoid preparations are also used to immunise animals for the production of antitoxin sera.

History

The concept grew from the work of Emil von Behring on diphtheria antitoxin in the 1890s. Gaston Ramon at the Pasteur Institute developed formaldehyde-inactivated diphtheria toxoid in 1923, and tetanus toxoid followed in 1924; both were used on a mass scale from the 1920s and 1930s onwards, dramatically reducing deaths from both diseases.
