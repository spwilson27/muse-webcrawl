The temperance movement is a social movement promoting temperance or complete abstinence from the consumption of alcoholic beverages. Participants typically criticize alcohol intoxication or promote teetotalism, emphasizing alcohol's negative effects on health, personality, family life, and public order. The movement was at its height in the 19th and early 20th centuries in English-speaking and Scandinavian countries, and it intertwined with abolitionism, women's suffrage, and evangelical Protestantism.

History

Organized temperance began in the United States in the 1820s — the American Temperance Society was founded in 1826 — amid alarm at rising whiskey consumption. Early advocates urged moderation; by the 1830s–40s many societies demanded total abstinence ("teetotalism"). Maine enacted the first statewide prohibition law in 1851 under Neal Dow. The movement revived powerfully after the Civil War: the Woman's Christian Temperance Union (1874), led from 1879 by Frances Willard, linked temperance to women's rights and social reform, while hatchet-wielding Carrie Nation dramatized the cause by smashing saloons in Kansas. The Anti-Saloon League (founded 1893) mastered single-issue political pressure.

Prohibition and decline

The movement's greatest victory was the Eighteenth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution (ratified 1919), which, with the Volstead Act, prohibited the manufacture and sale of alcohol nationwide from 1920. Prohibition reduced alcohol consumption and cirrhosis deaths but also fostered bootlegging, speakeasies, and organized crime. Growing disillusionment led to repeal by the Twenty-first Amendment in 1933. Temperance movements elsewhere won local-option laws, high taxes, and state monopolies (as in Scandinavia and Canada), some of which persist.

Legacy

The movement left lasting institutions — from Alcoholics Anonymous (1935) to modern public-health campaigns against alcohol abuse — and demonstrated the power of organized moral-reform lobbying in democratic politics.
