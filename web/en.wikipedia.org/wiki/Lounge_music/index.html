Lounge music is a type of easy listening music popular in the 1950s and 1960s. It may be described as a genre that evokes feelings of being in a place — a jungle, an island paradise, outer space — other than where one actually is, and was meant to conjure a sophisticated, relaxing atmosphere for the cocktail lounge environment from which it takes its name.

The term also encompasses the broader world of mid-century "mood music": lush orchestral arrangements, wordless vocals, vibraphones, bongos and bird calls. Lounge performers typically adapted popular standards, film themes and light classical pieces into smooth, unobtrusive sets designed not to interrupt conversation.

Exotica and space age pop

Two flamboyant offshoots defined the genre's golden age. Exotica, pioneered by Les Baxter and popularised by Martin Denny's 1957 album of the same name and his hit version of "Quiet Village", blended Afro-Cuban rhythms, Pacific and East Asian instrumentation and jungle sound effects. Space age pop, or bachelor pad music, embraced hi-fi stereo technology with composers such as Juan García Esquivel using panning, unusual instrumentation and "zu-zu-zu" wordless vocals to show off new stereo equipment.

Decline and revival

Rock and roll displaced lounge music in the 1960s, and the style came to be dismissed as kitsch. In the 1990s, however, a lounge revival swept alternative culture: reissue labels, compilations such as Capitol's Ultra-Lounge series, and bands like Combustible Edison brought the genre ironic and then genuine appreciation. Lounge aesthetics fed into the chill-out and downtempo electronic scenes, and the term "lounge" is now also applied loosely to relaxed electronic music intended for bars and cafés.
