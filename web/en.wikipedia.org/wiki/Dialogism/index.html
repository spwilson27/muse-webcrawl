Dialogism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Mikhail Bakhtin]

Dialogism is a literary and philosophical theory developed by the Russian philosopher and literary critic Mikhail Bakhtin (1895–1975), holding that language and thought are fundamentally dialogic: every utterance is addressed to someone, responds to previous utterances, and anticipates future responses. Meaning, for Bakhtin, does not reside in a single speaker or word but arises in the interaction between voices.

Key concepts

In works such as Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics (1929, revised 1963) and the essays collected in The Dialogic Imagination, Bakhtin contrasted the monologic — a single authoritative voice that finalises its characters and subjects — with the polyphonic novel of Dostoevsky, in which a plurality of independent consciousnesses interact without being merged into the author's own.

Related concepts include heteroglossia, the coexistence of multiple social languages and registers within a single national language or text; the utterance as the real unit of speech communication, bounded by a change of speaking subjects; addressivity, an utterance's quality of being directed to an addressee; and the interplay of centripetal forces that unify language and centrifugal forces that stratify and disperse it.

Influence

Bakhtin's dialogism, recovered and published widely only after his death, has influenced literary studies, linguistics, anthropology, education, and organisation theory. It has been read alongside, and sometimes against, the work of Martin Buber on dialogue and Emmanuel Levinas on the Other, and it underlies Bakhtin's account of carnival in Rabelais and His World.
