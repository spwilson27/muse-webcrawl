Breechloader

[Image placeholder: breech-loading mechanism]

A breechloader is a firearm or artillery piece in which the user loads the ammunition (cartridge or shell) via the rear (breech) end of its barrel, as opposed to a muzzleloader, which loads ammunition via the front (muzzle). Breech-loading allows much faster reloading, lets the shooter remain prone or behind cover, and permits the use of rifled barrels with tight-fitting projectiles.

History

Breech-loading designs date to the 14th century, but early cannon and small arms suffered from gas leakage at the breech, which limited their power and safety. Practical breechloaders emerged in the 19th century as machining and metallurgy improved. Notable early designs included the American Hall rifle (1819), the Prussian Dreyse needle gun (1841), the Sharps rifle of the American Civil War era, and the British Snider and Martini–Henry conversions. In artillery, the interrupted-screw breech and the sliding-wedge breech, paired with the de Bange obturator, made large breech-loading guns practical by the 1870s–1880s.

Advantages and disadvantages

Breechloaders reload far more quickly than muzzleloaders because the charge does not have to be rammed down the full length of the barrel. They can be reloaded while the user stays low or behind protection, and the projectile can fit the rifling tightly, improving accuracy. The principal challenges are sealing the breech against high-pressure propellant gases (obturation) and the mechanical complexity of the breech mechanism.

Modern usage

Virtually all modern firearms — pistols, rifles, shotguns, machine guns, and artillery — are breechloaders, most using self-contained metallic cartridges whose brass case expands on firing to seal the breech. The term is today most often used historically, or to distinguish breech-loading weapons in sports such as black-powder shooting.
