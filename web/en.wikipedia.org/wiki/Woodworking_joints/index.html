Woodworking joints

Woodworking joints are methods of connecting pieces of wood together in carpentry and cabinetmaking. Joints rely on geometry, glue area, and sometimes mechanical fasteners or interlocking shapes to achieve strength.

Edge and length joints

The butt joint is the simplest, joining end to face or edge with glue or fixings. The mitre joint joins two pieces at 45 degrees for a neat corner. Scarf and finger joints extend boards lengthwise, while biscuit, dowel and domino joints reinforce edge joints used in panels.

Corner joints

The dovetail joint, with trapezoidal pins and tails, is prized for drawers and boxes for its mechanical resistance to pulling apart. The box (finger) joint is a simpler interlocking alternative. Mortise and tenon joints, among the oldest known, frame doors, tables and chairs; variants include through, wedged and haunched tenons. Bridle, lap and halving joints cross or corner pieces with overlapping cuts.

Frame and panel joints

Dado (housing) and groove joints locate shelves and panels; tongue and groove edges align floorboards and panelling. The dovetail, mortise and tenon families remain standards in fine furniture.

Modern methods

Pocket-hole screws, loose tenons, plates and engineered connectors have become common in production furniture, but traditional joints are still taught and used for their durability and appearance.
