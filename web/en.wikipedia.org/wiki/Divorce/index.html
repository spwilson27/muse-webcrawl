Divorce

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Divorce is the legal dissolution of a marriage. Grounds and procedures vary enormously across history and jurisdiction: fault-based regimes (adultery, cruelty, desertion) dominated until the twentieth century's no-fault revolution — California's Family Law Act 1969 (effective 1970) is the usual landmark — after which irretrievable breakdown, proved by separation periods or mutual consent, became the standard ground in most Western systems (England and Wales adopted purely no-fault divorce in 2022; all US states have some no-fault route since New York's 2010 reform). Some systems retain fault alternatives; a few countries still restrict divorce heavily, and the Philippines remains the notable state without a general divorce law (annulment and legal separation only, alongside Muslim personal law divorce).

Divorce law arranges the consequences: division of matrimonial property (community property vs equitable distribution), maintenance (alimony/spousal support), and orders for children — custody, residence and contact — governed by the child's best interests. Religious divorces may diverge from civil status (the Jewish get and the problem of the agunah; Islamic talaq and khul'; Catholic annulment of a marriage held invalid from the start, distinct from dissolution). Rising divorce rates since the 1960s, peaking in many countries around the 1980s–2000s before stabilising or falling, remain a central dataset — and battleground — in debates on family change.
