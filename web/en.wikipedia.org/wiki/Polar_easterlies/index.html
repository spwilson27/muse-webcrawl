Polar easterlies

The polar easterlies are the dry, cold prevailing winds that blow around the high-pressure areas of the polar highs at the North and South Poles. Cold air subsides at the poles and then moves outward toward lower latitudes; deflected by the Coriolis effect, this flow becomes easterly — blowing from east to west — between roughly 60 and 90 degrees of latitude in each hemisphere. They are one of the three great surface wind belts in each hemisphere, alongside the trade winds (also easterlies) in the tropics and the westerlies of the mid-latitudes.

Characteristics

Unlike the well-defined trade winds, the polar easterlies are weak, shallow, and irregular, especially in the Northern Hemisphere, where the distribution of land masses and the seasonal migration of pressure systems frequently disrupt them. They are strongest and most persistent over Antarctica, where cold dense air over the high ice sheet drains outward, reinforced by katabatic winds descending from the interior plateau to the coast. Where the polar easterlies meet the warmer westerlies along the polar front, rising air feeds the subpolar lows and the travelling cyclones of the mid-latitudes; in the Southern Ocean this boundary is associated with the Antarctic circumpolar trough.

Role in circulation

Within the three-cell model of atmospheric circulation, the polar easterlies form the surface branch of the polar cell: air rises near 60 degrees latitude, flows poleward aloft, sinks over the poles, and returns equatorward at the surface, turned westward by the Coriolis effect (to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern). The winds help transport cold polar air masses toward lower latitudes and influence sea-ice drift and ocean currents in polar regions.
