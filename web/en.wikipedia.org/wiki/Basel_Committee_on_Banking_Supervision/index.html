Basel Committee on Banking Supervision

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) is a committee of banking supervisory authorities established by the central bank governors of the Group of Ten countries in 1974, following the collapse of Bankhaus Herstatt. Hosted by the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, Switzerland, it sets global standards for the prudential regulation of banks, though it has no formal legal authority.

Accords

The Committee's frameworks are known as the Basel Accords. Basel I (1988) introduced minimum capital requirements based on risk-weighted assets. Basel II (2004) added supervisory review and market discipline pillars and allowed internal risk models. After the 2008 financial crisis, Basel III (2010, with finalising reforms sometimes called Basel IV) raised capital quality and quantity, introduced leverage and liquidity ratios (LCR and NSFR), and capital buffers.

Governance and criticism

Members include central banks and regulators such as the Federal Reserve, ECB, and Bank of England. Standards take effect only when transposed into national law, as in the EU's Capital Requirements Regulation and U.S. rulemaking, where implementation timelines have been politically contested. Critics fault the accords' complexity and reliance on banks' own models before 2008. See also Financial Stability Board.
