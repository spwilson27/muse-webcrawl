The 2011 Canadian census was a detailed enumeration of the Canadian population conducted by Statistics Canada, with a census date of May 10, 2011. It counted a total population of 33,476,688, an increase of 5.9 percent over the 2006 census. Ontario remained the most populous province with nearly 12.9 million residents, followed by Quebec and British Columbia, while Alberta recorded the fastest growth among the provinces.

Long-form controversy

The 2011 census became politically contentious when the federal government of Stephen Harper replaced the mandatory long-form census questionnaire — sent to 20 percent of households since 1971 — with the voluntary National Household Survey (NHS). The decision prompted the resignation of the Chief Statistician of Canada, Munir Sheikh, who disputed ministerial claims that he had supported the change, and drew criticism from provincial governments, businesses, academics and community groups who warned of degraded data quality. The NHS ultimately achieved a response rate of about 69 percent, compared with over 93 percent for the previous mandatory long form, and Statistics Canada suppressed data for many small communities because of unreliability. The mandatory long form was restored for the 2016 census.

Findings

Results released through 2012 and 2013 covered population and dwelling counts, age and sex structure, families and households, language, and — through the NHS — immigration, ethnocultural diversity, Aboriginal peoples, labour, education and income. Among the headline findings, seniors aged 65 and over made up a record share of the population, children under five increased for the first time in decades, and more than 200 languages were reported as spoken in Canadian homes.
