Frederick Winslow Taylor

Frederick Winslow Taylor (March 20, 1856 – March 21, 1915) was an American mechanical engineer and management consultant who sought to improve industrial efficiency. He is known as the father of scientific management, set out in his book The Principles of Scientific Management (1911), and his system — often called Taylorism — profoundly shaped 20th-century industry and work organisation.

Born: March 20, 1856, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, U.S.
Died: March 21, 1915, Philadelphia, U.S.
Field: Industrial engineering, management theory
Known for: Scientific management; time study

Career

Taylor was born to a wealthy Quaker family in Germantown, Philadelphia. After abandoning plans for Harvard Law because of failing eyesight, he apprenticed as a patternmaker and machinist, then joined the Midvale Steel Works in 1878, rising from labourer to chief engineer in six years while earning an engineering degree by correspondence from Stevens Institute. At Midvale and later at Bethlehem Steel he developed time study, standardised tools and tasks, piece-rate pay systems and the functional foremanship that became his method; with Maunsel White he also invented high-speed steel, a major advance in metal cutting. Dismissed from Bethlehem in 1901, he spent his remaining years lecturing and writing as the leading apostle of efficiency.

Scientific management

Taylor's principles held that management should: (1) develop a science for each element of a worker's job, replacing rule-of-thumb methods; (2) scientifically select, train and develop workers; (3) cooperate with workers to ensure the work is done according to the science; and (4) divide work and responsibility between management and workers, with management taking over planning. His famous (and partly embellished) pig-iron handling studies with the labourer "Schmidt" at Bethlehem demonstrated large productivity gains. His testimony before Congress in 1912, after a strike at Watertown Arsenal, made his system a national controversy.

Legacy

Taylorism influenced Henry Ford's assembly lines, Lenin's industrial policy, and management education worldwide, while drawing lasting criticism for deskilling labour and treating workers as machine parts — criticisms voiced by unions and dramatised in works such as Charlie Chaplin's Modern Times.
