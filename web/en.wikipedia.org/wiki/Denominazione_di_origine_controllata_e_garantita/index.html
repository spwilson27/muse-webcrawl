Denominazione di origine controllata e garantita (DOCG; literally "controlled and guaranteed designation of origin") is the highest classification for Italian wines. Created in 1980 to strengthen the credibility of the DOC system, DOCG adds the word garantita — guaranteed — because every bottle must pass a government-supervised tasting and analysis, and yields and production rules are stricter than for DOC wines.

History

The DOC system of 1963 had grown rapidly and unevenly, and scandals and variable quality undermined its prestige. In July 1980 a presidential decree conferred the first DOCG status on four prestigious red wines: Barolo and Barbaresco from Piedmont and Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano from Tuscany; Chianti followed in 1984. The Goria Law of 1992 regularised the category. The number of DOCGs has grown steadily — reaching 77 — and now includes sparkling wines such as Franciacorta, Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco Superiore and Asti, whites such as Vermentino di Gallura (Sardinia's only DOCG for many years) and Fiano di Avellino, and sweet wines such as Ramandolo and Recioto di Soave.

Requirements

Compared with DOC, DOCG rules typically impose lower maximum yields, higher minimum alcohol levels, longer compulsory ageing, and bottling within the region of production. Each production lot must be submitted to an authorised tasting commission and laboratory analysis before release; bottles bear a numbered government strip — pink for red wines, green for whites in the traditional scheme — around the neck, guaranteeing traceability and offering protection against counterfeiting. Under the European Union's 2009 wine reform, DOCG wines are classified as DOP (Protected Designation of Origin), though Italy retained the DOC/DOCG terminology on labels.

List and significance

Piedmont has the most DOCGs (including Barolo, Barbaresco, Barbera d'Asti, Gattinara, Ghemme, Roero and Moscato d'Asti), followed by Tuscany (Brunello di Montalcino, Chianti Classico, Carmignano, Vernaccia di San Gimignano) and the Veneto (Amarone della Valpolicella, Soave Superiore, Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco). Critics note that the proliferation of DOCGs risks diluting the guarantee, and that some of Italy's most admired wines — notably the Super Tuscans — long stood outside the system entirely, under the humble IGT category, before some were accommodated by new or amended designations such as Bolgheri Sassicaia DOC.
