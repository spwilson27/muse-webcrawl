Buster Keaton

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Buster Keaton in The General (1926): the great stone face at the throttle of his beloved locomotive, deadpan amid chaos]

Joseph Frank 'Buster' Keaton (4 October 1895 – 1 February 1966) was an American actor, comedian and director, one of the supreme artists of silent cinema. Famous for his deadpan expression — 'the Great Stone Face' — and for physical comedy of engineering precision performed without doubles, he directed and starred in classics including Sherlock Jr. (1924), The General (1926) and Steamboat Bill, Jr. (1928).

Keaton was born into vaudeville (the Three Keatons, in which his father hurled the child Buster around the stage), entered films with Roscoe Arbuckle in 1917, and by 1920 had his own studio. His independent features of the 1920s — meticulously plotted machines of gags escalating to natural cataclysms (the cyclone in Steamboat Bill, the falling house-front in the same film, missing him by inches through an open window) — are now ranked with Chaplin's as silent comedy's summit.

Decline and rediscovery

Signing with MGM in 1928 — Keaton called it the worst mistake of his life — stripped his creative control; sound, alcoholism and divorce completed a twenty-year eclipse of gag-writing, cameos and poverty-row pictures. Rediscovery came in the 1950s–60s (a triumphant Paris season, an honorary Academy Award in 1960, his final role beside Chaplin in Limelight having come in 1952, and work with Samuel Beckett on Film, 1965). Critics and comedians since — from Jackie Chan to Pixar's animators — study his geometry of disaster as a master text.

The General

Now regularly placed in the highest reaches of all-films polls (it entered the top ranks of the Sight and Sound critics' poll in recent decades), The General — a Civil War locomotive chase, photographed in Oregon with real trains and a real bridge collapse — was a costly flop in 1926 whose reputation has done nothing but grow.
