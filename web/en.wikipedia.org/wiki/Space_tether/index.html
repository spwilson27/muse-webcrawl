Space tether

Space tethers are long cables used in space for propulsion, stabilisation, momentum exchange, or maintaining the formation of groups of spacecraft. A tether deployed from a satellite or space station experiences gravity-gradient forces that tend to align it with the local vertical, and can transfer momentum between connected objects without expending propellant. Conducting tethers moving through a planetary magnetic field can also generate electrical power or drag (electrodynamic tethers).

Types and applications

Momentum-exchange tethers (including rotating "rotovators" and skyhooks) could catch payloads in one orbit and release them into another, trading momentum with their host station. Electrodynamic tethers generate current as a conductor moves through Earth's magnetic field; run in reverse with supplied power, they provide thrust, and passively they can de-orbit debris. Formation-flying tethers hold satellites at fixed separations, and space elevators and orbital rings are very large tether concepts anchored to a planetary surface.

Experiments

Tether concepts were proposed by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and developed by Giuseppe Colombo and others. Spaceflight experiments include the Gemini 11 tether of 1966, the Italian Tethered Satellite System flights on the Space Shuttle (TSS-1 in 1992 and TSS-1R in 1996, which deployed nearly 20 km of tether and generated measurable current before the tether broke), the SEDS experiments, and the 2007 YES2 mission, which deployed a 31.7 km tether — the longest structure flown in space at the time. Tether severing by micrometeoroids and debris remains a major design concern, addressed by redundant multi-strand designs such as the Hoytether.
