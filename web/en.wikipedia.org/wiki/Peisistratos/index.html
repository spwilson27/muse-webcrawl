Peisistratos (also spelled Pisistratus; c. 600 BC – 527 BC) was a tyrant of ancient Athens. He seized power three times, ruling finally from 546 BC until his death in 527 BC. Although a tyrant in the Greek sense — a ruler who came to power unconstitutionally — he governed moderately, preserved Solon's constitution in form, and presided over a period of prosperity and cultural flourishing in Athens.

Rise to power

Peisistratos first gained fame as a general in Athens' war against Megara, capturing the port of Nisaea. Around 561 BC he staged a coup: appearing in the agora with self-inflicted wounds, he claimed his enemies had attacked him and was granted a bodyguard of club-bearers, with whom he seized the Acropolis. Driven out twice by the factions of Megacles and Lycurgus, he famously returned the second time with a tall woman named Phye dressed as Athena beside him in a chariot, which Herodotus reports the Athenians accepted as the goddess herself.

His final return came in 546 BC, financed by wealth from his exile in Thrace (including mining revenues from Mount Pangaeum) and allies in Thebes, Argos, and Eretria. He defeated his opponents at the Battle of Pallene and ruled undisturbed thereafter.

Rule

Peisistratos supported the poorer farmers with loans and land confiscated from exiled aristocrats, sent travelling judges into the countryside, and taxed produce to fund public works. He promoted the worship of Athena and Dionysus, expanded the Panathenaic festival with recitations of Homer's epics, and is credited by tradition with commissioning a definitive written edition of the Iliad and Odyssey. He began construction of the great temple of Olympian Zeus and built Athens' first aqueduct, the Enneakrounos fountain house.

Death and succession

Peisistratos died in 527 BC and was succeeded peacefully by his sons Hippias and Hipparchus, whose harsher rule (the Peisistratid tyranny) ended in 510 BC, paving the way for Cleisthenes' democratic reforms.
