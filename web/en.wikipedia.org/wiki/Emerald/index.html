Emerald

Emerald is a gemstone and a variety of the mineral beryl (Be3Al2(SiO3)6) colored green by trace amounts of chromium or sometimes vanadium. Beryl has a hardness of 7.5–8 on the Mohs scale. Most emeralds are highly included, so their toughness (resistance to breakage) is classified as generally poor. Emerald is a cyclosilicate.

Etymology

The word "emerald" is derived (via Old French esmeraude and Middle English emeraude) from Vulgar Latin esmaraldus, a variant of Latin smaragdus, which was borrowed from Ancient Greek smáragdos, meaning "green gem."

Properties determining value

Emeralds, like all colored gemstones, are graded using four basic parameters—the "four Cs" of connoisseurship: color, cut, clarity, and crystal (transparency). Color is by far the most important criterion. The finest emeralds are a pure verdant green hue with vivid color saturation and a tone not too dark. Clarity in emeralds is unusual: they almost always have visible inclusions, poetically called the jardin (French for garden).

Emerald mines

Since antiquity, emeralds have been mined in Egypt and Austria, and in modern times in Colombia, Zambia, Brazil, Afghanistan, and Russia. Colombian emeralds are generally the most prized, especially those from the Muzo, Coscuez, and Chivor mines east of Bogotá. Fine emeralds also come from the Kagem mine in Zambia and from Sandawana in Zimbabwe.

Treatments

Most emeralds are oiled as part of the post-lapidary process to improve their clarity. Cedar oil, having a similar refractive index, is often used. Other liquids such as synthetic oils and polymers are also used. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission requires disclosure of such treatments.

Synthetic emerald

Both hydrothermal and flux-growth synthetics have been produced. The first commercially successful emerald synthesis process was that of Carroll Chatham in the 1930s. Hydrothermal synthetic emeralds are marketed under names such as Biron Emerald and are chemically nearly identical to natural stones.
