Minimum railway curve radius

The minimum railway curve radius is the shortest allowable design radius for the centre line of railway tracks under a particular set of conditions, given limits on track gauge, vehicle design, cant (superelevation), and speed. It governs how sharply a railway line can turn: sharper curves force lower speeds, increase wheel and rail wear, and raise the risk of derailment. The minimum radius depends on whether the line is a high-speed main line, an ordinary railway, a tramway, or an industrial or narrow-gauge line.

Factors affecting minimum radius

Several factors determine how tight a curve can be. The gauge matters because the flanges of the wheels must clear the rails without binding, and longer rigid wheelbases need gentler curves. Cant deficiency — the difference between the superelevation provided and that needed to balance centrifugal force at speed — limits speed on a given radius. Vehicle bogies that can swivel allow tighter curves than fixed-frame designs. In stations and depots, very tight curves (down to a few tens of metres on standard gauge) can be traversed at walking pace, while high-speed lines require radii of several kilometres: Shinkansen lines, for example, use minimum radii around 4,000 m for the highest speeds.

Typical values

Standard-gauge main lines commonly have minimum radii of 300–600 m on conventional routes, while mountain and narrow-gauge railways may use radii as small as 40–100 m. Trams and streetcars routinely negotiate curves of 20–25 m radius in city streets. Specialised vehicles, such as the four-wheel railcars once used on lightly built branches, could traverse still sharper curves. Model railway standards define minimum radii in much smaller absolute terms, scaled to the prototype.

Speed limits and cant

The maximum safe speed on a curve is often estimated by formulae relating speed to radius, cant, and allowable cant deficiency. Raising the cant allows higher speeds on the same radius but limits how slowly heavy freight trains can pass without excessive wear on the inner rail. Tilting trains circumvent part of this constraint by leaning into curves, permitting higher cant deficiency and thus higher speeds on existing alignments.
