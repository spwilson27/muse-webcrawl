Forex scandal

The forex scandal (also known as the forex probe) is a financial scandal that involves the revelation, and subsequent investigation, that banks colluded for at least a decade to manipulate exchange rates on the foreign exchange market (Forex) for their own financial gain. The scope of the scandal included major international banks and multiple regulators and law enforcement agencies around the world. Market regulators in Asia, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, and the United States began investigating the US$5.3 trillion-per-day foreign exchange market after Bloomberg News reported in June 2013 that currency dealers said they had been front-running client orders and rigging the foreign exchange benchmark WM/Reuters rates by colluding with counterparts and pushing through trades during the 60-second window when the benchmark rates are set.

Manipulation of the fix

Traders at different banks formed informal groups via electronic chatrooms — with names such as "The Cartel", "The Bandits' Club" and "The Mafia" — in which they shared confidential client order information and trading positions. Around the daily 4pm London "fix", they coordinated trades to push the benchmark rate in a direction that would profit their own positions, a practice sometimes called "banging the close". The WM/Reuters fix is used by fund managers to value portfolios and settle index-tracking trades, meaning pension funds and ordinary investors were harmed by the rigging.

Fines and settlements

In November 2014, regulators in the UK, US and Switzerland fined six banks a combined total of roughly US$4.3 billion, including the UK Financial Conduct Authority's then-largest ever fines. In May 2015, five banks — Citicorp, JPMorgan Chase, Barclays, the Royal Bank of Scotland and UBS — agreed to plead guilty to US criminal charges and pay a further combined total of more than US$5.7 billion, including a US$2.5 billion criminal fine for Barclays and a US$925 million fine for Citicorp. Bank of America was separately fined US$205 million by the Federal Reserve. In total, fines worldwide exceeded US$10 billion.

Consequences

Dozens of traders were suspended or dismissed, and several were individually prosecuted, although some high-profile trials ended in acquittal or overturned convictions. The scandal led to reforms in benchmark setting: the fixing window was lengthened from one minute to five minutes, and the Financial Stability Board recommended wide-ranging changes to foreign exchange market structure and conduct, including a global code of conduct for the market.
