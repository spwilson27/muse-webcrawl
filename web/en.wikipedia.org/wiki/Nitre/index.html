Nitre

Nitre (also spelled niter) is the mineral form of potassium nitrate, KNO3, a naturally occurring nitrate mineral found as crusts and efflorescences on rocks, cave walls and soils in arid environments. In historical and alchemical usage, "nitre" was the essential oxidising ingredient of gunpowder, commonly called saltpetre (Latin: sal petrae, "rock salt"). The term was also loosely applied to other nitrate deposits, including soda nitre (sodium nitrate).

Occurrence and production

Nitre forms where nitrogenous organic matter decomposes in dry conditions, as in caves, rock shelters, and soils enriched by bat guano or manure. Historically, supplies were so strategically important that "nitre beds" were deliberately constructed: heaps of manure, earth, ash and straw watered with urine and turned for a year or more, from which potassium nitrate was leached and crystallised. In Britain, royal "saltpetremen" were licensed to dig nitrous earth from barns, stables and dovecotes, a practice that caused considerable resentment in the 16th and 17th centuries. Major natural sources included India, which supplied much of Europe's saltpetre during the early modern period, and later the sodium nitrate (caliche) deposits of Chile's Atacama Desert.

Uses

Nitre's principal use was as the oxidiser in black powder, typically about 75% of the mixture by weight, alongside charcoal and sulfur. It was also used in curing meats (giving cured meat its pink colour and inhibiting botulism), as a fertiliser, in glassmaking, in medicine historically as a diuretic, and in food preservation before refrigeration. The development of the Haber–Bosch process in the early 20th century made mined nitre largely obsolete for explosives and fertiliser.

Etymology and ancient usage

The word derives through Old French and Latin nitrum from Greek nitron, referring to natural soda (natron) deposits — in antiquity the terms for sodium carbonate and potassium nitrate were often conflated. Nitre is mentioned in ancient texts including the Bible translations and Roman natural histories, and Chinese texts describe its purification and use in early gunpowder formulas by the Tang and Song dynasties.
