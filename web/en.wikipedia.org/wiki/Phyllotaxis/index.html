Phyllotaxis

The seeds of a sunflower head display spiral phyllotaxis, with spirals counted in Fibonacci numbers.

Phyllotaxis or phyllotaxy is the arrangement of leaves on a plant stem. Phyllotactic spirals form a distinctive class of patterns in nature, visible in the arrangement of leaves, seeds, florets and scales of many plants. The term was coined by Charles Bonnet in 1754 for the study of these arrangements.

Leaf arrangement patterns

The basic arrangements of leaves on a stem are opposite (two leaves at each node, on opposite sides) and alternate (leaves arising singly at each node). Alternate arrangements may be spiral or distichous (in two vertical ranks). In a whorled arrangement, three or more leaves arise at each node.

Fibonacci phyllotaxis

In spiral phyllotaxis, the angle between successive leaves (the divergence angle) very often approximates the golden angle of about 137.5°, and the numbers of spirals visible in the two directions (parastichies) are typically consecutive Fibonacci numbers — for example 34 and 55, or 55 and 89 in a large sunflower head. This arrangement is thought to arise from the dynamics of the growing shoot apex, where each new primordium forms in the largest available gap, influenced by the plant hormone auxin.

Phyllotactic patterns appear not only in leaves but in the scales of pine cones, the florets of daisies and sunflowers, the eyes of pineapples and the bracts of many other plants, and analogous self-organising patterns occur in physical and chemical systems.
