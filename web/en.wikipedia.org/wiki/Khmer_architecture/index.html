Khmer architecture

Khmer architecture (Khmer: ស្ថាបត្យកម្មខ្មែរ), also known as Angkorian architecture, is the architecture produced by the Khmers during the Angkor period of the Khmer Empire from approximately the later half of the 8th century CE to the first half of the 15th century CE. It is characterised by stone temple-mountains, extensive bas-relief galleries, libraries, moats and reservoirs (barays), built primarily to serve Hindu, and later Mahayana Buddhist, worship and to glorify the god-kings of the empire.

Materials

The Khmers built secular structures of perishable materials such as wood and thatch, which have not survived; sacred buildings were made of brick, laterite and, above all, sandstone, quarried at Phnom Kulen and transported along canals. Corbelled vaults were used instead of true arches, limiting the width of galleries and doorways.

Features

  • Prasat — a stepped tower with a shrine on top, representing Mount Meru, the centre of the Hindu universe.
  • Temple-mountain — a pyramid of terraces surrounded by moats representing the cosmic ocean, as at Bakong, Pre Rup and Angkor Wat.
  • Enclosure walls and gopura — entrance pavilions, often cruciform in plan.
  • Libraries — small free-standing shrines within temple enclosures.
  • Lintels, pediments and devata carvings — richly sculpted stone ornament, including dancing figures and narratives from the Ramayana and Mahabharata.
  • Baray — immense rectangular reservoirs, such as the West Baray, combining ritual and hydraulic functions.

Periods and styles

Art historians divide Angkorian architecture into successive styles named after type monuments: Preah Ko, Bakheng, Koh Ker, Pre Rup, Banteay Srei, Khleang, Baphuon, Angkor Wat, and finally the Bayon style of Jayavarman VII, whose late-12th-century building programme produced Angkor Thom, Ta Prohm and Preah Khan. Angkor Wat itself — the largest religious monument in the world — is considered the supreme expression of the style.

Legacy

Khmer architecture profoundly influenced later Thai and Lao building, and its monuments, above all Angkor Wat (which appears on Cambodia's flag), remain central to Cambodian national identity and are protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
