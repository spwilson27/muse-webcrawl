Logarithmic scale

A logarithmic scale (or log scale) is a nonlinear scale used for quantities that span a wide range of values. Instead of equal spacing between consecutive values, as on a linear scale, each step along a logarithmic scale represents a multiplication by a constant factor — most often 10, so that the axis is divided into decades (1, 10, 100, 1000, …). Positions on the scale are proportional to the logarithm of the quantity rather than to the quantity itself.

Properties

On a log scale, equal distances correspond to equal ratios: the distance from 1 to 10 equals that from 10 to 100 and from 2 to 20. A quantity growing at a constant percentage rate (exponential growth) appears as a straight line on a semi-log plot, which makes log scales ideal for displaying compound growth, epidemic curves and long-run economic series. A major limitation is that zero and negative numbers cannot be shown, since logarithms are undefined for them.

Common uses

  • Seismology — the moment magnitude scale (successor to the Richter scale) for earthquake energy.
  • Acoustics — the decibel (dB) scale for sound intensity and signal levels.
  • Chemistry — pH, the negative logarithm of hydrogen-ion concentration.
  • Astronomy — stellar magnitude scales.
  • Electronics and signal processing — Bode plots and frequency responses plotted on log-frequency axes.
  • Finance and economics — log-scale price charts, in which equal percentage moves have equal size.
  • Computing instruments — the slide rule, whose scales are logarithmic, and log–log graph paper.

Perception

Human sensory perception is itself approximately logarithmic (the Weber–Fechner law): we perceive ratios rather than absolute differences in brightness, loudness and pitch, which is why many measurement scales based on perception — decibels, musical pitch, photographic stops (f-numbers) — are logarithmic in structure.
