Jam band

Jam bands are defined by live improvisation
Stylistic originsPsychedelic rock, blues rock, jazz fusion, folk rock, funk
Cultural origins1960s, United States
Typical traitsExtended improvisation, long live sets, fan taping culture

A jam band is a musical group whose live performances are known for extended musical improvisation ("jams") and a repertoire that crosses genre boundaries. The term is most closely associated with the Grateful Dead, whose fans — "Deadheads" — followed the band from city to city, and with Phish, who inherited much of that audience in the 1990s.

Characteristics

Jam bands typically perform songs differently at every concert, varying setlists, extending solos, and segueing between songs without pause. Concerts often feature two sets and a communal touring culture, including fan recording and trading of concert tapes — historically permitted, even encouraged, by the bands — and festival gatherings. Musically, jam bands draw on rock, jazz, bluegrass, funk, reggae, blues, folk, and electronic music, often within a single show.

History

The model emerged in the mid-1960s with the Grateful Dead and the Allman Brothers Band, whose live sets emphasized collective improvisation. After Jerry Garcia's death in 1995, the scene diversified: Phish, Widespread Panic, Dave Matthews Band, The String Cheese Incident, moe., and Umphrey's McGee anchored a festival circuit that included Bonnaroo, founded in 2002 largely around jam acts, and Phish's own large-scale festivals. Later acts such as Goose have brought the model to a new generation.

Business model

Jam bands historically relied less on radio hits and album sales than on constant touring, multiple-night stands, and direct-to-fan sales of live recordings — a model that anticipated aspects of the modern streaming-era live business.
