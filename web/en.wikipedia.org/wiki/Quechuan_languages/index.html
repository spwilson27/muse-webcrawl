Quechuan languages

Quechua is a family of Indigenous languages spoken primarily in the Andes of South America, derived from a common ancestral language. It is the most widely spoken pre-Columbian language family in the Americas, with speakers in Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Argentina, Colombia, and Chile. Varieties are grouped into branches such as Central Quechua (Quechua I) and Peripheral Quechua (Quechua II), the latter including Southern Quechua and Ecuadorian Kichwa.

Distribution of Quechuan varieties across the central Andes.

History

Quechua originated in central Peru and spread widely before and during the Inca Empire, whose administration promoted a variety based on the Cusco region. After the Spanish conquest, Quechua remained a lingua franca for centuries, used by the Catholic Church for evangelization, but it gradually lost prestige to Spanish. Today the number of speakers is estimated in the several millions, though many varieties are endangered.

Features

Quechuan languages are agglutinative, with extensive suffixation, a three-vowel system (/i/, /a/, /u/) with lowered allophones near uvular consonants, and evidential suffixes marking the source of information. Southern Quechua has a standardized written form and official status alongside Spanish in Peru and Bolivia; Ecuador unified its variety as Kichwa.
