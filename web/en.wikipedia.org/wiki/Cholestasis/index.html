Cholestasis

Bile plugs in cholestatic liver tissue

Cholestasis is a condition in which bile does not flow properly from the liver to the duodenum, either because of impaired bile formation within the liver (intrahepatic cholestasis) or obstruction of the bile ducts outside or inside the liver (extrahepatic cholestasis). The word derives from Greek cholē (bile) and stasis (standing still).

Signs and symptoms

Accumulation of bile constituents in the blood causes jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes), intense itching (pruritus), dark urine, and pale, fatty stools (steatorrhea). Prolonged cholestasis can cause malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K), bone disease, and, if unrelieved, secondary biliary cirrhosis and liver failure.

Causes

Extrahepatic obstruction is most often due to gallstones in the common bile duct, strictures, or tumours such as pancreatic cancer and cholangiocarcinoma. Intrahepatic causes include viral hepatitis, alcohol-related and metabolic liver disease, drug-induced injury, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, sepsis, and the genetic disorders of bile transport (progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis). A specific form, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, typically arises in the third trimester.

Diagnosis and treatment

Laboratory findings characteristically show elevated alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase disproportionate to transaminases, together with raised conjugated bilirubin. Imaging—ultrasound, MRCP, or endoscopic procedures—distinguishes obstruction from intrahepatic disease. Treatment addresses the underlying cause: relieving obstruction by endoscopic or surgical means, withdrawing offending drugs, and using agents such as ursodeoxycholic acid and bile-acid sequestrants for itch.
