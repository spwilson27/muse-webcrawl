ETIAS

Full name: European Travel Information and Authorisation System
Type: Electronic travel authorisation
Established by: Regulation (EU) 2018/1240
Operator: eu-LISA (IT systems); screening by Frontex-hosted ETIAS Central Unit

The European Travel Information and Authorisation System (ETIAS) is an electronic authorisation system of the European Union for visa-exempt visitors travelling to 30 European countries (the Schengen Area plus Cyprus) for short stays of up to 90 days in any 180-day period. Modelled in part on the United States ESTA and similar systems (Canada's eTA, the UK ETA), it was established by EU Regulation 2018/1240.

How it works

Travellers from more than 60 visa-exempt countries — including the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and Japan — must apply online before travel, providing personal and passport data, first country of stay, and answers about serious criminal convictions and travel to conflict zones. Applications are automatically screened against EU information systems (SIS, VIS, Europol data, Interpol databases). Most applications are expected to be approved within minutes; a small fraction are referred for manual processing. The fee is €20 (raised from the originally planned €7), with exemptions for travellers under 18 and over 70. An authorisation is valid for three years, or until the passport expires, and is linked electronically to the travel document.

Timeline

The European Commission proposed ETIAS in November 2016, and the regulation was adopted in September 2018. The launch was repeatedly postponed — from an initial 2021 target to 2022, 2023, 2024, and then in alignment with the EU Entry/Exit System (EES). EES began progressive operation in October 2025, and ETIAS is scheduled to follow in the last quarter of 2026, with transitional and grace periods meaning it will not become strictly mandatory until later in 2027.

Data protection and criticism

ETIAS data are retained for the validity of the authorisation plus defined periods, and applicants refused authorisation have a right of appeal in the country that refused it. Civil-liberties groups have raised concerns about algorithmic profiling and false positives, and the EU Fundamental Rights Agency has monitored the system's safeguards.
