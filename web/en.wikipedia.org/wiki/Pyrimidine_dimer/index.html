Pyrimidine dimer

Pyrimidine dimers are molecular lesions formed in DNA when adjacent pyrimidine bases — thymine or cytosine — become covalently linked after absorbing ultraviolet (UV) light. They are a major form of UV-induced DNA damage and a principal cause of sunburn and skin cancers such as melanoma.

Types

The most common type is the cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer (CPD), in which the carbon–carbon double bonds of two neighbouring pyrimidines form a four-membered cyclobutane ring. A second type, the (6–4) photoproduct, links the 6 position of one pyrimidine to the 4 position of its neighbour. Both distort the DNA helix and block replication and transcription if left unrepaired.

Repair

Many organisms repair dimers using photolyase enzymes activated by visible light (photoreactivation). Humans and other placental mammals lack functional photolyases and instead remove dimers by nucleotide excision repair. Defects in this pathway cause xeroderma pigmentosum, a disorder of extreme sun sensitivity and early skin cancer.
