Misandry

Misandry is the hatred of, contempt for, or prejudice against men or boys. It is the counterpart of misogyny, the hatred of women. The term combines the Greek miso- ("hatred") with andr- ("man"). Misandry can manifest in many ways, including social exclusion, sex discrimination, hostility, androcentrism, patriarchy, belittling of men, disenfranchisement, and violence against men.

History of the term

The word "misandry" is descended from the Greek roots misos ("hatred") and anēr, andros ("man"). Use of the term can be found as early as the 19th century, including an 1871 usage in The Spectator magazine. It appeared in the Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary (11th ed.) in 1952 and in the Oxford English Dictionary in 1975. A translation of the French term misandrie was recorded in 1885.

Use in discourse

The term is used in discussions of gender relations, the men's rights movement, and critiques of certain strands of feminism. Some writers, such as Warren Farrell and Christina Hoff Sommers, have argued that misandry is under-recognised compared with misogyny. Others contend that the two phenomena differ in institutional weight and historical context, and that misandry is rarely embedded in social structures in the way misogyny has been.

Criticism and debate

Academic debate over the term includes questions about whether misandry is systemic, whether it operates at an institutional level, and how it intersects with other forms of prejudice such as race and class. Some sociologists argue that while individual prejudice against men exists, it does not amount to a system of oppression comparable to sexism against women. Others, including some within men's studies, dispute this framing.
