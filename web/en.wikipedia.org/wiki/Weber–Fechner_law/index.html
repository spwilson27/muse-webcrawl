Weber–Fechner law

The Weber–Fechner laws are two related hypotheses in the field of psychophysics, known as Weber's law and Fechner's law. Both relate to human perception, more specifically the relation between the actual change in a physical stimulus and the perceived change. This includes stimuli to all sensory modalities: taste, smell, touch, hearing, and vision.

Weber's law

Ernst Heinrich Weber observed that the just-noticeable difference (JND) between two stimuli is proportional to the magnitude of the stimuli: ΔI / I = k, where I is the original intensity, ΔI is the smallest detectable increment, and k (Weber's fraction) is constant for a given modality — roughly 1/50 for lifted weights, for example. The law holds approximately in the middle range of intensities and breaks down at very low and very high levels.

Fechner's law

Gustav Fechner, a student of Weber's, proposed that subjective sensation is proportional to the logarithm of the stimulus intensity: S = k log I. By assuming every JND corresponds to an equal increment of sensation and integrating Weber's law, he derived the logarithmic relation, which can explain why scales such as decibels and stellar magnitudes are logarithmic.

Criticism and alternatives

The laws are approximations; Stevens's power law, proposed by Stanley Smith Stevens in the 20th century, describes many perceptual continua with a power function instead of a logarithm. Signal detection theory has also largely superseded classical psychophysical threshold methods. Nonetheless, Weber's law remains a foundational result of experimental psychology and sensory science.
