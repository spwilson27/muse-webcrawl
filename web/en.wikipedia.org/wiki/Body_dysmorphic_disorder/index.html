Body dysmorphic disorder

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), occasionally still called dysmorphophobia, is a mental disorder defined by an overwhelming preoccupation with a perceived defect or flaw in one's own appearance that is minor or not observable to others. People with BDD commonly engage in repetitive behaviors such as mirror checking, excessive grooming, skin picking, reassurance seeking, or comparing their appearance with that of others, and the preoccupation causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other areas of functioning.

Signs and symptoms

Concerns most often focus on the face or head — skin (acne, scars), hair (thinning, baldness), or nose (size, shape) — but any body part can be the focus, and multiple areas are common. The muscle dysmorphia specifier describes a form, seen mainly in males, in which the person believes their build is too small or insufficiently muscular. Insight in BDD is often poor, and about a third of patients hold delusional beliefs about their appearance. BDD is associated with high rates of psychiatric hospitalization, social isolation, and suicidality; studies report that a large majority experience suicidal ideation and roughly a quarter have attempted suicide.

Causes and mechanisms

The etiology is multifactorial, involving genetic vulnerability, neurobiological differences in visual processing (a bias toward local, detail-focused processing over holistic configural processing), and psychological and social factors such as childhood teasing or bullying about appearance, perfectionism, and cultural emphasis on attractiveness. BDD is classified in the DSM-5 among the obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, reflecting its kinship with obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires a preoccupation with one or more perceived defects not observable or appearing slight to others, repetitive behaviors or mental acts in response, and significant distress or impairment, not better explained by an eating disorder. BDD is frequently underdiagnosed because patients are ashamed to disclose their concerns or seek treatment from dermatologists and cosmetic surgeons instead; cosmetic procedures rarely relieve symptoms and may worsen them.

Treatment

The treatments of choice are cognitive behavioral therapy tailored to BDD — including exposure and ritual prevention and perceptual retraining — and serotonin-reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) medication, often at higher doses and longer trials than for depression. Combination treatment is commonly used for severe cases.

History

The condition was described in 1891 by the Italian psychiatrist Enrico Morselli, who coined "dysmorphophobia". It appeared in the DSM-III as an atypical somatoform disorder, was renamed body dysmorphic disorder in DSM-III-R, and moved to the obsessive-compulsive spectrum in DSM-5 (2013). The disorder gained wider public attention through works such as Katharine Phillips's The Broken Mirror.
