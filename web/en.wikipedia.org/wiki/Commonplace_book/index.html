Commonplace book

[Image: A handwritten commonplace book]

Commonplace books (or commonplaces) are a way to compile knowledge, usually by writing information into books. They have been kept from antiquity, and were kept particularly during the Renaissance and in the nineteenth century. Such books are similar to scrapbooks filled not just with clippings but with one's own written material, quotations, letters, poems, tables of weights and measures, proverbs, prayers, and legal formulas.

Entries are organised under systematic topical headings — the "common places" (Latin loci communes) of classical rhetoric — so that the compiler can retrieve material for use in speech, writing and argument. The practice descends from the rhetorical tradition of Greece and Rome, was revived by Renaissance humanists such as Erasmus and Rudolphus Agricola, and was codified for students in John Locke's influential essay on indexing commonplace books, published posthumously in 1706.

History

By the seventeenth century, commonplacing had become a formal method of study taught at universities including Oxford and Harvard. Philosophers and writers who kept famous commonplace books include John Milton, whose commonplace book survives in manuscript; John Locke; the poet W. H. Auden; and E. M. Forster. In the nineteenth century they were widely kept by women as well as men, blending literary extracts with recipes, remedies and household information.

Unlike a diary or journal, which is chronological, a commonplace book is organised by subject, and unlike a scrapbook it centres on the compiler's own selection and copying. Modern note-taking systems, personal wikis and "second brain" software are often described as digital descendants of the commonplace book.
