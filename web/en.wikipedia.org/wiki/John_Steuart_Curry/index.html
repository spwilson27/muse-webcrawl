John Steuart Curry (November 14, 1897 – August 29, 1946) was an American painter whose career spanned the 1920s and 1930s. Together with Grant Wood and Thomas Hart Benton, he is considered one of the three great painters of American Regionalism, depicting the land, weather and religious life of his native Kansas with dramatic, swirling energy.

Early life

Curry was raised on a farm near Dunavant, Kansas, in a devout household. He studied at the Kansas City Art Institute and the Art Institute of Chicago and worked as an illustrator for magazines such as The Saturday Evening Post. In 1926 he spent a year in Paris studying Old Master painting, particularly Rubens and Courbet, whose muscular figures influenced his style.

Regionalist works

Curry's breakthrough came with Baptism in Kansas (1928), a full-immersion baptism in a farm water tank, exhibited in New York to acclaim and bought by Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney for what became the Whitney Museum. Other famous canvases include Tornado over Kansas (1929), The Line Storm, Wisconsin Landscape, and his circus pictures, painted after travelling with the Ringling Bros. circus in 1932. His paintings celebrate — and sometimes unsettle with — Midwestern themes: storms, revivals, hog butchering, and the abolitionist John Brown.

Murals and later career

From 1936 Curry was artist-in-residence at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, among the first such appointments in America, charged with bringing art to rural communities. Under New Deal art programmes he painted murals for the Department of Justice and Department of the Interior buildings in Washington, D.C. His most famous mural, Tragic Prelude in the Kansas State Capitol (1938–40), shows a gigantic, wrathful John Brown; controversy over the murals led Kansas legislators to block completion of the cycle. Curry died of a heart attack in Madison in 1946, aged 48.
