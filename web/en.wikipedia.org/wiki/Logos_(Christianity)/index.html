Logos (Christianity)

Logos
GreekΛόγος (Lógos)
Literal meaningWord, reason, discourse
Key textGospel of John 1:1–14
Identified withJesus Christ

In Christian theology, the Logos (Greek: Λόγος, lit. "word", "discourse" or "reason") is a name or title of Jesus Christ, derived from the prologue to the Gospel of John: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1). The concept declares that Jesus existed before creation and was the agent through whom all things were made, and that the Logos became incarnate in Jesus.

Background

The term logos had a long history in Greek philosophy, where it denoted reason, speech, and the rational principle ordering the cosmos; it was used by Heraclitus and developed by the Stoics. The Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria combined this Greek concept with the biblical idea of the word of God, providing a bridge that early Christian writers adopted.

In the New Testament

The prologue of John's Gospel presents the Logos as pre-existent, divine, and the source of life and light, who "became flesh and dwelt among us". Related imagery appears in the Book of Revelation, where Christ is called "the Word of God".

Early church development

The second-century apologists, especially Justin Martyr, developed Logos theology to explain Christ's relationship to God the Father and to argue that all truth known to Greek philosophy flowed from the Logos. The doctrine was central to the Trinitarian and Christological controversies of the following centuries, in which the mainstream position held the Logos to be fully God, eternally begotten of the Father, against the Arian view that he was a created being.
