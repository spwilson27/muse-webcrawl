Lord Privy Seal

The Lord Privy Seal (or, more formally, the Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal) is the fifth of the Great Officers of State in the United Kingdom, ranking below the Lord High Treasurer but above the Lord Great Chamberlain. The holder was formerly responsible for the monarch's personal (privy) seal, as opposed to the Great Seal of the Realm, which is in the custody of the Lord Chancellor.

Though one of the oldest offices in the kingdom, it is now largely a sinecure: the holder has no significant duties beyond those of a government minister, and the post is frequently given to a senior politician who serves as Leader of the House of Lords or Leader of the House of Commons.

History

The Privy Seal became a distinct seal of the English sovereign during the reign of King John in the early 13th century, used to authenticate documents of a more personal or less formal character than those requiring the Great Seal. Its keeper, the clericus sigilli privati, rose in importance through the 14th century as the Privy Seal became the normal instrument for authorising warrants that passed under the Great Seal.

By the Tudor period the Lord Privy Seal was a major officer of the Crown. Thomas Cromwell held the office under Henry VIII, and the keepership was frequently combined with great political influence. The office's administrative functions gradually declined as departmental seals and ministerial signatures replaced sealing as the means of authentication.

Since the mid-19th century the post has been a cabinet sinecure without departmental responsibilities. Holders have included figures such as the Earl of Rosebery and, in the 20th century, it became conventional for the post to accompany the leadership of one of the two Houses of Parliament.

Functions

The Lord Privy Seal has no formal duties in relation to the Privy Seal itself, which has fallen into disuse. The office carries a cabinet seat (when so appointed) and a salary only when combined with another role. The incumbent typically undertakes whatever duties the Prime Minister assigns, often including chairing cabinet committees or leading government business in Parliament.

Scotland

Scotland had its own Keeper of the Privy Seal, an office that survived the Union of 1707 but became a sinecure and was eventually left vacant after the death of the last holder in the 20th century's predecessor appointments; Scottish privy seal business is now handled within the Scotland Office.
