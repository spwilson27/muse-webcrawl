Synthon

In retrosynthetic analysis, a synthon is a hypothetical unit within a target molecule that represents a potential starting reagent in the molecule's synthesis. The term was coined by E. J. Corey in 1967. A synthon is an idealized fragment — often an ion or radical — that corresponds to a real, available synthetic equivalent; the process of breaking a target molecule into synthons is called retrosynthetic (or antithetic) disconnection.

Examples

In planning the synthesis of phenylacetic acid, two synthons are identified: a nucleophilic "–COOH" fragment and an electrophilic "PhCH2+" fragment. Their actual synthetic equivalents are cyanide ion (later hydrolyzed to the carboxylic acid) and benzyl bromide. Likewise, an alkyl anion synthon may correspond in practice to a Grignard reagent, and an acyl cation synthon to an acid chloride.

Role in synthesis design

Synthons let chemists reason backward from product to reagents without immediately committing to specific compounds. Corey's formalism underlies computer-aided synthesis design and the system of transform-based retrosynthesis taught in organic chemistry.
