Willingness to pay

Willingness to pay (WTP) is the maximum amount an individual is willing to hand over to procure a good or avoid something undesirable. Since the willingness to pay differs among buyers, the market demand curve is a function of the marginal willingness to pay for each quantity purchased.

Definition

Formally, a consumer's willingness to pay for a good is the highest price at which they would still buy one unit — the price at which they are indifferent between buying and not buying. In demand theory, each point on an individual's demand curve represents the marginal willingness to pay for the corresponding unit, and the area between the demand curve (above the market price) and the price line measures consumer surplus.

Measurement

Because WTP is often unobservable for non-market goods (clean air, public health, ecosystem services), economists estimate it using:

  • Stated-preference methods — surveys such as contingent valuation and choice experiments that ask people what they would pay;
  • Revealed-preference methods — inferring values from actual behaviour, e.g. hedonic pricing of houses or travel-cost models for recreation sites;
  • Experimental methods — auctions and incentive-compatible mechanisms such as the Becker–DeGroot–Marschak procedure.

Uses

Willingness to pay is central to cost–benefit analysis, pricing and product design, environmental and health policy (for example, the value of a statistical life), and the valuation of public goods. Its counterpart in selling contexts is willingness to accept (WTA); experiments consistently show WTA exceeding WTP for the same good, a disparity known as the endowment effect.
