A deer (plural deer) or true deer is a hoofed ruminant mammal of the family Cervidae. The family is divided into the New World deer (Capreolinae), including moose, elk (wapiti), caribou/reindeer, and brocket deer, and the Old World deer (Cervinae), including red deer, fallow deer, sambar, and muntjacs. Male deer grow and shed antlers each year — bony structures unique to the family — while in most species females lack them; reindeer are the exception, with antlered females.

Biology

Deer are ruminants with four-chambered stomachs adapted to diets of leaves, shoots, grasses, and sedges; they range from the tiny pudú and mouse-deer-sized brockets to the moose, which can stand over 2 metres at the shoulder. Antlers grow covered in vascular "velvet", harden for the autumn rut (mating season), and are shed in winter or spring. Fawns are typically born spotted for camouflage.

Distribution and ecology

Deer evolved in Eurasia and North America and now inhabit habitats from Arctic tundra to tropical rainforest. In many regions, suppression of predators such as wolves has led to deer overabundance, damaging forests and crops and spreading tick-borne diseases such as Lyme disease. Deer are also significant prey species and a major cause of wildlife–vehicle collisions.

Human relations

Deer have been hunted for meat (venison), hides, and antlers since prehistory, and feature in the art and mythology of many cultures — from the Celtic god Cernunnos to the sacred deer of Nara, Japan. Several species are farmed; reindeer are semi-domesticated and central to Indigenous peoples of the Arctic.
