Forge welding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forge welding is a solid-state welding process that joins two pieces of metal by heating them in a forge and hammering them together. It is one of the oldest welding methods, practised by blacksmiths since the Bronze and Iron Ages, and was historically essential to the manufacture of tools, weapons, and complex built-up steel structures.

Process

The workpieces are heated to a welding temperature — for steel, a bright yellow-white heat — at which the surfaces become plastic. A flux, traditionally borax, is applied to prevent oxidation and dissolve scale. The pieces are then overlapped and hammered, causing the surfaces to fuse into a single piece. Forge welding is used in pattern welding (Damascus steel), in the manufacture of chain, and in traditional farriery and bladesmithing.

History

Forge welding was the principal method of joining iron and steel for millennia. Wrought iron, with its low carbon content, welds readily. Following the development of arc welding and other modern processes in the late 19th and 20th centuries, forge welding declined in industry but survives in artisan blacksmithing and in mechanised forms such as roll bonding and friction welding.
