"To His Coy Mistress" is a metaphysical poem written by the English author Andrew Marvell (1621–1678) during or shortly before the English Interregnum. It was published posthumously in 1681 in the collection Miscellaneous Poems. The poem is considered one of Marvell's finest works and is among the most famous examples of the carpe diem ("seize the day") genre of lyric poetry.

Structure and argument

The poem is written in iambic tetrameter couplets and is structured like a formal three-part syllogism. In the first section, the speaker tells his mistress that if they had unlimited time — "Had we but world enough and time" — her coyness would be no crime, and he could spend centuries admiring each part of her body.

The second section turns abruptly to the brevity of life: "But at my back I always hear / Time's wingèd chariot hurrying near." Beauty will fade, the grave awaits, and in death the lover's ardour will be useless.

The conclusion urges action: since they cannot stop the sun, they can make it run — "Now let us sport us while we may," seizing the pleasures of the moment while youth and passion last.

Themes

The poem is the classic expression of the carpe diem theme in English literature, combining wit, hyperbole and metaphysical conceits. Its famous opening line and the image of "Time's wingèd chariot" have made it one of the most widely quoted love poems in the language.

Influence

The poem has been alluded to in many later works. T. S. Eliot echoes it in "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" ("And would it have been worth it, after all..."), and its title and imagery influenced numerous writers. The line "world enough and time" became the title of works by Robert Penn Warren and others.
