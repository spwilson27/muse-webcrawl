Propaganda of the deed

Propaganda of the deed (or propaganda by the deed, from the French propagande par le fait) is a specific political direct action meant to be exemplary to others and serve as a catalyst for revolution. It is associated mainly with acts of violence perpetrated by proponents of insurrectionary anarchism in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

The concept emerged from the idea, articulated by Italian anarchists such as Carlo Pisacane, that insurrectionary deeds would prove more effective than words in awakening the oppressed to revolution. Pisacane wrote in 1857 that "ideas spring from deeds and not the other way around." The phrase itself was popularized by French anarchists including Paul Brousse, who used it in the 1870s.

History

The concept was adopted at anarchist congresses of the 1870s, notably by the anti-authoritarian Jura Federation and delegates at the Bern Congress of the International Workingmen's Association in 1876. Errico Malatesta and Carlo Cafiero argued in 1876 that the insurrectionary deed was an effective means of agitation because the masses "understand a deed better than thousands of pamphlets."

During the 1880s and 1890s, a wave of assassinations and bombings by anarchists — including the assassinations of Tsar Alexander II (by Narodnaya Volya, 1881), French President Sadi Carnot (1894), Spanish Prime Minister Antonio Cánovas del Castillo (1897), Empress Elisabeth of Austria (1898), King Umberto I of Italy (1900), and U.S. President William McKinley (1901) — became associated in the public mind with the doctrine. Not all anarchists endorsed such acts; Malatesta and Kropotkin expressed reservations, and the movement increasingly turned toward syndicalism and mass organization.

Interpretation

For its proponents, the deed — strikes, revolts, demonstrations, or attacks on symbols of authority — was intended to demonstrate that the state was vulnerable and to inspire imitation. Critics, both within and outside the anarchist movement, argued that individual acts of violence invited repression and alienated potential supporters. The concept has been compared to modern notions of "leaderless resistance" and remains a subject of historical debate about the relationship between violence and political communication.
