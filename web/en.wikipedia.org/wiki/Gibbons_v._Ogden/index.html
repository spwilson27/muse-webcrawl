Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States that gave the first expansive definition of Congress's power under the Commerce Clause of the Constitution. The Court struck down a state-granted steamboat monopoly and held that the federal power to regulate interstate commerce includes navigation and is plenary within its sphere.

Background

New York State had granted Robert Livingston and Robert Fulton an exclusive monopoly over steamboat navigation in New York waters, which they licensed to Aaron Ogden for the route between New Jersey and New York City. Thomas Gibbons — a former partner turned bitter rival of Ogden, whose boats were captained in part by a young Cornelius Vanderbilt — operated competing steamboats under a federal coasting licence issued under a 1793 act of Congress. Ogden sued, and the New York courts enjoined Gibbons from operating. Daniel Webster argued Gibbons's case on appeal to the Supreme Court.

Decision

Chief Justice John Marshall, writing for a unanimous Court, held that "commerce" in the Commerce Clause means more than buying and selling: it includes navigation and, indeed, all commercial intercourse. Commerce "among the several States" is commerce that concerns more than one state and does not stop at a state border; within that sphere Congress's power "is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and acknowledges no limitations, other than are prescribed in the constitution". Because the New York monopoly conflicted with Gibbons's federal licence, it was void under the Supremacy Clause.

Significance

Gibbons broke the Fulton–Livingston monopoly, opened American waterways to competition and helped fuel the rapid expansion of steamboat traffic. Legally, Marshall's broad reading of the commerce power became the foundation for two centuries of federal regulatory authority, cited in cases extending federal power to railroads, manufacturing and civil-rights legislation, and the case's logic underlies the doctrine that state laws burdening interstate commerce may be struck down even absent federal legislation (the dormant Commerce Clause).
