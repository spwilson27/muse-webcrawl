Cheap talk


Costless, non-binding communication

In game theory, cheap talk is communication between players that does not directly affect the payoffs of the game. Communicating in this way is "cheap" — hence the phrase — because it is costless, non-binding, and unverifiable: a player who lies or bluffs suffers no direct penalty within the game itself. The concept is used to study when talk can nonetheless influence outcomes, as in bargaining, coordination, auctions and political campaigns.

The Crawford–Sobel model

The foundational analysis is Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel's 1982 paper "Strategic Information Transmission". In their model, an informed sender observes a state of the world and sends a message to an uninformed receiver, who then acts. The sender's and receiver's interests diverge by some amount of bias. Crawford and Sobel showed that all equilibria are "partition" equilibria: the sender reveals only coarse information, dividing the state space into intervals. The larger the conflict of interest, the coarser the information transmitted; if interests diverge too much, only the uninformative "babbling equilibrium" — in which messages carry no information at all — survives.

Applications

Cheap talk models have been applied to monetary policy announcements, legislative debate, expert advice, and mediation. Experimental work tests whether real people transmit and use costless messages as theory predicts, generally finding that talk does improve coordination even where theory permits it to be ignored. Variants of the model allow for multiple senders, repeated interaction, or costly lying, which can restore more informative communication.
