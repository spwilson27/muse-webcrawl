Anatolian hieroglyphs are an indigenous logographic and syllabic script native to central Anatolia, used to write the Luwian language. The script consists of some 500 signs, combining pictorial logograms with a syllabary of signs mostly of CV (consonant–vowel) structure. It was once known as "Hittite hieroglyphs," but the language it encodes proved to be Luwian, not Hittite, and the term "Anatolian hieroglyphs" is now preferred.

History

Hieroglyphic signs appear first on seals from the Hittite Empire period (14th–13th centuries BC), where they typically give the names and titles of kings and officials alongside the cuneiform used for general writing. After the collapse of the Hittite Empire around 1180 BC, the script flourished in the Neo-Hittite (Syro-Hittite) states of south-eastern Anatolia and northern Syria, such as Carchemish, Melid and Sam'al, where it was used for monumental royal inscriptions on stone. The latest inscriptions date to about 700 BC, after which the script fell out of use under Assyrian domination.

Decipherment

Early travellers copied inscriptions from sites such as Hamath and Carchemish in the 19th century. Scholars including Archibald Sayce recognised the script's connection with the Hittites. Progress accelerated in the 1930s–1940s through the work of Emil Forrer, Helmuth Bossert, Bedřich Hrozný and others, and the discovery of the bilingual Karatepe inscription (Phoenician–Luwian) in 1946 confirmed many readings. In the 1970s J. D. Hawkins, Anna Morpurgo Davies and Günter Neumann established the corrected readings of several key signs, placing the interpretation of Hieroglyphic Luwian on a firm footing.

Corpus

Important texts include the inscriptions of Carchemish, the Karatepe bilingual, the İvriz rock relief inscription of Warpalawas of Tuwana, the Çineköy inscription, and hundreds of shorter texts on seals, lead strips and stone monuments catalogued in Hawkins's Corpus of Hieroglyphic Luwian Inscriptions (2000).
