The Second Stadtholderless Period (Dutch: Tweede Stadhouderloze Tijdperk) is the era in the history of the Dutch Republic between the death of Stadtholder William III in 1702 and the appointment of William IV as hereditary stadtholder of all the provinces in 1747. During this period the office of stadtholder was vacant in the provinces of Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland and Overijssel, although Friesland, Groningen and Drenthe retained stadtholders from the Nassau-Dietz line.

Background

William III died childless in March 1702, during the War of the Spanish Succession. Because his will designated John William Friso of Nassau-Dietz as heir but the States of Holland disputed the inheritance, the dominant province simply declined to appoint a successor. The regent oligarchy, led in Holland by Grand Pensionary Anthonie Heinsius (until 1720), governed without a stadtholder, in a conscious revival of the "True Freedom" ideology of the First Stadtholderless Period.

Course of the period

The Republic fought the War of the Spanish Succession to its conclusion in 1713, but the peace left it financially exhausted and its influence in European affairs diminished. The regent regime became increasingly oligarchic and closed; offices circulated within a narrow circle of families, and corruption and nepotism drew growing criticism. The barrier treaties and the costs of maintaining fortresses and a fleet strained provincial finances, while trade and industry stagnated relative to Britain and France.

In the provinces that kept a stadtholder, William IV of Orange-Nassau-Dietz gradually inherited the stadtholderates of Friesland, Groningen, Drenthe and (in 1722) Gelderland, building a base from which Orangist sentiment could revive.

End of the period

During the War of the Austrian Succession, French troops invaded the Republic's southern frontier in 1747, taking Bergen op Zoom and threatening Dutch territory directly. Popular panic and Orangist agitation swept the Republic: crowds demanded the appointment of William IV as stadtholder. In May 1747 the States of Holland and the other vacant provinces capitulated, and William IV became stadtholder of all seven provinces, with the office soon made hereditary in both the male and female lines — a constitutional transformation that effectively ended the stadtholderless experiment.
