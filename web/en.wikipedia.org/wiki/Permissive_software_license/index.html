Permissive software license

A permissive software license, sometimes called a BSD-like license, is a free software license that places minimal restrictions on how the software can be used, modified, and redistributed. Ordinarily the only requirements are that the original copyright notice and license text be preserved, and that the software is provided without warranty. Unlike copyleft licenses such as the GNU General Public License, permissive licenses do not require derivative works to be distributed under the same license, so permissively licensed code can be incorporated into proprietary software.

Examples

  • MIT License — a very short license originating at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.
  • BSD licenses — the 2-clause and 3-clause licenses from the University of California, Berkeley; the 3-clause version adds a non-endorsement condition.
  • Apache License 2.0 — a longer permissive license with an express patent grant and retaliation clause.
  • ISC license — a functionally equivalent, simplified license used by the Internet Systems Consortium and OpenBSD.
  • zlib license and Unlicense — brief licenses, the latter attempting to dedicate the work to the public domain.

Comparison with copyleft

The permissive–copyleft distinction is a central divide in free and open-source software. Advocates of permissive licenses argue they maximise freedom for developers and encourage commercial adoption; advocates of copyleft argue that share-alike conditions are necessary to keep software and its improvements free for users. Surveys of open-source projects show permissive licenses, led by the MIT license, accounting for a large and growing share of new projects, particularly in web and corporate-sponsored development.

Permissively licensed code is generally compatible in one direction with copyleft projects: it may be included in a GPL-licensed work, but GPL-licensed code may not be included in a work distributed under a permissive license alone.
