The LCVP (Landing Craft, Vehicle, Personnel), universally known as the Higgins boat, was a shallow-draft landing craft used extensively by the United States and Allied forces for amphibious landings in the Second World War. Its bow ramp allowed troops and light vehicles to disembark directly onto beaches.

Design

Andrew Higgins adapted his swamp workboats (the "Eureka" boats used in the Louisiana bayous) into a military design. A nearly flat bottom and a protected propeller let it run onto sandbars and beaches; a 1941 modification added the steel bow ramp that made the design iconic. The craft was built largely of plywood and carried a platoon of 36 men at about 9 knots, crewed by four.

Service

More than 23,000 were produced. LCVPs landed troops in North Africa, Sicily, Italy, Normandy on D-Day, and across the Pacific island campaigns. General Dwight D. Eisenhower later credited Higgins as "the man who won the war for us", since without such landing craft the strategy of open-beach amphibious assault would have been impossible.
