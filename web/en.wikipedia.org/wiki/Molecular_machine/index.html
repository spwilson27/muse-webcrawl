Molecular machine

FieldNanotechnology, supramolecular chemistry
ExamplesRotaxanes, catenanes, molecular motors
Key figuresJean-Pierre Sauvage, Fraser Stoddart, Ben Feringa
Recognition2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry

A molecular machine, or nanomachine, is a discrete number of molecular components that produce quasi-mechanical movements (output) in response to specific stimuli (input). The term most often applies to synthetic molecules — including molecular motors, switches, and shuttles based on mechanically interlocked architectures such as rotaxanes and catenanes — though biological systems such as ATP synthase and the ribosome are also molecular machines.

Design and synthesis

Synthetic molecular machines are built by threading, clipping, or interlocking molecular components so that one part can move relative to another: a ring shuttling along an axle in a rotaxane, or rings rotating within one another in a catenane. Directional rotation has been achieved in molecular motors powered by light or chemical fuel.

Jean-Pierre Sauvage, Sir Fraser Stoddart, and Bernard Feringa received the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry 'for the design and synthesis of molecular machines'.

Biological machines and applications

Cells are full of molecular machines: ATP synthase, a rotary motor that synthesises ATP; myosin and kinesin, which walk along filaments; and the flagellar motor that propels bacteria.

Proposed applications of synthetic molecular machines include molecular elevators and muscles, drug delivery, responsive materials, and information storage, though practical devices remain largely experimental.
