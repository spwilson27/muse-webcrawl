Clean hands, more formally the unclean hands doctrine, is an equitable defence in law. Expressed in the maxim of equity "he who comes into equity must come with clean hands", it holds that a court of equity will not grant relief to a claimant who has acted inequitably, fraudulently, or in bad faith in relation to the subject matter of the claim. The doctrine protects the integrity of the court itself: a judge may refuse to hear a case not because the defendant deserves to win, but because the claimant's own misconduct disentitles them to the court's assistance. It applies only to equitable remedies — such as injunctions, specific performance, and rescission — and not generally to claims at law for damages.

Requirements

For the defence to succeed, the defendant's misconduct alleged against the claimant must have an "immediate and necessary relation" to the equity the claimant seeks. General bad character or unrelated wrongdoing is not enough: the impropriety must infect the very transaction before the court. In the leading English authority, Moody v Cox [1917], the court stressed that the conduct complained of must be "in the transaction itself". American courts apply the same limitation, and in Keystone Driller Co. v. General Excavator Co., 290 U.S. 240 (1933), the United States Supreme Court refused relief to a patent holder that had deceived the Patent Office, holding that the doctrine closes the doors of equity to one "tainted with inequitableness or bad faith relative to the matter in which he seeks relief".

The defence may be raised by the court on its own motion, since it concerns the court's integrity rather than the parties' rights. It does not ordinarily destroy the underlying claim forever: a claimant whose hands have been "cleansed" — for example by abandoning the wrongful scheme — may return to court.

Applications

The doctrine appears across equitable practice. In land and contract cases, a buyer who has misled the seller may be denied specific performance. In intellectual property, trademark owners who have used their marks deceptively have been refused injunctions (the American doctrine also being known as "unclean hands" in patent and copyright misuse contexts). Courts have invoked it in family and fiduciary litigation where the claimant's fraud on the court or on the opposing party tainted the proceedings. It is frequently pleaded alongside, but is distinct from, the doctrines of laches (unreasonable delay), estoppel, and in pari delicto (where the parties are equally at fault, the court will not aid either).

Criticism

Commentators criticise the doctrine's vagueness — "clean" conduct is nowhere precisely defined — and its potential to produce windfalls for equally culpable defendants. Some courts therefore weigh the public interest and the relative culpability of the parties rather than applying the defence mechanically, and modern courts in several jurisdictions treat it as discretionary, to be applied only where doing so advances equity rather than punishing the claimant.
