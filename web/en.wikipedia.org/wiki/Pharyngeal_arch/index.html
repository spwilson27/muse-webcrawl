Pharyngeal arch

The pharyngeal arches, also called branchial arches or visceral arches, are a series of paired bar-like structures in the developing vertebrate embryo that give rise to much of the face, neck, and associated structures. In humans, five arches form (numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6 — the fifth is rudimentary or absent), appearing in the fourth and fifth weeks of development around the primitive pharynx. Each arch contains a core of mesoderm and neural crest cells covered externally by ectoderm and internally by endoderm, together with its own cranial nerve, artery (aortic arch), cartilage bar, and muscle component.

Derivatives

  • First (mandibular) arch — nerve: trigeminal (V). Gives rise to the maxilla, mandible, zygomatic and parts of the temporal bone, Meckel's cartilage, the malleus and incus, and the muscles of mastication.
  • Second (hyoid) arch — nerve: facial (VII). Gives rise to the stapes, styloid process, lesser horn and upper body of the hyoid bone, and the muscles of facial expression.
  • Third arch — nerve: glossopharyngeal (IX). Gives rise to the greater horn and lower body of the hyoid bone and the stylopharyngeus muscle.
  • Fourth arch — nerve: vagus (X, superior laryngeal branch). Contributes to the thyroid and other laryngeal cartilages and pharyngeal muscles.
  • Sixth arch — nerve: vagus (X, recurrent laryngeal branch). Contributes to the laryngeal cartilages and intrinsic muscles of the larynx.

Associated structures

Between the arches lie the pharyngeal pouches (endodermal, giving rise to structures such as the tympanic cavity, palatine tonsil, parathyroid glands, and thymus), the pharyngeal clefts or grooves (ectodermal), and the aortic arches, which are remodelled into the great arteries of the neck and chest. Developmental disturbances of the arches and pouches underlie congenital conditions such as DiGeorge syndrome (third and fourth pouch defects) and branchial cleft cysts.

Evolution

The pharyngeal arches are homologous to the gill-supporting arches of fishes; their evolutionary modification produced the jaws, ear ossicles, and hyoid apparatus of tetrapods, a classic example in comparative anatomy.
