Charles White Whittlesey (1884–1921) was a United States Army officer who commanded the famed "Lost Battalion" during the Meuse-Argonne Offensive of the First World War. A Wall Street lawyer in civilian life, he became one of the most celebrated American heroes of the war, though he was deeply troubled by his fame and by the losses of his men.

Early life

Whittlesey was born in Florence, Wisconsin, and raised in Pittsfield, Massachusetts. He graduated from Williams College in 1905 and Harvard Law School in 1908, then practiced law in New York City. When the United States entered the First World War in 1917, he enlisted, was commissioned through officer training at Plattsburgh, and was assigned to the 77th Division, a unit largely composed of New York City draftees.

The Lost Battalion

In October 1918, during the Meuse-Argonne Offensive, Major Whittlesey led a force of roughly 550 men of the 308th Infantry that advanced into the Argonne Forest beyond the units on its flanks and became surrounded by German forces in a ravine near Charlevaux Mill. For five days, cut off from supplies and communications (except by carrier pigeon, including the famous bird Cher Ami), the battalion held out under artillery fire — including friendly fire — and repeated attacks. When a German officer sent a surrender demand, Whittlesey is popularly said to have replied "Go to hell!" — a phrase he later denied using. Of the original force, only about 194 men walked out when the position was relieved on 7 October 1918.

Aftermath and death

Whittlesey was promoted to lieutenant colonel and awarded the Medal of Honor, one of the first three conferred for actions in the war. Haunted by the war and hounded by celebrity, he described himself as racked by guilt over his men. In November 1921, while traveling aboard the steamship SS Toloa en route to Havana, he disappeared from the ship and was presumed to have jumped to his death; his body was never recovered.

Legacy

The story of the Lost Battalion became a staple of American war memory, retold in books and a 2001 television film. Whittlesey's story is frequently cited in discussions of what would now be called post-traumatic stress and moral injury among veterans.
