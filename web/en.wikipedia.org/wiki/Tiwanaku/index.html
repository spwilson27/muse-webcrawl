Tiwanaku

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Gateway of the Sun at Tiwanaku, a monolithic andesite portal with carved calendar imagery]

Tiwanaku (also spelled Tiahuanaco) was a pre-Inca civilisation centred on the southern shore of Lake Titicaca in modern Bolivia, which flourished from about AD 500 to 1000 as one of the first great Andean states. Its capital city — with the Akapana pyramid, the Kalasasaya temple and the monolithic Gateway of the Sun, carved from single blocks of andesite transported for kilometres — housed tens of thousands and drew pilgrims and goods from an empire or ritual network reaching into Peru, Chile and Argentina. Tiwanaku's raised-bed (chinampa-like, suka kollus) agriculture on the cold altiplano supported its population at 3,850 metres above sea level.

The state

Tiwanaku's power combined colonies in the warm lowlands (for maize and coca), llama caravans, and a state religion whose 'Staff God' imagery spread across the south-central Andes, shared with its northern contemporary and sometime rival Wari — the two middle-horizon empires that first unified the Andes. Its stone-carving, textiles, polychrome pottery and ritual snuff paraphernalia display a coherent imperial style; whether control was direct, colonial or hegemonic-religious varies by region in current models.

Collapse and legacy

Around AD 1000 the capital was abandoned amid a prolonged regional drought documented in Titicaca lake cores (the timing and its causal weight are debated), and the polity fragmented into Aymara kingdoms. The Inca later revered Tiwanaku as the place where the creator god Viracocha made the first peoples, and built their own myths into its ruins. Spanish chroniclers recorded the site in wonder; modern archaeology and Aymara revival have made it a symbol of indigenous Andean civilisation.
