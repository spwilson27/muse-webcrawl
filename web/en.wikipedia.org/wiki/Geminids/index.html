Geminids

[Image placeholder: Geminid meteor shower]

The Geminids are a prolific meteor shower caused by the object 3200 Phaethon, an asteroid (or extinct comet) with a "rock comet" orbit. The meteors from this shower are seen in mid-December each year, with a peak typically around December 13–14. The shower is considered one of the richest and most reliable of the annual meteor showers, with zenithal hourly rates that can exceed 100–120 meteors per hour under dark skies.

The Geminids are named because their radiant — the point in the sky from which the meteors appear to originate — lies in the constellation Gemini, near the star Castor. Unlike most meteor showers, which originate from comets, the Geminids' parent body is an asteroid, which makes the shower unusual.

Characteristics

Geminid meteors are relatively slow-moving compared with other showers, entering Earth's atmosphere at about 35 km/s (22 mi/s), which makes them easier to observe and photograph. They are often bright and can appear in a range of colours. The shower has been intensifying over the decades, likely because Jupiter's gravity has been pulling the stream of debris closer to Earth's orbit.

History of observation

The Geminids were first observed in 1862 and appeared to be a new shower at the time, emerging as Earth began crossing the debris stream. The parent body, 3200 Phaethon, was identified only in 1983, when it was discovered in data from the IRAS satellite — the first asteroid discovered by a spacecraft — and its orbit was matched to the shower by Fred Whipple.
