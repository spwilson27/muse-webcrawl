The North Aegean (Greek: Βόρειο Αιγαίο, Vóreio Aigaío) is one of the thirteen administrative regions (peripheries) of Greece. It comprises the islands of the northeastern Aegean Sea except for Samothrace, which belongs to East Macedonia and Thrace, and Imbros and Tenedos, which belong to Turkey.

Geography

The region is made up of five regional units corresponding to its major islands: Lesbos, Chios, Samos, Ikaria and Lemnos, together with smaller islands such as Agios Efstratios, Fournoi, Oinousses and Psara. The capital and largest city is Mytilene on Lesbos. The islands lie close to the coast of Turkey, a position that has shaped their history from antiquity through the Greco-Turkish population exchange of 1923.

History

The islands were centres of ancient Greek culture — Lesbos was home to the poets Sappho and Alcaeus, Chios claimed as a birthplace of Homer, and Samos was the home of Pythagoras. They passed through Byzantine, Genoese and Ottoman rule before joining the modern Greek state during the First Balkan War in 1912. The islands were a major destination for Greek refugees from Asia Minor after 1922.

Economy

The regional economy rests on agriculture, fisheries and tourism. Lesbos and Samos produce well-known olive oil and wine; Chios is famed for its mastic gum, produced in the medieval "mastic villages" of the south; Lemnos is one of Greece's most fertile plains. During the European migrant crisis of 2015–16 the islands, as a first point of entry into the EU, received very large numbers of refugees and migrants, straining local reception facilities such as the Moria camp on Lesbos.

Administration

Like other Greek regions, the North Aegean has an elected regional governor and council under the Kallikratis reform of 2011. The region's seat is at Mytilene. Its regional units are subdivided into municipalities centred on each major island.
