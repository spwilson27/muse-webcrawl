Carl Philipp Gottfried (or Gottlieb) von Clausewitz (1 June 1780 – 16 November 1831) was a Prussian general and military theorist who stressed the moral, psychological and political aspects of waging war. His most famous work, Vom Kriege (On War), published posthumously by his wife Marie in 1832, is one of the most important treatises on political-military analysis ever written.

Life and career

Clausewitz joined the Prussian Army as a boy and fought in the French Revolutionary Wars. Captured after the disastrous Battle of Jena-Auerstedt (1806), he later worked with Gerhard von Scharnhorst on reforming the Prussian army. Opposing Prussia's alliance with France, he resigned in 1812 and served in the Russian army during Napoleon's invasion of Russia, helping to negotiate the Convention of Tauroggen. Returning to Prussian service, he fought in the Waterloo campaign as chief of staff of Thielmann's III Corps. From 1818 until 1830 he directed the War Academy in Berlin, where he developed his theory.

Ideas

Clausewitz is famous for the dictum that "war is the continuation of policy by other means" and for concepts including the fog of war, friction — the countless small impediments that make even simple operations difficult — the culminating point of an attack, and the "remarkable trinity" of passion, chance and reason that governs war's nature. His dialectical method contrasted absolute war with the restrained, politically directed conflicts of reality.

Influence

On War profoundly influenced later strategists, staff-college curricula, and thinkers as diverse as Helmuth von Moltke, Lenin, Mao Zedong and modern scholars of strategy and business.
