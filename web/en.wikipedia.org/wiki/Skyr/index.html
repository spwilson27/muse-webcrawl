Skyr

Skyr served with berries

Skyr (Icelandic pronunciation: [ˈscɪːr̥]) is an Icelandic cultured dairy product, technically a fresh acid-set cheese made from skimmed milk, with a consistency similar to strained yogurt but a milder flavor. It has been a staple of Icelandic cuisine since the settlement era, brought from Norway by Norse settlers, though the tradition died out in Scandinavia itself.

Production

Skyr is made by incubating skimmed milk with cultures of Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus; traditionally a small amount of skyr from a previous batch (the "skyr mother") is used to inoculate the milk. Rennet may be added, and after fermentation the whey is strained off, producing the characteristic thick texture. Roughly four times as much milk goes into a given volume of skyr as into ordinary yogurt.

Nutrition and use

Skyr is high in protein (typically about 11 grams per 100 grams), low in fat, and rich in calcium. In Iceland it is eaten plain, with cream and sugar, with berries or jam, or mixed with porridge into a dish called hræringur. In the 21st century it became a popular export and is sold internationally as a yogurt-style health food, including under the label "Icelandic Provisions" in the United States and by Arla in Europe.

History

Skyr is mentioned in medieval Icelandic sagas such as Egils saga. The word and the product survived in Iceland's isolated farming culture; versions were also historically made in Norway and Sweden under related names, but Iceland preserved the original method.
