Nonrecourse debt

Nonrecourse debt or a nonrecourse loan is a secured loan (debt) that is secured by a pledge of collateral, typically real property, but for which the borrower is not personally liable. If the borrower defaults, the lender may seize and sell the collateral, but the lender may not seek out the borrower for any further payment if the collateral does not cover the full amount of the loan. The borrower consequently risks only the value of the collateral.

Characteristics

Nonrecourse debt is typically used to finance commercial real estate, and is also common in project finance and in securities-based lending arrangements such as margin lending in some jurisdictions. Because the lender's remedies are limited to the collateral, nonrecourse loans carry greater risk for the lender than recourse loans, and therefore usually feature lower loan-to-value ratios and somewhat higher interest rates. Standard "carve-outs" — sometimes called "bad boy" provisions — make the borrower personally liable if the borrower commits fraud, misappropriation, voluntary bankruptcy filings, or other specified misconduct.

Nonrecourse and real estate

In the United States, some states have anti-deficiency statutes that make certain residential mortgages nonrecourse in practice: after a foreclosure, the lender cannot obtain a deficiency judgment against the borrower for the remaining balance. During the 2007–2008 financial crisis, the nonrecourse character of many home loans was widely discussed in connection with "strategic default", in which borrowers walked away from properties worth less than the outstanding mortgage balance.

Tax treatment

For United States federal income tax purposes, the discharge of nonrecourse debt in a foreclosure is treated as an amount realised on a sale of the property rather than as cancellation-of-debt income; the full outstanding balance of the nonrecourse debt is included in the amount realised even if the property's fair market value is lower, following the principle established in Commissioner v. Tufts (1983).
