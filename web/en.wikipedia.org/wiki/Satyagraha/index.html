Satyagraha

Satyagraha (Sanskrit: सत्याग्रह; satya, "truth", + āgraha, "insistence" or "holding firmly to") is a philosophy and practice of nonviolent resistance developed by Mohandas K. Gandhi. Gandhi coined the term in South Africa in 1906 to distinguish his method from "passive resistance": the satyagrahi seeks not to defeat the opponent but to convert them, appealing to their conscience through self-suffering and adherence to truth (satya).

Satyagraha
Founder: Mohandas K. Gandhi
Origin: South Africa, 1906
Principles: Truth, nonviolence (ahimsa), self-suffering, non-cooperation

Principles

Gandhi held that the means and ends of a struggle must be consistent: unjust ends cannot be reached by just means. A satyagrahi must refuse to hate the opponent, must accept suffering — including imprisonment and blows — without retaliation, and must remain open to negotiation. Campaigns typically proceed through investigation of grievances, petition and negotiation, publicity, and only then non-cooperation, civil disobedience of unjust laws, and constructive work such as spinning khadi or village uplift.

Major campaigns

Gandhi first applied satyagraha in the Indian community's resistance to discriminatory registration laws in the Transvaal (1906–1914). In India he led campaigns at Champaran (1917) and Kheda (1918), the nationwide non-cooperation movement (1920–22), the Salt March to Dandi (1930) against the British salt monopoly, and the Quit India movement (1942), all central to the Indian independence movement.

Influence

Satyagraha influenced later nonviolent movements worldwide, including Martin Luther King Jr. and the American civil rights movement, Nelson Mandela and the anti-apartheid struggle, and numerous pro-democracy movements. Scholars such as Gene Sharp systematised its methods into the theory of civil resistance.
