Derbyshire lead mining

The lead mining industry of Derbyshire, centred on the limestone uplands of the White Peak in the Peak District, was for many centuries one of Britain's most important sources of lead. The orefield stretches roughly between Wirksworth, Matlock, Bakewell, and Castleton, where veins of galena occur in Carboniferous limestone, often with baryte, fluorspar, and calcite.

History

Lead was mined in the area in Roman times — pigs of lead stamped with imperial inscriptions have been found near Matlock — and the industry is recorded in the Domesday Book. Medieval mining was governed by distinctive customary law administered through the Barmote Courts, which still exist, under which miners ("free miners") could claim veins by marking them out, subject to dues of ore (the "lot" and "cope") to the lord of the field or the Crown's Duchy of Lancaster.

Heyday and decline

The industry expanded greatly in the 17th and 18th centuries with deeper shafts, drainage levels (soughs) driven to unwater the mines, and eventually steam pumping engines; the Cromford and Wirksworth areas were leading centres, and the Magpie Mine near Sheldon, now preserved, dates from this period. Derbyshire produced a large share of British lead until cheap imports and the exhaustion of rich veins caused a collapse in the late 19th century. Residual working for fluorspar and baryte continued into the 20th century, and the Millclose Mine near Darley Dale was a major producer until 1939.

Legacy

The landscape of the White Peak preserves thousands of grassed-over shafts and "rakes" (surface workings along veins), now protected for their industrial archaeology and for rare metal-tolerant flora such as leadwort and spring sandwort that colonise the spoil.
