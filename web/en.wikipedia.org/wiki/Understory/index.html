Understory

In forestry and ecology, the understory (or understorey, underbrush, or undergrowth) is the layer of plant life growing beneath the forest canopy but above the forest floor. It typically consists of shade-tolerant shrubs, small trees, saplings of canopy species, ferns, herbs, and young plants that can persist in the low light — often only 1–5 percent of full sunlight — that penetrates the canopy.

Structure

Forest structure is commonly described in layers: the emergent layer, the canopy, the understory, the shrub layer, and the herb or ground layer, overlying leaf litter and soil. The understory receives more light than the forest floor and is more humid and buffered in temperature than the open air above the canopy. Gaps created when a canopy tree falls let in light and trigger rapid growth among waiting saplings — a process known as gap-phase regeneration.

Ecology

Understory plants provide food and cover for wildlife, nesting habitat for many birds, and browse for deer and other herbivores. Many understory species are adapted to flower and leaf out early in spring before the canopy closes (in temperate deciduous forests) or to photosynthesize efficiently at very low light levels. Excessive deer browsing can simplify the understory, while dense understory thickets of invasive shrubs such as honeysuckle or buckthorn can suppress the regeneration of native trees. In agroforestry systems such as shade-grown coffee and cacao, crops deliberately occupy the understory position beneath taller trees.
