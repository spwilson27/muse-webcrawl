Infante Dom Henry (Portuguese: Henrique; 4 March 1394 – 13 November 1460), known as Henry the Navigator, was a Portuguese prince and a central figure in the early days of the Portuguese Empire and in the 15th-century European maritime discoveries and expansion, often called the Age of Discovery. Through his patronage of voyages down the west coast of Africa, he is credited with beginning Europe's systematic exploration and maritime trade with other continents.

Life

Henry was the third son of King John I of Portugal and Philippa of Lancaster. In 1415 he took part in the conquest of Ceuta in Morocco, an experience said to have sparked his interest in Africa. As grand master of the Order of Christ (successors to the Portuguese Templars), he channelled the order's revenues into sponsoring voyages.

Exploration

From his base in the Algarve, near Sagres, Henry sponsored repeated expeditions southward past Cape Bojador — long feared impassable — which Gil Eanes rounded in 1434. Portuguese mariners went on to reach Cape Blanc, Cape Verde, Sierra Leone, and the Gulf of Guinea, and settled Madeira and the Azores. The motives combined trade (gold and, increasingly, enslaved people), a sea route to India, intelligence about Muslim powers, and the legend of Prester John. The famous "School of Sagres" is considered by modern historians to be largely a later myth, though Henry did gather pilots, chart-makers, and navigators.

Legacy

Henry never sailed on the voyages himself; the epithet "the Navigator" was coined by later English writers. His sponsorship laid the foundations of the Portuguese seaborne empire, but also of the Atlantic slave trade, in which Portuguese expeditions under his patronage participated from the 1440s.
