Sodium nitrite

Sodium nitrite is the inorganic compound with the chemical formula NaNO2. It is a white to slightly yellowish crystalline powder that is very soluble in water and is hygroscopic. From an industrial perspective, it is the most important nitrite salt. It is a precursor to a variety of organic compounds, such as pharmaceuticals, dyes, and pesticides, but it is best known as the food additive E250, used in cured meats to prevent botulism and to fix their characteristic pink color.

Production

Industrial production proceeds principally by absorbing nitrogen oxides (an equimolar mixture of nitric oxide and nitrogen dioxide, obtained by oxidation of ammonia) into aqueous sodium carbonate or sodium hydroxide solution. Sodium nitrite can also be prepared by reduction of sodium nitrate with metals such as lead or zinc, a historically used method.

Uses

Food preservation

Sodium nitrite is used in curing meats — bacon, ham, sausages, hot dogs — where it inhibits the growth of Clostridium botulinum, stabilizes the pink/red color of cured meat by forming nitrosomyoglobin, and contributes to flavor. Its use is regulated because nitrites can react with amino acids under acidic or high-heat conditions to form nitrosamines, some of which are carcinogenic; ascorbate or erythorbate is commonly added to inhibit this. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies ingested nitrite under conditions of endogenous nitrosation as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A).

Medicine and industry

Sodium nitrite is used together with sodium thiosulfate as an antidote for cyanide poisoning, inducing methemoglobin formation that sequesters cyanide. Industrially, it is used in diazotization reactions for azo dyes, as a corrosion inhibitor, in rubber manufacturing, electroplating, and textile bleaching and dyeing.

Toxicity

Sodium nitrite is toxic in sufficient doses: it oxidizes hemoglobin to methemoglobin, impairing oxygen transport, and has been implicated in accidental poisonings where it was mistaken for table salt. Fatal doses in adults are on the order of a few grams. Regulations therefore limit its concentration in foods and require careful labelling.
