Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) are a group of synthetic organic chemicals consisting of carbon, hydrogen, and chlorine atoms attached to a biphenyl molecule. There are 209 possible individual compounds, called congeners, depending on the number and position of the chlorine atoms. PCBs were valued for their chemical stability, non-flammability, high boiling point, and electrical insulating properties, and were manufactured from the late 1920s for use in transformers, capacitors, hydraulic fluids, plasticisers, paints, and carbonless copy paper.

Toxicity and contamination

The same stability that made PCBs industrially useful makes them persistent environmental pollutants: they resist breakdown, accumulate in fatty tissue, and biomagnify up food chains. Exposure is associated with cancer, immune suppression, reproductive and developmental effects, and endocrine disruption; in 2013 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified PCBs as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1). Mass poisonings, such as the Yushō disease incident in Japan (1968) and the Yu-cheng incident in Taiwan (1979), were caused by contaminated rice bran oil. Extensive contamination of the Hudson River by General Electric's discharges led to one of the largest Superfund cleanups in the United States.

Regulation

The United States banned PCB manufacture under the Toxic Substances Control Act in 1979. PCBs are listed for elimination under the 2001 Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants, although large quantities remain in old electrical equipment, building materials, and sediments, and remediation is ongoing worldwide.
