Tumor protein p53, also known as p53, cellular tumor antigen p53, phosphoprotein p53, tumor suppressor p53, antigen NY-CO-13, or transformation-related protein 53 (TRP53), is a regulatory protein that is often mutated in human cancers. The p53 proteins (originally thought to be, and often spoken of as, a single protein) are crucial in vertebrates, where they prevent cancer formation. As such, p53 has been described as "the guardian of the genome" because of its role in conserving stability by preventing genome mutation. Hence TP53 is classified as a tumor suppressor gene.

The name p53 was given in 1979 describing the apparent molecular mass; SDS-PAGE analysis indicates that it is a 53-kilodalton (kDa) protein. However, the actual mass of the full-length p53 protein (p53α), based on the sum of masses of the amino acid residues, is only 43.7 kDa. This difference is due to the high number of proline residues in the protein, which slow its migration on SDS-PAGE, thus making it appear heavier than it actually is.

Discovery

p53 was identified in 1979 by several groups, including Arnold Levine's at Princeton and David Lane's at Imperial Cancer Research Fund, as a protein bound to the large T antigen of the SV40 virus in transformed cells. It was initially mistaken for an oncogene because early clones came from tumors and appeared to immortalize cells. By 1989 it became clear that the wild-type protein is in fact a tumor suppressor, and that the earlier results reflected mutant forms; mutations in TP53 were soon shown to be among the most common genetic alterations in human cancer.

Function

In a normal cell, p53 is kept at low levels by its negative regulator MDM2, which ubiquitinates it for degradation. DNA damage, oncogene activation, hypoxia and other stresses stabilize p53, which then acts as a transcription factor, binding specific DNA response elements and activating or repressing hundreds of target genes. Depending on the severity of the damage, p53 induces cell-cycle arrest (largely through the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21) so that DNA can be repaired, senescence, or programmed cell death (apoptosis) through targets such as BAX, PUMA and NOXA. p53 also regulates metabolism, autophagy, ferroptosis and stem-cell behavior.

p53 in cancer

TP53 is mutated in more than half of human cancers — the most frequently altered gene known in cancer. Most mutations are missense changes clustered in the DNA-binding domain, and mutant p53 often accumulates to high levels in tumor cells, sometimes acquiring gain-of-function activities that promote invasion and drug resistance. Germline TP53 mutations cause Li–Fraumeni syndrome, a hereditary condition of greatly increased risk of early-onset cancers including breast cancer, sarcomas, brain tumors and adrenocortical carcinoma. Because of its central role, restoring p53 function — for example with MDM2 antagonists or mutant-p53 reactivating compounds — has been a long-standing goal of cancer drug development.
