The Horse Fair is an oil-on-canvas painting by French artist Rosa Bonheur, first exhibited at the Paris Salon in 1853. Measuring approximately 2.45 by 5.07 metres (8 by 16.5 feet), it is among the largest and most celebrated animal paintings of the nineteenth century. The work depicts the horse fair held in Paris on the tree-lined Boulevard de l'Hôpital, near the Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital.

Background

Bonheur prepared for the painting for about a year and a half, making regular visits to the Paris horse market twice a week. Because women were not permitted to wear trousers in public without official dispensation, she obtained a permission de travestissement from the Paris police so that she could dress in men's clothing and move freely among the dealers and animals. She made numerous studies of Percheron and other draught horses, observing their movement, musculature, and behaviour.

Description

The painting presents a spirited parade of horses being shown to prospective buyers: powerful Percheron draught horses, half-wild steeds rearing and straining against their handlers, and riders in blue smocks guiding the animals around the market ground. A strong diagonal recession and a dramatic, luminous sky give the scene both documentary accuracy and romantic energy. Bonheur placed a small self-portrait, in the guise of one of the male handlers near the centre of the composition.

Reception and history

Exhibited at the Salon of 1853, The Horse Fair was an immediate sensation and established Bonheur's international reputation; Queen Victoria requested a private viewing when the painting toured England. It was later exhibited in the United States, where it was purchased by Cornelius Vanderbilt II in 1887 and donated to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, where it remains one of the museum's most popular works. Bonheur also painted a smaller autograph version, now in the National Gallery, London.

Legacy

The painting is frequently cited as a landmark in the careers of women artists: its monumental scale, traditionally the preserve of history painting, and its commercial success demonstrated that a woman could triumph in the most demanding genres of academic art. Bonheur became the first woman artist to receive the Legion of Honour, promoted to officer of the order in 1894.
