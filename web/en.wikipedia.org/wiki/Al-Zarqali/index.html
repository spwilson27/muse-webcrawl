Al-Zarqali

Abū Isḥāq Ibrāhīm ibn Yaḥyā al-Naqqāsh al-Zarqālī
Born: c. 1029, Toledo, Al-Andalus
Died: c. 1100 (some sources 1087), Seville or Córdoba
Known for: Toledan Tables, universal astrolabe (saphaea), solar apogee motion

Abū Isḥāq Ibrāhīm ibn Yaḥyā al-Naqqāsh al-Zarqālī (c. 1029 – c. 1100), known in Latin as Arzachel or Azarquiel, was an Andalusian astronomer and instrument maker, among the most prominent scientists of Islamic Iberia. He worked in Toledo at the court of al-Ma'mun of Toledo and later in Seville.

Astronomical work

Al-Zarqali was the principal author of the Toledan Tables, astronomical tables computed from observations made at Toledo in the 1060s–1070s, which — in Latin translation and the adaptations associated with Gerard of Cremona — became the standard tables in Europe for centuries. From his own long series of solar observations (over some 25 years) he determined that the Sun's apogee moves relative to the fixed stars at about 12 arcseconds per year in excess of precession, an early recognition that the solar apogee has its own proper motion; the modern value of the quantity he isolated is close to his estimate. He also measured the obliquity of the ecliptic and parameters of the Moon and planets, and wrote on the trepidation of the equinoxes.

Instruments

Trained first as a metalworker and engraver — his nisba al-Naqqash means "the engraver" — al-Zarqali became famous for his instruments. He invented the saphaea (al-ṣafīḥa al-zarqāliyya), a universal astrolabe whose stereographic projection was arranged so that it could be used at any latitude, a major refinement of the planispheric astrolabe. He also constructed celebrated water clocks for Toledo, described as showing the hours and the phases of the Moon, which operated until dismantled in the 12th century.

Influence

His works were cited by later astronomers including Copernicus, who mentions Arzachel in De revolutionibus, and the lunar crater Arzachel is named after him.
