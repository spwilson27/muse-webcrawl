Underconsumption is a theory in economics that recessions and stagnation arise from inadequate consumer demand for goods and services relative to their output. In this view, production grows faster than the purchasing power of the population — particularly where income is unequally distributed, so that wages lag behind output while the wealthy save more than they can profitably invest — leading to unsold goods, idle capacity and unemployment.

History

Ideas of underconsumption date to the early nineteenth century, in the work of Sismondi and Malthus, and were developed most fully by the English economist J. A. Hobson, who traced crises to oversaving by the rich. Rosa Luxemburg argued in The Accumulation of Capital (1913) that capitalism requires non-capitalist markets to absorb its surplus. John Maynard Keynes treated Hobson as an important precursor: his own theory of effective demand in The General Theory (1936) incorporated a similar mechanism, though framed through the propensity to consume and the inducement to invest.

Criticism

Mainstream and Marxian critics alike have challenged simple underconsumptionism: classical economists held that supply creates its own demand (Say's law), while many Marxists argue the root cause of crises lies in the falling rate of profit and disproportionality rather than consumption shortfalls alone. Modern descendants of the theory appear in debates over secular stagnation, global savings gluts and wage-led growth.
