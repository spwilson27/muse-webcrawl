In physics and electrical engineering, a conductor is an object or type of material that allows the flow of electric charge in one or more directions. Materials made of metal are common electrical conductors. Electric current is generated by the flow of negatively charged electrons, positively charged holes, and positive or negative ions, depending on the medium.

Properties

A conductor is characterised by its electrical resistivity: the lower the resistivity, the better the conductor. Silver has the lowest resistivity of common materials, but cost makes copper the standard for wiring, with aluminium used where weight and cost matter, such as overhead power lines. In an ideal conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field inside is zero and any charge resides on the surface. Real conductors oppose current flow, converting some electrical energy to heat (Joule heating); their resistance generally increases with temperature.

Mechanism

In metals, atoms form a lattice while their outer electrons are delocalised into a "sea" that moves freely under an applied field. Quantum band theory refines this picture: conductors have overlapping or partially filled energy bands, so electrons can gain energy and move. Superconductors, below a critical temperature, lose all electrical resistance and expel magnetic fields. Semiconductors sit between conductors and insulators, with conductivity tunable by doping, temperature and light.

Forms and applications

Conductors are made into wires, cables, busbars, foils, traces on printed circuit boards, and transparent films such as indium tin oxide for touchscreens. The ampacity (current-carrying capacity) of a conductor depends on its cross-section, material, insulation and cooling conditions. Non-metallic conductors include electrolytes, graphite, plasmas and conductive polymers.
