Nucleotide analogue

Nucleotide analogues are molecules that structurally resemble natural nucleotides — the building blocks of DNA and RNA — closely enough to be incorporated into a growing nucleic acid chain by polymerases, but differ in ways that alter stability, pairing, or further elongation. Closely related nucleoside analogues lack the phosphate group and are converted to nucleotide form inside cells.

Mechanism of action

Many analogues act as chain terminators: they lack the 3′-hydroxyl group required to attach the next nucleotide, so incorporation halts DNA synthesis. Others introduce mutations by ambiguous base pairing, or inhibit viral polymerases preferentially over host polymerases, giving them selective toxicity against viruses.

Medical uses

Nucleoside and nucleotide analogues are mainstays of antiviral therapy. Examples include aciclovir against herpesviruses, zidovudine (AZT) and tenofovir against HIV, lamivudine and entecavir against hepatitis B, and sofosbuvir against hepatitis C. Remdesivir, a nucleotide analogue prodrug, was used against Ebola and COVID-19. Analogues such as cytarabine and gemcitabine are also used in cancer chemotherapy, and azathioprine/mercaptopurine act as immunosuppressants through purine analogue metabolites.

Research uses

Dideoxynucleotides were the basis of Sanger DNA sequencing, in which controlled chain termination generates the fragments whose sizes reveal the sequence.
