Ductus venosus

The ductus venosus in the fetal circulation

The ductus venosus is a blood vessel of the fetal circulation that shunts oxygenated blood returning from the placenta in the umbilical vein directly toward the inferior vena cava, largely bypassing the fetal liver. It is one of the three physiological shunts of the fetus, along with the ductus arteriosus and the foramen ovale, that allow the fetal circulatory system to function before the lungs and liver take over their postnatal roles.

Function

Roughly half of the umbilical venous blood passes through the ductus venosus (the proportion varies with gestation and fetal state), delivering well-oxygenated blood preferentially across the foramen ovale to the left side of the heart and hence to the coronary and cerebral circulations. A sphincter-like mechanism at the origin of the ductus, innervated via the vagus, regulates the flow.

After birth and clinical significance

At birth, with the cessation of umbilical flow, the ductus venosus closes functionally within hours to days and later anatomically, becoming the fibrous ligamentum venosum of the liver. Failure of closure (patent ductus venosus) is a rare congenital portosystemic shunt that can cause hyperammonaemia and hepatic encephalopathy. Doppler ultrasound of ductus venosus flow has become an important clinical tool: an absent or reversed a-wave in the first trimester is associated with chromosomal abnormality and congenital heart disease, and abnormal waveforms in later pregnancy indicate fetal compromise in growth-restricted fetuses.
