Extraterrestrial life

Extraterrestrial life, or alien life, is life which does not originate from Earth. No extraterrestrial life has yet been conclusively detected, although the possibility is investigated by the fields of exobiology and astrobiology. Such life might range from simple single-celled organisms comparable to bacteria to intelligent beings far more advanced than humans.

Icy moons such as Europa are among the most promising places to search for life.

Basis for the search

Arguments for the plausibility of life elsewhere rest on the vast number of stars and planets — thousands of exoplanets have been confirmed, many in their stars' habitable zones — and on the discovery that terrestrial life tolerates extreme cold, heat, acidity, radiation and pressure. Liquid water is usually treated as the key requirement, which makes Mars (ancient rivers and subsurface ice), Europa and Enceladus (subsurface oceans), and Titan (hydrocarbon lakes, water-ammonia ocean) prime targets within the Solar System.

Searches

Search strategies include direct sampling (Mars rovers and sample-return), plume fly-throughs at Enceladus, remote detection of biosignature gases such as oxygen, methane and phosphine in exoplanet atmospheres, and SETI searches for artificial radio or optical signals. Claims have periodically excited attention — the 1976 Viking biology experiments, the 1996 ALH84001 "microfossil" announcement, and the contested 2020 report of phosphine on Venus — but none has withstood scrutiny as conclusive evidence.

Implications

The apparent absence of detectable civilisations despite the galaxy's age and size is the Fermi paradox. A confirmed discovery of even microbial extraterrestrial life would be one of the most consequential in science, bearing on the origin of life, the probability terms of the Drake equation, and philosophy and religion.
