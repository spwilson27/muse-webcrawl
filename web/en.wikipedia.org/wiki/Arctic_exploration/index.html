Arctic exploration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arctic exploration is the physical exploration of the Arctic region of the Earth. For centuries the principal goals were the discovery of the Northwest Passage across North America and the Northeast Passage along Siberia, both promising shorter trade routes between Europe and Asia, and later the attainment of the North Pole itself.

Early voyages

Norse seafarers reached Greenland and North America around 1000 AD. Systematic European exploration began in the 16th century with voyages by John Cabot, Martin Frobisher, John Davis, and Henry Hudson, each searching for a passage to the Pacific. William Barentsz reached Svalbard and Novaya Zemlya in the 1590s while probing the Northeast Passage.

The Northwest Passage

The British Admiralty mounted many expeditions in the 19th century. Sir John Franklin's 1845 expedition with HMS Erebus and HMS Terror was lost with all 129 men, prompting a series of search expeditions that mapped much of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. Roald Amundsen made the first complete transit of the Northwest Passage in 1903–1906 aboard Gjøa. The Northeast Passage was first completed by Adolf Erik Nordenskiöld in 1878–1879.

Race for the Pole

Fridtjof Nansen's drift of the Fram across the Arctic Ocean (1893–1896) revolutionised understanding of the polar sea. Frederick Cook (1908) and Robert Peary (1909) made rival claims to have reached the North Pole; Peary's claim was long accepted but remains debated. The first undisputed attainment of the Pole was by air: Roald Amundsen, Lincoln Ellsworth, and Umberto Nobile flew over it in the airship Norge in 1926. The first confirmed surface party to reach the Pole was led by Ralph Plaisted in 1968, and Wally Herbert's team crossed the Arctic Ocean on the surface in 1968–1969.

Modern era

Twentieth-century exploration added submarines — USS Nautilus crossed under the Pole in 1958 — research icebreakers, drifting stations, and satellites. Today exploration is largely scientific, focusing on sea ice, climate change, and the mapping of the continental shelves.
