Judaeo-Spanish, commonly called Ladino (also Judezmo, Spanyol and other names), is a Romance language derived from Old Spanish. It was spoken by the Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain by the Alhambra Decree of 1492, who carried it to the Ottoman Empire — to cities such as Salonica (Thessaloniki), Istanbul, Sarajevo and İzmir — and to North Africa and later diasporas.

Features

Because it was cut off from Spain in 1492, Ladino preserves many features of fifteenth-century Spanish lost in the modern standard, such as the sounds written dj and sh, alongside vocabulary borrowed from Hebrew, Turkish, Greek, Arabic and Balkan languages. It was traditionally written in Hebrew characters (Rashi script for print, Solitreo in handwriting) and is now usually written in the Latin alphabet. In strict usage, "Ladino" sometimes refers to the word-for-word calque language used for translating sacred texts, as distinct from the spoken vernacular, though the terms are commonly interchanged.

Status

The destruction of communities such as Salonica in the Holocaust and assimilation in Israel and elsewhere drastically reduced the number of speakers. Ladino is classified as endangered; revival efforts include the Autoridad Nasionala del Ladino in Israel, academic programmes, literature and music. Spain's 2015 nationality law for Sephardic descendants renewed international attention to the language.
