Tuned radio frequency receiver

A tuned radio frequency receiver (TRF receiver) is a type of radio receiver composed of one or more tuned radio frequency (RF) amplifier stages followed by a detector (demodulator) circuit to extract the audio signal, and usually an audio frequency amplifier. It was the most popular type of receiver during the 1920s, the first decade of radio broadcasting, before being largely displaced by the superheterodyne receiver in the 1930s.

Operation

In a TRF receiver, all amplification of the weak signal from the antenna takes place at the frequency of the station being received. Several cascaded RF amplifier stages, each with a tuned circuit (an LC resonator), are tuned together ("ganged") to the same frequency, simultaneously providing gain and selectivity: the tuned circuits pass the desired station while rejecting others. The amplified RF signal is then demodulated — historically by a grid-leak or diode detector — and the resulting audio is amplified for a loudspeaker or headphones.

Advantages and disadvantages

The TRF design is simple in concept but difficult in practice. Each RF stage must be tuned in unison, which on early sets meant adjusting several separate dials; tracking errors made tuning fiddly. Gain and bandwidth varied across the tuning range, and cascaded tuned amplifiers were prone to self-oscillation, requiring careful neutralisation (as in the Neutrodyne circuit). The superheterodyne, which converts the incoming signal to a fixed intermediate frequency where gain and selectivity are easier to achieve, solved most of these problems and became the standard architecture for nearly all radio and television receivers.
