The Haggadah (Hebrew: הַגָּדָה, "telling"; plural: Haggadot) is a Jewish text that sets forth the order of the Passover Seder. According to Jewish practice, reading from the book during the Seder fulfils the commandment incumbent on every Jew to tell their children the story of the Exodus of the Israelites from slavery in Egypt, as described in the Book of Exodus. The Haggadah contains the narrative of the Exodus, blessings, commentaries from the Talmud and Midrash, and traditional songs.

History

The core of the Haggadah derives from the Mishnah (tractate Pesachim, chapter 10), compiled around 200 CE, which prescribes the Four Questions asked by the youngest child, the recounting of the Exodus, the first part of the Hallel psalms before the meal and the second part after it, and the song of praise. The text grew by accretion during the Talmudic and Geonic periods; the first separate Haggadah manuscripts survive from the medieval period. With the advent of printing, the first printed Haggadah appeared in Guadalajara, Spain (c. 1482), followed by famous illustrated editions from Prague (1526), Mantua and Venice. It is among the most frequently printed Jewish books, with thousands of editions.

Contents of the Seder

The Haggadah guides the fifteen steps of the Seder, conventionally remembered by a rhyme: Kadesh (blessing over wine), Urchatz (washing hands), Karpas (green vegetable), Yachatz (breaking the middle matzah), Maggid (telling the story — including the Four Questions, the Four Sons, the Ten Plagues, and the song Dayenu), Rochtzah (washing before the meal), Motzi Matzah (blessings over matzah), Maror (bitter herb), Korech (the Hillel sandwich), Shulchan Orech (the festive meal), Tzafun (eating the hidden afikoman), Barech (grace after meals), Hallel (psalms of praise), and Nirtzah (conclusion, with songs such as Echad Mi Yodea and Chad Gadya, and the wish "Next year in Jerusalem").

Illuminated manuscripts

Medieval Haggadot are among the glories of Jewish art. The Sarajevo Haggadah, made in Barcelona around 1350 and now kept in the National Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina, is famed for its miniatures and for its survival through the Second World War and the Bosnian War. Other celebrated examples include the Golden Haggadah (c. 1320), the Birds' Head Haggadah (Germany, c. 1300, whose human figures have birds' heads), and the Washington Haggadah (1478), written and illuminated by Joel ben Simeon.

Modern Haggadot

Contemporary communities produce Haggadot for every movement and interest — feminist, vegetarian, social-justice, children's and interfaith editions — and the commercial Maxwell House Haggadah, distributed free in the United States from 1932, became for many American families the standard text.
