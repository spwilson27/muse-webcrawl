Personal jurisdiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Personal jurisdiction (or in personam jurisdiction) is a court's power to render a judgment binding a particular defendant. Traditionally based on physical presence and service within the forum, the modern doctrine — reshaped by International Shoe Co. v. Washington (1945) — asks whether the defendant has “minimum contacts” with the forum such that maintaining the suit “does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.”

Two forms are distinguished: general jurisdiction, allowing suit on any claim where the defendant is “at home” (domicile for individuals; incorporation and principal place of business for corporations, narrowed by Daimler AG v. Bauman, 2014), and specific jurisdiction, over claims arising from the defendant's forum-directed activities (Burger King v. Rudzewicz, 1985; Ford Motor Co. v. Montana, 2021), exercised under state long-arm statutes and tested against due process. Consent, waiver, tag jurisdiction (transient presence, Burnham, 1990) and the internet's borderless contacts (Zippo sliding scale and its successors) continue to generate doctrine. A defendant may challenge jurisdiction by special appearance or motion, and default judgments entered without it are void.
