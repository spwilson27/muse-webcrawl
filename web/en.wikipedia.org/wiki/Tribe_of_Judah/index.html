According to the Hebrew Bible, the Tribe of Judah (Hebrew: שֵׁבֶט יְהוּדָה, Shevet Yehudah) was one of the twelve tribes of Israel, descended from Judah, the fourth son of Jacob and Leah. It became the pre-eminent southern tribe: the Kingdom of Judah took its name, the words "Jew" and "Judaism" derive from it, and its symbol, the lion, remains an emblem of Jerusalem.

Biblical account

In Jacob's blessing (Genesis 49), Judah is praised as a lion's whelp from whose line the sceptre would not depart — a passage read as foretelling the Davidic monarchy. In the allotment of the land under Joshua, Judah received the largest southern territory, stretching from the Dead Sea to the Mediterranean coastlands (shared with Simeon and Dan) and including Hebron, Beersheba and the Negev fringe. The tribe produced the judge Othniel (through Caleb's family) and, crucially, King David, who was of Judah, from Bethlehem; after Solomon's death the tribes split, and Judah, with Benjamin, formed the southern kingdom ruled by David's descendants from Jerusalem.

History

The Kingdom of Judah survived the destruction of the northern kingdom of Israel by Assyria (722 BCE) but fell to Babylon: Jerusalem and its Temple were destroyed in 586 BCE and much of the population deported. After the Persian conquest, returnees rebuilt the Temple and the province of Yehud, and Judah's identity — now "Jewish" — became the enduring identity of the Israelite people, since the other tribes were largely lost to history. In Jewish and Christian tradition the Messiah is expected from Judah's royal line, and the "Lion of the tribe of Judah" is a title applied to Christ in the Book of Revelation.
