The Kurdish languages (Kurdish: کوردی, Kurdî) are a group of closely related Northwestern Iranian languages spoken by the Kurds in the region known as Kurdistan, spanning southeastern Turkey, northwestern Iran, northern Iraq, and northern Syria, as well as large diaspora communities. Whether the varieties form a single language or a dialect continuum remains debated.

Classification and varieties

The three main groups are:

  • Northern Kurdish (Kurmanji) — the most widely spoken, used in Turkey, Syria, northern Iraq and Iran, and the diaspora; written in a Latin-based alphabet.
  • Central Kurdish (Sorani) — spoken in Iraqi Kurdistan and western Iran; the official variety in Iraq's Kurdistan Region, written in a modified Perso-Arabic script.
  • Southern Kurdish — spoken in Iran's Kermanshah and Ilam provinces and adjacent parts of Iraq.

The Zaza and Gorani languages are sometimes grouped with Kurdish, though many linguists class them as separate languages within the Northwestern Iranian branch.

History

Kurdish descends from the Iranian languages of the ancient Medes, though direct attestation begins in the Islamic era. A written literature developed from the 16th–17th centuries — the poet Ehmedê Xanî's epic Mem û Zîn (1692) is its most celebrated work. In the 20th century the public use and teaching of Kurdish were long restricted in Turkey, while in Iraq and Iran its status fluctuated with politics. Since the 1990s, Kurdish-language media, publishing, and broadcasting have expanded rapidly.

Grammar

Kurdish varieties are subject–object–verb languages. Kurmanji preserves grammatical gender (masculine/feminine) and an oblique case, and shows split ergativity in past-tense transitive constructions; Sorani has largely lost gender and case but uses a complex system of pronominal clitics. Vocabulary is basically Iranian with extensive loans from Arabic, Turkish, and Persian.
