Antiquities Act

Antiquities Act of 1906
EnactedJune 8, 1906
Signed byPresident Theodore Roosevelt
Codification54 U.S.C. §§ 320301–320303

The Antiquities Act of 1906 (54 U.S.C. §§ 320301–320303) is a United States law that authorizes the President to declare by proclamation historic landmarks, structures and other objects of historic or scientific interest on federal land to be national monuments. It also establishes penalties for excavating or removing antiquities from federal land without a permit.

History

The Act was passed after years of concern about looting and vandalism of Native American archaeological sites in the Southwest, including the cliff dwellings at Mesa Verde. Congressman John F. Lacey of Iowa and archaeologist Edgar Lee Hewett were central to drafting it. President Theodore Roosevelt signed it on June 8, 1906, and within months proclaimed Devils Tower in Wyoming the first national monument.

Use

Presidents of both parties have used the Act more than 150 times. Early monuments such as the Grand Canyon (1908) and Olympic (1909) were later redesignated as national parks. The Act states monuments should be confined to "the smallest area compatible" with care of the protected objects, a phrase that has fuelled legal and political disputes over large marine and landscape monuments, including Bears Ears and Grand Staircase–Escalante in Utah.

Amendments

After President Jimmy Carter designated 56 million acres in Alaska in 1978, the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 required congressional approval for future large withdrawals in Alaska. A 1950 amendment similarly constrained monument creation in Wyoming.
