Farthest North describes the most northerly latitude reached by explorers before the conquest of the North Pole rendered the term obsolete. The Arctic polar regions are much more accessible than those of Antarctica, so Arctic exploration developed earlier. Records of Farthest North were contested for centuries by European and later American explorers competing for national prestige and funding.

History of the record

In antiquity, Pytheas of Massalia (c. 325 BC) reported sailing to "Thule" six days north of Britain. In the Age of Discovery, Martin Frobisher reached about 63°N in 1578, Henry Hudson 80°23′N north of Svalbard in 1608, and William Edward Parry reached 82°45′N by ship in 1827 — a record that stood for 49 years.

Charles Francis Hall's USS Polaris reached 82°11′N in 1871; the British Arctic Expedition of George Nares reached 82°48′N by ship and 83°20′N by sledge in 1876. Adolphus Greely's Lady Franklin Bay Expedition reached 83°24′N in 1882. Fridtjof Nansen and Hjalmar Johansen, leaving the drifting Fram, reached 86°13.6′N in April 1895 — the title of Nansen's famous account of the expedition was Farthest North (1897).

The Duke of the Abruzzi's expedition reached 86°34′N in 1900, and Robert Peary claimed 87°06′N in 1906. Peary then claimed to have reached the North Pole itself on 6 April 1909, a claim disputed by supporters of Frederick Cook, who said he had reached it in April 1908; neither claim is now generally accepted as proven. The pole was indisputably overflown by Roald Amundsen's airship Norge in 1926, and first confirmed surface attainment came with Ralph Plaisted's snowmobile party in 1968 (aviation-supported landings date to 1948).

Related terms

The corresponding term for the south is Farthest South, used during Antarctic exploration until Roald Amundsen reached the South Pole in 1911.
