Textile arts of indigenous peoples of the Americas

The textile arts of indigenous peoples of the Americas are among the oldest and most sophisticated craft traditions in the world. Fibre arts — spinning, dyeing, weaving, embroidery, beadwork and featherwork — have been practised across North, Central and South America for thousands of years, and textiles carried deep social, religious and economic significance.

Andean woven textiles

Materials and techniques

Materials varied by region: cotton and camelid wool (alpaca, llama, vicuña) in the Andes; cotton and agave fibre in Mesoamerica; buffalo wool, cedar bark, spruce root and mountain-goat wool on the Northwest Coast; and dog hair among Coast Salish peoples. Techniques included backstrap and upright looms, twining, sprang, brocade, tapestry weave, and the knotted-string recording devices known as quipu in the Inca Empire. Dyes were drawn from plants, minerals and insects such as cochineal, which became a major colonial export.

Regional traditions

The Paracas and Nazca cultures of Peru produced extraordinarily fine embroidered mantles over 2,000 years ago, and Andean weaving remains a living tradition. In the North American Southwest, Pueblo and later Navajo weavers became famous for their blankets and rugs. Northwest Coast Chilkat blankets, woven from goat wool and cedar bark, displayed formline designs of clan crests. In Mesoamerica, Maya and Zapotec weavers still use backstrap looms to produce garments rich in symbolic motifs.

Significance

Historically textiles often functioned as wealth, tribute and currency — the Inca state taxed in cloth and distributed fine qompi garments as rewards. Today indigenous textile arts are both an economic mainstay and a powerful assertion of cultural identity.
