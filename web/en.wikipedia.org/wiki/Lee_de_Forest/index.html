Lee de Forest (26 August 1873 – 30 June 1961) was an American inventor, electrical engineer and an early pioneer in electronics of fundamental importance. He invented the first practical amplifying electrical component, the Audion (a triode vacuum tube), in 1906. This made possible live radio broadcasting and became the key component of all radio, telephone, and television systems until the transistor largely replaced it in the 1950s. De Forest held more than 300 patents and is often styled the "father of radio."

Early life and education

De Forest was born in Council Bluffs, Iowa, the son of a Congregational minister who became president of Talladega College in Alabama. Educated at Mount Hermon School in Massachusetts and at Yale's Sheffield Scientific School, he completed a PhD in physics in 1899 with a dissertation on the reflection of Hertzian waves — among the first American doctorates devoted to wireless telegraphy.

The Audion

Working in wireless telegraphy in the early 1900s, de Forest experimented with detectors and, in 1906, added a third element — a zigzag wire grid between the filament and plate of a two-element vacuum tube. The resulting "Audion" could not only detect radio signals but also amplify them, because a small voltage on the grid controlled a much larger current through the tube. De Forest did not fully understand the physics of his device, and its value for amplification was demonstrated by others (notably Edwin Armstrong's regenerative circuit), leading to bitter and protracted patent litigation that de Forest ultimately won in the courts. In 1912 his Audions were used in the first long-distance telephone amplifiers, and the triode became the foundation of electronics.

Broadcasting pioneer

De Forest tirelessly promoted radio as a medium for music and news rather than point-to-point messaging. He broadcast phonograph music experimentally from 1907, transmitted a live performance from the Metropolitan Opera in 1910, reported the 1916 presidential election returns from his Highbridge, New York, station 2XG, and in 1920 his San Francisco station (later KZY) began regularly scheduled broadcasts. His companies, however, repeatedly failed financially, and he was tried (and acquitted) for mail fraud in 1913 over allegedly worthless stock.

Sound film and later life

In the 1920s de Forest developed Phonofilm, an optical sound-on-film system, and produced short sound films years before The Jazz Singer (1927), though the Hollywood studios declined to adopt his patents and he lost the resulting litigation. Later honours included the Institute of Radio Engineers' Medal of Honor, election to the National Academy of Sciences, and an honorary Academy Award in 1960 "for his pioneer inventions which brought sound to the motion picture." He died in Hollywood in 1961.
