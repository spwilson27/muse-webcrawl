Shona people

The Shona people are a Bantu ethnic group native to southern Africa, primarily in Zimbabwe, where they form the majority of the population, with significant communities in Mozambique, South Africa, and Botswana. They speak Shona (chiShona), a Bantu language with several dialect groups including Karanga, Zezuru, Manyika, Ndau, and Korekore, unified in a standard written form.

History

Shona-speaking peoples built the great stone city of Great Zimbabwe, which flourished from roughly the 11th to the 15th century as the capital of a wealthy trading kingdom dealing in gold and ivory with the Swahili coast. Later Shona states included the Mutapa Empire and the Rozvi Empire, the latter destroyed in the upheavals of the early 19th century. Shona resistance to colonisation featured in both Chimurenga wars, and spirit mediums such as Nehanda became enduring national symbols.

Culture

Traditional Shona religion centres on Mwari (God) and veneration of ancestral spirits (vadzimu), often through spirit mediums. Shona culture is renowned for its mbira music — the lamellophone central to the bira ceremony — and for modern stone sculpture (Zimbabwean stone sculpture), which gained international fame from the mid-20th century. The mbira dzavadzimu was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Demographics

Shona speakers number well over ten million. Most Shona are now Christians, often combining church affiliation with traditional practices.
