National Forest (England)

The National Forest is an environmental project in central England covering about 200 square miles (520 km2) across parts of Derbyshire, Leicestershire, and Staffordshire. Created in the 1990s, it aims to blend ancient woodlands such as Needwood and Charnwood with new planting across a landscape once marked by coal mining and clay extraction, linking the cities of Burton upon Trent, Ashby-de-la-Zouch, Coalville, and Swadlincote.

Creation and planting

The idea of a new national forest was proposed in the late 1980s; the area was selected in 1990, and the National Forest Company was established in 1995 to lead the work, funded by the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs. More than nine million trees have since been planted, raising woodland cover within the boundary from about 6 per cent in 1991 to more than 22 per cent, on the way toward an eventual goal of roughly one-third tree cover. Planting has been carried out by private landowners, charities, local authorities, and community groups, with species chosen to reflect local character and increasingly to build resilience against climate change and tree disease.

Landscape and attractions

The forest contains Conkers, an award-winning visitor centre at Moira, the National Memorial Arboretum just outside its boundary near Alrewas, and historic estates such as Calke Abbey. Former colliery sites have been transformed into woodlands and country parks, including Donisthorpe Woodland Park on a former pit. The project is credited with boosting tourism — visitor numbers to the area have grown substantially since the 1990s — improving biodiversity, and pioneering large-scale landscape regeneration later emulated by other community forests and by the Northern Forest project launched in 2018.
