Oregano

Oregano (Origanum vulgare) is a flowering plant in the mint family (Lamiaceae) native to the Mediterranean region. It is a perennial herb growing 20–80 cm tall, with opposite leaves and purple flowers. Its dried leaves are one of the most widely used culinary herbs in the world, a staple of Italian, Greek and other Mediterranean cuisines, as well as Mexican cooking, where the related Lippia graveolens (Mexican oregano) is also used.

Culinary use

Oregano's flavour is aromatic, warm and slightly bitter, and it is usually more flavourful dried than fresh. It is a defining ingredient of pizza and tomato sauces, Greek salad, grilled meats and the Argentine chimichurri. The herb became popular in the United States after the Second World War, when soldiers returning from Italy brought a taste for pizza with them.

Chemistry and other uses

The essential oil of oregano is rich in carvacrol and thymol, compounds also found in thyme, which give the herb its characteristic pungency and have been studied for antimicrobial properties. In folk medicine oregano was used for respiratory and digestive complaints. The name derives from the Greek origanon, from oros (mountain) and ganos (joy) — "joy of the mountain".
