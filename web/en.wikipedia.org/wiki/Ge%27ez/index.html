Ge'ez (ግእዝ, Gəʿəz) is an ancient South Semitic language originating from present-day northern Ethiopia and Eritrea in the Horn of Africa. It later became the official language of the Kingdom of Aksum and the Ethiopian imperial court, and it remains the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and the Beta Israel Jewish community.

History

Inscriptions in Ge'ez date to at least the 4th century CE in the Aksumite Empire. As a spoken vernacular it was gradually replaced by Amharic, Tigrinya, and Tigre, but it survived as the language of scripture, liturgy, and scholarship for over a millennium, much as Latin did in Europe.

Script

Ge'ez is written in the Ge'ez script, an abugida in which each character represents a consonant–vowel syllable. The script has 26 basic consonantal characters, each with seven vocalic forms, and it also serves to write Amharic, Tigrinya, and other languages.

Literature

An extensive body of religious literature exists in Ge'ez, including the Ethiopian biblical canon — the broadest of any Christian tradition — as well as chronicles, hagiographies, and works such as the Kebra Nagast.
