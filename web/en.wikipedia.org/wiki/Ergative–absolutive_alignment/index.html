Ergative–absolutive alignment

Ergative–absolutive alignment is a type of morphosyntactic alignment in which the subject of an intransitive verb behaves like the object of a transitive verb.

Ergative–absolutive alignment is a type of morphosyntactic alignment in which the single argument of an intransitive verb (S) is treated grammatically the same as the object of a transitive verb (P), while the agent of a transitive verb (A) is treated differently. This contrasts with the nominative–accusative alignment familiar from English and most European languages, in which S and A are grouped together as the nominative case and P stands apart as the accusative.

Terminology

In an ergative language, the case that marks both S and P is called the absolutive, while the case marking A alone is the ergative. The terms derive from the Greek ergon, "work", reflecting the idea that the ergative argument is the one "doing the work" of the transitive action. Ergativity may be expressed through case marking on nouns, through verbal agreement (cross-referencing) morphology, or both, and a language may be ergative in one part of its grammar and accusative in another — a phenomenon known as split ergativity.

Distribution

Ergative alignment is widespread across the world's languages. It is characteristic of many Australian Aboriginal languages such as Dyirbal, of Basque, of the Mayan languages, of many languages of the Caucasus such as Georgian, of Inuit languages, and of numerous languages of South Asia and the Americas. Dyirbal is famous in the literature because its ergativity extends into syntax: in coordinate clauses, the shared argument must be in the absolutive (S or P) in order to be omitted, the pattern called syntactic ergativity.

Split ergativity

Many languages show ergative case marking only under certain conditions. In Hindi-Urdu, ergative marking on the transitive subject appears in perfective aspect clauses but not in imperfective ones. In other languages the split depends on the person or animacy of the arguments: in Dyirbal, first- and second-person pronouns follow a nominative–accusative pattern while full noun phrases are ergative, reflecting the nominal hierarchy. Some Australian languages split according to tense, with ergativity confined to past tenses.

Examples

A schematic example from Basque illustrates the pattern. In the intransitive sentence Gizona etorri da "The man has arrived", gizona "the man" bears absolutive marking. In the transitive Gizonak mutila ikusi du "The man saw the boy", the agent gizonak takes the ergative suffix -k, while the patient mutila "the boy" remains absolutive, as does the auxiliary verb agreement pattern.
