The Drachenfels ("Dragon's Rock") is a hill, 321 metres (1,053 ft) high, in the Siebengebirge uplands between Königswinter and Bad Honnef in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. Crowned by a ruined castle built in 1138–1149 by Archbishop Arnold I of Cologne, it is one of the most famous landmarks of the Rhine valley and was an icon of German Romanticism.

Legend

According to legend, the hero Siegfried of the Nibelungenlied slew the dragon Fafnir on the Drachenfels and bathed in its blood, making himself invulnerable. A dragon's cave (Drachenhöhle) is shown on the hillside, and the nearby Nibelungenhalle displays dragon-themed art. The hill's trachyte rock was quarried from Roman times; its stone was used in the building of Cologne Cathedral.

Tourism

The Drachenfels became a premier destination of Rhine tourism in the 19th century, praised by Lord Byron in Childe Harold's Pilgrimage. The Drachenfelsbahn, a rack railway opened in 1883, is the oldest cog railway still operating in Germany and carries visitors from Königswinter to the summit. At the foot of the hill stands Schloss Drachenburg (1882–84), a flamboyant palace-villa built for the financier Stephan von Sarter and now a museum. The summit plateau offers views over the Rhine, Bonn and the Siebengebirge, and the area forms part of one of Germany's oldest nature reserves, protected since the 1920s–30s.
