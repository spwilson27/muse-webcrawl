Fire support

Fire support is a military term used to describe the application of firepower — from artillery, mortars, naval guns, rockets, missiles and attack aircraft — in support of manoeuvring forces such as infantry and armour. Its purpose is to suppress, neutralise or destroy enemy forces and positions so that friendly troops can move, attack or defend more effectively. Fire support is a central element of combined-arms warfare and of the United States and NATO doctrine of joint fires.

Types of fire support

Fire support is delivered by several means: indirect fire from field artillery and mortars, which engage targets the gunners cannot see; naval gunfire support from warships offshore; and close air support from fixed-wing aircraft and attack helicopters operating near friendly troops. Historically, machine guns and direct-fire weapons also provided supporting fire, and in modern usage the concept extends to electronic warfare and other non-kinetic "fires."

Coordination

Because supporting weapons often fire beyond visual range, fire support must be carefully coordinated to avoid fratricide and to concentrate effects where they are needed. Forward observers and joint terminal attack controllers accompany manoeuvre units, identify targets and call for and adjust fire. Fire support coordination measures — such as fire support coordination lines, no-fire areas and restrictive fire areas — deconflict the use of surface and air fires. Planning is normally centralised at the headquarters controlling the battle, while execution is decentralised so that observers can request fire quickly.

Tasks and effects

Supporting fires may be preparatory, delivered before an attack to soften defences; suppressive, to keep enemy heads down while friendly forces move; counter-battery, against enemy artillery; or interdiction, against reserves and supply routes. Smoke screens fired by artillery are also used to conceal movement. The increasing range and precision of modern systems — guided rockets, GPS-guided shells and loitering munitions — have made fire support a dominant source of battlefield effects in recent conflicts.

History

Systematic fire support developed during the First World War, when massed artillery, creeping barrages and predicted fire were used to support infantry assaults on trench systems. The Second World War added integrated naval gunfire and air support, famously in the amphibious landings in Normandy and the Pacific. Digital communications and precision-guided munitions have since compressed the time between a call for fire and its delivery from hours to minutes.
