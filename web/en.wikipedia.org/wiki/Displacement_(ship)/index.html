Displacement (ship)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The displacement or displacement tonnage of a ship is its weight. As the term indicates, it is measured indirectly, using Archimedes' principle, by first calculating the volume of water displaced by the ship, then converting that value into weight. Traditionally, various measurement rules have been in use, giving rise to measures such as displacement in long tons or metric tonnes.

By contrast, other tonnage measures — gross tonnage and net tonnage — are based on the ship's internal volume, not its weight. A ship's displacement varies with its loading condition: a fully loaded cargo ship sits lower in the water and displaces more than the same ship empty.

Types of displacement

  • Light displacement — the weight of the ship excluding cargo, fuel, ballast, stores, passengers and crew, but with water in the boiler to steaming level.
  • Loaded (full load) displacement — the weight when fully loaded to the summer load line in seawater of standard density.
  • Standard displacement — defined by the Washington Naval Treaty of 1922: the ship complete, fully manned, engined and equipped ready for sea, but without fuel or reserve feed water.
  • Normal displacement — standard displacement plus two-thirds of fuel and reserve feed water, used in some treaty-era references.

Calculation

Displacement is computed from the submerged hull volume, obtained by integrating cross-sectional areas along the ship's length (for example with Simpson's rules) and multiplying by water density — about 1.025 t/m³ for seawater. The change in displacement per centimetre of immersion (tonnes per centimetre, TPC) is used in loading calculations, and hydrostatic curves relate displacement to draught.

Naval usage

For warships, displacement is the standard measure of size: battleships of the treaty era were limited to 35,000 long tons standard displacement, and modern aircraft carriers displace around 100,000 tonnes at full load. Submarines quote surfaced and submerged displacements, the latter including the water in the main ballast tanks.
