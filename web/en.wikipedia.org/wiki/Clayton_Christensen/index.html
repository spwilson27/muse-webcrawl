Clayton Christensen

Born: April 6, 1952, Salt Lake City, Utah, U.S.

Died: January 23, 2020 (aged 67), Boston, Massachusetts, U.S.

Occupation: Academic, author, business consultant

Known for: Theory of disruptive innovation

Clayton Magleby Christensen (April 6, 1952 – January 23, 2020) was an American academic and business consultant who developed the theory of "disruptive innovation", first introduced in his 1997 book The Innovator's Dilemma. Christensen was the Kim B. Clark Professor of Business Administration at the Harvard Business School and was also a leader and writer in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.

Christensen was also a co-founder of Rose Park Advisors, a venture capital firm, and Innosight, a management consulting and investment firm specializing in innovation.

Early life and education

Christensen was born in Salt Lake City, Utah, the second of eight children. He grew up in Salt Lake City and later in Rose Park. He studied economics at Brigham Young University, graduating summa cum laude in 1975, and then studied applied econometrics at the University of Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, receiving an MPhil in 1977. He earned an MBA with high distinction from Harvard Business School in 1979 and a DBA in 1992.

Career

Before joining the Harvard faculty, Christensen worked as a consultant and project manager with the Boston Consulting Group and served in the Reagan administration as a White House Fellow. From 1984 to 1991 he was chairman and president of Ceramics Process Systems Corporation (now CPS Technologies), a company he co-founded with several MIT professors.

Disruptive innovation

Christensen is regarded as the world's foremost authority on disruptive innovation, a theory describing how large, successful companies can fail by doing everything "right" — concentrating on their most profitable customers and on sustaining improvements — while smaller entrants with simpler, cheaper offerings gain a foothold in low-end or new markets and eventually displace them. His later work extended the idea to national economies, healthcare, and education, including The Innovator's Prescription and Disrupting Class.

His final major book, The Prosperity Paradox (2019), co-authored with Efosa Ojomo and Karen Dillon, argued that market-creating innovations are the most reliable path to prosperity in developing economies.

Personal life and death

Christensen married Christine Quinn in 1976; they had five children. A devout member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, he served in a number of church leadership roles. He died on January 23, 2020, in Boston, from complications of leukemia.
