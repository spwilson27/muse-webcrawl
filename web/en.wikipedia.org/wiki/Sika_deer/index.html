The sika deer (Cervus nippon), also known as the spotted deer or Japanese deer, is a species of deer native to much of East Asia. It is one of the few deer species that retains its white spots into adulthood. Medium-sized — adults stand roughly 50–110 cm at the shoulder — sika are highly adaptable forest herbivores and are famous for the bowing deer of Nara Park in Japan, where they are protected as natural monuments and messengers of the gods.

A sika stag; the species keeps its spots as an adult, unlike most deer.

Distribution and introductions

Sika once ranged from Vietnam to the Russian Far East, but hunting and habitat loss eliminated them from much of the mainland range, and several subspecies are now endangered; the species survives most abundantly in Japan. Sika have been widely introduced elsewhere — to Britain and Ireland, mainland Europe, New Zealand, Australia and the United States — where they are often treated as invasive: they damage forestry and native woodland through over-browsing and bark-stripping, and in Britain they hybridise with native red deer, threatening the red deer's genetic integrity.

Behaviour

Sika are active mainly at dawn and dusk. During the autumn rut, stags hold harems and advertise with a distinctive repertoire of whistles, squeaks and groans. Velvet from growing antlers has historically been prized in traditional Chinese medicine, and sika are farmed for it in China and New Zealand. In overpopulated Japanese forests, sika overgrazing is now a major conservation problem of the opposite kind.
