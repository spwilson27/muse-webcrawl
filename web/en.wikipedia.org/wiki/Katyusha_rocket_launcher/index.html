The Katyusha (Russian: Катюша) is a family of Soviet multiple rocket launchers first used in the Second World War. The best-known type, the truck-mounted BM-13, could fire a salvo of sixteen 132 mm rockets in seconds, delivering a devastating if inaccurate area bombardment. "Katyusha" became the generic name — and later a term applied to simple rocket artillery worldwide.

Development and use

The launcher grew out of Soviet rocket research in the 1930s and was approved for production on 21 June 1941, the day before Germany invaded the USSR. Its first combat use came on 14 July 1941 at Orsha in Belarus, where an experimental battery under Captain Ivan Flerov destroyed a concentration of German troops at the railway station. The weapon's howling sound unnerved enemy soldiers, who nicknamed it "Stalin's organ".

The nickname "Katyusha" — a diminutive of Ekaterina, from a popular song — was reportedly taken from factory workers who marked the first launchers with the name; soldiers also painted it on their vehicles. The launchers were cheap and quick to produce, mounted on trucks (including American Studebakers supplied under Lend-Lease), tanks and even sledges. Larger BM-30 "Andryusha" systems fired 300 mm rockets.

Legacy

More than 10,000 launchers were built during the war. The Katyusha's success established the multiple rocket launcher as a standard artillery weapon, leading to systems such as the Soviet BM-21 Grad and copies used around the world; the name is still applied to improvised rockets fired by non-state groups.
