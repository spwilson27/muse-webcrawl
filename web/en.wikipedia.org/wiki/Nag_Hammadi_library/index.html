The Nag Hammadi library (also known as the Chenoboskion Manuscripts and the Gnostic Gospels) is a collection of early Christian and Gnostic texts discovered near the Upper Egyptian town of Nag Hammadi in 1945. Thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices buried in a sealed jar were found by local farmer Muhammad al-Samman. The writings in these codices comprise fifty-two mostly Gnostic treatises, including the complete text of the Gospel of Thomas, previously known only from fragments. The manuscripts, translated from Greek originals into Coptic, were probably copied in the third and fourth centuries and buried near the Pachomian monasteries, possibly in response to Athanasius of Alexandria's Festal Letter of 367 CE condemning non-canonical books.

A codex of the Nag Hammadi library, now in the Coptic Museum in Cairo.

Discovery

In December 1945, Muhammad al-Samman and his brothers, digging for fertiliser (sabakh) at the foot of the Jabal al-Tarif cliff near the ancient site of Chenoboskion, unearthed a large earthenware jar containing the codices. Fearing a curse, Muhammad hesitated to break it open; some leaves were reportedly burned as fuel by his mother before the manuscripts passed through dealers to antiquities authorities. Codex III was acquired by the Coptic Museum in 1946, and the so-called Jung Codex (Codex I) was bought for the C. G. Jung Institute in Zurich in 1952 through the efforts of scholar Gilles Quispel. Publication was slow — delayed by ownership disputes, the Suez crisis, and scholarly rivalries — until a UNESCO-backed project produced a facsimile edition in the 1970s and the complete English translation edited by James M. Robinson in 1977.

Contents

The codices contain 52 tractates (with some duplicates, 46 distinct works). Major texts include:

  • The Gospel of Thomas — a sayings gospel (Codex II)
  • The Gospel of Philip and the Gospel of Truth
  • The Apocryphon of John — a Sethian Gnostic revelation dialogue, in three copies
  • The Hypostasis of the Archons and On the Origin of the World
  • The Thunder, Perfect Mind — a striking first-person aretalogy
  • Valentinian works such as the Tripartite Tractate (Codex I)
  • Non-Gnostic material: a Coptic excerpt from Plato's Republic and Hermetic writings including a fragment of the Asclepius

Significance

Before 1945, Gnosticism was known mainly through the hostile reports of heresiologists such as Irenaeus. The Nag Hammadi texts gave scholars the Gnostics' own voices, transforming the study of early Christianity, late-antique religion, and the formation of the biblical canon, and they remain central to debates about Christian diversity in the second to fourth centuries. Elaine Pagels's popular study The Gnostic Gospels (1979) brought the library to wide public attention.
