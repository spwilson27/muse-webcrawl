Chromatic aberration

Chromatic aberration

CauseDispersion: refractive index varies with wavelength
TypesLongitudinal (axial), lateral (transverse)

Chromatic aberration is a type of optical distortion in which a lens fails to focus all colours of light to the same convergence point, because the refractive index of glass varies with wavelength (dispersion). The result is visible colour fringing — purple, red or green halos around high-contrast edges — particularly in photographs and telescopes.

Types

Longitudinal (axial) aberration occurs when different wavelengths focus at different distances along the optical axis; short (blue) wavelengths are refracted more strongly than long (red) ones. It can be reduced by stopping down the lens. Lateral (transverse) aberration causes different wavelengths to be magnified differently, producing radial colour fringes that worsen toward the image edges; stopping down does not remove it.

Correction

Achromatic doublets combine positive and negative elements of glasses with different dispersion (crown and flint) to bring two wavelengths to a common focus; apochromatic lenses correct three wavelengths. Mirror telescopes avoid the problem entirely since mirrors reflect all wavelengths equally. In digital photography, much residual fringing is removed in software, and smartphone computational imaging routinely corrects it. Intentional chromatic aberration is also emulated in video games and cinema as a stylistic effect.
