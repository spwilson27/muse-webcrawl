Umbrian school

The Umbrian school was a group of Italian painters of the Renaissance who worked in Umbria, in central Italy, during the late 15th and early 16th centuries. Centred on Perugia, the school was noted for its clear, luminous landscapes, serene religious feeling, and graceful figures set in spacious, airy settings.

Artists

The leading figure of the school was Pietro Perugino (c. 1450–1523), whose balanced compositions and sweet-faced Madonnas made him one of the most sought-after painters in Italy around 1500. His most famous pupil was Raphael, who absorbed the Umbrian clarity of design before developing his mature style in Florence and Rome. Other painters associated with the school include Pinturicchio (Bernardino di Betto), whose decorative fresco cycles enlivened chapels in Rome and Siena; Lo Spagna (Giovanni di Pietro); Fiorenzo di Lorenzo; and Matteo da Gualdo. Benedetto Bonfigli is often regarded as an early master of the Perugian tradition.

Characteristics

Umbrian painting is characterised by transparent, light-filled backgrounds that recede in gentle hills and umbrella pines, a legacy of the region's own landscape. Figures are typically calm and devout, with soft modelling and a lyrical, almost melancholic mood, as opposed to the more dramatic, sculptural style of contemporary Florentine painting. The school greatly influenced the formation of the High Renaissance through Raphael.
