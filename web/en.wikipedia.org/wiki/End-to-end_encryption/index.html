End-to-end encryption

End-to-end encryption (E2EE) is a system of communication where only the communicating users can read the messages. The data is encrypted on the sender's device and decrypted only on the recipient's device, so that eavesdroppers, including telecommunications providers, internet service providers, and even the provider of the communication service itself, cannot access the cryptographic keys needed to decrypt the conversation.

How it works

In an end-to-end encrypted system, each participant holds a pair of cryptographic keys: a public key, which is shared and used by others to encrypt messages to them, and a private key, which is kept secret on the device and used to decrypt received messages. Because decryption keys never leave the endpoints, intermediaries that relay the ciphertext learn nothing about its contents.

Modern implementations, such as the Signal Protocol, combine the Extended Triple Diffie-Hellman (X3DH) key agreement protocol with a Double Ratchet algorithm, providing forward secrecy and post-compromise security: even if a device key is later compromised, previously sent messages remain protected.

History

The concept originated with public-key cryptography in the 1970s. Phil Zimmermann's PGP (1991) brought end-to-end encryption to email. In the 2010s, default end-to-end encryption spread to consumer messaging: WhatsApp completed its rollout of the Signal Protocol to over a billion users in 2016, followed by services such as Signal, iMessage, and Facebook Messenger's "secret conversations".

Security considerations

End-to-end encryption protects message content in transit and at rest on servers, but it does not by itself hide metadata such as who communicated with whom and when. Endpoint security remains critical: malware on a device can read messages before encryption or after decryption. Many systems let users verify keys out of band (for example by comparing "safety numbers") to guard against man-in-the-middle attacks in which a server substitutes keys.

Debate

Law-enforcement agencies in several countries have argued that widespread E2EE hampers investigations, proposing mechanisms such as key escrow, "ghost" participants, or client-side scanning. Cryptographers and civil-liberties groups counter that any exceptional-access mechanism weakens security for all users. Regulations such as the EU's proposed "Chat Control" measures have kept the debate prominent.
