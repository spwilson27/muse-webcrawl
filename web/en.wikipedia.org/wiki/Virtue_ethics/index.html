Virtue ethics

Virtue ethics is a family of normative ethical theories that emphasise the character of the moral agent rather than rules (as in Deontology) or consequences (as in Consequentialism). Its classical source is Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, which defines virtue as a settled disposition to feel and act rightly, lying in a mean between excess and deficiency, and aimed at eudaimonia — flourishing over a whole life. Practical wisdom (phronesis) is the virtue of judging rightly in particular cases.

History and revival

Virtue-centred ethics were also developed in the Confucian tradition, which prizes ren (benevolence) and ritual propriety, and in medieval Christian thought, where Aquinas combined the Aristotelian cardinal virtues with the theological virtues of faith, hope and charity. Rule- and consequence-based theories dominated modern moral philosophy until G. E. M. Anscombe's 1958 paper "Modern Moral Philosophy" argued for a return to Aristotle; Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre (After Virtue, 1981) and others developed the revival.

Concepts and criticism

Contemporary virtue ethics debates lists of the virtues, the unity of the virtues, whether virtues are global character traits (challenged by situationist psychology), and how virtue theory guides action in applied fields such as medical and business ethics. Neo-Aristotelian versions ground the virtues in human nature and flourishing; agent-based versions, such as Michael Slote's, ground rightness directly in virtuous motivation.
