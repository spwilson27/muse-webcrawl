Advanced life support

Image placeholder: an ALS resuscitation in progress

Advanced life support (ALS) is a set of life-saving protocols and skills extending beyond basic life support (BLS), used by trained healthcare professionals — paramedics, nurses and physicians — to treat cardiopulmonary arrest and other medical emergencies. In North America the equivalent scope of practice is often called advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) when focused on cardiac arrest.

Components

ALS interventions typically include advanced airway management such as endotracheal intubation and supraglottic airway devices; cardiac rhythm monitoring and ECG interpretation; defibrillation and transcutaneous pacing; intravenous or intraosseous access; administration of drugs such as adrenaline (epinephrine), amiodarone and fluids; and identification and treatment of reversible causes of arrest (the "four Hs and four Ts": hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypo/hyperkalaemia and metabolic disorders, hypothermia; tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins and thromboembolism).

Algorithms and training

Resuscitation councils — including the European Resuscitation Council, the Resuscitation Council UK and the American Heart Association — publish ALS algorithms and run standardised courses covering the systematic ABCDE assessment, post-resuscitation care and management of peri-arrest arrhythmias. Certification usually requires periodic renewal.

Pre-hospital and in-hospital use

In many emergency medical systems, ALS is delivered by paramedic crews, sometimes with physician response units, while in hospitals it is provided by medical emergency and cardiac arrest teams. Evidence for the benefit of individual ALS interventions varies: high-quality chest compressions, early defibrillation and treatment of reversible causes have the clearest support.
