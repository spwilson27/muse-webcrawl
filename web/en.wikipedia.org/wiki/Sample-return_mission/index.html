Sample-return mission

TypeSpaceflight mission category
PurposeCollect material from another body and return it to Earth
First successApollo 11 (1969), lunar samples
TargetsMoon, asteroids, comets, Mars (planned)

A sample-return mission is a spacecraft mission to collect samples of material from an astronomical body — such as the Moon, an asteroid, a comet, or the solar wind — and return them to Earth for laboratory analysis. Returned samples allow measurements far more precise than remote instruments permit, including isotopic dating and the detection of organic compounds.

History

The first sample returns were the crewed Apollo missions (1969–1972), which brought back 382 kg of lunar rock and soil. The Soviet Luna programme returned small lunar samples robotically in the 1970s. Later robotic missions include NASA's Genesis (solar wind particles, 2004), Stardust (comet Wild 2 dust, 2006), JAXA's Hayabusa (asteroid Itokawa, 2010) and Hayabusa2 (asteroid Ryugu, 2020), China's Chang'e 5 (2020) and Chang'e 6 (2024, the first samples from the far side of the Moon), and NASA's OSIRIS-REx, which returned material from asteroid Bennu in 2023.

Techniques

Sample collection methods range from surface scoops and drills to touch-and-go sampling devices and aerogel collectors for high-speed dust. The return leg requires a capsule able to survive hypersonic atmospheric entry; samples are then curated in specialised facilities under nitrogen and strict contamination controls, with planetary protection protocols applied where a risk of biological contamination is conceivable.

Scientific value

Lunar samples established the Moon's age and the giant-impact hypothesis context, while asteroid samples from Ryugu and Bennu have revealed abundant carbon-rich material and amino acids, informing models of how the building blocks of life reached Earth. Sample return to Mars, long planned jointly by NASA and ESA and under restructuring, remains a central goal of planetary science.
