Theta wave

FrequencyCommonly about 4–8 Hz (definitions vary)
Recorded byEEG, MEG and intracranial electrodes
Prominent inHippocampus during movement and REM sleep; cortex in drowsiness and memory tasks
Related rhythmsAlpha, beta, gamma, delta

Theta waves or theta rhythms are neural oscillations in roughly the 4–8 hertz band. They are especially prominent in the hippocampus of mammals during locomotion, exploratory behaviour and rapid-eye-movement sleep, and they appear in human scalp electroencephalography during drowsiness, memory encoding, meditation-like states and some pathologies. The label describes frequency, not a single mechanism: hippocampal theta, frontal midline theta and developmental cortical theta have different generators and functions.

Hippocampal theta

In rats and other rodents, hippocampal theta is a large, regular local field rhythm present when the animal walks, runs, rears or attends to stimuli. It depends on inputs from the medial septum and diagonal band, entorhinal cortex and brainstem arousal systems. Two pharmacological types were historically distinguished: movement-related theta resistant to atropine and an atropine-sensitive type associated with immobility under some conditions. Modern work treats theta as a dynamic coordination signal rather than a mere correlate of movement.

Theta organizes neuronal firing in time. Place cells fire at progressively earlier phases of successive theta cycles as an animal crosses a place field—phase precession—compressing spatial sequences into short windows suitable for synaptic plasticity. Sharp-wave ripples during rest and sleep, gamma oscillations nested in theta, and theta sequences during behaviour are all studied as mechanisms for memory consolidation and planning.

Human theta

Human hippocampal theta is less continuous and often slower or burst-like than rodent theta, reflecting differences in behaviour, recording method and perhaps neural organization. Intracranial studies link theta power and phase to successful encoding and retrieval of episodes and navigation. Scalp EEG frontal midline theta increases with working-memory load, cognitive control and error monitoring, likely involving medial frontal cortex.

In children, posterior theta can be normal; in adults, excessive diffuse theta in wakefulness may indicate drowsiness or encephalopathy. Clinicians interpret theta in context with age, state, medication and the rest of the EEG rather than as a diagnosis by itself.

Methods and cautions

Theta is measured in the local field potential, EEG or magnetoencephalography and analysed with spectral power, phase coherence and cross-frequency coupling. Volume conduction, muscle artefact and reference choice can mimic or mask rhythms. Researchers increasingly combine oscillation measures with single-unit firing and causal perturbations to avoid treating every spectral peak as a functional clock.

Significance

Theta rhythms provide a window into how brains coordinate distributed activity across time. They connect cellular excitability to behaviour: navigation, learning, sleep and attention. The same frequency band can serve different computations in different circuits, so the scientific question is not simply whether theta is present, but which structures generate it, what phase relations it creates and how those relations change with task demands.
