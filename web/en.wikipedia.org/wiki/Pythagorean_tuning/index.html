Pythagorean tuning is a system of musical tuning in which the frequency ratios of all intervals are based on the ratio 3:2, the perfect fifth. This system is sometimes called 3-limit just intonation, because its intervals are generated by stacking perfect fifths and octaves alone. It is ascribed to the ancient Greek philosopher Pythagoras, though similar systems arose independently in China.

Construction

Starting from a reference note, each successive note of the scale is reached by multiplying by 3/2 (going up a fifth) or 2/3, with multiplication or division by 2 used to fold notes back into a single octave. Twelve fifths, however, do not equal seven octaves: (3/2)12 exceeds 27 by the small interval known as the Pythagorean comma, about 23.46 cents. Consequently, one fifth in any closed twelve-note Pythagorean system must be narrowed by that comma, producing the harsh "wolf fifth."

Intervals

Pythagorean major thirds are formed from four fifths minus two octaves, giving the ratio 81:64 (about 408 cents)—noticeably sharper than the pure 5:4 major third (386 cents). This sharpness, the syntonic comma discrepancy, is the system's chief drawback for harmony rich in thirds, and it was a principal reason later musicians adopted meantone temperaments and eventually equal temperament. Pythagorean fifths and fourths, by contrast, are acoustically pure, and the tuning remained standard in Western theory through the Middle Ages and into the Renaissance for melodic and organum contexts.
