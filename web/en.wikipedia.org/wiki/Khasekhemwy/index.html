Khasekhemwy

Khasekhemwy (c. 2690 BC) was the last pharaoh of the Second Dynasty of Egypt. He succeeded Khasekhem, with whom he is often identified: many Egyptologists believe Khasekhem changed his name to Khasekhemwy ("the Two Powers have arisen") after reuniting Upper and Lower Egypt following a civil war. His reign brought the conflicts of the late Second Dynasty to an end and paved the way for the Old Kingdom.

Reign

Khasekhemwy is unique among Egyptian kings in bearing both the falcon of Horus and the animal of Set atop his serekh, surmounted by a crown — a symbol interpreted as the reconciliation of the two gods, and of the two lands of Egypt. Inscriptions record a campaign against "northern enemies", and the bases of his statues from Hierakonpolis depict slain northerners, with a tally sometimes read as 47,209 enemies.

He undertook major building works, including a vast mudbrick funerary enclosure at Hierakonpolis known as "the Fort", and a stone enclosure at Abydos, the Shunet el-Zebib, which still stands. His tomb at Umm el-Qa'ab (Tomb V) is the largest and most complex of the early dynastic royal tombs, built partly of stone — an innovation foreshadowing the Step Pyramid of his probable son and successor Djoser.

Family

His wife Nimaathap was honoured as "Mother of the King's Children" and is often regarded as the mother of Djoser, founder of the Third Dynasty, which would make Khasekhemwy the link between the Second and Third Dynasties.
