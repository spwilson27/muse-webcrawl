Alfred Russel Wallace

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913) was a British naturalist, explorer and geographer who independently conceived the theory of evolution by natural selection. His paper on the subject, sent from the Malay Archipelago in 1858, prompted Charles Darwin to publish On the Origin of Species; the two men's ideas were presented jointly that year. Wallace is also regarded as the father of biogeography for his work on the distribution of animal species.

Life and travels

Wallace earned his living collecting specimens: first in the Amazon basin (1848–1852, where most of his collection was lost in a ship fire), then for eight years in the Malay Archipelago, where he collected over 125,000 specimens and identified the faunal divide now called the Wallace Line, separating Asian from Australasian animal groups. His book The Malay Archipelago (1869) remains a classic of travel and natural history.

Contributions and later life

Beyond natural selection, Wallace founded the study of animal distribution, wrote early warnings about human-caused extinction and deforestation, and campaigned on social issues including land reform and women's suffrage; he also embraced spiritualism, which distanced him from parts of the scientific establishment. He received the Royal Society's Copley Medal in 1908. Historians debate why Darwin overshadowed him — class, publicity and Wallace's own modesty all played parts.
