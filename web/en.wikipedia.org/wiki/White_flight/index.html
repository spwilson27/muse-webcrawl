White flight

White flight, also known as white exodus, is the sudden or gradual large-scale migration of white people from areas becoming more racially or ethnoculturally diverse. In the United States the term is most associated with the movement of white residents from cities to suburbs in the decades after the Second World War.

Causes

Historians and sociologists point to a combination of factors: federally subsidised mortgages and highway construction that made suburban homes cheap for white buyers; racially restrictive covenants and redlining that kept the new suburbs white; blockbusting by estate agents; anxiety over desegregation of schools following Brown v. Board of Education (1954); and the migration of Black Americans to northern and western cities during the Great Migration.

Effects

White flight drained city tax bases, accelerated urban disinvestment, and reinforced residential and school segregation. Many metropolitan areas became divided between largely Black, poorer central cities and largely white, wealthier suburbs. Scholars also use the term for related patterns elsewhere, including the departure of white residents from parts of post-apartheid South African cities and from some European neighbourhoods.

Recent trends

Since the 1990s, some American city centres have experienced gentrification and a partial return of affluent white residents, reversing the mid-century pattern in certain districts, while suburban areas have themselves become more diverse.
