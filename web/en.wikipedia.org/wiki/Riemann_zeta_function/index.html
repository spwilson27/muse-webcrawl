Riemann zeta function

The Riemann zeta function, ζ(s), is a function of a complex variable s that analytically continues the sum of the infinite series Σ 1/ns, which converges when the real part of s is greater than 1. It is named after Bernhard Riemann, whose 1859 memoir "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude" established its deep connection to the distribution of prime numbers.

ζ(s) = Σn=1∞ n−s = Πp prime (1 − p−s)−1

Euler product and analytic continuation

Leonhard Euler showed in the 18th century that the series equals an infinite product over all primes, linking ζ(s) to prime numbers. Riemann extended ζ(s) by analytic continuation to a meromorphic function on the whole complex plane, with a single simple pole at s = 1 with residue 1, and proved a functional equation relating ζ(s) to ζ(1 − s). Special values include ζ(2) = π²/6 (the Basel problem) and ζ(−1) = −1/12.

Zeros and the Riemann hypothesis

The function has "trivial" zeros at the negative even integers −2, −4, −6, … All other zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < Re(s) < 1. The Riemann hypothesis — one of the Millennium Prize Problems — asserts that every non-trivial zero has real part exactly 1/2. The first non-trivial zeros lie at s ≈ 1/2 ± 14.134725i. The hypothesis has been verified numerically for trillions of zeros but remains unproved.

Applications

Beyond analytic number theory, the zeta function appears in probability, statistics, and physics, and its generalisations — Dirichlet L-functions and automorphic L-functions — sit at the centre of the Langlands program.
