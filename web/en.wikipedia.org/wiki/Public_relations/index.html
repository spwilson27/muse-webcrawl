Public relations

Public relations (PR) is the practice of managing the spread of information between an organisation or individual and the public, with the aim of building and maintaining a favourable public image. Public relations professionals shape how a client is perceived through media coverage, public events, speeches, publications and, increasingly, social media, rather than through paid advertising space.

Definitions

Professional bodies such as the Public Relations Society of America define public relations as a strategic communication process that builds mutually beneficial relationships between organisations and their publics. The field overlaps with marketing, advertising and advocacy, but is distinguished by its reliance on earned media — coverage obtained without payment — and on reputation management over the long term.

History

The modern profession emerged in the early twentieth century. Ivy Lee, often called the father of modern public relations, issued a "Declaration of Principles" in 1906 promising accurate information to the press, and advised industrial clients such as the Rockefellers. Edward Bernays, a nephew of Sigmund Freud, applied psychology and propaganda techniques to consumer campaigns and set out the theory of the field in his 1928 book Propaganda. Governments institutionalised public information work during the two world wars, and the post-war growth of corporations, television and consumer culture made PR a standard corporate function.

Methods

Common techniques include press releases, press conferences, media kits, product launches, sponsorships, public appearances, newsletters and social media management. Practitioners cultivate relationships with journalists, monitor coverage, and prepare executives for interviews. Specialisms include financial PR, public affairs (engaging with government), internal communications and community relations. In political campaigns, PR techniques overlap with grassroots lobbying and "spin" — the strategic framing of events for media consumption.

Crisis communication

A major branch of the discipline, crisis communication, deals with protecting an organisation's reputation during emergencies such as product recalls, industrial accidents or scandals. Standard doctrine emphasises responding quickly, acknowledging facts, expressing concern for those affected and demonstrating corrective action; delayed or misleading responses are considered especially damaging.

Ethics and criticism

Critics associate public relations with manipulation, euphemism and the manufacturing of consent, pointing to campaigns run on behalf of tobacco companies, authoritarian governments and polluting industries. Professional associations maintain codes of ethics requiring honesty and disclosure of clients, though enforcement is limited and the term "PR" itself has acquired negative connotations — a failed effort is often dismissed as "just a PR stunt".
