In radio communications, a radio receiver, also known as a receiver, a wireless, or simply a radio, is an electronic device that receives radio waves and converts the information carried by them to a usable form. It is used with an antenna. The antenna intercepts radio waves (electromagnetic waves of radio frequency) and converts them to tiny alternating currents which are applied to the receiver, and the receiver extracts the desired information.

The receiver uses electronic filters to separate the desired radio-frequency signal from all the other signals picked up by the antenna, and an electronic amplifier to increase the power of the signal for further processing, and finally recovers the desired information through demodulation.

Types

The earliest receivers were crystal sets, which used a galena crystal detector and required no power source beyond the signal itself. Tuned radio frequency (TRF) receivers amplified the signal at its original frequency through several stages. Edwin Armstrong's superheterodyne design, patented in 1918, converts the incoming signal to a fixed intermediate frequency (IF) where most amplification and filtering occur; it remains the dominant architecture in analogue receivers.

Modern receivers increasingly use direct conversion and software-defined radio (SDR), in which analogue-to-digital converters digitize the signal and digital signal processing performs filtering and demodulation.

Key characteristics

Receiver performance is described by sensitivity (the weakest usable signal), selectivity (the ability to reject adjacent channels), dynamic range, and image rejection. Demodulators recover amplitude modulation (AM), frequency modulation (FM), single sideband (SSB), and digital formats.
