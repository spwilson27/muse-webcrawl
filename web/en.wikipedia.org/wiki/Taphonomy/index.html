Taphonomy

Taphonomy is the study of how organisms decay and become fossilised, and more broadly of everything that happens to organic remains between an organism's death and their recovery by a researcher. The term (from Greek taphos, "burial", and nomos, "law") was coined in 1940 by the Russian palaeontologist Ivan Efremov.

Scope

Taphonomy distinguishes the history of an assemblage of remains: processes before burial (sometimes called biostratinomy) include death, scavenging, disarticulation, transport, weathering, and trampling; processes after burial (diagenesis) include compaction, permineralisation, dissolution, and recrystallisation that transform remains into fossils. Because these processes filter and bias what survives, taphonomists emphasise that the fossil record is not a census of past life but a heavily edited sample — soft-bodied organisms, juveniles, and organisms living away from depositional environments are systematically underrepresented.

Applications

In palaeontology, taphonomic analysis helps reconstruct palaeoenvironments and distinguish genuine ecological signals from preservational artefacts; exceptional deposits that preserve soft tissues are called Lagerstätten. In archaeology and zooarchaeology, taphonomy distinguishes human butchery marks from carnivore gnawing and natural breakage, informing debates such as whether early hominins hunted or scavenged. In forensic science, forensic taphonomy studies the decomposition and dispersal of human remains to estimate time since death and reconstruct events at a scene.

Experimental taphonomy

Modern taphonomists conduct actualistic experiments — monitoring carcasses as they decay, tumbling bones in flumes, or burying specimens — to calibrate how particular marks and patterns form, a uniformitarian approach pioneered in African savanna studies of bone assemblages in the 1970s–80s.
