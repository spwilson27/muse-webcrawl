Bordeaux is a city on the Garonne River in southwestern France and the prefecture of the Gironde department and the Nouvelle-Aquitaine region. It is world-famous as the capital of one of the greatest wine regions on earth, and its historic centre — the Port of the Moon — is a UNESCO World Heritage Site for its outstanding 18th-century urban ensemble.

History

Founded as the Celtic settlement of Burdigala and developed under Rome, Bordeaux prospered on trade with Britain and northern Europe. From 1154, under Henry II of England and Eleanor of Aquitaine, it belonged to the English crown for three centuries, during which the export of its wines to England became the basis of its wealth; it returned to France after the Battle of Castillon in 1453. The 18th century was its golden age: colonial trade, including the slave trade, financed the grand quays, the Place de la Bourse and the Grand Théâtre. In the 20th century the French government retreated to Bordeaux in both World Wars, and in 1970 it briefly became the seat of government again during a crisis. Major urban renewal under Mayor Alain Juppé from the 1990s — cleaning the limestone facades, restoring the quays and building a tramway — revived the city.

Wine

The Bordeaux vineyard, planted along the Garonne, Dordogne and their estuary, the Gironde, produces some 700 million bottles a year, overwhelmingly red blends of Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc, plus prestigious whites and the sweet wines of Sauternes. Famous districts include the Médoc, Saint-Émilion (itself a World Heritage Site), Pomerol, Graves and Margaux; the Bordeaux Wine Official Classification of 1855 still shapes the prestige of the great châteaux.

Landmarks

Sights include the Place de la Bourse with its Miroir d'eau reflecting pool, the Gothic cathedral of Saint-André and the Tour Pey-Berland, the medieval Porte Cailhau and Grosse Cloche, and the Cité du Vin wine museum (opened 2016). The university and a large student population give the city a youthful character.
