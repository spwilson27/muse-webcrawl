Deed of trust

[Image: A deed of trust document]
A deed of trust used in a real estate transaction

A deed of trust is a legal instrument used in real property transactions in many jurisdictions of the United States, in which the legal title to property is transferred to a trustee, who holds it as security for a loan (a debt) between a borrower and a lender. The equitable title remains with the borrower. It functions much like a mortgage, but involves three parties instead of two: the trustor (borrower), the beneficiary (lender), and the trustee.

Structure and foreclosure

The deed of trust is recorded in the county land records where the property is located. It contains a power-of-sale clause allowing the trustee, upon default, to sell the property without court proceedings — a process called non-judicial foreclosure — which is typically much faster than the judicial foreclosure required for mortgages. When the loan is repaid in full, the beneficiary directs the trustee to execute a deed of reconveyance, returning legal title to the borrower.

Usage by jurisdiction

Deeds of trust are the standard security instrument in states including California, Texas, Virginia, Colorado and many other western and southern states, while mortgages predominate in others. In some states both instruments are used. Outside the United States, "deed of trust" may refer more generally to a document establishing a trust, such as a charitable trust deed.
