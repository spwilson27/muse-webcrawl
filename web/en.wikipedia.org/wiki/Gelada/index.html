The gelada (Theropithecus gelada, Amharic: ጭላዳ, č̣əlada), sometimes called the bleeding-heart monkey or the gelada baboon, is a species of Old World monkey found only in the Ethiopian Highlands, at elevations of roughly 1,800–4,400 metres. It is the only living member of the genus Theropithecus, a lineage once widespread across Africa and into Asia. Despite one of its common names, it is not a true baboon.

Description

Geladas are large, heavily built monkeys with shaggy brown coats. Adult males have a long cape of hair over the back and a distinctive hourglass-shaped patch of bare red skin on the chest, which gives rise to the name "bleeding-heart monkey". Females also have a chest patch, whose colour and swelling vary with reproductive state. Geladas are the most terrestrial of primates after humans: they spend most of the day sitting and shuffling along as they pluck grass with their remarkably dexterous hands and opposable thumbs.

Diet and behaviour

Uniquely among primates, geladas are primarily graminivores — grass makes up the great majority of their diet, supplemented by seeds, roots, and occasionally crops. At night they sleep on cliff ledges, climbing down to the grasslands in the morning. The basic social unit is a one-male group of females and young; these units aggregate into bands and herds that may number several hundred, among the largest aggregations of any primate. Geladas communicate with an unusually rich repertoire of vocalisations, including lip-flip gestures and contact calls sometimes compared to rudimentary speech.

Conservation

The species is listed as Least Concern, but its population, concentrated in places such as the Simien Mountains National Park, faces pressure from habitat conversion to farmland and from competition with livestock.
