Dinosaur tracks

Dinosaur tracks are fossil footprints left by dinosaurs in soft sediment that later lithified into rock. As trace fossils, they record the behaviour of the living animal rather than its body, and they form a major branch of the study of vertebrate ichnology. Trackways — sequences of footprints made by one animal — allow scientists to estimate speed, gait, posture, and sometimes group behaviour.

Track shape depends on foot anatomy, the softness of the substrate, and later erosion; a single animal can leave quite different impressions in mud, sand, or shallow water. Three-toed theropod tracks, broad four-toed sauropod tracks (with smaller crescent-shaped manus prints), and the hand-like prints of ornithopods are all classified by ichnogenera based on form rather than on the identity of the trackmaker.

Famous localities include Dinosaur Ridge and the Purgatoire River tracksite in Colorado, the Connecticut River Valley (where Edward Hitchcock in the 1830s mistook giant three-toed tracks for those of huge birds), Cal Orck'o in Bolivia, and extensive trackways in South Korea and China. Track evidence was pivotal in showing that sauropods walked on land with straight limbs rather than sprawling, and some parallel trackways have been interpreted as evidence of herding.
