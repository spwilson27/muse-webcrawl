The Bay of Pigs Invasion (Spanish: Invasión de Bahía de Cochinos) was a failed military landing operation on the southwestern coast of Cuba in April 1961 by the United States Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and the Cuban exile Brigade 2506, intended to overthrow the government of Fidel Castro. The invasion was defeated within three days by the Cuban Revolutionary Armed Forces, and most of the invaders were killed or captured.

Background

After Castro's revolution triumphed in 1959, relations with the United States collapsed amid land reform, nationalisations and Cuba's turn toward the Soviet Union. In March 1960 President Dwight D. Eisenhower authorised the CIA to train Cuban exiles for an invasion; the plan passed to President John F. Kennedy, who insisted the American role remain deniable and curtailed the air support on which the plan depended. The exiles — about 1,500 men — trained in Guatemala and embarked from Nicaragua.

The invasion

On 15 April, exile-piloted B-26 bombers painted in Cuban markings attacked Cuban airfields but failed to destroy Castro's small air force. In the early hours of 17 April the brigade landed at Playa Girón and Playa Larga in the Bay of Pigs, a remote swampy area chosen partly because it was thought defensible — but the coral reefs wrecked landing craft, and the causeways across the Zapata swamp channelled the invaders into kill zones. Surviving Cuban Sea Fury and T-33 aircraft, which the preliminary strike had missed, sank the supply ships Houston and Río Escondido, leaving the brigade short of ammunition. Castro personally directed the counter-attack, committing some 20,000–25,000 troops with Soviet-built tanks. Kennedy refused direct US intervention; a final unmarked US Navy air sortie arrived an hour out of sync with the exile bombers and was shot down. By 19–20 April the beachhead had collapsed: 114 exiles were dead and 1,202 captured.

Aftermath

The prisoners were ransomed in December 1962 for $53 million in food and medicine. The fiasco humiliated the Kennedy administration, strengthened Castro's position, pushed Cuba closer to the USSR — a dynamic that led to the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 — and prompted Kennedy to take greater control of subsequent covert action. A CIA inspector general's report, highly critical of the operation, remained classified for decades. In Cuba the victory at Playa Girón is commemorated as a landmark of national resistance.
