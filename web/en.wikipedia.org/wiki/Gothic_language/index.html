Gothic is an extinct East Germanic language spoken by the Goths. It is the Germanic language with the earliest extensive attestation, largely thanks to the 4th-century Bible translation by the bishop Ulfilas (Wulfila), who devised the Gothic alphabet for that purpose. Gothic is invaluable to comparative linguistics because it preserves archaic features lost in other Germanic languages.

History and attestation

The principal surviving text is the Codex Argenteus, a 6th-century manuscript of Ulfilas' Bible written in silver and gold ink on purple parchment, now held in Uppsala, Sweden. Other remains include the Skeireins, a commentary on the Gospel of John, legal documents from Italy, glosses, and a small corpus of Crimean Gothic words recorded in the 16th century by the Flemish diplomat Ogier de Busbecq — evidence that a form of Gothic survived in Crimea into the early modern period.

Phonology and grammar

Gothic retains features such as a dual number, reduplicating verbs in the past tense (as in Greek and Sanskrit), and a synthetic passive voice. Its sound system shows the effects of Grimm's law but not the High German consonant shift. Nouns decline in several stem classes across three genders, four cases, and two numbers.

Decline

With the fall of the Visigothic and Ostrogothic kingdoms and the assimilation of Gothic populations into Romance- and Slavic-speaking societies, the language died out in most regions by the 9th–10th centuries, lingering longest in Crimea.
