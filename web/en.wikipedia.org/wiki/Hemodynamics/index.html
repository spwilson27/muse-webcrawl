Hemodynamics

Hemodynamics or haemodynamics are the dynamics of blood flow. The circulatory system is controlled by homeostatic mechanisms of autoregulation, just as hydraulic circuits are controlled by control systems. The hemodynamic response continuously monitors and adjusts to conditions in the body and its environment. Hemodynamics explains the physical laws that govern the flow of blood in the blood vessels.

Blood flow ensures the transportation of nutrients, hormones, metabolic wastes, oxygen, and carbon dioxide throughout the body to maintain cell-level metabolism, the regulation of pH, osmotic pressure and temperature of the whole body, and the protection from microbial and mechanical harms.

Blood

Blood is a non-Newtonian fluid: its viscosity varies with the rate of shear. It is composed of plasma and formed elements — red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. The rheology of blood is determined by hematocrit, red cell deformability and aggregation, and plasma viscosity. Because of the Fahraeus–Lindqvist effect, the apparent viscosity of blood falls in small vessels as red cells migrate toward the centre of the stream.

Key parameters

  • Cardiac output (CO) — the volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute, the product of heart rate and stroke volume (normally about 5 L/min at rest).
  • Blood pressure — the pressure exerted by circulating blood on vessel walls, usually expressed as systolic over diastolic pressure; mean arterial pressure drives tissue perfusion.
  • Vascular resistance — systemic vascular resistance relates mean arterial pressure to cardiac output; resistance of a vessel depends strongly on its radius, following Poiseuille's law, in which flow varies with the fourth power of radius.
  • Compliance — the ability of a vessel to expand with increased pressure, greatest in the veins, which act as the capacitance vessels of the circulation.

Flow patterns

Blood flow in most vessels is laminar, with concentric layers of fluid moving at different velocities. Flow may become turbulent — as described by the Reynolds number — at high velocities, in large vessels, or downstream of narrowed valves and stenoses, producing murmurs and bruits audible with a stethoscope. Pulsatile arterial flow is shaped by the Windkessel effect of the elastic aorta and large arteries, which smooths the intermittent output of the heart into steadier peripheral flow.

Regulation

Hemodynamics is regulated by autonomic control of heart rate, contractility, and vascular tone, by baroreceptor reflexes, by circulating hormones such as adrenaline, angiotensin II, and vasopressin, and by local autoregulation in tissues such as the brain, heart, and kidneys, which maintain relatively constant flow over a range of pressures.

Clinical significance

Hemodynamic monitoring — measurement of pressures and flows using arterial lines, central venous catheters, pulmonary artery catheters, and echocardiography — is central to intensive care medicine and anaesthesia. Disturbances of hemodynamics underlie shock, heart failure, hypertension, and valvular heart disease.
