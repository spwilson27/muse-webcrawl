Long March

Date16 October 1934 – 22 October 1936
LocationChina
ParticipantsChinese Red Army (Chinese Communist Party)
OutcomeRed Army escapes encirclement and reaches Shaanxi; Mao Zedong consolidates leadership

The Long March (Chinese: 长征; pinyin: Chángzhēng) was a military retreat by the Chinese Red Army, the armed forces of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), from advancing Nationalist forces during the Chinese Civil War. Between October 1934 and October 1936, several Red Army groups abandoned their bases in the south and marched through some of the most difficult terrain in China to reach Shaanxi in the north-west. The most famous column, the First Front Army under Mao Zedong, covered roughly 9,000 km (about 6,000 miles) in some 370 days.

Background

Chiang Kai-shek's fifth encirclement campaign against the Jiangxi Soviet, using blockhouse tactics advised by German officers, forced the Communists to abandon their base in October 1934. Some 86,000 troops and party members broke out, carrying heavy equipment that slowed them disastrously; the crossing of the Xiang River cost perhaps half the column.

The march

At the Zunyi Conference in January 1935 Mao Zedong effectively took control of the march and redirected it toward lighter, mobile warfare. Celebrated episodes include the seizure of the Luding Bridge over the Dadu River, the crossing of the Jade Dragon Snow Mountains and the Zoigê marshes, where many died of hunger and exposure. Mao's forces reached northern Shaanxi in October 1935 with perhaps 7,000–8,000 survivors of the original column; other Red Army groups arrived the following year.

Legacy

Though a military retreat that cost the great majority of those who set out, the Long March became the founding legend of the CCP: it confirmed Mao's leadership, spread Communist organisation across rural China, and later served as a central symbol of endurance in party propaganda.
