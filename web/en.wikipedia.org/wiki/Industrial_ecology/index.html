Industrial ecology

Industrial ecology is the study of material and energy flows through industrial systems. It takes an analogy from natural ecosystems — in which the waste of one organism becomes food for another — and applies it to industry, seeking to design industrial processes, products, and networks so that resources are used efficiently and waste is minimised or reused as input elsewhere.

Concepts

Key tools and concepts include life-cycle assessment (evaluating a product's environmental impact from raw material extraction to disposal), material flow analysis, design for environment, dematerialization, and industrial symbiosis, in which geographically close firms exchange by-products, energy, and water. The best-known example of industrial symbiosis is the eco-industrial park at Kalundborg, Denmark, where a power station, refinery, and other plants exchange steam, gas, gypsum, and other flows.

History

The field coalesced in the late 1980s and early 1990s. A 1989 article by Robert Frosch and Nicholas Gallopoulos, "Strategies for Manufacturing," is often credited with popularising the idea of the industrial ecosystem. The Journal of Industrial Ecology was founded in 1997, and professional societies and university programmes followed.

Relation to the circular economy

Industrial ecology provides much of the analytical foundation for the broader "circular economy" agenda — keeping materials in use, designing out waste, and decoupling economic growth from resource consumption — though the two terms are not identical: industrial ecology is the scientific discipline, while the circular economy is a policy and business framework.
